Now think differently

Strategic thinking isn't about having the answer — it's about asking the right questions first.

Before you act, answer these: What is actually happening? What does your competitor know that they're responding to? Who's vulnerable to their offer and who's not? What can they do that you can't? What can you do that they can't?

Six Moves for Strategic Thinking

Click each move. These are the thinking tools that separate strategic leaders from tactical responders.

1. Diagnose Before Prescribing +

Why it matters: The urgent thing is rarely the real thing. You have to look deeper.

Surface problem: "Competitor is taking our customers with a 30% lower price."
Real diagnosis: "Our customers are becoming price-sensitive. That suggests either our differentiation isn't working, or the market has commoditized."

The benefit: Now you're solving the real problem, not the symptom.

2. Name the Strategic Choice: Show There Are Real Options +

Why it matters: Every strategic situation has 2-3 real options. Your job is to name them, not pretend there's only one path.

Option A: Match their price. Compete on cost. Option B: Differentiate further. Move upmarket. Own premium. Option C: Find a different market. Retreat and rebuild elsewhere.

The benefit: You're not reacting. You're choosing. And the team sees the logic behind your choice.

3. The Trade-Off Frame: Every Option Has Costs +

Why it matters: Leaders who pretend their choice has no costs lose trust when the costs appear. Name the trade-off upfront.

"Option A (match price) gives us competitive coverage. But it erodes margins and signals we're not confident in our differentiation."
"Option B (go premium) protects margins. But it means we'll lose some customers and cede market share."

The benefit: You're being honest. And you're showing you've thought through the consequences.

4. The Time Horizon: Short-Term vs Long-Term Tension +

Why it matters: Most strategic disagreements are really about time horizon. Some decisions look good short-term but hurt long-term, or vice versa.

Cutting price looks good short-term (we keep customers). Looks bad long-term (margins compress, we become a commodity).
Going premium looks bad short-term (we lose share). Looks good long-term (we own the premium space, margins stay strong).

The benefit: You're clear about what you're optimizing for. That clarity guides everything downstream.

5. Scenario Planning: If X Happens, We Do Y +

Why it matters: You can't predict the future. But you can think about contingencies. Scenario planning is contingency thinking.

"If our premium positioning works and we gain 15% margin improvement, we reinvest in product innovation."
"If they drop price further and we lose 20% of mid-market, we accelerate the premium-only strategy."

The benefit: You're not caught off-guard. You have decision rules ready.

6. The Strategic Question: Reframe the Problem +

Why it matters: The way you frame the problem determines the solution. Reframing the question often reveals new options.

Bad frame: "How do we respond to the competitor's lower price?"
Better frame: "The question isn't whether to cut price. The question is whether we compete on price or on something else. And if we compete on something else, what is it?"

The benefit: You're not trapped in their game. You're playing your game.

Strategic Analysis: Pick Your Situation

Pick one of three strategic scenarios. Deliver a 3-minute strategic analysis and recommendation. You have 3 minutes to prepare. Speak for 3 minutes.

A. Market Disruption

The situation: Your core market is being disrupted. A new technology is making your main offering obsolete in 18-24 months. You have good cash now, but limited time to pivot.

Your challenge: Diagnose what's really happening. Name the strategic choices. Show the trade-offs. Recommend a path. Explain why your path is better than the alternatives.

B. Talent Crisis

The situation: Your best people are leaving. They're going to competitors or startups. You can raise salaries, but your margins don't support 30% raises across the board.

Your challenge: Diagnose why people are really leaving. Is it money? Growth? Culture? How do you keep A-players without bankrupting the company?

C. Major Client Loss

The situation: Your largest client — 25% of revenue — is leaving. They're not unhappy. They're consolidating vendors. You can't change their mind.

Your challenge: What's the strategic response? Do you aggressively pursue new clients? Shift to retention of remaining ones? Move upmarket? Explain the logic of your choice.

Reflection & Language Bank

What Makes Strategic Thinking

Diagnosis first. Options second. Choice third. Most leaders skip diagnosis and jump to their preferred choice. That's not strategy — that's preference.

Strategic leaders show their work. They don't just say "Here's what we should do." They say "Here's what's actually happening. Here are the real options. Here's the trade-off. Here's why I'm choosing this one."
