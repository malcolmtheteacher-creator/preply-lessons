BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

First Man

The most watched moment in history, told from inside the helmet of a man who would not talk about it — and about the child he lost seven years before.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of First Man — a quiet engineer who survives one accident after another and ends up standing somewhere no one had ever stood — discover how much of it actually happened and where the film invents, and master the way English leaves words out and swaps them for short forms: ellipsis and substitution. It is the language of clipped cockpit talk, and it is what makes ordinary speech sound natural instead of laboured.
Before we begin — talk it through: Some people go quiet under pressure and some talk more. Which are you? Do you admire people who keep their feelings to themselves, or does it worry you? And how much risk is a great achievement worth?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. Her new colleague was taciturn: in three weeks of sharing an office he had offered perhaps forty words in total, and half of those were "morning".
taciturn (adj) — saying very little by nature; habitually silent or sparing with words.
2. For weeks after the funeral the neighbours kept leaving food on the step. They knew that in the first raw months of bereavement nobody remembers to cook.
bereavement (n) — the state or period of having lost someone close to you through death. The adjective is bereaved.
3. The race was gruelling — eleven hours across open sand in forty-degree heat. By the finish a third of the runners had dropped out and the rest could barely stand.
gruelling (adj) — extremely demanding and exhausting; punishing to get through.
4. The barrel came loose at the top of the hill and hurtled down the street, splintering a fence before anyone could get out of its way.
to hurtle (v) — to move very fast and with little or no control, in a way that feels dangerous.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. stoical is closest in meaning to: (a) complaining loudly   (b) enduring pain without showing it   (c) easily frightened
(b) enduring pain without showing it — stoical (adj) means accepting hardship calmly and without complaint. The noun is stoicism.
6. precarious is closest in meaning to: (a) unsteady and likely to fail   (b) settled and secure   (c) unusually expensive
(a) unsteady and likely to fail — precarious (adj) means held in place by very little; one small change and it collapses.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. Too heavy to climb over the mountains, the crew jettisoned the spare tanks and half the cargo into the sea.
To jettison something is: (a) to load it carefully; (b) to throw it off in order to save the rest; (c) to sell it quickly.
(b) — to jettison (v) — to throw something off a ship or aircraft, or to abandon it, in order to survive or move on.
8. They won by the narrowest margin in the club's history: one point, decided in the final second of the match.
Here a margin is: (a) the blank edge of a page; (b) a written rule; (c) the small amount by which one thing beats another, or the amount you have spare.
(c) — a margin (n) — the difference or the room to spare between success and failure. Compare a margin of error, a narrow margin.

D. Collocation choice — which word completes the phrase?

9. Even as the alarms sounded and the cabin filled with smoke, the captain never lost her ______ : her voice over the intercom stayed level and slow.
(a) composition   (b) composure   (c) compensation
(b) composure (n) — calm self-control when everything around you is going wrong. We keep, lose or regain our composure.
10. Every expedition sets out with a ______ plan: what we do if the weather turns, if someone breaks a leg, if the radio dies.
(a) contemporary   (b) continuous   (c) contingency
(c) contingency (n) — a possible future event, usually a bad one, that you prepare for in advance. Hence a contingency plan.

The film, in three parts

Damien Chazelle's First Man (2018), with Ryan Gosling as Neil Armstrong and Claire Foy as his wife Janet. Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A rocket plane and a hospital room

The film opens inside a helmet. It is 1961, and Neil Armstrong is a research pilot in the Californian desert, flying an experimental rocket plane so high that the sky outside the window turns black. Something goes wrong on the way down: the aircraft skips off the top of the atmosphere like a stone off water, and he has to fly a long, careful arc back to a dry lake bed. The camera almost never leaves the cockpit. We see rivets shaking and a strip of desert through a small window, and we hear metal under strain.

Then he drives home. His two-year-old daughter Karen has a tumour in her brain. He is an engineer, so he does what engineers do — he keeps a notebook, plots her treatments, tries to think his way to an answer. There is no answer. She dies. He shuts himself in a room alone, and the next morning he goes back to work.

Not long afterwards he applies to the astronaut programme. At the interview someone asks whether his daughter's death will affect what he does up there; he replies, flatly, that it would be unreasonable to assume it would not. That is very nearly the last time he mentions her out loud. The family move to Houston, to a street of identical houses full of other test pilots and other wives, and the funerals begin: a friend killed in a training flight, and then three men, including the neighbour across the road, killed in a fire during a ground test of a capsule that never left the pad.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What two things happen to Armstrong at the start of the film — one in the air, one at home?

2. How does he respond to his daughter's death, and how does the film show that?

1. He nearly loses control of an experimental rocket plane at the edge of the atmosphere; and his two-year-old daughter Karen dies of a brain tumour.

2. He treats it as a problem to be worked — notebooks, data — grieves alone behind a closed door, and then goes back to work. The film shows the grief almost entirely through silence and things left unsaid.

Discuss:
  • The film gives its hero almost no speeches. Does silence make a character more powerful on screen, or does it keep us at a distance? Which do you feel here?

Part 2 — Two accidents and one conversation

Armstrong is given command of a two-man mission whose job is to join two spacecraft together in orbit for the first time. The joining works. Minutes later the two craft begin to roll, and when they separate, the roll gets worse — not better. His capsule starts to spin, faster and faster, until the stars smear through the window and both men are close to blacking out. He shuts the whole steering system down and uses the small thrusters meant for coming home, spending most of that fuel to stop the spin. The mission is over almost before it began. Back on Earth some engineers wonder aloud whether the pilots caused it.

Then a second accident, this time a few metres above the desert: he is hovering in an ungainly training machine built to imitate a landing on the Moon when the controls give out. He ejects, the machine falls and burns, and his parachute puts him down in the dust a short walk away. He goes back to his office the same afternoon and carries on with the paperwork.

The hardest scene in the film is not in the air at all. On the night before he leaves for the Moon, Janet — who has watched him pack a bag and walk out towards the car without saying anything to their sons — makes him sit down at the table and tell the boys, properly, that he might not come back. He does it badly and formally, like a man answering questions at a press conference, until one of them asks whether he is going and he simply shakes his hand.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What goes wrong on the orbital mission, and how does Armstrong stop it?

2. Why is the scene at the kitchen table so difficult for him?

1. After the first-ever joining of two spacecraft, the capsule begins to spin faster and faster and the crew are close to losing consciousness. He shuts down the main steering system and uses the re-entry thrusters to stop the spin, which ends the mission early.

2. Janet forces him to tell their sons he may die. He has spent the whole film not saying things, and the film makes us watch him try — and mostly fail — to say this one.

Discuss:
  • Janet's argument is that his silence is not strength but a way of leaving other people to carry the fear alone. Is she right? Where is the line between composure and avoidance?

Part 3 — Descent

July 1969. The last twenty minutes of the film are a landing. The lander drops towards the surface with alarm codes flashing on the computer; the ground tells them to continue. Out of the small triangular window Armstrong can see that the machine is flying them into a field of boulders around a wide crater, so he takes the controls himself and flies on, low and flat, looking for anywhere clear, while his crew-mate reads out speed, height and — eventually — how little fuel is left. Dust boils up. A light says contact. The engine stops. There is a long, strange silence before anyone speaks.

Outside, the film does something quietly astonishing: it drops most of the sound. The famous words are said. The flag stands on the surface. And then Armstrong walks away from the lander, alone, to the edge of a small crater — and here the film makes its boldest choice, having him take out his daughter's bracelet and let it fall into the dark.

He comes home to a hero's welcome he never asked for, and to weeks of medical isolation. The last shot is not a parade. It is Janet and Neil facing each other through a pane of glass, unable to touch, saying nothing at all — and putting their hands up to the window instead.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does Armstrong take manual control during the landing?

2. What does he do at the crater, and how does the film end?

1. The computer is taking them into a field of boulders around a crater, so he flies the lander on by hand to find clear ground — with the fuel running down as he does.

2. He drops Karen's bracelet into the crater. The film ends with him in isolation after the flight, facing Janet through glass, their hands on either side of the window, in silence.

Discuss:
  • A film about the loudest achievement of the twentieth century ends with two people saying nothing through a window. Is that the right ending — or does it shrink something enormous down to one marriage?

Grammar — ellipsis & substitution

Listen to real pilots, and to real people, and you notice they almost never say a whole sentence twice. English has two neat habits for this: ellipsis (leave the repeated words out entirely) and substitution (swap them for a tiny stand-in word). Get these right and you stop sounding like a textbook.

1. Substitution with one / ones — replaces a countable noun you have already mentioned:
• "There were two dry lake beds he could aim for. He chose the nearer one."
• "The gloves he trained in were far stiffer than the ones he flew in."
Use ones for plurals. Note: we don't use one for uncountable nouns — "Is there fuel left?" "A little." (not a little one).
2. Substitution with do / does / did (and the more formal do so) — replaces a whole verb phrase:
• "He never spoke about his daughter. His wife wished he did."
• "They were told to abort the landing, and he refused to do so."
do so is the tidy, slightly formal version, common in reports and briefings; plain do / did is the everyday one.
3. So / Neither + auxiliary + subject — and auxiliary-only replies
• Agreeing with a positive: "She's afraid." — "So is he; he just doesn't show it."
• Agreeing with a negative: "He didn't mention Karen." — "Neither did anyone else."
Notice the inversion: the auxiliary comes before the subject.
• Short replies use the auxiliary alone, and nothing else: "Have you packed?" — "I have." / "Are you nervous?" — "I'm not." Never repeat the main verb ("Yes, I have packed" is heavy; "Yes, I like" is simply wrong).
4. Ellipsis after and / but / or — when the subject (and often the verb) is the same, drop it:
• "He walked to the pad and climbed in." (not and he climbed in)
• "She wanted the truth but wasn't given it."
• "You can ask him now or wait."
This is the single easiest way to stop your English sounding laboured.

1. Substitution — say it once

Fill each gap with one, ones, or a form of do / do so.

1. He had flown a great many dangerous aircraft, but this frightened even him.

2. Reporters asked hundreds of questions; the personal he simply refused to answer.

3. The board asked him to describe the accident, and he in four flat sentences.

1. one (= this aircraft)

2. ones (= the personal questions — plural, so ones)

3. did · so (= described the accident; the formal report-style substitution)

2. Agreeing, and answering short

Complete the replies. Use only auxiliaries — never repeat the main verb.

1. "I'm going to watch the launch." — " I." (you too — positive)

2. "He hasn't slept for two nights." — " she." (her too — negative)

3. "Did you tell the boys?" — "No, I ." (short reply, auxiliary only)

1. So · am ("So am I." — auxiliary before subject)

2. Neither · has ("Neither has she." — negative agreement, and the reply itself stays positive in form)

3. didn't ("No, I didn't." — the main verb tell disappears)

3. Leave it out — then put it all together

(a) Each sentence below repeats itself. Write only the words that should survive after and / but / or.

1. He climbed into the capsule, and he closed the hatch. → He climbed into the capsule and the hatch.

2. She was frightened, but she was not going to say so. → She was frightened but going to say so.

3. You can watch the launch, or you can look away. → You can watch the launch or away.

(b) Now complete the interview. Use a substitute or a short auxiliary reply in each gap.

"Have you ever thought about not coming back?" — "I ."

"Your wife must worry about that." — " the other wives."

"Which risks frighten you? All of them?" — "The I can't fly my way out of."

(a) 1. closed  ·  2. wasn't (or was not)  ·  3. look

(b) I have.  ·  So do the other wives.  ·  The ones I can't fly my way out of.

The Real Story — the man, not the mission

Historians rate First Man as one of the most accurate films ever made about spaceflight. So the interesting question is not "did they land?" — it is: what kind of person was actually inside that suit, and which of the film's most moving moments never happened?

🚀 Before the Moon, he had already survived three times

Neil Alden Armstrong (1930–2012) flew 78 combat missions in the Korean War. On 3 September 1951 his jet struck a cable strung across a valley during a low bombing run, tearing away about two metres of one wing; he flew clear of enemy territory and ejected. He then spent years as a research pilot in the Mojave Desert, where he flew the rocket-powered X-15 seven times. On 20 April 1962 — the flight the film opens with — his aircraft ballooned on the way down, the control surfaces found no air to bite on at over 63 kilometres up, and he sailed far south of his landing site before regaining control and turning back. That flight remains the longest in the X-15 programme's history at about twelve and a half minutes.

The spin, and the machine that nearly killed him

On 16 March 1966, Armstrong and David Scott achieved the world's first docking of two craft in orbit, joining Gemini 8 to an unmanned Agena rocket stage. Then a thruster on Gemini's manoeuvring system stuck open — later blamed on an electrical short — and the joined vehicles began to roll. Undocking made it worse: the capsule's spin approached one revolution per second, vision blurred, and the two men were close to losing consciousness. Armstrong shut the whole manoeuvring system down and used the thrusters reserved for coming home, burning roughly three-quarters of that propellant to quell the spin. Mission rules then forced an immediate return: they splashed down in the western Pacific just 10 hours 41 minutes after launch. Scott's verdict on his commander was blunt — he knew the system so well that he found the solution and acted on it under extreme conditions. Armstrong was, incidentally, NASA's first civilian astronaut to fly in space.

Two years later, on 6 May 1968, he was hovering in the Lunar Landing Research Vehicle — an open framework on legs, built to fly like a lander — when a loss of helium pressure drained the propellant feeding the backup attitude thrusters. He ejected at roughly 200 feet; the machine hit the ground about four seconds later and burned. He was back at his desk the same day. The film's version is not a computer effect: the production built a full-scale replica of the training vehicle and deliberately crashed it.

The landing — the alarms, the boulders, and that famous fuel figure

On 20 July 1969, during the final descent, the guidance computer threw 1201 and 1202 program alarms: it had more work than it could finish in real time, so it restarted, shed the low-priority jobs and kept flying. Engineer Jack Garman, working from an alarm-code list he had prepared beforehand, told guidance officer Steve Bales the alarms were survivable — and the descent continued. Seeing the computer heading for boulders strewn around a large crater (later called West crater), Armstrong took semi-manual control and flew on to clear ground. Touchdown was at 20:17:40 UTC.

Now the number everyone repeats. How much fuel was left is genuinely uncertain, and it is worth knowing why. In real time the callouts implied only about 25 to 30 seconds of flying left before they would have had to land or abort. But as Armstrong pitched the lander over to fly past the crater, the propellant sloshed in its tanks, and the low-level sensor latched roughly thirty seconds early — the gauges were simply not telling the truth. Post-flight analysis put around 770 pounds of propellant still aboard, of which about 100 pounds could never have been used, leaving perhaps 45 to 50 seconds — and some of that had to be held back for an abort. So "25 seconds" and "50 seconds" are both defensible: they answer slightly different questions, and the honest version is "somewhere in that range, and nobody in the cabin knew which".

And the line itself. Armstrong always said he intended "one small step for a man" — without the article it means the same thing twice over. The "a" is not audible in the transmission. A 2006 audio analysis by Peter Shann Ford claimed to find it, and Armstrong called that persuasive; a 2013 study of speakers from his part of Ohio found that they routinely blend "for a" into a single sound. It has never been settled.

Karen — and the bracelet the film invented

Karen Anne Armstrong was real. Born on 13 April 1959, she was diagnosed in mid-1961 with a malignant tumour in her brain stem and died on 28 January 1962, three months short of her third birthday. Armstrong applied to the astronaut corps a few months later. The film builds its whole emotional arc on that loss, and on the fact that he almost never discussed it.

The bracelet at the crater, however, did not happen. James R. Hansen's authorised biography lists what Armstrong carried to the surface — medallions, a piece of the Wright brothers' aeroplane, his wife's jewellery, his fraternity pin — and records Armstrong saying he brought nothing else for himself. NASA's then chief historian, Bill Barry, stated plainly that the scene was created for the movie and that there is no specific evidence Armstrong left any memorial item on the Moon. Hansen, who worked on the film, conceded it was dramatic licence "and it's a fairly big one", arguing that we cannot know for certain what Armstrong did in those minutes. What is documented is that he walked alone to a small crater away from the lander — the walk is real; the bracelet is the invention. Elsewhere the film adds tension with invented cockpit warnings and makes the lunar craters deeper and more dramatic than they really are.

One last row worth knowing. Before release in 2018, the film was attacked — including by Senator Marco Rubio and by President Trump — for not showing the American flag being planted. What the film actually shows is the flag standing on the Moon, in several shots; it simply skips the moment of planting to stay inside Armstrong's private experience. Armstrong's sons Rick and Mark, together with Hansen, issued a statement pointing out that "there are numerous shots of the American flag on the moon" and that "we do not feel this movie is anti-American in the slightest". Ryan Gosling's defence was about character rather than politics: he did not think Armstrong saw himself as an American hero, and the film was made to reflect the man.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Which surprised you more — that the accidents were real, or that the most emotional moment in the film was invented?
  • Hansen argued that sometimes poetry beats the uncertainty of fact. Is a film allowed to invent a scene like the bracelet, if it tells a truth about the man? Where would you draw the line?
  • The fuel figure has been reported as 25 seconds and as 50 for over fifty years. Does an honest "we don't know exactly, and here's why" make the story stronger or weaker?

Task B · Silence: strength or damage? (4 minutes)

The film's central argument is about a man who will not talk. To NASA, his containment is exactly what makes him the right pilot — the man who quells a spin instead of panicking. To Janet, it is a way of making everyone around him carry the fear alone. Build a case: is emotional silence a form of courage, or a form of harm? Take a side, defend it, then argue the opposite side for a minute. Bring in people you know, jobs where composure is the job, and cultures where saying less is respected.

Task C · True stories on screen (4 minutes)

The accidents in this film happened; the bracelet did not. When a film is based on real people, how much may it invent? Make your case.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Whether a film should have to tell the audience which scenes are invented.
  • A historical film or series from your country that got the facts noticeably wrong.
  • Whether the enormous cost of the Moon programme was justified — and who decides.
  • A real person you think deserves a film, and the one scene you would build it around.
  • Whether dangerous exploration — deep sea, high mountains, space — is worth the lives it costs.
  • A book or documentary that changed what you thought you knew about a famous event.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Half a billion people watched the landing; the film spends those minutes on one man's private grief. Is that a truer way to tell it, or a smaller one?
  2. Armstrong survived a cable in Korea, a spin in orbit and a crash in the desert. Does surviving that much make someone brave, or simply very good at the job — and is there a difference?
  3. If the record is genuinely uncertain — the fuel, the missing "a", what he did at the crater — how should a storyteller handle the gaps?
