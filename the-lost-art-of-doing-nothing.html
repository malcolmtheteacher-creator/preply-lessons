The Lost Art of Doing Nothing

C1 Level | Collocations Focus

Why Have We Forgotten How to Do Nothing?

When was the last time you sat and did absolutely nothing - no phone, no book, no music, no plans - just... existed?

For most people, that question is surprisingly difficult to answer. We live in an age where every moment must be optimised, productive, or at least entertaining. But it wasn't always this way.

47%
of people would rather receive electric shocks than sit alone with their thoughts for 15 minutes (University of Virginia, 2014)

The Science of Stillness

Neuroscientists have discovered that doing nothing activates the brain's "default mode network" - the area responsible for creativity, self-reflection, and making connections between ideas. When we're constantly busy, this network never gets a chance to work.

2.5 hours
Average daily screen time has increased since 2019, leaving almost no time for genuine mental rest (Global Web Index)

Maria, 68, Lisbon: "My mother would sit on the balcony for an hour after lunch, just watching the street. Now I catch myself feeling guilty if I'm not doing something useful. When did rest become something to feel ashamed of?"

Henrik, 72, Copenhagen: "The Danes have 'hygge', but we've forgotten 'at slansen' - to simply loaf around. My grandchildren are scheduled every minute. They don't know how to be bored. I think that's tragic."

The Busyness Trap

Sociologists note that being "crazy busy" has become a status symbol. We wear exhaustion like a badge of honour. Yet cultures that traditionally valued leisure - the French afternoon, the Italian passeggiata, the Spanish siesta - are slowly losing these rhythms to global productivity culture.

Which cultures still know how to do nothing well? And is this disappearing everywhere?

Collocations for Talking About Rest and Pace of Life

Click each card to reveal the meaning and practice.

1. to while away the hours

Click to reveal
Meaning
To spend time in a relaxed, leisurely way - often without a specific purpose
Form
while away + time expression (the hours / the afternoon / a Sunday)
Register
Slightly literary, nostalgic. Suggests peaceful pleasure.

Examples:

"We whiled away the afternoon watching boats on the river."

"She could happily while away an entire Sunday with nothing but coffee and a crossword."

NOW YOU TRY: Describe a perfect way to while away a summer afternoon. Where would you be? What would you (not) be doing?

2. to recharge one's batteries

Click to reveal
Meaning
To rest and recover energy after a period of hard work or stress
Form
recharge + my/your/one's batteries (possessive required)
Register
Common, all contexts. Acceptable in both casual and professional speech.

Examples:

"I need a week by the sea to recharge my batteries."

"After that project, the whole team needed time to recharge their batteries."

NOW YOU TRY: What's your favourite way to recharge your batteries? Has this changed since you retired?

3. to savour the moment

Click to reveal
Meaning
To fully appreciate and enjoy an experience while it's happening, rather than rushing through it
Form
savour + the moment / the experience / every minute
Register
Warm, reflective. Often used when giving advice or describing mindfulness.

Examples:

"Don't rush - savour the moment. This view is extraordinary."

"Looking back, I wish I'd savoured those years more instead of always planning ahead."

NOW YOU TRY: Tell me about a moment you're glad you savoured - or one you wish you had.

4. to take life at a slower pace

Click to reveal
Meaning
To deliberately live more slowly, with less rushing and fewer obligations
Form
take life / things + at a slower pace (NOT "in a slower pace")
Register
Neutral to positive. Often describes retirement or lifestyle changes.

Examples:

"Since moving to the countryside, we've been taking life at a slower pace."

"The doctor told him to take things at a slower pace after his heart scare."

NOW YOU TRY: Did you consciously decide to take life at a slower pace after retiring, or did it happen naturally?

5. to be in no particular hurry

Click to reveal
Meaning
To have no pressing deadline or urgency; to be relaxed about time
Form
be + in no particular hurry (+ to do something)
Register
Calm, peaceful tone. Can sound luxurious or enviable to busy people!

Examples:

"Take your time - I'm in no particular hurry this morning."

"One of the joys of retirement is being in no particular hurry to get anywhere."

NOW YOU TRY: What do you enjoy doing when you're in no particular hurry? How is it different from when you have a deadline?

Your Turn: Discussion

Choose 2-3 cards that interest you most. Use the collocations naturally!

1

Cultural Traditions

Some cultures seem better at rest - the Italian passeggiata, the Spanish siesta, the Danish hygge. Does your culture have traditions of slowness? Are they surviving?

2

The Guilt Factor

Do you ever feel guilty when you're not being productive? Where does this guilt come from? Has retirement changed your relationship with "useful" time?

3

Boredom: Lost Skill?

Children today are never bored - there's always a screen. What did boredom teach your generation that this one might miss? Is boredom actually valuable?

4

The Ideal Day

Describe your perfect "do nothing" day - from morning to night. Where are you? What aren't you doing? Who (if anyone) is with you?

5

Generational Shift

Your parents' generation, yours, and today's young adults - how has the relationship with rest and leisure changed? Which generation got it right?

6

Advice for the Busy

If a stressed, overworked younger person asked for your advice on slowing down, what would you tell them? What took you years to learn?

Challenge A: The Lost Rhythms

Talk about daily or weekly rhythms from your childhood that no longer exist. The long Sunday lunch, the evening promenade, the afternoon rest. What happened to them, and what have we lost?

Challenge B: Defending Idleness
