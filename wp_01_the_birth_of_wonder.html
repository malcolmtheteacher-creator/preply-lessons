A number to hold in your mind

2,600 years. That is roughly how long ago a man stood on the coast of what is now Turkey and asked a new kind of question. Every culture already had rich, powerful stories about where the world came from — creation myths, divine narratives, sacred cosmologies. Thales did not reject those stories. He simply asked a different question: not who made the world, but what is it made of? And with that, a new way of thinking was born — one that would sit alongside, and sometimes in tension with, the older ways.

Philosophy did not replace myth or faith. It opened a new door. What Thales began was the tradition of trying to understand the world through unaided human reason — and the story of that tradition, with all its triumphs and limits, is what this course is about.

II. The Story — A Man from Miletus

In the sixth century before Christ, the most important city in the Greek world was not Athens. It was Miletus — a prosperous trading port on the western coast of Anatolia, modern Turkey. Ships arrived from Egypt, Phoenicia, and Babylon, carrying not just goods but ideas. It was in this crossroads of cultures that three men asked questions nobody had asked before.

Thales (c. 624–546 BC)

Thales is traditionally called the first philosopher. Aristotle, writing two centuries later, described how Thales and his contemporaries approached the question of first principles:

Of the first philosophers, most thought the principles which were of the nature of matter were the only principles of all things. That of which all things that are consist, the first from which they come to be, the last into which they are resolved — this, they say, is the element and this the principle of things. — Aristotle, Metaphysics, Book I, 983b

Thales’ answer was water. Everything, he claimed, was ultimately made of water. It seems absurd at first — but think about what he was really doing. He was saying: behind the apparent chaos of the world, there is one underlying substance, and human reason alone might be able to discover it.

Why water? We don’t know Thales’ exact reasoning, since none of his writings survive. Aristotle speculated that Thales observed water’s ability to take three forms (solid, liquid, gas), its role in sustaining life, and its presence everywhere. Modern scholars note that Babylonian and Egyptian creation myths also featured primordial water — but what distinguished Thales was his approach: rather than explaining water’s role through a divine narrative, he proposed it as a material principle discoverable through observation and reasoning. It was a different kind of answer to an ancient question.

Thales was also said to have predicted a solar eclipse in 585 BC — the first recorded prediction of a natural event using natural reasoning. Whether the story is true or not, it tells us how the Greeks remembered him: as a man who could read the natural world through careful observation and reasoning.

Anaximander (c. 610–546 BC)

Thales’ student Anaximander thought his teacher was on the right track but made one crucial objection: why water and not fire? Or earth? If you pick any one element, you have to explain why it should be privileged over the others. Anaximander’s solution was breathtaking: the fundamental substance must be something beyond any particular thing. He called it the apeiron — the boundless, the infinite, the indefinite.

Anaximander said that the principle and element of existing things was the apeiron, being the first to use this name for the first principle. He says it is neither water nor any other of the so-called elements, but some other indefinite nature, from which all the heavens arise and the worlds within them. — Simplicius, quoting Theophrastus, Commentary on Aristotle’s Physics

Anaximander also proposed that human beings evolved from fish-like creatures that once lived in the sea — an idea strikingly close to evolutionary theory, arrived at 2,400 years before Darwin.

Anaximenes (c. 586–526 BC)

The third Milesian, Anaximenes, proposed air as the fundamental substance. His key insight was the mechanism: air becomes different things through condensation and rarefaction. When air thins, it becomes fire. When it thickens, it becomes wind, then cloud, then water, then earth, then stone. This was a remarkable early attempt to explain change through a physical mechanism — a single process that could account for the diversity of the material world.

What They Shared

The Milesian Revolution

These three men — Thales, Anaximander, Anaximenes — disagreed about the answer. But they agreed on something far more important: the question. They assumed that behind the apparent chaos of the world, there was a rational principle that could be discovered by human thought alone — without appeal to sacred tradition or received authority.

This was not a rejection of the sacred. Many later philosophers would find that reason and faith illuminate different aspects of the same reality. But the Milesians opened a new path — the path of rational inquiry — and it is that path we follow in this course. Where it meets, enriches, or challenges the paths of faith and tradition is one of philosophy’s most enduring stories.

Notice something, though: the Milesians asked “What is everything made of?” They did not yet ask the question that would come to matter even more: “How should we live?” Both questions run through the entire history of philosophy. This course follows them both — and watches for where they converge.

III. The Ideas — What the Milesians Really Discovered

The Milesian philosophers were wrong about water and air and the apeiron. But they were right about something much more important. Let’s break down their contribution into three ideas that still shape how we think today.

Idea 1: Naturalism

The world can be investigated through natural causes using reason.

Alongside the rich mythological tradition that explained the world through divine action, the Milesians proposed something new: that we could also look for material causes by observing nature and reasoning about it. Thunder might be explained as a clash of clouds, not only as Zeus’s anger.

This approach — seeking natural explanations through observation and reasoning — would eventually develop into what we now call natural philosophy and, later, science. It did not claim to have all the answers. But it opened a powerful new line of inquiry.

Idea 2: Monism

Behind the many, there is the one.

The world appears to contain millions of different things: rocks, rivers, stars, people, trees. The Milesians said: no. Underneath all this diversity, there is one fundamental thing. Finding that one thing is the task of philosophy (and later, of physics).

Modern physics still pursues this dream. The quest for a “Theory of Everything” — a single equation that explains all forces and particles — is a direct descendant of Thales asking “what is everything made of?”

Idea 3: Rational Inquiry

Questions can be settled by the quality of the argument.

Notice that Anaximander disagreed with Thales — his own teacher. And Anaximenes disagreed with both. What made this remarkable was the grounds for disagreement: not rival authorities, not competing revelations, but better reasoning. Anaximander did not say “a different tradition told me so.” He said “here is a stronger argument.”

This tradition of open, rational dialogue — where the argument matters more than who said it — is one of philosophy’s most enduring contributions. It does not claim to be the only way of knowing. But it insists that reason deserves a seat at the table.

The Big Question They Left Behind

The Milesians assumed that the world was fundamentally one thing that underwent change. But this raises a devastating problem that the next generation of philosophers would wrestle with:

Think about it before clicking: If everything is really one substance, how does change happen? How does water become fire? How does the one become the many?

This is the Problem of Change — and it will dominate the next three lessons. Heraclitus will say everything is constantly changing. Parmenides will say change is an illusion. Democritus will say everything is made of tiny unchanging atoms moving in empty space. The argument is still not settled.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Why water?

Thales said everything is water. What might have led him to this answer? And does the answer even matter — or was the question itself the breakthrough?

The courage of Anaximander

Anaximander publicly disagreed with his own teacher, Thales. In many ancient cultures, this would be unthinkable. What does it tell us about Greek intellectual culture that a student could say “my teacher is wrong”?

Myth vs. Reason

Before the Milesians, people explained the world through myths. After them, through reason. But has myth really disappeared? Where do we still use mythological thinking today?

The Theory of Everything

Modern physicists are still looking for a single theory that explains everything. Is this the same quest Thales began? Or is the modern version fundamentally different?

Anaximander’s evolution

Anaximander said humans came from fish-like creatures. He arrived at this 2,400 years before Darwin. How did he get there through pure reasoning?

Where did philosophy begin?

Philosophy began not in Athens but in Miletus — a trading port where many cultures met. Is this a coincidence? Does philosophy need a crossroads?

The question they didn’t ask

The Milesians asked “what is everything made of?” but not “how should we live?” Which question matters more? Can you answer one without answering the other?

The Shape of the Course

This is a 50-lesson journey through 2,600 years of philosophy. It follows the timeline honestly. But it is also a story with a shape — a question that deepens as we go. The Milesians asked “what is everything made of?” Later philosophers will ask “how should we live?” And the deepest question of all — whether anything can free a person from guilt, from fear, from the weight of being human — will emerge slowly and find its answer in an unexpected place. Pay attention to what each philosopher leaves unresolved. The gaps matter as much as the answers.
