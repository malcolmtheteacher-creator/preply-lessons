Before the Law

Franz Kafka — a man waits his whole life at an open door he is afraid to enter.

Want the full version? Read this lesson at B2/C1 level →

Today: you'll read a very short, strange story about a man, a door and a guard — and you'll practise can, can't and let to talk about permission (what you are allowed to do).
Before you read — talk a little. When do you need permission to do something? Who gives you permission — a boss, a teacher, the police? Have you ever waited a long time for a "yes"? Say one or two sentences.

The words for today's story

Each word is in a sentence. Read it and guess the meaning, then click Reveal.

1. In this story, "the Law" is behind a great door — something important that everyone wants to reach.
the Law (n) — the rules of a country; here, it also means something big and important that people hope to reach.
2. A doorkeeper (a guard) stood in front of the door and watched who came.
a doorkeeper / guard (n) — a person who stands at a door and controls who goes in.
3. The man wanted permission to go in — he wanted the guard to say "yes".
permission (n) — when someone says you are allowed to do something.
4. He wanted to enter, but he did not dare to walk through the door.
to enter (v) — to go into a place.
5. So he decided to wait. He sat down and waited for permission.
to wait (v) — to stay in a place until something happens.
6. He gave the guard gifts — money and other things — to try to change his mind.
a gift (n) — something you give to someone; a present.
7. He was patient and calm for many years, and he never got angry.
patient (adj) — able to wait a long time without getting angry.
8. After many years, the man became old and weak.
weak (adj) — not strong; here, tired and old.
9. At the end of his life, he asked one last question.
a question (n) — something you ask to get an answer.
10. Then the guard walked to the door and began to shut it.
to shut (v) — to close (a door).

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then answer the quick questions (try from memory first), and talk about one thing.

Part 1 — The door and the guard

A man from the country came to a great door. This was the door to the Law. In front of it stood a doorkeeper — a guard. The man wanted to go in.

"Can I enter?" he asked. "Not now," said the guard. The door was open — the man could see inside — but the guard did not let him pass. "Maybe later," said the guard. So the man decided to sit down and wait.

Quick check — answer, then reveal:

1. What did the man want to do, and what did the guard say?

2. Was the door open or closed?

1. He wanted to enter the door to the Law; the guard said "Not now — maybe later," and did not let him pass.

2. The door was open the whole time — the man could see inside — but he waited instead of walking in.

Discuss:
  • The door is open, but the man waits for permission. Have you ever waited for a "yes" when you could have just acted? Why do people do this?

Part 2 — The long wait

The man waited. He waited for days, then months, then years. He asked the guard many questions. He gave him gifts — money and small things — to try to change his mind. The guard took the gifts, but he still did not let the man enter.

The man was patient. He never pushed past the guard; he never simply walked through the open door. Slowly, he grew old. His eyes became weak, and his hair turned white. He spent his whole life sitting in front of that one open door.

Quick check — answer, then reveal:

1. What things did the man try, to be allowed in?

2. What happened to the man as the years passed?

1. He asked the guard many questions and gave him gifts, but he never walked through the open door himself.

2. He grew old and weak; his eyes failed and his hair went white — he spent his whole life waiting there.

Discuss:
  • The man is very patient. Is being patient always a good thing, or can waiting too long be a mistake?

Part 3 — The last question

At the very end of his life, the old man was almost too weak to speak. He had one last question. He asked the guard: "Everybody wants to reach the Law. So why, in all these years, has no one else come to this door?"

The guard saw that the man was dying. He spoke loudly into his ear: "No one else could come here. This door was only for you. And now I am going to shut it." Then the guard closed the door.

Quick check — answer, then reveal:

1. What was the man's last question?

2. What did the guard tell him, and what did he do?

1. He asked why no one else had ever come to this door, if everyone wants to reach the Law.

2. The guard said the door was only for him — and then shut it, just as the man was dying.

Discuss:
  • The door was "only for him" — but he never went in. Whose fault was that: the guard's, or the man's own?

Grammar — can / can't & let (permission)

This whole story is about permission — who is allowed to go in. We use can / can't and let to talk about it.

The rules:
• Can I…? — to ask for permission: "Can I enter?"
• You can / You can't — to give or refuse permission: "You can wait here." "You can't go in yet."
• let + person + base verb — to allow someone to do something: "The guard did not let him enter." (Not "let him to enter".)

1. Complete with can, can't or let

1. " I go in now?" asked the man. (ask permission)

2. "You enter yet," said the guard. (refuse)

3. The guard did not the man pass. (allow)

4. "You wait here if you like," said the guard. (give permission)

1. Can · 2. can't (cannot) · 3. let · 4. can

2. Fix the verb after "let"

Remember: let + person + base verb (no "to").

1. The guard would not let him in. (go)

2. Please let me you a question. (ask)

3. They did not let the old man the Law. (reach)

1. go · 2. ask · 3. reach   (all base verbs — no "to")

3. Complete the paragraph

The man asked, " I enter?" The guard said, "You come in yet." Year after year, the guard would not him pass. The door was open, but the man never walked in — so in the end, no one him reach the Law but himself.

Going deeper — talk more

Talk about these questions out loud. There are no wrong answers — just say what you think.

Useful language: "I would…", "I think he should have…", "It's the guard's / the man's fault because…", "In real life, people…".
1 · Why didn't he just go in?

The door was open the whole time. Why do you think the man waited for permission instead of walking through it? What stopped him?

2 · Wait or act?

Imagine you are the man. The guard says "not now", but the door is open. Do you wait politely, or do you walk in? Why? Is it braver to wait or to act?

3 · Doors in real life

Sometimes people wait years for permission — for a job, a visa, a chance — when they could act sooner. Do you know a situation like this? What advice would you give?

Task B · The conversation (4 minutes)

Act out a short conversation between the man and the guard about permission. Use "Can I…?", "You can't… yet", "Let me…". Then say what you would really do in the man's place.

Task C · Rules and permission (4 minutes)

Talk about rules in a place you know (a school, a job, an airport). What can you do there? What can't you do? Who lets you do things, and who says no? Nothing personal is needed — describe the place.

To finish — bigger questions

  1. The man waited his whole life for a "yes" that never came. What is the story warning us about?
  2. Was the guard cruel, or was he just doing his job?
  3. The door was "only for him". Do we sometimes create our own barriers — and then blame other people for them?
