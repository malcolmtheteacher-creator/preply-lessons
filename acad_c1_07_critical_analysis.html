A red teamer systematically attacks arguments to expose weaknesses. Here are the six core critical analysis moves:

1Identify Hidden Assumptions ▼

What it means: Every argument rests on unstated assumptions. Your job: make them visible and question whether they're justified.

The move: Ask "What does this argument take for granted?" What would have to be true for the argument to work?

In the remote work argument: The assumption is that productivity can be measured purely by output metrics, and that 13% improvement in ONE study generalises to all workers and all industries. Is that assumption justified?
2Evaluate Evidence Quality ▼

What it means: Not all evidence is created equal. Ask: Is the source credible? Is the sample representative? Is it recent? Is correlation being confused with causation?

The move: "That Stanford study sounds credible, but what was the sample size? Were they measuring actual productivity or self-reported productivity? Could something else explain the difference?"

Red team question: The Stanford study is real, but it measured specific roles (tech/accounting). Does that result hold in customer service, education, or healthcare? The survey (77% focused) is self-reported—people might be biased in favour of the work arrangement they chose.
3Name the Logical Fallacy ▼

Common fallacies to watch for:

  • Hasty Generalisation: "One study shows X, therefore X is always true."
  • False Cause: "Remote workers are more productive, so remote work causes productivity."
  • Slippery Slope: "If we allow remote work, soon nobody will work."
  • Appeal to Authority: "Stanford says X, so X must be true" (without evaluating the actual evidence).
  • False Dichotomy: "Either we go fully remote or productivity crashes" (ignoring hybrid options).
The remote work argument commits hasty generalisation: One Stanford study (specific context) is used to justify a universal policy ("companies should shift to permanent remote work").
4Find Missing Counterevidence ▼

What it means: What studies, data, or perspectives would challenge this argument?

The move: "What would we need to know to prove this wrong? What research might contradict this?"

Missing evidence: Studies showing remote work harms onboarding, mentoring, or creative collaboration. Data on burnout in always-on remote cultures. Industries where remote work reduced productivity. Research on the mental health costs of isolation.
5Question Key Definitions ▼

What it means: Key terms might be defined loosely or ambiguously. Tighter definitions might change the conclusion.

The move: "What exactly do you mean by 'productivity'? Output? Hours worked? Quality? Innovation? Would the conclusion change if we defined it differently?"

The argument assumes "productivity" = output. But if productivity includes innovation, mentorship, team cohesion, or culture, remote work might not be the winner. Different definitions, different conclusions.
6Test the Conclusion ▼

What it means: Does the evidence actually support the conclusion, or does it only support a weaker version?

The move: "Even if everything you said is true, does that conclusion really follow? Could a different conclusion also fit the facts?"

Weaker version: "Remote work may increase productivity for some knowledge workers under certain conditions."

Original conclusion: "Companies should shift to permanent remote work" is much stronger than the evidence actually supports.
