Academic Reading at PhD Speed

C1 Academic English · Three Reading Speeds · Argument Structure

The PhD reading problem

You will read more papers in your first PhD year than you read in your entire master's. The volume forces a different relationship with reading: you cannot deep-read every paper, and you must not skim every paper. The skill is choosing the right speed for the right text.

The single rule

Match the reading speed to the question you're answering, not the paper. Most papers get read at multiple speeds — and only some of them deserve the deep read.

Strategy & mechanics — pair this lesson with the masterclass

This lesson teaches the strategy layer — when to skim, when to scan, when to deep-read. For the mechanics layer — reducing eye fixations, reading in chunks, eliminating regression, and the pointer technique — pair this lesson with the Speed Reading Masterclass. Strategy without mechanics still leaves you reading slowly. Mechanics without strategy still leaves you reading the wrong papers.

1. SKIM

5–10 minutes per paper

"Is this paper worth my time at all?"

  • Title + abstract
  • Introduction (first & last paragraph)
  • Section headings
  • Conclusion
  • Skim references for names you recognise

2. SCAN

20–30 minutes per paper

"What does this paper actually say?"

  • Read intro fully
  • First sentence of each paragraph
  • All figures and captions
  • The discussion section
  • Note 2-3 key terms or claims

3. DEEP READ

60–120 minutes per paper

"How exactly does this argument work?"

  • Read the entire paper
  • Take Cornell or Zettelkasten notes
  • Reconstruct the argument step by step
  • Note the assumptions
  • Identify what would have to be true for this to fail

How to decide which speed

  • SKIM a paper that landed in your search results to decide if it's relevant.
  • SCAN a paper that is relevant but not central — you need to know what it says, not how it says it.
  • DEEP READ only the papers that sit at the centre of your argument: your key theoretical sources, your direct interlocutors, and anything you intend to disagree with.

For most working researchers: 70% skim, 25% scan, 5% deep read. The deep-read pile is small and sacred.

Reading for argument, not for content

At PhD level, reading well means reading structurally. You're not just looking at what the author says — you're looking at how they're trying to convince you, where the load-bearing claims are, and where the argument is weakest.

The five-part anatomy of an academic argument

1. The thesis (the central claim)

Usually in the abstract, the introduction, and restated in the conclusion. If you can't summarise this in one sentence in your own words, you haven't read the paper yet.

2a. Evidence — empirical

Data, experiments, observations, case studies, statistics. Ask: how strong is this? How was it gathered? Are the inferences from data to claim sound?

2b. Evidence — theoretical

Citations of authoritative thinkers, definitions, logical reasoning. Ask: are the cited thinkers themselves contested? Is the chain of reasoning valid?

3. Counter-arguments addressed

Mature academic writing acknowledges opposing views. Ask: which counter-arguments does the author engage seriously, and which are mentioned only to dismiss?

4. Assumptions (often unstated)

What does the author take as given? These are usually the weakest point — and the most interesting place for your own work to push.

5. Implications and limitations

What follows if the thesis holds? What does the author not claim, and why not?

The PhD reader's question:

Where is this argument weakest? Not where you disagree emotionally — where the load-bearing assumption is most exposed. That's where your contribution might begin.

Signal words — the road signs of academic prose

Academic writers use a small vocabulary of signal words to tell you what kind of move they're making. Reading for these signals is faster than reading for meaning, and reveals the structure underneath the prose.

Claim is coming

  • This paper argues that…
  • I contend that…
  • The central claim of this study is…
  • Here we propose…

Evidence is coming

  • The data show that…
  • As demonstrated by…
  • The findings indicate…
  • For example,…
  • In support of this,…

Counter-argument is coming

  • While it is often argued that…
  • Critics have suggested…
  • Some scholars maintain…
  • One might object that…
  • It might be supposed that…

Hedging — the claim is not absolute

  • This suggests…
  • It is likely that…
  • In most cases…
  • Tends to…
  • Could be interpreted as…

Conclusion is coming

  • It follows that…
  • Therefore…
  • Thus…
  • Consequently…
  • Taken together, these findings…

Reservation / limitation

  • Although…
  • Despite this…
  • It must be acknowledged that…
  • One limitation of this approach is…
  • Nevertheless,…

The fast technique

For a quick scan, run your eye down a paper looking only at the start of sentences. The first 3–5 words of most academic sentences carry a signal. If you train yourself to spot the signals, you can map the argument of a paper in 10 minutes — without reading every word.

A short passage to practise on

Read this passage twice. First time, just read for the gist. Second time, identify the thesis, the evidence, the counter-argument, and the hedging.

It is widely assumed that increased exposure to a second language inevitably accelerates acquisition. This view, common in both popular discourse and a substantial body of pedagogical literature, holds that immersion environments — study abroad, total-immersion classrooms, workplace usage — produce faster gains than instruction in isolation. While this assumption is intuitive, it is not always borne out in the empirical record.

Recent longitudinal studies suggest a more complex picture. Subjects placed in high-exposure environments without structured input often plateau at intermediate levels, while learners receiving moderate exposure paired with deliberate, focused practice tend to outperform their immersed counterparts on measures of grammatical accuracy and lexical range. Hu and Watkins (2023), for example, found that learners in a structured part-time programme outperformed full-immersion learners on every measure except conversational fluency.

One might object that conversational fluency is itself the principal goal of language acquisition. Yet this objection assumes a narrow definition of competence that excludes the very dimensions on which academic and professional language work most depends. It might be more accurate to say that exposure and instruction are not in competition but in productive tension — and that the best outcomes appear when both are present in measured proportion.
