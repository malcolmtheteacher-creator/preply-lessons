Check your idea CLICK
Two things changed: "I" became "he" (Ben is talking about Jack, not himself), and "am" became "was" (Jack's "now" is in the past by the time Ben repeats it). This happens every time you tell someone what somebody else said.
Today: say and tell, the one-step-back tense shift, and the pronoun changes that go with them — so you can report what people actually say to you in everyday conversation.

Say It, or Tell Someone

Two ways to report what someone said — and they don't work the same way.

Spot the pattern

Three things people said this morning:

  • "I'm starving," Leila said. → Leila said (that) she was starving.
  • "I love this café," Leila said to her flatmate. → Leila told her flatmate (that) she loved the café.
  • "We're late," the twins said to the waiter. → The twins told the waiter (that) they were late.

Look at "said" and "told" here. When does a person's name or "her flatmate" / "the waiter" come straight after the verb, with no "to"? Say your answer before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
tell always needs a person straight after it: told her flatmate, told the waiter — never "told to". say never takes a person directly after it: you can't "say somebody" — only "say (to somebody)".
say (to somebody) + (that) + clause
tell + somebody + (that) + clause

From direct words to reported words

"I'm hungry," she said to me.
→
She told me (that) she was hungry.

Once "me" is there, you can't keep "said" — it becomes "told me".

One Step Back in Time

When you report what someone said, the tense usually moves one step into the past.

Spot the pattern

Three things people said:

  • "I live in Manchester," Sam said. → Sam said (that) he lived in Manchester.
  • "I don't eat meat," Grace told us. → Grace told us (that) she didn't eat meat.
  • "The train leaves at six," the guard said. → The guard said (that) the train left at six.

Compare "live / lived", "don't eat / didn't eat", "leaves / left". What's the pattern? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
The present simple moves one step back to the past simple: live → lived, don't eat → didn't eat, leaves → left. What was true "now" for the speaker is in the past by the time you report it.
am / is / are → was / were
verb (do / does) → verb + ed (did)
don't / doesn't → didn't
The negative shifts the same way: don't and doesn't both step back to didn't — the main verb after it stays plain (didn't eat, not didn't ate).

→
Tom said (that) he didn't like queuing.

Whose Words? Changing the Pronoun

"I" and "my" only make sense for the person speaking. Once you report it, they usually have to change.

Spot the pattern

Three things people said:

  • "I'm on holiday," Marta said. → Marta said (that) she was on holiday.
  • "We live next door," the Kowalskis said. → The Kowalskis said (that) they lived next door.
  • "My flight is delayed," Oskar told the receptionist. → Oskar told the receptionist (that) his flight was delayed.

Look at "I" → "she", "we" → "they", "my" → "his". Why do you think these have to change? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Because you're not the one speaking anymore — you're telling someone else what Marta, the Kowalskis, or Oskar said. "I" becomes "she/he", "we" becomes "they", and "my" becomes "his/her/their" — matching the original speaker, not you.
I → she / he    we → they
my → his / her / their    me → him / her / them

→
Aisha told her mother (that) she loved her job.
