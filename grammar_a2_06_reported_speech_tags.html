Why This Matters

In conversation, you don't always repeat exactly what someone said. You report it: "She said she was tired." You also check information with questions: "You live in Berlin, don't you?" These are basic patterns in everyday English.

Today you'll learn how to do both — smoothly and accurately.

Reported Speech: Telling Others What Someone Said

When you report what someone said, you change the structure slightly. This is called "reported speech."

Teacher Models

The Pattern

Direct speech: He said, "I like coffee."

Reported speech: He said (that) he liked coffee.

What changes:

  • Pronouns: "I" → "he"
  • Tense: "like" → "liked" (one step back in time)
  • No quotation marks or direct quotes

Simple Reported Speech Chart

Direct (Original Words) Reported (What You Tell Others)
"I like ice cream." She said (that) she liked ice cream.
"I'm busy today." He said (that) he was busy.
"I've finished my work." She said (that) she had finished her work.
"I'm going to London." He said (that) he was going to London.

Key rule: The word "that" is optional. You can say "He said he liked ice cream" or "He said that he liked ice cream."

Question Tags: Check Information Quickly

A question tag is a small question at the end of a sentence. You use it to check if something is true or to get agreement.

Teacher Models

The Pattern

Statement + Tag (question at the end)

"You like coffee, don't you?"

The tag is short — it asks the listener to agree or confirm.

How to Make a Question Tag

Rule: The tag is opposite to the statement.

  • Positive statement → negative tag: "You live here, don't you?"
  • Negative statement → positive tag: "You don't live here, do you?"

Examples

Statement Question Tag
"You're from Spain," aren't you?
"She likes chocolate," doesn't she?
"They've finished," haven't they?
"You don't smoke," do you?
"She's not tired," is she?

Pronoun rule: Use the same pronoun that the statement uses.

"You like coffee, don't you?" (You → You in the tag)

"She's a doctor, isn't she?" (She → she in the tag)

Negative Questions: Ask with Surprise or Concern

Sometimes you ask a question in the negative. This shows surprise, concern, or that you expected something different.

Teacher Models

The Pattern

Normal question: "Are you coming?"

Negative question: "Aren't you coming?"

The negative question sounds like you're surprised. Maybe you expected them to come!

When Do You Use Negative Questions?

  • Surprise: "Didn't she finish school?" (You thought she did)
  • Concern: "Isn't it too expensive?" (You're worried about the price)
  • Checking information: "Didn't you say you were busy?" (I heard you say that)

Examples

Positive Question Negative Question (with feeling)
"Do you like coffee?"
Neutral — just asking		 "Don't you like coffee?"
Surprised — expected you to like it
"Is she Italian?"
Just asking		 "Isn't she Italian?"
You thought she was
"Did they arrive?"
Asking if it happened		 "Didn't they arrive?"
Surprised they might not have arrived

How to make it: Put the negative (n't) right after the auxiliary verb (do, is, are, did, has, etc.)

A: "Do you speak French?" vs. B: "Don't you speak French?" Click to compare

A is neutral. Just asking a question.

B shows feeling. The speaker expected you to speak French. Maybe surprised you might not.

When would you use: "Isn't that expensive?" Click to see the answer

When you think something costs too much. You're expressing concern or surprise about the price. You're not just neutrally asking.
