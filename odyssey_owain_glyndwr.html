The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The rising

Owain Glyndŵr was a Welsh landowner descended from the princes of Powys and Deheubarth. He had studied law in London and served English kings. A land dispute with his neighbour, Lord Grey of Ruthin, and anger at English rule led to revolt. On 16 September 1400 his supporters proclaimed him Prince of Wales.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What sparked the rising?

2. When was he proclaimed?

1. A land dispute with Lord Grey of Ruthin, and anger at English rule.

2. 16 September 1400.

Discuss:
  • How can a small dispute grow into a war?

Part 2 — A Welsh state

By 1404 Owain controlled much of Wales. He captured Harlech and Aberystwyth castles and held a parliament at Machynlleth. In the Pennal Letter of 1406 to the King of France, he planned an independent Welsh church and two universities, one in the north and one in the south.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did he hold a parliament?

2. What did the Pennal Letter plan?

1. Machynlleth.

2. An independent church and two universities.

Discuss:
  • Why would universities matter to a rebel?

Part 3 — The vanishing

From 1405 English forces, led by Prince Henry (the future Henry V), slowly won back the castles. Owain's wife and daughters were captured in 1409. Owain himself was never caught or betrayed. He refused pardons and disappeared around 1412. He probably died about 1415, possibly at the home of a daughter in Herefordshire — but nobody knows for sure.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to his family?

2. What happened to Owain?

1. His wife and daughters were captured in 1409.

2. He disappeared and probably died around 1415.

Discuss:
  • Why might people prefer a hero who vanished?

Grammar — Past Modals of Deduction

When the evidence is thin, historians must deduce. Past modals show how certain we are about what happened.

How it works:
• must have + past participle — nearly certain: "He must have known."
• might / may / could have — possible: "The princes may have been killed."
• can't have — nearly impossible: "He can't have been in London that night."

1. How sure are we?

Complete each sentence about Owain Glyndŵr. Use the word in brackets.

1. He have died around 1415. (may / can't)

2. His friends hidden him for years. (must have / can't have)

3. He been captured — no record exists. (can't have / must have)

Going deeper

Owain's dream of a Welsh parliament and universities came true centuries later — the University of Wales and the Senedd (Welsh Parliament), founded in 1999.

Worth noticing: 16 September is celebrated as Owain Glyndŵr Day in Wales.
Learning from the past — for today: Big visions outlast failure. Owain lost the war but his ideas — self-government, education — came back. Write down your vision; ideas can succeed even when the first attempt fails.
