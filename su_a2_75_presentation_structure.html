A Perfect Presentation Structure

A2 Level · Presentation Skills

First Thoughts

Think about presentations you've watched. Some are easy to follow. Others are confusing. What makes the difference?

What makes a presentation easy to follow?

  • Clear structure — you know where the speaker is going
  • Good transitions — clear signals between ideas
  • Organized thinking — ideas connect logically
  • Repetition — key points are reinforced

Think about it: Think of a presentation you found really easy to follow. What helped you understand it?

Three Types of Presentation Structure

Professional presentations use three main structures. Each one organizes ideas in a different way, but all three have one thing in common: they have exactly three parts.

Structure 1: Problem / Solution / Outcome

Use this when: You want to show how something improved or how to fix something.

  • Problem: What was the challenge?
  • Solution: What did you do about it?
  • Outcome: What happened as a result?

Structure 2: Rationale / Change / Benefits

Use this when: You want to persuade people to accept a new idea or change.

  • Rationale: Why do we need to change?
  • Change: What is the new idea or approach?
  • Benefits: What advantages will this bring?

Structure 3: Past / Present / Future

Use this when: You want to show how something developed over time.

  • Past: Where did this begin?
  • Present: Where are we now?
  • Future: Where are we going?

Real-World Example

Aargson Drinks Presentation (Past / Present / Future):
"To start with, I'll provide a brief outline of how Aargson Drinks started. Then I'll run through our current product range and why we believe they will appeal to your customers. I'll finish by sharing some of the exciting new products we are launching next month."

Notice: The speaker tells you the structure upfront. This helps the audience follow along.

The Power of Three

All three structures have something in common: three parts. Why three? Because:

  • Three parts feel complete and balanced
  • Three parts are easy to remember
  • Three parts create a natural rhythm

Vocabulary

Structure The organized arrangement of ideas in a presentation
Rationale The reason or logic behind something
Outcome The result or final consequence of something
Persuade To convince someone to believe or do something
Appeal To be interesting or attractive to someone

The 3 Vs of Transition Signals

A good presenter doesn't just move from one idea to the next. They use signals to help the audience know what's happening. These signals work in three ways:

1. Verbal Transitions (What the audience understands)

These are words and phrases that show connection between ideas:

  • To start with — introducing the first idea
  • Then — moving to the next idea
  • Finally / In conclusion — wrapping up
  • However / On the other hand — contrasting ideas
  • As a result / Therefore — showing consequence

Example: "To start with, I'll provide a brief outline... Then I'll run through... Finally, I'll share..."

2. Vocal Transitions (What the audience hears)

These are the sounds and delivery techniques that guide your listener:

  • Pause: A silent moment shows you're moving to a new idea
  • Tone change: Speaking more loudly, more quietly, or with different emotion
  • Pace change: Speaking faster or slower
  • Stress on key words: Speaking with emphasis

Example: Stop. Take a breath. Then speak your next point with a slightly different pace or tone.

3. Visual Transitions (What the audience sees)

These are the things your audience sees that show a change:

  • Move on the slide: New slide, new idea
  • Body movement: Step to a different part of the room
  • Gesture: Hand movement that shows connection
  • Eye contact: Where you look tells the story

Example: Click to the next slide. Move slightly. Change what appears on screen.

Practice: Match the Transition

Sentence:
"We started in 2010. However, our biggest growth came after 2015."

Key Takeaway

All great presentations share one thing: clear structure with strong transitions. When you tell the audience where you're going, guide them with verbal, vocal, and visual signals, and reinforce your message, you become a powerful communicator.
