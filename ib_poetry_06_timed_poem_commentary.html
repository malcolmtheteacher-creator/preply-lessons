The Second Coming · W. B. Yeats (1920)

1Turning and turning in the widening gyre
The falcon cannot hear the falconer;
Things fall apart; the centre cannot hold;
Mere anarchy is loosed upon the world,
5The blood-dimmed tide is loosed, and everywhere
The ceremony of innocence is drowned;
The best lack all conviction, while the worst
Are full of passionate intensity.
Surely some revelation is at hand;
10Surely the Second Coming is at hand.
The Second Coming! Hardly are those words out
When a vast image out of Spiritus Mundi
Troubles my sight: somewhere in sands of the desert
A shape with lion body and the head of a man,
15A gaze blank and pitiless as the sun,
Is moving its slow thighs, while all about it
Reel shadows of the indignant desert birds.
The darkness drops again; but now I know
That twenty centuries of stony sleep
20Were vexed to nightmare by a rocking cradle,
And what rough beast, its hour come round at last,
Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?

Your question

How does the poet convey a sense of a world losing control?

In your answer, consider the poem’s imagery, sound, tone and structure, and the effect on the reader.

Need a nudge? Look only after you have a plan.

Angle 1: image of lost control. “Turning and turning in the widening gyre / The falcon cannot hear the falconer”: a spiral that cannot be reversed and a trained bird no longer obeying. Angle 2: antithesis and abstraction. “The best lack all conviction, while the worst / Are full of passionate intensity”: the balanced structure shows the moral order inverted. Angle 3: structure and shift. The first stanza of description gives way to the revelation (“Surely some revelation is at hand”): the turn from chaos to a terrifying new image. Angle 4: sound and ending. “Slouches towards Bethlehem to be born?”: the verb “slouches” and the rhetorical question end on menace, not resolution.

Compare with a model idea

A strong commentary might argue that Yeats makes the loss of control feel inevitable by making the poem itself lose control: the opening spiral (“widening gyre”) imitates a pattern that cannot hold; the antithesis of “the best” and “the worst” inverts moral order; and the shift to a vision (“a shape with lion body and the head of a man”) replaces explanation with menace. The final rhetorical question refuses closure, so the reader is left, like the speaker, waiting for something monstrous to be born.

I can: plan and write a timed commentary on an unseen poem that argues one idea, uses image, sound and structure as evidence, and ends on the effect on the reader.
gyre (noun) — a spiral or circular movement. Example: "The widening gyre suggests loss of control."
anarchy (noun) — a state of disorder without authority. Example: "The poem warns of anarchy."
antithesis (noun) — a sharp contrast of ideas in balanced phrases. Example: "The antithesis of best and worst inverts moral order."
apocalyptic (adjective) — describing the end of the world or great destruction. Example: "The tone is apocalyptic."
revelation (noun) — a sudden disclosure of something hidden. Example: "The poem turns on a revelation."
menace (noun) — a sense of threat. Example: "The ending leaves a sense of menace."
inversion (noun) — a reversal of the usual order. Example: "The poem presents an inversion of values."
commentary (noun) — a piece of writing that analyses a text. Example: "You wrote a timed commentary."
