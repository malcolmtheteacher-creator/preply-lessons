Decision-Making Language

Learn the key phrases for discussing options and making decisions together in the workplace.

Giving Your Opinion

OPINION
In my opinion, [option] would be the best.
Start with your main idea about what you think is best
OPINION
I think [option] is the best choice because…
Give your opinion and explain your reason

Making Suggestions

SUGGESTION
I propose having [option] because…
Formal way to recommend an idea to a group
SUGGESTION
I suggest [option] because…
Direct way to put forward your idea

Asking for Opinions

QUESTION
What do you think?
Simple, open question about someone's opinion
QUESTION
What's your opinion?
More formal way to ask someone to share their view
QUESTION
Do you agree?
Check if someone has the same idea as you

Responding: Agreeing

AGREEMENT
Yes, I agree with you.
Direct agreement with someone's idea
AGREEMENT
That's a good idea.
Positive response showing approval

Responding: Agreeing with Reservations

CAUTIOUS AGREEMENT
I like the idea of [option], but/however…
Agree with the basic idea but point out a problem
CAUTIOUS AGREEMENT
I'd like [option], but there's a problem with…
Support the idea while highlighting a concern

Using Would and Might for Discussing Options

When discussing possible options or hypothetical scenarios at work, use "would" and "might" instead of "will".

Would vs. Will

Hypothetical — using would:

The June option would be best.

A complete change would be better.

Moving the conference would be a good idea.

(These are options we're considering, not decisions made yet)

Decided — using will:

We think that most people will be happy with September.

(We have decided. This is now a prediction based on a real decision)

Might for Uncertainty

More certain:

In September the venue would be cheaper.

Less certain:

In September the venue might be cheaper.

(We're not sure if this is true, but it's a possibility)

Examples in Context

OPTION
Holding the event in September would give us better weather.
This is a possible advantage if we choose September
OPTION
June might not work because people are often on holiday then.
This is a possible problem — we're not certain but it's likely
OPTION
Moving to September would bring something new to the event.
This is a possible benefit of changing the month

Practice: Conference Planning

Read the scenario. Your manager has sent you an email about the company's annual conference. Look at the options and think about the advantages and disadvantages.

The Situation

Your company needs to decide when to hold the annual conference next year. Currently, the conference is always in June, but there are some problems:

  • June is very hot
  • Many people are on holiday in July and August
  • The venue is more expensive in June

Option 1: Keep June

Advantage: People expect it in June. They plan holidays around it.
Disadvantage: It is very hot. The venue is expensive. Many people find June difficult for travel.

Option 2: Move to September

Advantage: Better weather. The venue might be cheaper. People are back at work and settled.
Disadvantage: People might be confused about the change. September might be too late in the year.

Your Task

Which option would you choose? Write your decision and your reasons using the phrases you learned.

Speaking Task: Staff Party & Awards

Your HR manager has asked for your opinions. Read the decisions that need to be made. Discuss the options and decide together. Remember to use the language you learned for making suggestions and giving opinions.

Decision 1: When to Hold the Staff Party

Option A: Friday evening after work (17:00-21:00)

Good for: People are already at the office. Easy for everyone to come.

Problem: Some people might be tired. Hard to leave if something is planned.

Option B: Saturday afternoon (14:00-18:00)

Good for: More relaxed. People can stay longer. Family-friendly.

Problem: People might have other plans. Parents need to arrange childcare.

Decision 2: How to Celebrate the Awards

Option A: Give awards at the party (announce winners and give small gifts)

Good for: Fun and surprising. Everyone learns about the winners at the same time.

Problem: Some people might feel bad if they don't win. The party becomes too formal.

Option B: Give awards in a separate ceremony (formal meeting during work hours)

Good for: More respectful. Winners are properly recognized. Easier to prepare.

Problem: Separate events cost more money. People have to attend two things.

What to do:

  • Discuss both decisions (party time and awards)
  • Give your opinion on each option
  • Use phrases like: "In my opinion…", "I propose…", "I like the idea of… but…"
  • Ask what I think: "What's your opinion?"

Remember to use:

  • Making suggestions: "I propose…", "I suggest…"
  • Opinions: "In my opinion…", "I think that…"
  • Hypothetical language: "This would be…", "It might be…"
  • Responding: "Yes, I agree.", "That's a good idea, but…"
