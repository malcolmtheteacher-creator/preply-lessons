The story, in three parts

Part 1 — The sacred games of Olympia

The story begins not in a stadium but in a sanctuary. At Olympia, a quiet valley in the west of Greece, there stood a great sacred site dedicated to Zeus, king of the gods. Tradition dates the first games there to 776 BC, and for the Greeks they were far more than sport — they were a religious festival held in the god's honour, one of the oldest and most important events in the whole ancient world.

Every four years, from across a Greece that was usually divided into rival, warring city-states, athletes and spectators travelled to Olympia. To let them come in peace, heralds proclaimed a sacred truce: wars were paused, weapons set aside, and safe passage guaranteed. The competitors were free men who spoke Greek, and they competed naked, their bodies oiled and gleaming, as an offering of human excellence to the gods.

The events grew over the centuries — foot races the length of the stadium, wrestling, boxing, the brutal all-in fighting of the pankration, and the thundering, dangerous chariot races. There were no silver or bronze medals and no cash prizes. The victor received only a simple crown of olive leaves — but with it came something priceless: glory, and the eternal pride of his home city, which might build statues in his honour and feed him for life.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why were the ancient games far more than just a sporting event?

2. What was the sacred truce, and why was it necessary?

1. They were a religious festival held at Olympia in honour of Zeus; competing was seen as an offering of human excellence to the gods.

2. A period of peace declared every four years so that wars stopped and athletes and spectators could travel safely to and from Olympia.

Discuss:
  • The winner received only a crown of leaves, yet athletes gave everything to earn it. What does that tell us about what people truly compete for?

Part 2 — Fifteen centuries of silence

For nearly twelve hundred years the games at Olympia flourished, surviving even the coming of Rome. When the Romans conquered Greece, they did not abolish the games; some emperors admired them, and the notorious emperor Nero even took part — winning, unsurprisingly, every event he entered, including a chariot race from which he fell out and did not finish. The festival had become grander, more famous and more crowded than ever.

But the ancient world was changing. As Christianity spread and became the official religion of the Roman Empire, a festival built around the worship of the old pagan gods came to be seen not as sacred but as sinful. Around AD 393, the emperor Theodosius I banned such pagan celebrations, and the games at Olympia were brought to an end. In the centuries that followed, earthquakes toppled the temples and rivers buried the sanctuary in mud and silt. The site was lost, and the very idea of the Olympics faded into legend.

For roughly fifteen hundred years there were no Olympic Games at all. Then, in the nineteenth century, European archaeologists dug down through the earth at Olympia and uncovered the temples, the workshops and the ancient running track. As the buried stadium came back into the light, so did an old, powerful idea — that the whole world might one day gather again, not to make war, but to compete.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the Romans do with the games when they conquered Greece?

2. Why were the ancient games finally abolished, and what then happened to the site?

1. They kept them going; some emperors admired them and Nero even competed. The festival grew grander and more famous under Rome.

2. As Christianity became the official religion, the pagan festival was seen as sinful and was banned around AD 393; earthquakes and rivers then buried and lost the site for centuries.

Discuss:
  • An idea can vanish for fifteen centuries and still return. Why do some old ideas refuse to die, even when everything physical connected to them is destroyed?

Part 3 — The revival of 1896

The man who brought the games back was a French aristocrat, Baron Pierre de Coubertin. Educated and idealistic, he was convinced that sport built character, courage and understanding between nations — and he was inspired both by the rediscovery of ancient Olympia and by the organised sport he had seen in British schools. In 1894 he gathered delegates from many countries in Paris, founded the International Olympic Committee, and won their agreement to revive the games. Fittingly, the first modern Olympics would be held in the country where it all began: Greece.

In April 1896, in Athens, the first modern Olympic Games opened before a huge, roaring crowd in a gleaming marble stadium. Around 240 athletes from 14 nations competed — a tiny gathering by today's standards, and all of them men. The undoubted hero of those first games was a Greek water-carrier named Spyridon Louis, who won the marathon, a brand-new race inspired by an ancient legend, running into the stadium to the wildest celebration the young nation had ever seen.

From that modest beginning, the modern Olympics grew into the largest and most watched sporting event on the planet, uniting thousands of athletes and billions of viewers every four years. The revival carried the ancient spirit forward — the truce, the pursuit of excellence, the dream of nations meeting in peace — but it also inherited the modern world's tensions: politics, money, and the pressure to be ever bigger. The longest echo of all is the simplest: more than two and a half thousand years after Olympia, we still gather to ask the oldest sporting question of who is faster, higher and stronger.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who revived the games, and what convinced him it was worth doing?

2. What made the 1896 Athens games historic, and who was their great hero?

1. Baron Pierre de Coubertin, a French aristocrat; he believed sport built character and brought nations together, and was inspired by the rediscovery of Olympia and by British school sport.

2. They were the first modern Olympics (Athens, 1896), with around 240 male athletes from 14 nations; the hero was Spyridon Louis, a Greek who won the marathon.

Grammar — comparatives & superlatives

No subject needs comparison quite like sport. The whole Olympic motto is built on it — faster, higher, stronger — and the story you've just read is full of "the greatest", "the oldest", "more famous than ever". Get these forms right and you can describe any contest with precision.

The basic pattern:
• Short adjectives (one syllable): add -er / -est — fast → faster → the fastest; strong → stronger → the strongest. (Double the final consonant after a short vowel: big → bigger → the biggest.)
• Adjectives ending in -y: change y to i — early → earlier → the earliest.
• Longer adjectives (two+ syllables): use more / the most — famous → more famous → the most famous; important → more important → the most important.
Watch out — irregular and structural points:
• Irregular forms: good → better → the best; bad → worse → the worst; far → farther/further → the farthest/furthest.
• Comparatives take than: "The marathon is longer than the sprint."
• Superlatives usually take the, and often in or of: "the oldest event in the world", "the greatest of all the athletes."
• For emphasis, strengthen a comparative with far, much, considerably: "far more famous", "much stronger" — or soften it with slightly, a little.
