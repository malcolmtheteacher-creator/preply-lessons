Check your idea CLICK
Message 2 — because it uses joining words (and, but) to show HOW the ideas connect. "And" adds one thing to another; "but" shows the surprise (shopping done, except for one thing). Short sentence after short sentence sounds like a list, not like speech. Today you'll build sentences the second way.

Joining Two Ideas: "and / but / so / or"

For two complete ideas that belong together — add, contrast, show a result, or offer a choice.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "I finished the report early, and I sent it straight away."
  • "I like the flat, but the rent is too high."
  • "The car wouldn't start, so I called a mechanic."

Look at what's before and after the comma in each one. Say what you notice before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Before the comma: a full sentence that could stand alone. After the joining word: another full sentence that could also stand alone. Comma + and / but / so / or joins two complete sentences into one — each half keeps its own subject and verb.
sentence 1 , and / but / so / or sentence 2

and adds one thing to another · but shows a contrast · so gives a result · or offers a choice.

"I finished the report early. I sent it straight away."
→
"I finished the report early, and I sent it straight away."

Giving Reasons & Conditions

because, although, when, if, after, before — one idea depends on, contrasts with, or happens around another.

Spot the pattern

  • "I was late because the bus didn't come."
  • "Although it was raining, we walked to the market."
  • "When I get home, I usually make dinner."

These joining words can go in the middle of the sentence or right at the start. When one starts the sentence, what punctuation follows the first part? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
A comma. "Although it was raining, we walked..." — clause first, comma, then the rest. But in the middle, no comma: "I was late because the bus didn't come." Same meaning, same joining word, different position.
sentence + because / although / when / if / after / before + clause
Because / Although / When / If / After / Before + clause , sentence

because = reason · although = contrast · when = a moment in time · if = something that might happen · after / before = order in time.

Adding Extra Information: "who / which / that"

Instead of two separate sentences about the same person or thing, add the extra detail inside one sentence.

Spot the pattern

  • "The woman who lives next door works as a nurse."
  • "I bought a jacket that was on sale."
  • "My neighbour, who works nights, sleeps until noon."

Which word describes a person, and which describes a thing? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
who = people. which = things. that = people or things — very common in speech for both, especially when the detail isn't extra, it's essential to knowing which one you mean (like the jacket).
who = people
which = things
that = people or things (informal, everyday)
"I have a friend. She speaks four languages."
→
"I have a friend who speaks four languages."

2. Say it out loud: what's the difference between a comma splice and a properly joined sentence? Give one example of each.

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: a comma splice is two full sentences stuck together with only a comma — "It was cold, I put on a coat." A properly joined sentence adds a joining word to show HOW the ideas connect — "It was cold, so I put on a coat." The comma alone doesn't tell you if it's a reason, a contrast, or just two facts; the joining word does.
