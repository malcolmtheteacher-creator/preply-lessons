Handling Media Interviews

C2 Level · Public Communication

The Challenge of Media Scrutiny

Journalists are trained to elicit newsworthy responses—which often means pushing for conflict, controversy, or contradiction. A well-prepared executive understands this isn't personal; it's professional. Your task is not to win against the journalist but to communicate your message clearly whilst remaining composed.

The stakes are high. A careless phrase, recorded and decontextualised, can drive headlines for days. A moment of defensiveness can be characterised as arrogance. Conversely, clarity and measured composure communicate confidence and credibility. The executives who handle media best are those who prepare relentlessly—not to sound robotic, but to speak authentically under pressure.

Consider the distinction between "on the record," "off the record," and "on background." Once you speak to a journalist, assume everything is on the record unless explicitly negotiated otherwise—and even then, assume it might be used. There is no genuinely safe assumption with media.

Consider: Think of a CEO interview you've watched. What moments made them seem credible? What moments undermined their credibility? How much was preparation versus genuine composure?

Techniques for Staying On Message

Interview preparation involves identifying three to five key messages you want to communicate, then finding natural ways to weave them into answers regardless of the question asked.

Vocabulary Cards

On the Record Statements a journalist can quote and attribute to you by name; assume all media conversation is on the record.
Off the Record Theoretically, non-attributable background; in practice, assume this offer is a trap. Anything you say can be used.
Sound Bite A brief, quotable phrase; journalists seek 10–15 second clips for broadcast. Prepare memorable, concise statements.
Deflect To redirect an unwanted question; acknowledge without answering. "That's interesting, but what's actually important is..."
Pivot To move from the journalist's question to your message. Bridge techniques allow graceful pivoting.
Gaffe An unguarded or embarrassing remark; becomes headline-generating when recorded and decontextualised.
Spin Presenting information with an angle favourable to your interests; done skillfully, it's persuasion; done crudely, it's obvious manipulation.
Embargo An agreement that information cannot be published until a specified time; protects exclusive coverage rights.

Bridging & Message Control

Bridging Techniques: "That's a fair question. What I think is really important here is..." or "I appreciate the question, and it relates to a broader point..." These phrases acknowledge the journalist's question whilst allowing you to transition to your message. Used skillfully, they feel conversational, not evasive.

Flagging Important Points: "The key point is..." or "Here's what matters..." These signal emphasis without being heavy-handed. They help editors identify quotable material and ensure important nuance isn't lost.

Handling Hostile Questions: Never become defensive. Pause, breathe, and respond calmly. "I understand why you'd frame it that way. Here's the fuller context..." You can disagree with a premise without attacking the questioner. Visible anger is career-damaging; measured firmness is credible.

When You Don't Know: Don't speculate. "That's a detailed question I want to answer accurately. Let me get back to you with specifics." Buying time is better than inventing facts. Journalists respect honesty; they punish fabrication.

Role-Play: The Hostile Interview

Scenario: Your company faces allegation of safety violations in a manufacturing plant. An investigation is underway. A journalist has scheduled a live interview about the company's safety record and whether management failures contributed to the problem.

Your Interview Preparation

Identify three key messages: (1) safety is paramount, (2) investigation is rigorous and transparent, (3) systemic improvements are underway
Prepare specific data: safety investment over past three years, independent audits conducted, incident rate trends
Anticipate hostile questions: Why didn't you catch this earlier? Is your safety record worse than competitors? Are you prioritising profit over people?
Develop bridge language for each difficult question
Practice remaining composed under pressure; record yourself
Acknowledge the seriousness without admitting fault prematurely
Communicate accountability and commitment to remediation, not defensiveness

Execution

Ideally, work with a peer role-playing the hostile journalist. They should ask unexpected follow-ups, interrupt, and challenge your answers. This pressure teaches you to stay composed and on-message. Aim for 5–7 minutes of live interview simulation. Pay attention to body language, pace, and tone of voice, not just words.

Reflection: Transparency vs PR Strategy

There is tension between genuine transparency and strategic communication. The cynical view: PR is sophisticated lying. The naive view: complete transparency is always best. The reality is more nuanced.

Ethical Questions: Is it ethical to prepare sound bites? Is deflecting an uncomfortable question dishonest? When is staying on message appropriate, and when does it become evasive?

Strong media relations aren't about outsmarting journalists; they're about earning trust. The executives journalists trust are those who are honest within appropriate boundaries, who provide context when asked, and who don't pretend to have answers they don't. Those who are caught in deception or evasion lose credibility permanently.

Social media has democratised scrutiny. A careless phrase can go viral before your PR team even learns it was said. This makes authenticity more important, not less. Executives who have thoughtful beliefs and communicate them clearly are more credible than those who hide behind purely strategic messaging.
