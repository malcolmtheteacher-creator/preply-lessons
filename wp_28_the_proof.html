A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XXVIII of L

The Proof

In which a monk tries to prove God exists using nothing but logic—and starts an argument that is still going a thousand years later

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet Anselm, who believed that if you can think of something, and that something is greater in reality than in thought alone, then it must exist. This is the Ontological Argument—and it will obsess philosophers for centuries. But notice: it proves God exists, not what God is like. This is the crucial lesson: proof and encounter are not the same.

I. Before We Begin

What do you know about proofs for God's existence? Is it possible to prove God logically? What would such a proof look like?

♦ ♦ ♦

A Thought to Hold

Can you think of something greater than God? If not, then God exists. This is Anselm's argument, and it is the most unusual proof ever written. It doesn't point to the world. It doesn't rely on observation. It relies only on the logic of the concept.

For over a thousand years, philosophers have been unable to decide whether it works. And that very inability tells us something important about the nature of proof itself.

II. The Story—A Monk in Prayer

Anselm was born in 1033 in Italy, and became a monk at the monastery of Bec in Normandy. He was not trying to be a philosopher. He was trying to be a monk. His project was expressed in four words: faith seeking understanding. He believed in God, and he wanted to understand what he believed.

The Quest

Anselm meditated on the nature of God. God, he said, must be “that than which nothing greater can be thought.” In other words, God is the being who possesses all perfections in the fullest possible way. No greater being can even be conceived.

Now, if God is that than which nothing greater can be thought, and if something can be greater when it exists in reality than when it exists only in the mind, then God must exist in reality. Why? Because if God only existed in thought, we could think of a being greater—one that also exists in reality. But we cannot think of anything greater than God. Therefore, God must exist in reality.

That than which nothing greater can be thought cannot exist in the understanding alone. For if it exists merely in the understanding, it can be thought to exist in reality as well, which is greater. If, then, that than which nothing greater can be thought exists in the understanding alone, then that than which nothing greater can be thought is that than which something greater can be thought. This is a contradiction. — Anselm, Proslogion, Chapter 2

The Response

Almost immediately, a monk named Gaunilo objected. He said: I can conceive of a perfect island. Does that prove the perfect island exists? Obviously not. So Anselm's argument must be flawed.

Anselm replied: The argument works for God alone, because God is the only thing whose very concept requires existence. You can conceive of a perfect island, but your concept doesn't require it to exist. But the concept of God (the greatest conceivable being) does require existence. If God doesn't exist, then the concept fails—because a non-existent being cannot be the greatest.

The Fate of the Argument

For a thousand years, philosophers have debated this. Aquinas tried to refute it (saying it doesn't prove what it claims). Descartes tried to defend it (saying it proves God must exist). Kant tried to dissolve it (saying existence is not a predicate). Hegel tried to revive it. Gödel tried to formalize it mathematically. And the argument is still debated today.

Why is it so hard to dismiss? Because it seems to have a logical force. But why is it hard to accept? Because it doesn't feel like a real proof. It doesn't show you God. It just rearranges concepts.

A Key Insight from Anselm

Near the end of his life, Anselm summed up his entire philosophical project in one sentence:

I do not seek to understand in order to believe, but I believe in order to understand. — Anselm, Proslogion, Preface

This is crucial. Anselm was not a rationalist trying to deduce everything from pure reason. He was a believer trying to understand his faith rationally. The difference matters. His proofs were never meant to convince the atheist. They were meant to help the believer see the rationality of what he already believed.

III. The Ideas—Proof, Encounter, and Understanding

The Ontological Argument is famous. But three other ideas matter more for our journey.

Idea 1: The Concept of Greatness

Existence in reality is greater than existence in the mind alone.

This seems obvious. But it's the foundation of Anselm's proof. The idea is that if you compare two things that are otherwise identical—except one exists only in thought and one exists in reality—the one that exists in reality is greater. Therefore, the greatest conceivable being must exist in reality.

The question is: does this actually work? Or is existence not a kind of “greatness” that can be compared like this?

Idea 2: Proof Is Not the Same As Encounter

A logical proof that God exists is not the same as an encounter with God.

This is the deepest insight in the lesson. Anselm proves (or attempts to prove) that God must exist. But the proof tells you nothing about what God is like. The proof doesn't show you God's character, God's will, God's love. A proof is intellectual. An encounter is personal. The medieval philosophers often missed this distinction.

Idea 3: Faith Seeking Understanding

Reason is not a substitute for faith, but a way of deepening it.

Anselm's entire project is captured in this phrase. He believes already. He then uses reason to understand what he believes. This is the proper relationship between faith and reason: not that reason proves faith, but that reason clarifies and explores faith. The believer seeks to understand what he has already encountered.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Thesis Seed

Anselm tries to prove God by logic. He creates a proof that has never been fully refuted and never been fully accepted. And he raises a question that the next philosophers will struggle with: Can you know about God without knowing God? Can proof substitute for encounter? Can the intellect grasp what only love can know?

The answer is hidden in Anselm's own words: faith seeking understanding, not understanding producing faith. The deepest knowledge of God comes not from the proof that God exists, but from the encounter with God as love.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Does the Argument Work?

Anselm's proof: if God is the greatest conceivable being, God must exist in reality (not just in thought). Does this convince you? Or does Gaunilo's island objection refute it?

Proof vs. Encounter

The lesson says the proof tells you God exists but not what God is like. Is that a limitation of the proof? Or is it a feature?

Existence as a Perfection

Anselm assumes that existing in reality is greater than existing only in thought. Is that true? Is existence a kind of “perfection” that can be compared?

Faith Seeking Understanding

Anselm is not trying to convince atheists. He believes already and seeks to understand. Is this the right role for reason in faith?

A Thousand Years Later

Why has this argument survived for a thousand years if it doesn't convince people? What power does it have?

Perfect Things

Can you conceive of a perfect island? A perfect person? A perfect pizza? Why does perfection work for God but not for other things?

Understanding vs. Proof

Anselm is not a rationalist. He wants to understand what he already believes. Does that change how we should evaluate his argument?

2. The Ontological Argument

Describe Anselm's argument in your own words:

God is that than which nothing greater can be thought. Existence in reality is greater than existence in the mind alone. Therefore, if God exists only in the mind, we could think of something greater (God existing in reality). But this contradicts the definition of God as the greatest conceivable being. Therefore, God must exist in reality.

3. Gaunilo's Objection

What was Gaunilo's counterargument?

Gaunilo objected that by Anselm's logic, a perfect island must exist, because if it only existed in thought, we could conceive of a greater one (one that also exists in reality). But obviously a perfect island doesn't exist just because we can conceive of it. So Anselm's argument must be flawed.
