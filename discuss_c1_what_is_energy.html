Today's aim: Today we're working on explaining what the conservation of energy does and does not say, so you can discuss what energy is without relying on slogans.

Words you'll need

energy (n) — a quantity that can make things move, heat up or change, and that is converted between forms. Example: "The kettle converts electrical energy into heat."
conserve (v) — to keep a quantity unchanged in total over time. Example: "In an isolated system, energy is conserved."
convert (v) — to change from one form into another. Example: "A battery converts chemical energy into electrical energy."
kinetic (adj) — relating to movement. Example: "A moving car has kinetic energy."
potential (adj) — stored and able to be released later. Example: "A ball held high has potential energy."
thermal (adj) — relating to heat. Example: "Friction turns motion into thermal energy."
quantity (n) — an amount that can be measured or calculated. Example: "Energy is a quantity we calculate, not a substance we can see."
transfer (v) — to move something from one place or object to another. Example: "Heat transfers from the hot cup to the cool air."
symmetry (n) — a property of staying the same when something is changed. Example: "The laws of physics have a symmetry in time."
tally (n) — a running total. Example: "The accountant kept a tally of every pound."

The hook: the missing building block

The physicist Richard Feynman compared energy to a child's wooden blocks. A parent counts 28 blocks every evening. One day there are only 27, but the missing one is found under the sofa and the count is restored. Another day the count is off, until they weigh the toy box and work out how many blocks are inside it.

Energy is like that: nobody has ever seen the "blocks". What we have is a number that stays the same, once we add up every place energy could be hiding.

Say it aloud: Before you read on, say what this analogy suggests about whether energy is a physical substance.

Roughly: energy is a quantity we calculate, with strict rules about how it changes form, not a stuff that can be poured from one object to another. That raises a sharper question: why does the total never change? The answer turns out to involve symmetry, and links back to the first lesson in the series.

Where does the energy go?

Talk it through:

For each scene, name the "from" and "to". What is the common pattern? Hint: in every case, count everything that warms up or moves, not only the main object. Then test your pattern on the claim: "The swing lost its energy."

In every scene the total is the same before and after; only the form and location change, often ending as a small amount of heat spread among many particles. "Lost" is the wrong word: the energy is still there, but in a form that is very hard to use again. That link to spreading out connects directly to the arrow of time in lesson 1.

Three ideas, in small steps

Idea 1 · Energy changes form, but the total stays constant

The law of conservation of energy says that in an isolated system the total energy does not change. A falling ball loses stored energy and gains movement energy by exactly the same amount.

Idea 2 · An apparent loss usually means we have not counted everything

Friction turns motion into thermal energy: the surfaces warm up very slightly. In the 1840s James Joule measured this precisely: stirring water with a paddle warms it by an amount that matches the work done.

Idea 3 · Why is energy conserved at all?

In 1918 the mathematician Emmy Noether proved that conservation laws follow from symmetries. Energy conservation follows from the fact that the laws of physics do not change over time. Strictly speaking, in an expanding universe this symmetry holds only approximately on the largest scales, so physicists are careful about what "the total energy of the universe" means.

Try it: follow the energy of a bouncing ball

A ball is dropped from a height. The three bars show where its energy is: stored (because it is high up), movement (because it is moving) and heat (a tiny warming of the floor and ball). The grey bar adds all three together. Say aloud what you expect to happen to the grey bar, then press Drop.

Say it aloud:

1. With a perfect bounce, what happens to the stored and movement bars as the ball falls?
2. Switch to the real bounce. What happens to the heat bar at each bounce?
3. When the ball finally stops, where has its energy gone? Does the grey bar ever change?

What you just saw

1 · Trading places
As the ball falls, stored energy becomes movement energy, bar for bar.
2 · Some becomes heat
With a real bounce, a share of the energy becomes heat each time.
3 · The total is constant
The grey bar never changes, even when the ball has stopped.

The ball did not "use up" its energy. The energy changed form, and most of it ended up as heat.

So what is energy?

Everyone agrees that the total is conserved. Whether energy is a real "thing" is less clear. Three views are on the table:

PositionSupporters argue…Critics counter…
A · A substance-like quantityEnergy behaves like something that moves between objects, and it is useful to talk about it flowing.There is nothing to pick up or store: it is a number we calculate, and the "thing" picture breaks down, since movement energy depends on the observer's frame.
B · A bookkeeping deviceEnergy is a number we define so that a total stays constant: useful, but not a stuff.It does physical work: it is equivalent to mass (E = mc²) and shapes space-time, so "just bookkeeping" undersells it.
C · A consequence of symmetryEnergy is the quantity conserved because the laws do not change over time, which gives it a deep reason.That symmetry does not hold exactly in the whole expanding universe, so conservation of total energy is not guaranteed there.

Precision toolkit: qualifying a claim and challenging a metaphor

Qualifying the claim
· To a very good approximation, …
· In any isolated system, …
· As long as we count …
· Strictly speaking, …
Challenging a metaphor
· That metaphor only goes so far.
· The analogy breaks down because …
· It is more accurate to say …
· That risks implying that …
Rewrite it: rewrite each overstated claim so that it is accurate and carefully hedged. Attempt first, then compare.

1. "Energy can never be lost."

2. "Energy is a fluid that flows from one object to another."

3. "Perpetual motion machines fail because energy is conserved."

1. In an isolated system the total energy stays constant, although it may be converted into forms, such as spread-out heat, that are hard to put to use.
2. It can be useful to picture energy as flowing, but energy is a quantity we calculate rather than a substance, so the fluid analogy only goes so far.
3. Machines that would produce energy from nothing are ruled out by conservation of energy, while those that merely avoid losing energy to heat run into the second law of thermodynamics.
