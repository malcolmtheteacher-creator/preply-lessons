The Salem Witch Trials

A frightened village, a court, and a panic that no one could stop.

Today: tell the story of the Salem witch trials, and talk about fear in a group. Grammar: reported speech with say and tell.

Key words — match them first

Before we read, let's learn the important words. Match each word (1–8) to its meaning (A–H). Say your answers out loud — "I think 1 is D" — then check.

Words

  1. a witch
  2. to accuse
  3. a confession
  4. evidence
  5. fear
  6. to blame
  7. innocent
  8. to hang

Meanings

  • A. facts that show something is true
  • B. a bad feeling when you think something dangerous will happen
  • C. to say someone did something wrong
  • D. a person (often a woman) people believed had evil magic
  • E. not guilty; you did nothing wrong
  • F. when you say you did something wrong
  • G. to kill someone with a rope around the neck
  • H. to say a problem is someone's fault

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — The sick girls

In the winter of 1692, in a small village called Salem in Massachusetts, two young girls became very strange. Their names were Betty and Abigail. They screamed, they shook, and they said something was hurting them. But no one could see anything.

A doctor came. He could find no illness. So he said the girls were "bewitched" — that a witch was hurting them with magic. The people of Salem were very religious, and they believed the Devil was real. They were also afraid: there had been war, sickness, and cold winters. Fear was already in the air.

The adults asked the girls one big question: "Who is hurting you?" So the girls said names. One of the first was Tituba, a woman who was a slave in the house. People asked her many questions, and in the end she gave a confession. She said there were other witches in the village, too. After that, the people could not stop. If there was one witch, maybe there were many.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the doctor say the girls were "bewitched"?

2. Why was Tituba's confession so dangerous for the village?

1. He could not find any illness, so he said a witch was hurting them with magic.

2. She said there were other witches too, so people thought the problem was big. The accusations could not stop at one person.

Talk about it:
  • The adults asked the girls "Who is hurting you?" — and the girls gave names. Can the way we ask a question change the answer people give?

Part 2 — The court and the panic

In the spring of 1692, more and more people were accused. Neighbour accused neighbour. Some people used old fights about land or money to hurt people they did not like. The fear spread very fast.

So the leaders made a special court. The judges were sure that witches were real and dangerous. But the court used very weak evidence. A person could say, "Your spirit came to me at night and hurt me." No one else could see this spirit. But the court believed it. So it was almost impossible to say you were innocent.

There was also a cruel rule. If you said "Yes, I am a witch", and you named other people, they let you live. But if you said "No, I am innocent", you were in great danger. About two hundred people were accused. Here is an important fact: the people were not burned. Many people think this, but it is wrong. In Salem, nineteen people were hanged. One old man, Giles Corey, would not speak in court, so they put heavy stones on him until he died. A few more people died in prison.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was it almost impossible to say you were innocent?

2. How did the nineteen people die — and why is "burned" wrong?

1. The court used weak evidence. A person could say "your spirit hurt me", and no one else could see it, so you could not prove it was false.

2. They were hanged (with a rope), not burned. Many people think they were burned, but that is a myth. (Giles Corey died under heavy stones, and a few died in prison.)

Talk about it:
  • In this court, saying "Yes, I am a witch" kept you alive, but saying "No, I am innocent" was dangerous. Can we trust a confession when people are so afraid?

Part 3 — The end and the apology

In the autumn of 1692, people started to doubt the court. Important religious leaders said the "spirit" evidence was too dangerous. They said the Devil could even look like an innocent person. So how could the court be sure?

Then the accusations reached more powerful families. People say someone even accused the governor's own wife. The governor, William Phips, was now worried. He stopped the court from using the "spirit" evidence. He closed the special court. A new court let the other prisoners go free. By May 1693, the trials were over.

Later, Salem was very sorry. The village had killed twenty of its own people on bad evidence. In 1697, one of the judges, Samuel Sewall, said sorry in his church in front of everyone. Years later, in 1706, Ann Putnam — a girl who had accused people — also said sorry. She said she had been wrong. Today, we still talk about Salem. When people are accused on weak evidence and shared fear, we call it a "witch hunt".

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What finally stopped the trials?

2. How did Salem say sorry later?

1. Religious leaders said the "spirit" evidence was too dangerous, and the accusations reached powerful families. The governor stopped the court and freed the prisoners. The trials ended in May 1693.

2. A judge (Samuel Sewall) said sorry in his church in 1697; the accuser Ann Putnam said sorry in 1706. Later, the names of the dead were cleared.

Talk about it:
  • The apology came many years too late for the people who died. Is an apology still important when the harm is already done?

Grammar: reported speech with say and tell

This story is full of words: people said things and told the court things. When we report what a person said, we often use say or tell.

say or tell?
• We tell someone something. We always need a person after tell: "She told the judge that…"
• We say something. We do not need a person: "She said that…"
• The verb often moves back one step in time: "I see a ghost" → She said that she saw a ghost. "I am innocent" → She said that she was innocent.

1. say or tell?

Write said or told in each gap. Remember: tell needs a person.

1. She that she saw a ghost.

2. She the judge that she was innocent.

3. They the people that Tituba was a witch.

1. said (no person after the verb)

2. told (there is a person: "the judge")

3. told (there is a person: "the people")

2. Make reported speech

Change the words to reported speech. Use said or told, and move the verb back one step.

1. "I am a witch." → She said that she a witch.

2. "I see the Devil." → He said that he the Devil.

3. "You are guilty." → They told her that she guilty.

1. was (am → was)

2. saw (see → saw)

3. was (are → was)

Task B · How fear spreads (3 minutes)

In Salem, fear spread through the whole village very fast. Why does fear spread so quickly in a group? When people are afraid, why do they sometimes blame other people?

Task C · A "witch hunt" today (3 minutes)

We still say "witch hunt" today. Can you think of any time — in history, in the news, or in your own life — when people blamed someone on weak evidence? Tell the story: how did it start, and how did it end?

To finish

  2. The panic needed a first accuser and a crowd who believed it. Who do you think was most responsible — the first accuser, the court, or the village?
