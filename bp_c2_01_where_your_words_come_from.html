The Three Layers of English

English is not one language — it's three languages stacked on top of each other. Think of it as a geological formation: each layer was deposited by a different historical event, and each carries a completely different emotional weight.

Layer 3: Latin & Greek The Library Science · Medicine · Law · Philosophy — 4+ syllables
Layer 2: French / Norman The Court Government · Cuisine · Fashion · Art — 2-3 syllables
Layer 1: Anglo-Saxon (Old English) The Home · The Heart · The Gut Body · Family · Nature · Emotion · Work — 1 syllable

When? The Renaissance (1400s-1600s) and the Scientific Revolution. Scholars deliberately borrowed from Latin and Greek to create "learned" vocabulary.

Where? Universities, laboratories, courtrooms, hospitals.

Feeling: Distant. Clinical. Authoritative. Impressive — but cold.

When? After the Norman Conquest of 1066. French-speaking Normans ruled England for 300 years. French became the language of power, law, and culture.

Where? Government, courts, high society, dining rooms.

Feeling: Polished. Refined. Administrative. Social distance.

When? Since the very beginning. These words have been in English for over 1,500 years — brought by the Angles, Saxons, and Jutes.

Where? The home, the field, the body, the heart. The everyday.

Feeling: Direct. Physical. Emotional. Honest. Real.

The Key Insight

About 80% of the 1,000 most common English words are Anglo-Saxon. But if you open a dictionary of 100,000 words, the majority are Latin and French.

We live in Anglo-Saxon. We work in Latin.

And that tension — between the short, gutsy words we use at home and the long, polished words we use in meetings — is what makes English so powerful. And so dangerous, when you choose the wrong layer.

Why This Matters in Business

Every time you speak in a meeting, write an email, or give a presentation, you are — consciously or not — choosing between these three layers. And that choice changes how people feel about what you're saying.

The Churchill Principle

"Short words are best, and old words when short are best of all."
— Winston Churchill

Churchill was one of history's greatest speakers precisely because he understood this. His most famous speeches are built almost entirely from Anglo-Saxon words.

"We shall fight on the beaches..." — How many of these words are Anglo-Saxon? Click to reveal

Almost every single word. "We," "shall," "fight," "on," "the," "beaches," "landing," "grounds," "fields," "streets," "hills" — all Anglo-Saxon. The only possible exception is "surrender" (French origin). Churchill instinctively reached for the oldest, shortest words to convey courage and defiance.

The principle for business: When you want to persuade, inspire, or connect — go Anglo-Saxon. When you need to classify, analyse, or demonstrate expertise — reach for Latin. The best communicators move between layers deliberately.

The Swearing Secret

Here's a fascinating detail: almost all English swear words are Anglo-Saxon. The "polite" alternatives are French or Latin. This is a direct legacy of the class system — the ruling Normans considered the peasants' Germanic words to be vulgar.

This means Anglo-Saxon words carry more emotional charge than their Latinate equivalents. When you stub your toe, you don't say "Malediction!" — you reach for the oldest, shortest word available.

In business, this emotional charge is your secret weapon. A CEO who says "We failed" (Anglo-Saxon) is more powerful than one who says "We experienced a suboptimal outcome" (Latin). The short word is harder to hide behind.

The "Anglish" Extreme

In 1989, science fiction writer Poul Anderson wrote an entire essay about atomic theory using only Germanic-root words. He called it "Uncleftish Beholding" — "uncleftish" being his Anglo-Saxon replacement for "atomic" (from "un-cleft" = unsplittable).

This is the extreme — and it's playful, not practical. But it shows us how deeply Latin has penetrated our "expert" vocabulary. The point isn't to reject Latin — it's to choose consciously.

Without looking at any notes or previous tabs, answer these questions out loud:

1. What are the three layers of English, and what historical event created each one? Attempt first → Click

Layer 1: Anglo-Saxon — brought by Germanic tribes (Angles, Saxons, Jutes) from the 5th century. The bedrock.

Layer 2: French — arrived with the Norman Conquest of 1066. The language of power and administration for 300 years.

Layer 3: Latin/Greek — imported during the Renaissance and Scientific Revolution. The language of learning and expertise.

2. Give three "power pairs" — an Anglo-Saxon word and its Latinate equivalent. Attempt first → Click

Any three from: ask/interrogate, help/facilitate, buy/procure, end/terminate, sweat/perspiration, fear/trepidation, begin/initiate, think/contemplate, house/domicile, gut/viscera, etc.

3. In business, when would you use Anglo-Saxon words and when would you use Latin words? Attempt first → Click

Anglo-Saxon: When you want to connect emotionally, build trust, inspire, apologise, tell a story, or be direct.

Latin: When you need precision, legal protection, technical authority, or diplomatic distance.

The best communicators move between layers deliberately.

4. Why are body part words (eye, arm, leg, hip) so short? Attempt first → Click

Because they're Anglo-Saxon — the oldest layer of English. The most frequently used words in any language tend to be the shortest (Zipf's Law). These words have been worn down over 1,500 years of daily use, losing suffixes and prefixes until only the core syllable remains.
