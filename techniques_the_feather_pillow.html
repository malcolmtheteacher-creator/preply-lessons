The Feather Pillow

Horacio Quiroga (1907) — a young bride, a cold marble house, an illness no doctor can explain, and a pillow that is strangely heavy.

Today: you'll tell a South American horror story that unfolds one degree at a time, and use the language of gradual change — weaker and weaker, the more… the less…, ever paler, with each night — to make a listener feel something slipping away.
Before we read:
  • Think of a house you found cold or unwelcoming the first time you entered it. What made it so — the light, the size, the silence?
  • Have you ever watched someone become ill slowly, with no clear cause? What did the not-knowing do to the people around them?
  • What is the most frightening thing: a monster you can see, or a change you can only measure?

Key words — think first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Think first — the Reveal only confirms.

A. Work it out from the sentence

Read each sentence aloud and say what the bold word means from the rest of the sentence. Then reveal.

1. He loved her deeply but never showed it; his impassive face gave nothing away, and she learned not to look for anything in it.
impassive (adj) — showing no emotion; expressionless.
2. The illness crept in insidiously: a slight flu that dragged on for days, then weeks, until nobody could say when it had become something else.
insidiously (adv) — gradually and harmfully, in a way that is not noticed until it is too late. (adj: insidious)
3. The bite was almost imperceptible — a tiny mark, no pain — which is why nobody thought to look for it.
imperceptible (adj) — so small or slight that it cannot be noticed.
4. Something was draining her, night after night: each morning she woke whiter and weaker, as if life were leaking out of her while she slept.
to drain (v) — to empty gradually of liquid or strength; to be drained of blood / energy.

B. Build the word

You know one form of the word. Say the form the sentence needs, then reveal.

5. You know hallucinate (to see things that are not there). What is the noun — she began to have ___?
hallucinations (n) — things seen or heard that are not really there. hallucinate → hallucination
6. You know delirious (confused and raving, from fever). What is the noun — she sank into ___?
delirium (n) — a state of confused, wild talk caused by fever or illness. delirious → delirium
7. You know parasite (a creature that lives by feeding on another). What is the adjective — a ___ insect?
parasitic (adj) — living on and feeding from another creature. parasite → parasitic

C. Choose the meaning

One sentence, three options. Say a, b or c — then reveal.

8. The glacial shine of the white walls made the whole house feel like a palace in winter. Glacial means: (a) icy, extremely cold (b) shining like gold (c) very old
(a) — glacial (adj) — icy cold; of manner, cold and unfriendly. (from glacier)
9. The doctors diagnosed a severe anaemia that they could not explain. Anaemia is: (a) a fever (b) a lack of red blood, causing weakness and pallor (c) a broken bone
(b) — anaemia (n) — a condition in which the blood lacks red cells, causing weakness and paleness. (American: anemia)
10. Her honeymoon was one long shudder. A shudder is: (a) a smile (b) a sudden shaking movement from cold, fear or disgust (c) a holiday
(b) — a shudder (n) / to shudder (v) — to shake suddenly with cold, fear or horror.

The story, in three parts

Quiroga's story, translated here from the Spanish and only slightly shortened — it is short, and every sentence is doing something. Read each part; after it, try the quick check from memory, then talk about the question. Don't skip ahead: the last paragraph is the whole point.

Part 1 — The house of marble

Her honeymoon was one long shudder. Fair, angelic and timid, she found that her husband's hard character froze the childish dreams she had brought to marriage. She loved him very much, all the same, though sometimes with a slight shiver when, walking home together at night, she glanced up at the tall figure of Jordán, who had not spoken for an hour. He, for his part, loved her profoundly, without letting it show.

For three months — they had married in April — they lived a peculiar happiness. No doubt she would have wished for less severity in that rigid heaven of love, for a more open and careless tenderness; but her husband's impassive face always held her back.

The house they lived in had something to do with her shivers. The whiteness of the silent courtyard — friezes, columns, marble statues — gave the autumnal impression of an enchanted palace. Inside, the glacial shine of the stucco, without the slightest scratch on the high walls, confirmed that sensation of unwelcoming cold. Crossing from one room to the next, footsteps echoed through the whole house, as if long abandonment had made it sensitive to every sound.

In that strange nest of love Alicia spent the whole autumn. She had ended by drawing a veil over her old dreams, and she lived like a sleeper in the hostile house, trying not to think of anything until her husband came home.

It was not surprising that she grew thin. She had a slight attack of influenza that dragged on insidiously for days and days; Alicia never quite recovered. At last, one afternoon, she was able to go out into the garden on his arm. She looked indifferently from side to side. Suddenly Jordán, with deep tenderness, passed his hand over her hair — and Alicia burst instantly into sobs and threw her arms round his neck. She wept out all her silent terror for a long time, weeping harder at the least attempt at a caress. Then the sobs slowed, and still she stayed a long while hidden against his neck, without moving or saying a word.

That was the last day Alicia was up. The next morning she woke in a faint.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the marriage like, and what is the house like?

2. What happens in the garden, and what happens the next morning?

1. He loves her deeply but shows nothing; she loves him but shivers at his hard, silent character. The house is white marble and glacial stucco, silent and echoing — 'an enchanted palace', unwelcoming and cold.

2. When Jordán tenderly touches her hair she breaks down and weeps out 'all her silent terror'. It is the last day she is ever up; next morning she is found unconscious.

Discuss:
  • Quiroga gives us a husband who loves 'profoundly, without letting it show'. Is his coldness part of what is killing her — or is that what the story wants you to suspect?

Part 2 — 'A serious case'

Jordán's doctor examined her with the greatest care and ordered complete rest and calm. 'I don't know,' he told Jordán at the street door, his voice still low. 'She has a great weakness that I can't explain, and no vomiting, nothing… If she wakes tomorrow as she is today, call me at once.'

The next day Alicia was worse. There was a consultation. They found an anaemia of the most acute kind, completely inexplicable. Alicia had no more fainting fits, but she was visibly going towards death. All day the bedroom stood with its lights on and in total silence; hours passed without the slightest sound. Alicia dozed. Jordán lived almost entirely in the drawing-room, also with every light burning, pacing ceaselessly from one end to the other with tireless obstinacy. The carpet swallowed his footsteps. Now and then he went into the bedroom and continued his mute pacing along the length of the bed, looking at his wife each time he walked towards her.

Soon Alicia began to have hallucinations — confused and floating at first, and then descending to the level of the floor. The young woman, her eyes enormously open, did nothing but stare at the carpet on either side of the bedhead. One night she suddenly stared fixedly. After a moment she opened her mouth to scream, and her nose and lips were pearled with sweat. 'Jordán! Jordán!' she cried, rigid with terror, never taking her eyes from the carpet. Jordán ran to the bedroom, and at the sight of him Alicia gave a shriek of horror. 'It's me, Alicia, it's me!' Alicia looked at him wildly, looked at the carpet, looked at him again, and after a long moment of stupefied confrontation she grew calm. She smiled and took her husband's hand between hers, stroking it, trembling.

Among her most persistent hallucinations there was an ape-like creature, resting its fingers on the carpet, with its eyes fixed on her.

The doctors came back, uselessly. There before them was a life ending, bleeding away day by day, hour by hour, and nobody knew how. At the last consultation Alicia lay in a stupor while they took her pulse, passing the lifeless wrist from one to another. They watched her a long time in silence and went on into the dining-room. 'Hm…' Her doctor shrugged, discouraged. 'It's a serious case… there's little to be done.' 'That's all I needed!' Jordán snorted, and drummed his fingers sharply on the table.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What do the doctors find, and what can they do?

2. What does Alicia see on the carpet, and how does she react when Jordán comes in?

1. An acute, completely inexplicable anaemia — she is 'bleeding away day by day' with no visible cause. They can do nothing but shrug: 'a serious case… little to be done'.

2. Hallucinations, one of them an ape-like creature with its fingers on the carpet and its eyes on her. When Jordán appears she shrieks in horror, as if he were the thing — then recognises him, smiles, and strokes his hand.

Discuss:
  • She screams at the sight of her husband before she knows him. Quiroga puts that scene right in the middle of the story. What is he letting you think?

Part 3 — The pillow

Alicia went on fading in a half-delirium of anaemia, worse in the evenings but always easing in the first hours of the day. By day her illness made no progress, but every morning she woke livid, almost in a faint. It seemed that only at night did her life go out of her in fresh waves of blood. Each time she woke she had the feeling of lying crushed into the bed with a million kilos on top of her.

From the third day this sinking never left her. She could hardly move her head. She would not let them touch the bed, nor even straighten her pillow. Her twilight terrors advanced in the shape of monsters that crawled to the bed and climbed laboriously up the counterpane.

Then she lost consciousness. For the last two days she raved without ceasing in a low voice. The lights stayed funereally lit in the bedroom and the drawing-room. In the dying silence of the house nothing could be heard but the monotonous delirium from the bed and the muffled sound of Jordán's eternal footsteps.

She died, at last. The servant who came in afterwards to strip the bed, alone now, looked for a moment in surprise at the pillow. 'Sir!' she called to Jordán in a low voice. 'There are marks on the pillow that look like blood.' Jordán came quickly and bent over it in his turn. Indeed, on the pillowcase, on both sides of the hollow that Alicia's head had left, there were small dark spots. 'They look like bites,' the servant murmured after a moment of motionless observation. 'Hold it up to the light,' Jordán said. The servant lifted it — and let it fall at once, and stood staring at it, livid and trembling. Without knowing why, Jordán felt his hair stand on end. 'What is it?' he murmured hoarsely. 'It's very heavy,' the servant got out, still trembling.

Jordán lifted it; it was extraordinarily heavy. They carried it out, and on the dining-room table Jordán cut open the case and the ticking with one stroke. The upper feathers flew up, and the servant gave a scream of horror with her whole mouth open, her hands clenched at her hair: at the bottom, among the feathers, slowly moving its hairy legs, was a monstrous animal, a living, viscous ball. It was so swollen that its mouth could scarcely be made out.

Night after night, from the time Alicia took to her bed, it had stealthily applied its mouth — its proboscis, rather — to her temples, sucking her blood. The bite was almost imperceptible. The daily shaking of the pillow had no doubt hindered its growth at first; but from the moment the young woman could no longer move, the sucking became vertiginous. In five days, in five nights, it had emptied Alicia.

These parasites of birds, tiny in their usual surroundings, can under certain conditions reach enormous proportions. Human blood seems to be particularly favourable to them, and it is not unusual to find them in feather pillows.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What pattern do the nights and days follow, and what does Alicia refuse to let anyone do?

2. What does the servant find, and what does Jordán do?

1. By day the illness makes no progress; each night 'fresh waves of blood' leave her and she wakes livid, feeling crushed under a million kilos. She will not let anyone touch the bed or even straighten her pillow.

2. Small dark spots like bites on both sides of the hollow where Alicia's head lay, and a pillow that is extraordinarily heavy. Jordán cuts it open on the dining table: inside, among the feathers, is a swollen, hairy, living creature that has drained Alicia's blood night by night.

Discuss:
  • The last paragraph is written like a footnote in a natural-history book. Why does Quiroga end a horror story with a calm scientific explanation — and does it make the story less frightening, or more?

The grammar this story runs on: describing gradual change

Nothing in this story happens suddenly. Alicia gets thinner, paler, weaker; the house gets quieter; the nights get worse. English has a whole toolkit for change that happens by degrees — and Quiroga's horror depends on it.

1. Doubled comparatives: -er and -er / more and more.
• "She grew weaker and weaker." • "The house became more and more silent." • "He spoke less and less."
For a formal or literary tone: ever + comparative — "ever paler, ever more silent".
2. The more…, the less… — two changes linked.
• "The longer she lay there, the heavier the pillow grew." • "The more the doctors examined her, the less they understood."
Both halves take the + comparative; the verb comes after the subject in each half.
3. Step by step: each / every + time word, and verbs of gradual change.
• "Each morning she woke a little whiter." • "With every night the creature grew." • "Day by day, hour by hour, she was bleeding away."
Verbs: to fade, to dwindle, to drain away, to creep in, to wear down, to swell.

2. The more…, the less…

Join the two changes with the + comparative… the + comparative. Say it, then reveal.

1. She lay still for longer. → The pillow got heavier. (long / heavy)

2. The doctors examined her more. → They understood less. (much / little)

3. The nights got darker. → Her terrors got worse. (dark / bad)

4. Jordán loved her more. → He showed it less. (much / little)

5. The creature drank more blood. → Its bite became harder to see. (much / hard)

Why this story stays with you

Horacio Quiroga (1878–1937) was born in Uruguay and spent much of his life in the jungle province of Misiones in northern Argentina, writing stories in which the natural world is patient, indifferent and lethal. His life was a catalogue of catastrophe — his father, stepfather, best friend, first wife and, eventually, he himself all died by violence or suicide — and he learned his craft from Poe. 'The Feather Pillow' appeared in 1907 and was collected in Stories of Love, Madness and Death (1917), a title that describes the whole of his work.

The story works like a magic trick. For three pages it lets you believe you are reading a Gothic tale about a cold husband and a marble house — the shivers, the silence, the wife who screams at the sight of him — and every reader quietly suspects Jordán. Then the pillow is cut open, and the horror turns out to be smaller, stranger and more real than a cruel husband: a thing that lived under her head, feeding, while a man who loved her walked up and down in the next room. The final paragraph, written in the flat voice of an encyclopaedia, refuses to comfort you. It says: this can happen. Check your pillow.

Look again at the hallucination. Alicia sees 'an ape-like creature, resting its fingers on the carpet, with its eyes fixed on her'. Everyone calls it delirium. Read the ending, then read that sentence again. What was she seeing?

Go deeper — talk it through

Discuss:
  • Quiroga makes us suspect the husband for three pages and then clears him. Is Jordán innocent — or is a man who loves 'without letting it show', in a house that cold, part of what made her unable to fight?
  • The creature is 'imperceptible' and the illness 'inexplicable'. Which is the true subject of the story: a monster, or the limits of what doctors — and husbands — can see?
  • The last paragraph is scientific, calm and (roughly) true. Why is that more frightening than a ghost? What does it do to you tonight?
  • Quiroga's other stories are about snakes, rivers, sunstroke, machinery — nature and things that kill without malice. What is the story's attitude to the creature? Is it evil?

Task B · The case against Jordán (4 minutes)

The pillow killed her. Or did it? Prosecute the husband: the cold house, the silence, the wife who wept 'all her silent terror' at his first tender touch, the pacing instead of looking. Then defend him for one minute: he loved her profoundly, he called every doctor, and nobody in 1907 could have known.

Task C · Something that grows by degrees (4 minutes)

Describe a change that happened so gradually that nobody noticed until it was too late — in a person, a place, a relationship, a country, a body. Use doubled comparatives and the more… the less…, and save the explanation for the end, as Quiroga does.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • A friendship that cooled one cancelled coffee at a time.
  • A town whose shops closed one by one until nobody remembered when it had died.
  • A habit — a drink, a screen, a debt — that grew a little every week.
  • A river, a glacier or a forest that shrank while everyone said it looked the same as last year.
  • A boss whose small unkindnesses added up to something nobody could name.
  • An illness in a family, told from the point of view of the person who paced the corridor.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Quiroga makes the horror tiny, real and biological. Is that more or less frightening than a ghost or a murderer — and why?
  2. 'Her honeymoon was one long shudder.' Would Alicia have survived in a warmer house, with a warmer husband? Does the story think so?
  3. Are there things in your life that are 'imperceptible' now, and growing?
