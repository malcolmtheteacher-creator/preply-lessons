The story, in three parts

Part 1 — Nine knights and a road full of danger

Around the year 1119, the roads to Jerusalem were lethal. The First Crusade had left the holy city in Christian hands roughly two decades earlier, and pilgrims poured in from across Europe to walk where they believed Christ had walked. But the journey through the Holy Land was long and lawless: bandits robbed and killed unarmed travellers on the mountain passes, and no one protected them.

Into this stepped a French knight named Hugh de Payns and a small band of companions — the sources say around nine men. They made an unusual vow. They would live like monks, poor, chaste and obedient, yet they would also carry swords and fight. Their single purpose was to guard the pilgrim roads. The king of Jerusalem gave them quarters in a wing of the royal palace, on the site said to be the ancient Temple of Solomon. From that temple came their name: the Poor Fellow-Soldiers of Christ and of the Temple — in short, the Knights Templar.

At first they were genuinely poor; their seal is said to have shown two knights sharing a single horse. But their idea — holy warriors sworn to protect the faithful — captured the imagination of Christian Europe. A famous churchman, Bernard of Clairvaux, championed them, and in 1139 the Pope placed the order under his own protection alone. From that moment the Templars answered to no king and no local bishop — only to Rome. Gifts of land and money began to flood in.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why were the Templars founded, and what was unusual about the vow they took?

2. What did the Pope grant the order in 1139, and why did it matter?

1. To protect Christian pilgrims travelling to Jerusalem from bandits; unusually, the men lived as monks (poor, chaste, obedient) yet also fought as knights.

2. He placed them under the protection of Rome alone, so they answered to no king or local bishop — only the Pope. This gave them extraordinary independence, and gifts flowed in.

Discuss:
  • A soldier and a monk seem like opposites. What might have made the idea of a "warrior-monk" so powerful to people at the time?

Part 2 — The richest order in Christendom

Over the next century the Templars became something no one had planned: the closest thing medieval Europe had to an international bank. A pilgrim setting out from, say, London could deposit his money at a Templar house, receive a coded letter of credit, and withdraw the equivalent — give or take the fees — from a Templar house near Jerusalem. His gold never travelled the dangerous roads; only the paper did. It was, in effect, an early cheque system, stretching across thousands of miles.

Trusted, disciplined and answerable only to the Pope, the order was soon holding treasure, guarding royal jewels, and lending vast sums to the crowned heads of Europe. Kings borrowed from them to fund wars and weddings. The Templars owned farms, mills, vineyards and whole fleets of ships; by some estimates they held upwards of nine thousand properties across Europe and the East. Their fortified houses doubled as strongrooms, and their word was as good as gold.

On the battlefield they were feared as an elite shock force, sworn never to retreat unless hopelessly outnumbered. But the Crusader kingdoms were slowly collapsing. In 1291 the last great Christian stronghold in the Holy Land, the city of Acre, fell after a brutal siege, and the Templars lost the very frontier they had been created to defend. An order of warrior-monks with no war left to fight — but with immense wealth and no clear master — was now a dangerous thing to be.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the Templar "banking" system let a pilgrim move money safely?

2. What happened in 1291, and why did it leave the order exposed?

1. He deposited gold at one Templar house, took a coded letter of credit, and withdrew the equivalent at a Templar house near his destination — so only the paper travelled the dangerous roads, not the gold.

2. Acre, the last major Christian stronghold in the Holy Land, fell. The Templars lost the frontier they existed to defend — leaving a hugely wealthy order with no war to fight and no clear protector.

Discuss:
  • The Templars' great strength — being trusted, independent and rich — became their great danger. Why can wealth and independence make an organisation less safe, not more?

Part 3 — Friday the 13th

By the early 1300s, King Philip IV of France was deep in debt — much of it owed to the Templars — and desperate for money. He had already expelled other groups and seized their property. The order, with its treasure and its independence from royal control, was an obvious and tempting target. Philip needed only a reason that would satisfy the Church.

On Friday, 13 October 1307, acting on the king's secret orders, royal officers across France arrested the Templars in a single coordinated dawn sweep — the Grand Master, Jacques de Molay, among them. The charges were shocking and, most historians agree, fabricated: heresy, secret rituals, and betrayal of the faith. Under prolonged and brutal interrogation, many of the men confessed to things they had almost certainly not done. Some later withdrew their confessions; a number were condemned to death. The proceedings were grim, and driven far more by the king's need for money and control than by any real evidence.

Under heavy pressure from Philip, the Pope dissolved the entire order in 1312. In 1314, the aged Grand Master Jacques de Molay was executed in Paris. According to a legend that grew up afterwards, he called from the flames for the king and the Pope to answer for their deeds before God within the year — and, as it happened, both men were dead within months. The Templars' lands were broken up, and the order vanished from history. But the mystery did not. Where had their fabled treasure gone? What secrets had they really kept? For seven hundred years, the silence has been filled with legend.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did King Philip IV move against the Templars, and what happened on 13 October 1307?

2. What became of the order and its Grand Master, and what do most historians think of the charges?

1. Philip was heavily in debt to them and wanted their wealth and independence gone. On Friday 13 October 1307 his officers arrested the Templars across France in one coordinated dawn operation, including the Grand Master Jacques de Molay.

2. The Pope dissolved the order in 1312; de Molay was executed in 1314. Most historians regard the charges of heresy and secret crimes as fabricated, and the confessions as forced under interrogation.

Discuss:
  • The charges were probably invented, yet they destroyed the order all the same. Why can an accusation do so much damage, even when the evidence behind it is weak?

Grammar — Approximation & vague language

Almost nothing about the Templars comes down to us as a firm figure. How many knights founded the order? How rich were they? How many properties did they hold? The honest answer is usually "we're not sure." English has a whole toolkit for exactly this — phrases that let you give a figure while signalling that it is an estimate, not a fact. Used well, they make you sound careful and precise, not vague.

The main expressions:
• roughly / around / about / approximately — close to a number, not exact: "roughly nine knights", "around the year 1119", "approximately two centuries".
• in the region of — a more formal way to say "approximately", often with large sums: "a fortune in the region of several million".
• upwards of — that amount or more: "upwards of nine thousand properties" (= nine thousand, possibly many more).
• well over / well under — comfortably more / less than a figure: "well over a century", "well under half survived".
• give or take — added after a figure to show a margin of error: "two hundred years, give or take."
Watch the direction each one points:
• around / roughly / about / approximately / in the region of = could be a little more or a little less.
• upwards of and well over = at least that much, probably more.
• well under = clearly less than that.
• give or take usually comes at the end, and can take a specific margin: "1307, give or take a year."
