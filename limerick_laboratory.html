What is a Limerick?
The world's most dangerous poem.
The Hook

Limericks are 500 years old. They started in Ireland, probably in pubs.

They were designed to be rude, funny, and impossible to forget.

Edward Lear made them famous in 1846. He wrote 212 of them. He never explained the AABBA rule — he just wrote so many that people figured it out.

The most famous limerick ever written has no author. It appeared on a wall in Pompeii — 2,000 years ago. Short, funny poems about real people have existed as long as writing has.

The Pattern
AABBA. Five lines. One punchline.
The Structure
ALine 1 — introduces someone or somewhere (8-9 syllables)
ALine 2 — continues, rhymes with line 1 (8-9 syllables)
BLine 3 — shorter twist (5-6 syllables)
BLine 4 — continues, rhymes with line 3 (5-6 syllables)
ALine 5 — THE PUNCHLINE. Rhymes with 1 and 2. (8-9 syllables)
The secret weapon: The punchline in line 5 should surprise the reader. You set up a situation in lines 1-4, then the last line hits them with something unexpected, ridiculous, or brilliant.
The Rhythm — Da-DUM

Limericks have a bouncy beat. Listen to the stress pattern:

there ONCE was a MAN from Nan-TU-cket who KEPT all his CASH in a BU-cket but HIS daugh-ter NAN ran A-way with A man and AS for the BU-cket, Nan-TU-cket

Read it out loud. Tap the table on the CAPITALS. Feel the beat?

You don't need to count syllables perfectly. If it sounds bouncy when you read it out loud — it's working.

The Golden Rule

A limerick is bad if:

  • The rhymes are forced or wrong
  • It doesn't have a punchline in line 5
  • It sounds flat — no bounce — when you read it out

A limerick is great if:

  • It makes someone laugh or groan
  • The punchline surprises you
  • It's impossible to read it without bouncing

Watch the Thinking Process

Click each step to reveal — discuss before revealing the next one.

Thinking out loud: The Gull is our character. What rhymes easily with something connected to Gutenberg or printing?

"press" → yes, bless, mess, address — lots of options
"ink" → think, drink, brink, sink — also good
"book" → took, look, cook, hook — very easy rhymes

Let's go with "press". Now I need a strong line 1.
Lines 1 and 2 (A rhymes with "press"):

A gull named old Johannes had a press,
He printed all the books — what a success!

Notice: Line 1 sets up the character and situation. Line 2 gives a small fact and rhymes.
Lines 3 and 4 (B — shorter, new rhyme):

What rhymes well for the short lines? I want to set up the punchline...
"ink" → think, drink, brink

He was covered in ink,
(more than you'd think),

Good. Short. Bouncy. Sets up a reveal about the ink...
Line 5 — the punchline (must rhyme with "press" and "success"):

The best punchlines connect back to something funny or surprising. Gutenberg's printing press changed the whole world — so I want to end with that sense of scale, but make it ridiculous...

But the whole world still reads — more or less.

"More or less" is the joke — after all this effort, all these books... "more or less." Modest. Funny.
A gull named old Johannes had a press, He printed all the books — what a success! He was covered in ink, (More than you'd think), But the whole world still reads — more or less.
Read it out loud. Does it bounce? Does line 5 surprise you a little? That's what makes it a limerick.

We Do — Together
Let's build a limerick about the Fish (Fibonacci).
The Setup

The Fish = Fibonacci. He discovered the number sequence found everywhere in nature: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21...

It appears in sunflowers, shells, hurricanes, galaxies. Fibonacci found it in rabbit populations. True story.

For the limerick: think about what's funny or strange about a fish who obsesses over numbers. A fish who counts things underwater. A fish who sees spirals everywhere. A fish who can't stop...
Step 1 — Find your A rhyme

What's a good strong rhyme word connected to Fibonacci or numbers?

Brainstorm with your teacher. What words are connected to maths, counting, sequences, fish?

Pick one. Now find 2-3 words that rhyme with it. You'll need 3 rhymes total for your A lines.
