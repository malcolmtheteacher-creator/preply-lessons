A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XXXV of L

The Blank Slate

John Locke: Empiricism, natural rights, and the mind as a wax tablet

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet the philosopher who made sensation the foundation of knowledge. John Locke (1632–1704) said: the mind begins as a blank slate. Nothing is innate. All knowledge comes from experience—from what our senses deliver to us. This empiricism became the foundation of modern science. But it created a puzzle: if all knowledge comes from sensation, how do we know the world itself? And in politics, Locke offered an alternative to Hobbes: not absolute sovereignty but natural rights, consent of the governed, and the right to revolution. You will place Locke and empiricism in your palace, and ask whether Augustine was right: we are restless without God.

I. Before We Begin

Where does knowledge come from?

Are we born knowing anything? Or must we learn everything from experience?

♦ ♦ ♦

A question to carry

If we begin as blank slates, how do we know anything about God? Augustine believed God writes himself on our hearts. Locke says there is no innate knowledge. We learn about God the same way we learn about anything else: through experience. But what if the experience never comes?

II. The Story — The Mind as a Blank Tablet

John Locke (1632–1704) lived through England's religious and political upheaval. He believed knowledge and political freedom were both founded on the same principle: respect for experience and evidence, not received authority.

Tabula Rasa

Locke's revolutionary claim: the mind begins as tabula rasa—a blank slate. Nothing is innate. Not ideas of God, not moral principles, not even the concept of substance. Everything must be learned through the senses.

Let us then suppose the Mind to be, as we say, white Paper, void of all Characters, without any Ideas; How comes it to be furnished? Whence comes it by that vast store, which the boundless Fancy of Man has painted on it? To this I answer, in one word, From Experience. — John Locke, Essay Concerning Human Understanding, Book II, Chapter 2

This was a direct challenge to Descartes and the rationalists, who believed in innate ideas. Locke said: no. The mind is empty at birth. Experience fills it.

Sensation and Reflection

Knowledge comes through two channels: sensation (the five senses) and reflection (the mind operating on what the senses deliver). From these two sources, all ideas are built. Complex ideas are constructed from simple ideas. Nothing is added that was not first in sensation.

Primary and secondary qualities: Locke distinguished between properties that really belong to objects (primary: shape, size, motion) and properties that are created by our minds in response to objects (secondary: color, taste, smell). This distinction would be fiercely debated. Berkeley would say all qualities are secondary. We construct the world through our sensations.

Natural Rights and Consent

Locke's empiricism had political consequences. He argued that humans have natural rights—to life, liberty, and property—that do not come from government but from nature. Government exists to protect these rights. And if government violates them, the people have the right to revolt.

All peaceful people have a fundamental right to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. Governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed. Whenever any Form of Government becomes destructive of these Ends, it is the Right of the People to alter or to abolish it. — Ideas from John Locke's Second Treatise of Government (echoed in the American Declaration of Independence)
♦ ♦ ♦

The Question He Left Us

If the mind is a blank slate, then everyone is born equal. No one is naturally superior. This is the philosophical foundation of democracy. But Locke left a puzzle: if knowledge only comes from experience, and different people have different experiences, how do we ever agree on truth? Or are we doomed to radical disagreement?

III. The Ideas — The Foundation of Modern Knowledge

Idea 1: Empiricism and Tabula Rasa

All knowledge comes from experience. The mind begins blank.

This became the foundation of modern science. You do not reason your way to truth; you observe nature and let nature teach you. This empiricist approach produced modern physics, chemistry, biology. But it also creates a philosophical problem: if all knowledge comes from sensations, what guarantees that our sensations correspond to reality?

Idea 2: Primary and Secondary Qualities

Some properties belong to objects; others are created by our minds.

The distinction between what is really "out there" (shape, size, solidity) and what is "in us" (color, taste, sound) opened a door that philosophy has been walking through ever since. If color is not really in the object, what is? If sensation constructs experience, how does sensation connect to reality?

Idea 3: Natural Rights and Consent

Humans have natural rights that precede government. Authority comes only from consent.

This was revolutionary. It meant no one—not king, not church, not tradition—has absolute authority. Government exists to serve those who are governed. And if government fails, the people have the right to change it. This philosophy became the foundation of American democracy.

VI. Your Turn

Is the mind really blank?

Locke said we are born knowing nothing. But modern psychology suggests infants have some innate abilities. Is Locke right, or did he underestimate human nature?

Does experience guarantee truth?

If all knowledge comes from sensation, how do we know sensations are reliable? Can two people with different experiences both be right?

Natural rights

Locke said humans have natural rights to life, liberty, and property. But on what basis? If the mind is blank, where do rights come from?

Augustine vs. Locke

Augustine said the soul is restless until it finds God. Locke said the mind is blank until experience fills it. Which vision of humanity is true?

Primary and secondary qualities

Is color really out there in the world, or in our minds? What is the difference between appearance and reality?

The foundation of democracy

If all minds begin equal and blank, then all are equal in potential. Does this justify democracy? Or is something more needed?

VII. Final Retrieval

1. Tabula Rasa

What does this mean, and what is Locke's point?

A blank slate. The mind begins empty, void of all ideas. Knowledge does not come from reason alone, but from experience. Sensation and reflection are the only sources of ideas.

2. How does knowledge arise?

Through two channels: sensation (the five senses) and reflection (the mind's operations on sensations). Simple ideas come from sensation. Complex ideas are built from simple ideas. Nothing in the intellect was not first in sensation.
