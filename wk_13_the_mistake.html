The Mistake

You sent the pricing file to ninety-one people outside the company. You noticed forty minutes ago and you have spent thirty-five of them writing an email you haven't sent. Now you're walking to her desk, and the order you say things in matters more than what you say.

The Scene 🎬

Forty minutes ago. That's the number that will matter.

You attached the wrong file. The pricing sheet — every client, every rate — went to an external distribution list of ninety-one people, three of whom are on it.

Everything in you wants to fix it first and confess afterwards, or to arrive with an explanation ready. Both are worse than the truth delivered early. Halina doesn't need your reasons in the first two minutes; she needs the facts, and she needs to know what you've already done, because she has calls to make.

There's also a reason it happened — a shared inbox nobody has fixed — and you are going to have to not use it. Not yet.

  1. What's the worst mistake you've made at work? How did you tell people — and how long did you wait?
  2. What makes someone easy to bring bad news to? Think of a manager you'd have told immediately.
YouYou did it, you know you did it, and you've wasted thirty-five minutes on an email instead of a conversation.
HalinaYour manager. Will go operational rather than angry — short questions, fast. The anger, if it comes, comes later and briefly.
First: read the conversation and watch the order — ownership, then facts, then what's already done, then help. The reason it happened comes last, and only when she asks.

  2. What decision does You explicitly leave to Halina, and what reason is given?
  3. What does Halina say at the end that tells you the delivery worked?
1. "Sorry, I was about to explain how it happened, and that's not what you need right now." Catching yourself is better than being stopped — it shows you know the difference between information and self-defence.

2. Whether to ring the affected clients — "that's your call, and I didn't want to make it worse." It's above your authority and she has relationships you don't.

3. "Forty minutes is fast. I've had people sit on things like this for two days." The speed is the thing being praised, not the mistake being forgiven.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for owning a serious error fast. Each does one job, and each works on any mistake in any job — swap the noun and they stand. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Fear makes the voice fast and high, which turns a competent report into a confession and makes your manager more worried than the facts warrant. Slow down deliberately. Short sentences, full stops, small pauses. The same words delivered steadily sound like someone managing a problem; delivered fast they sound like someone falling apart.

🚨 Leading with it

Phrase 1

The job: opening so she knows immediately this is urgent and not a chat. Which one?

A. "Sorry, is now a bad time? It's not urgent, but when you've got a sec…"
B. "I've made a mistake and you need to know about it now, not later."
C. "You know the pricing file? There might be a bit of an issue with it."
B — "I've made a mistake and you need to know about it now, not later."
A buries it and she'll deal with you in an hour. C hedges into the passive — there might be an issue — as though the file did it. B names the category, claims it, and states the urgency in one sentence, so she can drop what she's doing with cause. "Now, not later" is the part that gets her full attention immediately.

Take it anywhere: "I've made a mistake and you need to know about it now, not later."
Phrase 2

The job: closing off any question of blame before it opens. Put the pieces in order:

Nobody else touched it.It's mine.I did it.
"It's mine. I did it. Nobody else touched it."
Three short sentences, no passive voice, no "the file got sent". The third one is the valuable one: it pre-emptively clears your colleagues, which stops her spending twenty minutes working out who else to talk to. Total ownership costs you nothing you weren't going to pay anyway, and it buys you every ounce of credibility you'll need in the next ten minutes.

Take it anywhere: "It's mine. I did it." Any error, anywhere.

📋 The facts, flat

Phrase 3

The job: giving the shape of it while being honest about the gaps — because the gaps are what she'll ask about next. Complete it:

"Here's exactly what's happened, and here's what I ___ ___ yet."

"Here's exactly what's happened, and here's what I don't know yet."
Naming your own unknowns is what makes the known parts trustworthy. If you only present what you know, she has to work out where the holes are; if you flag them, she can act on the solid parts immediately. It also stops you guessing to fill a silence, which is how a bad situation gets misreported.

Take it anywhere: "Here's what I know, and here's what I don't know yet." Any incident, update or handover.
Phrase 4

The job: the scale of it, in numbers, with nothing softening them. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Ninety-one people, outside the company, forty minutes ago."
Three facts, no verb, no cushioning. Numbers are what a manager needs to size a problem, and offering them unprompted saves several rounds of questions. "Outside the company" is the phrase that tells her how bad it is — internal would be embarrassing, external is a different order of problem. Give the worst number first.

Take it anywhere: how many · who · how long ago. In that order.

🔧 What you've already done

Phrase 5

The job: showing you acted before you came — which is the difference between a report and a rescue. Put the pieces in order:

and I've stopped itI've started the recall,going any further.
"I've started the recall, and I've stopped it going any further."
Two actions, both already taken. A recall is the attempt to withdraw a sent message. The second half matters more than the first — containment beats reversal, because you usually can't undo it but you can always stop it spreading. Arriving with this done changes what you are in the conversation: not someone bringing a problem, someone reporting progress on one.

Take it anywhere: "I've done ___, and I've stopped it ___."
Phrase 6

The job: naming the thing you deliberately did not do, and why. Which version?

A. "I've rung the three clients already to get ahead of it."
B. "I haven't rung the clients — that's your call, and I didn't want to make it worse."
C. "Do you want me to ring the clients?"
B — "I haven't rung the clients — that's your call, and I didn't want to make it worse."
A is the disaster: initiative applied above your authority, and now there's a second problem. C is fine but passive — it hands her a decision without showing you'd already thought about it. B does both: reports the restraint as a deliberate choice, and gives the reason. That's your call is a genuinely useful phrase for marking the line between your judgement and theirs.

Take it anywhere: "I haven't ___ — that's your call."

🚫 Not defending yourself

Phrase 7

The job: acknowledging there's an explanation while refusing to deploy it. Complete it:

"There's a reason it happened but I'm not going to use it as an ___."

"There's a reason it happened but I'm not going to use it as an excuse."
You will almost always have a reason — a broken process, a shared inbox, a colleague's error. Saying it in the first ten minutes makes everything before it sound like preamble to a defence. This sentence banks the reason for later without spending it now, and it's far more persuasive than the reason itself would have been.

Take it anywhere: "There's a reason, but I'm not going to use it as an excuse."
Phrase 8

The job: putting the telling-off in a queue and getting to work. Complete it:

"Tell me off later. Right now, what do you ___ from me?"

"Tell me off later. Right now, what do you need from me?"
To tell someone off is to reprimand them. This does two things at once: it accepts that consequences are coming — so she doesn't have to threaten them — and it moves both of you to the useful question. It also quietly reminds her that the next hour matters more than her feelings about it, which she knows but may need help remembering.

Take it anywhere: "Right now, what do you need from me?" The most useful sentence in any crisis.

🔒 Afterwards

Phrase 9

The job: explaining why you came straight away — framed as your interest, not your virtue. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "I'd rather be the one who tells you than the one you find out about."
A stated self-interest is more credible than claimed honesty. It's also the sentence that shapes how you're seen for the rest of your time there — organisations forgive errors far more readily than concealment, and this tells her which sort of person she's managing.

Take it anywhere: "I'd rather be the one who tells you than the one you find out about."
Phrase 10

The job: promising it won't recur, in a way that means something. Which version?

A. "I'm so sorry. I promise you it will never happen again."
B. "It won't happen again, and that's not a promise, it's a change to how I do it."
C. "I'll be much more careful in future."
B — "It won't happen again, and that's not a promise, it's a change to how I do it."
A and C both offer more care, and care is not a system — everybody intends to be careful and everybody is occasionally tired. B distinguishes between intention and mechanism, and commits you to producing the mechanism. Say it only if you'll actually bring one; here, on Monday.

Take it anywhere: "That's not a promise, it's a change to how I do it."

🔑 Make them yours

These work for any error, in any job — and most of them work outside work entirely. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"It's mine. I did it." — think of something you've got wrong. Say the two-sentence version, with no reason attached.

"Here's what I know, and here's what I don't know yet." — what would your unknowns be?

"That's not a promise, it's a change to how I do it." — what would the actual change be?

⚡ The twist

Halina goes to make her calls. Then it emerges that the shared-inbox setup which let this happen is one you flagged as dangerous eight months ago, in writing, and were told not to worry about — by Halina.

You now have the perfect defence in your pocket, and using it today would be the worst thing you could do in this room. What do you say, and when do you raise it?

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you open with the urgency rather than an apology?
  • Can you own something in three short sentences with no passive voice?
  • Can you give numbers before adjectives?
  • Can you hold back a genuine excuse until it's useful?
  • Can you promise a mechanism instead of more care?

The Echo 🕰️

At 1.23 in the morning on 26 April 1986, the fourth reactor at Chernobyl exploded. Within hours the people in charge knew roughly what had happened. They did not say so.

The town of Pripyat, two miles away, was not evacuated for thirty-six hours. Children went to school. People stood on their balconies to watch the strange light over the plant. Moscow was given a version smaller than the truth, and acted on it, because that was the version it had.

Almost nothing in your working life is Chernobyl. But the shape is the same, and it's oddly comforting: the mistake was never the unsurvivable part. The gap between knowing and saying was.
