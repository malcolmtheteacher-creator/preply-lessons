English at Work · Role Play · B2/C1 · 50 min

The Mistake

You've just sent the pricing file to ninety-one people outside the company. Three of them are clients. In about ninety seconds you have to say so, out loud, to your boss.

The Scene 🎬

It was an ordinary Tuesday until about twenty past two. You attached the Q3 pricing workbook — every discount, every margin, every special deal — and you picked the distribution list from the drop-down without really looking at it, because you have done that same thing four hundred times before and nothing has ever happened.

Ninety-one people outside the company can now see what everybody pays. Three of them are clients who are on the list themselves, which means they can see what the other two are paying. You noticed forty minutes ago. You have spent thirty-five of those minutes writing your manager an email that you have opened, rewritten and not sent, and about five of them being quietly sick in the toilets on the second floor.

Now you're walking to Halina's desk, and your brain is offering you three ways out. Arrive with a defence. Arrive with the whole long story of how it happened, so she understands it wasn't really your fault. Or wait — just an hour — until you've fixed it, so you can tell her about a problem that's already solved. All three are worse than the truth, delivered early, with the recall already running.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of a time you had to tell someone at work that something had gone wrong. How long did you wait before you told them — honestly — and what were you doing in that gap?
  2. When somebody brings you bad news, what do you actually want from them in the first thirty seconds: an apology, an explanation, or the facts? And is that what you give people when the roles are reversed?

Who's who

You
The One Who Did It 📎

Frightened, slightly shaky, and carrying a mistake that is entirely yours. You have one advantage and it's a real one: you noticed it yourself, and you're walking towards her rather than away.

The other voice
Halina — Your Manager 🖥️

Twenty years of messes behind her. In the first ten minutes she won't shout, because shouting doesn't recall an email; she'll ask short, closed questions and want short, closed answers. The feelings come later, if at all.

First: read the conversation in the next tab — but read it for the order, not the content. Watch what arrives first, second and third: the mistake, then the ownership, then the facts, then what's already been done, and only much later anything resembling a reason. That order is the entire skill. The same words in a different order — reason first, mistake somewhere near the end — would be a completely different conversation, and a much worse one.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Halina asks a question the speaker genuinely cannot answer. What was it — and what did the speaker do instead of guessing?

2. Near the start, the speaker begins going into detail about tabs and drop-downs, then stops themselves. What were they starting to do, and why does Halina not need it at that moment?

3. Halina asks what the speaker was doing for forty minutes. What was the real answer — and what does that gap tell you about how frightened people behave?

1. She asks whether clients were on the list. The speaker can see three, but some of the addresses are shared inboxes, so they can't say who is really behind them — and rather than guessing at a number to sound on top of it, they say plainly that this is the part they don't know yet, and give a time by which they will know. A guess here would have been worse than the mistake itself, because Halina would have made her next decision on it.
2. They were starting to explain how it happened, in detail, before Halina had even been told what had happened. That's the fear talking — explaining feels like doing something, and it postpones the sentence you don't want to say. She doesn't need any of it yet, because none of it changes what she has to do in the next ten minutes.
3. Thirty-five minutes writing an email they never sent, and five minutes being sick. The instinct when we've done something bad is to work on the telling rather than to tell — and every minute spent on the perfect wording is a minute the file is out there and the manager can't act. The fear feels like care, but it's just delay.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the worst ninety seconds of your working month. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

How you say these matters as much as which ones you pick. Panic pushes the voice up and speeds it up, and a fast, high voice turns a competent report into a confession — which invites the other person to comfort you or to tell you off, when what you actually want is for them to start working. Take one breath before the first sentence, then say it slower than feels natural. The same words, delivered at half the speed, stop sounding like someone falling apart and start sounding like someone handing over information.

🚩 Leading with it

Phrase 1

You're at her desk. She looks up. This is your first sentence, and it decides the shape of everything after it. Which one?

A. "Have you got five minutes? There's been an issue with one of the files — I think it got sent to the wrong list somehow."
B. "I've made a mistake and you need to know about it now, not later."
C. "I'm dealing with something — I should have it sorted within the hour and then I'll walk you through it."
B — "I've made a mistake and you need to know about it now, not later."
It names the thing in the first four words and it hands her the urgency, so she can decide immediately whether to keep listening or pick up the phone. Look at what the other two are really doing. A says "it got sent", which is the passive escaping route — the file sent itself, apparently, and nobody is holding anything. C is the one that tempts almost everybody, because waiting until it's fixed feels responsible, but it isn't: it's an hour in which she could have been acting and wasn't, and if she hears about it from anyone else in that hour, you have gone from someone who made a mistake to someone who hid one.
Phrase 2

She asks who else has been working in that file. There is a colleague whose name you could very reasonably mention. Complete the chunk — one word: "It's ___. I did it. Nobody else touched it."

"It's mine. I did it. Nobody else touched it."
Three short sentences that close the door on the search for someone to share it with, which is the search your manager would otherwise have to run herself. Saying it before she asks twice is worth a great deal, because the alternative — a slow drip of names — makes everybody look worse, including you.

📋 The facts, flat

Phrase 3

She's told you to sit down. You have roughly one sentence to set up what's coming, and you're aware that parts of it are still unclear to you. Which opener?

A. "Right — so you know how the two lists have almost the same name? That's basically the whole story here."
B. "Here's exactly what's happened, and here's what I don't know yet."
C. "It's probably nothing serious, but I wanted to flag it in case."
B — "Here's exactly what's happened, and here's what I don't know yet."
The second half is what makes it work. Most people report a mess as though they understand all of it, because admitting a hole feels like admitting incompetence twice over — and then the manager builds her next hour on a fact you invented under pressure. Announcing the hole up front tells her which parts she can act on and which parts to wait for. A is the defence arriving before the news; C is minimising, and if it turns out to be serious after all, she'll remember that you told her it wasn't.
Phrase 4

She cuts across you: "How many, and when—". She wants the scale in one breath. Complete the chunk — one word: "Ninety-one people, ___ the company, forty minutes ago."

"Ninety-one people, outside the company, forty minutes ago."
Three facts, no verb, no adjectives, no "unfortunately" — a number, a boundary, a clock. "Outside" is the word that changes everything, because ninety-one people inside the company is an embarrassment whereas ninety-one people outside it is a different category of problem, and she needs to know which one she's in before she does anything else. When someone asks a closed question in a crisis, answer it closed. Everything you add is something she has to wade through.

🔧 What you've already done

Phrase 5

She asks what you've done about it. You want to tell her, in one line, that you both tried to undo it and shut the door behind it. Put it in order:

going any furtherand I've stopped itthe recall,I've started
"I've started the recall, and I've stopped it going any further."
The recall is the function that tries to pull an email back out of people's inboxes after you've sent it; in British offices you "recall" a message, and everybody knows it mostly doesn't work, which is exactly why the second half of the sentence matters more. Starting the recall is the obvious move; stopping the spread — forwarding rules, shared links, the copy sitting on a drive — is the one that actually limits the damage. Two verbs in the perfect tense, both done, neither promised.
Phrase 6

Three clients have the file. You could ring them yourself right now and start apologising. Should you — and how do you say what you've decided?

A. "I've rung two of the three already and left messages — I thought it was better coming from me."
B. "I haven't rung the clients — that's your call, and I didn't want to make it worse."
C. "I'll leave the clients to you, obviously. Not really my area."
B — "I haven't rung the clients — that's your call, and I didn't want to make it worse."
"That's your call" means that's your decision to make, not mine — a call here is a judgement, nothing to do with telephones, even though the sentence happens to be about ringing people. It's a way of handing a decision upwards without sounding either helpless or grand. A looks like initiative and is actually the opposite: a client conversation that hasn't been agreed with anyone can commit the company to things you have no authority to commit it to, and it takes the choice out of her hands. C hands the decision over too, but with a shrug attached, which is the difference between deferring and dumping.

🛡️ Refusing to defend yourself

Phrase 7

She asks how the external list came up at all. You have a genuinely good answer — the two lists sit next to each other and are almost identically named, and this has nearly gone wrong before. But it's ten minutes into a live problem, and a reason offered now will land as an excuse. Say it in your own words: acknowledge that there is an explanation, and refuse to hide behind it.

"There's a reason it happened but I'm not going to use it as an excuse."
The sentence does two things at once, which is why it's worth having ready. It protects the reason — you're not pretending it doesn't exist, and you've flagged that there's something here worth fixing later — while refusing to spend it now, when it would only sound like you wriggling. Note what it is not: it's not "it wasn't my fault, the lists are badly named", and it's not the false modesty of "no, no, it was entirely me, I'm useless", which is just fishing for reassurance. Add "ask me tomorrow and I'll tell you the whole thing" and you've made an appointment for the reason instead of burying it.
Phrase 8

Her temper surfaces — half a sentence of it, then she catches herself. You could apologise again, or you could put the room back to work. Complete the chunk: "Tell me off later. Right now, ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ ___?"

"Tell me off later. Right now, what do you need from me?"
To tell someone off is to scold them, to give them a telling-off — it's what a parent does to a child who's broken something, and using it about yourself, lightly, is a way of accepting that the scolding is coming and is legitimate. What the sentence really does is separate the two conversations that are trying to happen at the same time: the emotional one, which can wait, and the operational one, which cannot. And the second half hands her back control, which is the thing she's just lost and the reason she nearly shouted.

🕰️ Afterwards

Phrase 9

The immediate work is allocated and you're about to leave her desk. You want to say one thing about why you came straight over — without asking to be thanked for it. Put it in order:

than the onewho tells youI'd rather be the oneyou find out about
"I'd rather be the one who tells you than the one you find out about."
It's quiet, and it should stay quiet — said once, on the way out, not offered as a defence. The point underneath it is real: your manager is going to hear about this either way, and the only variable you controlled was whether she heard it from you or from someone forwarding her the email. That choice is the whole of your reputation in this, and it's worth naming exactly once. Say it twice and it becomes a bid for credit.
Phrase 10

Last thing before you go. "It won't happen again" on its own is what everybody says and nobody means, and she's heard it a hundred times. Say it in your own words so that it isn't a feeling but a mechanism — something you'll actually do differently, so that the mistake becomes hard rather than merely regretted.

"It won't happen again, and that's not a promise, it's a change to how I do it."
A promise relies on you being careful, and you were careful this morning, which is rather the problem. A change is something in the process: the list gets renamed, the file gets a check before it goes, someone else's eyes go over anything with pricing in it. Follow the sentence with the actual change if you have it — "the external list gets renamed today and I'm not sending pricing without a second pair of eyes" — because the sentence is only worth anything if the next line fills it in. Without that, it's just a better-dressed version of what everybody says.

🔑 Make them yours — before you need them

You will not be composing sentences on the day; you'll be reaching for whatever's already there. Pin three of these to something real, and say each answer out loud until it comes without effort.

Something at work that could genuinely go wrong through you — not a disaster, just a plausible one. Write your first sentence to the person you'd have to tell.

Think of the last time something went wrong and you had a good reason for it. Say the reason — then say how you'd have declined to use it.

"Tell me off later. Right now, what do you need from me?" — when would you have to be brave to say this, and to whom?

The Twist

🎯 The defence in your pocket

Run it again — but this time, halfway through, it surfaces. The two distribution lists sit next to each other with almost the same name, and eight months ago you wrote that down. You raised it, in an email you can find in about nine seconds, and you were told it was fine and not to worry about it. Halina is the person who told you not to worry about it, and there's a decent chance she's just remembered that.

So you are holding a perfect defence. It's true, it's documented, and it would move the weight of this off you and onto her in a single sentence — and saying it, here, now, in this conversation, would be the worst thing you could do in this room. Play it out. Feel how badly you want to reach for it. Don't. Get through the ten minutes without it, and find the sentence that puts the fix on the table without putting the blame on her — because the list still needs renaming, and somebody has to say so.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said out loud. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • My first sentence named the mistake — I didn't warm her up, soften it, or ask if she had a minute.
  • I said out loud what I didn't know, instead of guessing to sound in control.
  • I took all of it, even when I was offered a name to share it with.
  • I had a reason and I didn't spend it — not even when she asked me directly.
  • I ended by handing her control, and I left with a change rather than a promise.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that'll go wrong on the day. Run the twist again and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Organisations get the mistakes they punish. Not fewer of them — the same number, roughly, because people are people and lists sit next to each other — but quieter ones, found later, by someone else, when they cost ten times as much to put right. The error is almost never the expensive part. The forty minutes are the expensive part, and in most places those forty minutes are forty days.

The instinct to hide is not cowardice, really; it's an entirely reasonable reading of what happened to the last person who owned up. So people learn to work on the telling rather than to tell — the email that gets rewritten, the fix attempted alone at eleven at night, the hope that nobody notices — and every hour of that is an hour the organisation is flying with an instrument it doesn't know is broken.

Which is why the person who tells you early is worth more than the person who never breaks anything, and why anyone who has run a team for long enough knows it. Someone who never breaks anything is often just someone who never touches anything, or someone whose breakages you haven't found yet. The one who walks over grey in the face and says it in the first four words is the one who is telling you the truth about your own company — and there is no other way of getting it.

Two bigger questions

  1. Think about a place you've worked. What actually happened to people who admitted a serious mistake — and how did everyone else learn what it was safe to say? Nobody is ever told this directly, so how did you learn it?
  2. If a manager wants to be told early, they have to react well to being told badly, at the worst possible moment, by someone who is frightened. Is that fair on the manager — and if not, who should carry the cost of a culture where people bring you the bad news?
