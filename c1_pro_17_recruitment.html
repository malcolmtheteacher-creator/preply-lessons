Running Great Interviews

C1 Level · HR Skills

Hiring Mistakes: Why Bad Hires Happen

Bad hiring decisions are expensive: they cost money, time, team morale, and they're hard to undo. Yet they happen constantly. Why?

Reflect:

  • Think of a bad hire you've made or witnessed. What went wrong?
  • How long did it take to realise it was a mistake?
  • What red flags were missed in the interview?
  • What would have caught it?

Common Hiring Mistakes

Gut feelings over evidence: "I just had a good feeling about them." Gut is faster than rigorous assessment, but it's often wrong. Interviews are poor predictors of job performance when they're unstructured.

Unconscious bias: We tend to hire people like us. Same background, same university, same communication style. This limits diversity and often means we miss the best candidates.

Talking more than listening: Some interviewers spend the interview selling the role instead of genuinely assessing. You learn nothing about the candidate.

Not probing deep enough: Someone says they "led a team." You nod and move on. But did they actually lead, or were they a coordinator? What was the outcome?

Confirmation bias: Once you like someone, you filter out negative signals. Once you dislike them, you filter out positive ones.

Selling skills over actual capabilities: Some people interview brilliantly but can't deliver. They're articulate about what they "can do" but vague about what they've actually done.

Ignoring culture fit—or overweighting it: "Culture fit" is often code for "people like me." But actual fit means values alignment and ability to thrive in the environment.

What Good Interviews Measure

Interviews can reliably assess: communication skills, thinking process, values alignment, and how they respond under pressure. They're terrible at predicting technical ability (that's what skills tests are for) or job performance six months in.

The best interviews go for depth on a few dimensions rather than breadth on many. You want evidence, not opinions. You want to understand how they think, what they value, and whether they can succeed in your environment.

Consider: What do you actually assess for in interviews? Are you measuring what matters?

Master the language and structure of effective interviews.

The Anatomy of a Good Interview

Opening (5 minutes): Build rapport. Welcome them. Brief agenda. Make them feel comfortable enough to be themselves.

Your story (5 minutes): Tell them about the role, the team, the challenges. Give them context so they can assess fit too.

Their story (20-25 minutes): "Walk me through your background" or "Tell me about a recent project you're proud of." Listen more than you talk. Ask follow-up questions. Probe for evidence.

Competency questions (10-15 minutes): "Tell me about a time you..." Examples: disagreed with a manager, handled failure, led change, worked in a difficult team dynamic. Listen for what they did, not what they say they would do.

Their questions (5-10 minutes): What they ask tells you what they value. Strong candidates ask thoughtful questions about the role, the team, the culture.

Closing (2 minutes): Next steps. Timeline. Invite them to ask anything else.

Powerful Interview Phrases

Opening: "Thank you for coming. I want to understand your background, what you're looking for, and whether there's a good fit both ways. Please be yourself."

Probing: "Tell me more about that. What specifically did you do?" or "How did that turn out?" or "What would you do differently?"

Assessing thinking: "Walk me through your thinking on that decision" or "What alternatives did you consider?"

Understanding values: "What's important to you in a role?" or "What kind of team environment brings out your best work?"

Addressing concerns: If they have a gap or weakness: "Tell me about that. How did you handle it?" (Non-judgmental, curious tone.)

Closing: "Any questions for me? Anything we haven't covered you want to discuss?"

Scenario: Run a Structured Interview

You're interviewing a candidate for a Marketing Manager role. They look good on paper: relevant experience, strong resume. Now you need to dig deeper and assess how they think, what they value, and whether they can succeed in your team.

10:00

Part 1: Opening & Their Story

Write your opening (welcome, agenda, brief about the role). Then imagine they respond to "Tell me about your marketing background." Write 2-3 follow-up questions you'd ask to understand their experience more deeply.

Opening example: "Thanks for taking the time to meet with us. I want to understand your background in marketing, what kind of role you're looking for, and whether there's a good fit here. We'll also talk about the role and answer your questions. Sound good?"

Follow-up questions: "What were the key results you delivered?" (Want numbers, not vague claims.) "What was your biggest challenge in that role, and how did you handle it?" (Want to see problem-solving.) "What did you enjoy most? Least?" (Want to understand values and fit.)

Part 2: Competency Assessment

Ask two behavioural questions to assess key competencies for the role. Then write what follow-up questions you'd ask based on typical answers.

Question 1: "Tell me about a time you had to influence others without direct authority." (Leadership/influence without hierarchy.) Follow-up: "What was the outcome? How did they respond?"

Question 2: "Tell me about a campaign or project that didn't go as planned. How did you handle it?" (Resilience, learning.) Follow-up: "What would you do differently?" (Growth mindset.)

Why these work: They require specific examples (no hypotheticals), they reveal thinking and values, and they're hard to fake. You'll learn how they actually respond under pressure.

Part 3: Values & Fit

You want to understand how they make decisions, what they value, and whether they align with your team. Write 1-2 questions to assess this.

Values assessment question: "What's important to you in a manager and team environment?" or "When have you felt most engaged and productive at work? What was true about that situation?"

Why: Listen not just to content, but to what they prioritise. Do they talk about autonomy, team, impact, learning, stability? Does it align with your team culture?

Probing fit: If they want deep autonomy and you have a collaborative, consensus-driven culture, that's useful information. Not a dealbreaker necessarily, but something to discuss openly.

Beyond the Interview

Good interviews are necessary but not sufficient. Hiring is a system: assessment, interview, reference checks, offer, onboarding.

Reflection questions:

  • How structured are your current interviews? Are all candidates asked the same questions?
  • How much do you learn about someone's actual capability vs. their interview performance?
  • What biases do you notice in your hiring? Age? Gender? Background?
  • How diverse is your team compared to your candidate pool?
  • How many bad hires have you made? What would have caught them?

The Role of AI in Recruitment

AI is entering recruitment through resume screening, assessment tools, and interview analysis. This can reduce bias (algorithms don't have gut feelings) but can also amplify bias (if the training data is biased).

Use tools thoughtfully. They can help scale screening and reduce unconscious bias. But they can also create false precision and miss good candidates who don't fit the pattern.

The human judgment—especially in assessing fit and potential—remains critical.

Diversity in Hiring

Research is clear: diverse teams make better decisions and produce better results. Yet hiring remains biased toward people like the existing team.

To improve:

  • Structured interviews: Reduce bias by using same questions for all candidates.
  • Diverse hiring panels: Different people see different things. A panel catches biases an individual might miss.
  • Candidate sourcing: Actively recruit from diverse backgrounds. Don't rely on referrals alone.
  • De-bias your criteria: Do you actually need a degree? 10 years experience? Or are you screening for things that correlate with diversity rather than competence?
  • Culture add over culture fit: Look for people who share your values but bring different perspectives.

From Hiring to Onboarding

The best hiring process is only as good as the onboarding that follows. A great hire can fail if they're not set up for success in the first 90 days.

Onboarding is where you build relationships, communicate culture, clarify expectations, and help them become productive. It's where initial impression becomes actual integration.

Invest here. Your hiring investment is only realised if onboarding works.

Action step: Review your last three hires. Were they successful? What made the difference? What would you change about how you hired or onboarded them?
