Doing Things Well (or Badly)

Words that describe HOW you do something, and where to put them in a sentence.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "Please close the door quietly, the baby's asleep."
  • "He answered every question correctly."
  • "Drive carefully, the roads are icy tonight."

Look at quietly, correctly and carefully. What do you add to the adjective (quiet, correct, careful) to make each one? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Just add -ly: quiet → quietly, correct → correctly, careful → carefully. Small spelling note: if the adjective ends in -y, change it to -i before adding -ly: easy → easily, happy → happily.
adjective + -ly → adverb (careful → carefully, easy → easily)

Where does it go?

"She finished quickly her lunch." (sounds wrong)
→
"She finished her lunch quickly."

The adverb usually goes after the verb, or after the verb and its object, never in between them.

Well, Fast, Hard: Choosing the Right Word

Some adverbs don't end in -ly at all, and some words look the same as their adjective.

Spot the pattern

  • "She's a good driver, she drives well."
  • "He's a fast runner, he runs fast."
  • "They're hard workers, they work hard."

Compare the two halves of each sentence. Which word describes a person (a noun), and which describes an action (a verb)? Do fast and hard change at all? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
good / fast / hard describe the noun (driver, runner, workers): these are adjectives. well / fast / hard describe the verb (drives, runs, work): these are adverbs. Notice: fast and hard stay exactly the same in both jobs. Good is the odd one out: its adverb is well, never "goodly" (that word doesn't exist).
adjective (describes a noun): good, fast, hard, careful...
adverb (describes a verb): well, fast, hard, carefully...
good → well (irregular)  |  "goodly" ✗
"My mum is a good cook."
→
"My mum cooks well."

How Often? Stepping Up from Always and Never

You already know always, usually, sometimes and never. Now let's add exact frequency, and one tricky word.

Spot the pattern

  • "I go to the gym once a week."
  • "She calls her parents every day."
  • "He hardly ever drinks coffee."

Compare "once a week" and "hardly ever": where do they go in the sentence, the beginning, the middle, or the end? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Phrases with a number, once a week, twice a month, three times a year, usually go at the end of the sentence. Hardly ever (= almost never) behaves like always/never: it goes in the middle, before the main verb, or after am/is/are.
subject + verb ... + once a week / twice a month / every day  (end)
subject + hardly ever + verb  (middle, like never)
subject + am/is/are + hardly ever  (after be)
Careful: hardly ever = almost never (how OFTEN). Hardly alone = barely, very little (how MUCH) — a different idea. "I hardly slept" doesn't mean "I rarely sleep"; it means last night you slept very little.

Your turn

1. My brother hates the dentist, but he still goes for a check-up in spring and again in autumn.

"He goes to the dentist ."

2. My uncle almost never eats breakfast, maybe once a month at most.

"He eats breakfast."

3. Last night I only slept for about two hours.

"I slept last night."
