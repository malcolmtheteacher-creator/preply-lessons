The Epicurean Path — Lesson 13 of 30

The Virtues as Means

Why virtue matters in Epicureanism — but not in the way you'd expect

Before We Begin...

🧠 What Do You Already Know?

In a hedonistic philosophy where pleasure is the highest good, what role should virtue play? Is virtue important? Is it required? Why or why not?

Today: We're exploring one of Epicureanism's most surprising (and misunderstood) claims: virtue is good — but only as a means to pleasure. Virtue isn't the highest good; pleasure is. But virtue is the practical tool that gets you there.

The Tension

By now you might sense a tension in Epicureanism:

  • Pleasure is the only intrinsic good
  • But pursuing pleasure naively (indulgence) creates pain
  • So we need wisdom and self-control to choose rightly
  • These are virtues — but they seem to contradict the hedonistic goal

This lesson resolves the tension by clarifying the Epicurean understanding of virtue.

Virtue and Hedonism

The Challenge

Virtue is often understood as something good in itself. A just person is good because they're just, regardless of the outcomes. A courageous person is good because they're courageous, even if courage brings suffering.

But Epicureanism says: nothing is good in itself except pleasure. So what does Epicurus do with virtue?

🔍
Key Distinction:
Intrinsic Good: Good for its own sake (Epicureanism says: pleasure)
Instrumental Good: Good as a means to something else (Epicureanism says: virtue, friendship, knowledge)

Epicurus's Answer

Virtue is instrumentally good. It's valuable because it leads to pleasure (ataraxia and aponia), not because it's good in itself.

This is radical. Most ethical systems say virtue is intrinsically good — worth pursuing for its own sake, even at the cost of happiness. Epicureanism flips this: pursue virtue because it leads to happiness, not the other way around.

Who do you think is the competitor with the gods, who is blessed? It is the person who refrains from baseness and injustice, yet speaks boldly on behalf of wisdom and the virtues.

— Epicurus (Vatican Sayings)

Your turn: Epicurus says the virtuous person is "like a god." Does this mean virtue is intrinsically good? Or does virtue lead to the tranquility and freedom that makes someone like a god?

The latter: virtue is a means to god-like status.

Recall from Lesson 9: Epicurus believed the gods live in perfect tranquility (ataraxia), untroubled by human concerns. The virtuous person achieves something similar — not through divine grace, but through wisdom and disciplined choice.

The reasoning: A just person has a clean conscience. They don't fear punishment or shame. An honest person doesn't fear exposure. A temperate person doesn't suffer from overindulgence. A courageous person doesn't live in cowardly fear.

So virtue leads to ataraxia — tranquility through freedom from fear and shame. That's why the virtuous person is god-like. Not because virtue is intrinsically divine, but because virtue produces the peace that characterizes the divine.

Epicurean Virtue

Epicurus accepts the traditional cardinal virtues (wisdom, courage, temperance, justice) but reinterprets them through an Epicurean lens.

🧠 Prudence (Phronesis / Practical Wisdom)

Definition: The ability to calculate which desires will lead to pleasure and which to pain
How it works: Prudence is the master virtue. It's the practical reasoning that applies the desire classification. Should I pursue this? Will it lead to ataraxia or complications? Will the pleasure be worth the risk?
Instrumental value: Obviously instrumental — it's a tool for achieving pleasure through wise choice

⚖️ Justice

Epicurean view: Justice isn't a virtue of the soul or a divine law. It's a social contract.
The logic: We establish laws and justice because it's mutually beneficial to not harm each other. "Don't rob and we won't rob you" is a deal that makes everyone safer and happier.
Where justice breaks: The contract only holds when it benefits you. If you could rob without being caught or punished, and the gain exceeded the risk, Epicureanism doesn't condemn it on principle — only if it would create complications (guilt, fear of discovery, social retaliation) that outweigh the gain.
Instrumental value: Justice is valuable because it creates stable, peaceful communities where you can live in ataraxia without fear

🤐 Temperance (Moderation)

Definition: Self-restraint in the pursuit of pleasures
Epicurean version: Not the suppression of desire, but the intelligent choice about which desires to satisfy
Example: Drinking wine isn't intemperate; constant heavy drinking is, because it creates pain. A temperate person drinks moderately if at all, avoiding both the dissatisfaction of abstinence and the suffering of excess.
Instrumental value: Temperance leads to aponia (freedom from pain) and ataraxia (freedom from the anxiety and shame of overindulgence)

💪 Courage

Epicurean interpretation: Not the desire for military glory or heroic sacrifice, but the ability to face life's difficulties without fear
What it means: The courage to think independently about death and the gods (against popular opinion). The courage to choose philosophy and truth over conventional status.
Example: Epicurus was politically unpopular for his skeptical views about religion and his unconventional community (women and slaves as equals). This required courage — not in war, but in thought and lifestyle.
Instrumental value: Courage enables ataraxia by freeing you from fear of social judgment and the courage to live authentically

The Pattern

Notice: all virtues lead to ataraxia and aponia. They're not pursued for their own sake but for the peace they enable. This is radical — it inverts the traditional hierarchy where virtue is the goal and happiness is the reward. For Epicureanism, pleasure is the goal and virtue is the means.

Friendship: The Greatest External Good

A Special Status

Friendship holds a unique position in Epicureanism. It's arguably the most important good besides pleasure itself.

Ancient sources report that Epicurus valued friendship above almost everything else. The community of the Garden was fundamentally built on friendship. And his last words, according to his letter, were about missing his friend Metrodorus.

Your turn: If pleasure is the only intrinsic good, how can friendship be so important? Is friendship a natural/necessary desire? Or is it something else?

Friendship is a natural and necessary desire.

In Lesson 11, we identified friendship as a natural and necessary desire — essential for wellbeing, not just nice to have. This makes it fundamentally important to the Epicurean life.

Why friendship is necessary:

  • Practical security: Friends help in emergencies and crisis. Food, shelter, protection — friends provide these
  • Emotional support: Friends console in grief and celebrate in joy, reducing suffering and increasing pleasure
  • Intellectual stimulation: Philosophical conversation (Epicurus's favorite activity) requires friends
  • Sense of belonging: Humans need community and acceptance; friends provide this

Of all the things that wisdom provides for living a blessed life, none is more important than friendship.

— Epicurus

The Paradox of Epicurean Friendship

Here's something subtle: Epicurus says we choose friends because friendship is beneficial to us. We pursue friendships for pleasure. But genuine friendship requires caring about the friend's wellbeing for their own sake, not just because they benefit you.

Is this a contradiction? Not quite. Epicureanism says:

  • You start pursuing friendship because it benefits you (prudent choice)
  • But genuine friendship develops when you care about your friend's wellbeing as an extension of your own
  • The friend becomes part of your life; their happiness contributes to yours
  • This creates a stable, deep pleasure far greater than isolated self-interest could achieve

Why Friendship is "External"

Internal goods: Virtue, wisdom, self-control — these are within your control
External goods: Friendship, health, modest wealth — these depend partly on luck and circumstances
Epicureanism's view: The greatest good is ataraxia/aponia (internal). But the greatest external good is friendship. You need friendship to achieve maximum pleasure, but you can't fully control it (you need friends to cooperate).

The Limitation of Solitary Pleasure

Interestingly, Epicureanism doesn't promote isolationism or solipsistic pleasure-seeking. The highest pleasures require friendship. Philosophical conversation, shared meals, mutual care — these are the peak experiences of the Epicurean life. And they're impossible alone.

A wise hedonist realizes: I can't maximize my pleasure by treating others as mere instruments. I need genuine relationships. I need to care about others. And when I do, I achieve far deeper pleasure than selfish exploitation ever could.

Virtue in Epicureanism:

  • Instrumentally good, not intrinsically — valuable as a means to pleasure (ataraxia/aponia), not for its own sake
  • This is radical: inverts traditional ethics where virtue is the goal
  • But virtuous people are "like gods" because virtue leads to tranquility

The Four Virtues:

  • Prudence/Wisdom: The ability to calculate which desires lead to pleasure — the master virtue
  • Justice: A social contract; we agree not to harm each other for mutual benefit and peace
  • Temperance: Self-restraint in pursuing pleasures; avoiding pain through moderation
  • Courage: Intellectual honesty and living according to your values despite social pressure

Justice and the Social Contract:

  • Justice isn't a cosmic virtue or divine law — it's a practical agreement
  • "Don't harm me and I won't harm you" makes everyone safer and happier
  • The contract holds as long as it benefits you; Epicureanism doesn't condemn breaking it on principle (only on practical grounds)
  • But stable, just communities are essential for ataraxia

Friendship as Greatest External Good:

  • Internal goods (virtue, wisdom) are within your control
  • External goods (friendship, health, wealth) depend partly on luck
  • Friendship is the greatest external good because it's necessary for maximum pleasure
  • Genuine friendship requires caring about the friend's wellbeing for their own sake, not just instrumentally
  • This creates deeper, more stable pleasure than selfish strategies
