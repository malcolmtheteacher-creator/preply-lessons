Academic writing normally requires that you support your ideas and opinions with facts, statistics, quotations, and similar information. You get these details from outside sources such as books, magazines, newspapers, websites, and personal interviews.

Facts versus opinions

Opinions are subjective statements based on a person's beliefs or attitudes:

  • Men are better drivers than women.
  • Smoking is a bad habit.
  • English is an easy language to learn.

Opinions are not acceptable as support. It is fine to express opinions in academic writing; most professors want your own ideas. But if you express an opinion, you must support it with facts. Facts are objective statements of truths:

  • At sea level, water boils at 100 degrees Celsius.
  • Women live longer than men.
  • Cigarettes are addictive.

Sometimes even facts need proof. All three above are facts, but the last two need proof: your readers may not believe them. Use specific supporting details (examples, statistics, quotations) to prove they are true.

OpinionPhotographs of ultrathin fashion models send the wrong message to girls and young women.
Fact, but needs proofFashion models are unnaturally thin.
Specific supporting detailThe average model weighs 25 percent less than the average woman of the same height.

Using outside sources

Where can you find specific supporting details? For some assignments you can use your own experience, or gather quotations and statistics by running an experiment, taking a survey or interviewing people. For others you must research in a library or on the Internet.

There are three ways to put outside information into your writing:

1. Quote itCopy the exact words, in quotation marks. (This chapter.)
2. Summarize itReport the main points briefly, in your own words. (Chapter 8.)
3. Paraphrase itRestate it in your own words. (Chapter 8.)

Plagiarism

Plagiarism is using someone else's words or ideas as if they were your own. It is a serious offense: students who plagiarize may fail a class or even be expelled. If you use information from an outside source without acknowledging it, you are guilty of plagiarism.

One way to avoid it is to always put quotation marks around words you copy exactly. (You don't need them if you change the words.) You are also guilty of plagiarism if you fail to cite the source, that is, tell where you got the information.

Is this plagiarism? Choose yes or no.
A student copies two sentences word for word from a website, with no quotation marks and no source.
A student copies a sentence word for word, puts it in quotation marks and adds a citation.
A student uses a statistic from an article in their own words but never says where it came from.

Citing sources: two steps

  1. Insert a short reference in parentheses at the end of each piece of borrowed information: the in-text citation.
  2. Prepare a list describing all your sources fully, titled "Works Cited", as the last page of your paper.
In-text citation
According to the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, "Communities don't have the resources to allow police to patrol intersections as often as would be needed to ticket all motorists who run red lights" ("Q&A").

Entry in the works-cited list
"Q&A: Red Light Running." Insurance Institute for Highway Safety June 2003. 26 Feb. 2004 <http://www.hwysafety.org/safety-facts/quanda/rlc.htm>.

Notice the citation sits at the end of the borrowed information, before the final period. The abbreviation "Q&A" is the first element of the article's title, because there was no author. Readers who want more can turn to the works-cited list.

In the works-cited entry, what does "26 Feb. 2004" tell us?

Quotations

Quotations from reliable, knowledgeable sources are good support. There are two kinds. In a direct quotation you copy another person's exact words (spoken or written) inside quotation marks. In an indirect quotation you report the words without quotation marks, using a reporting expression such as according to XYZ or XYZ believes that.

Model: direct quotations

Drugs and the Olympic Games 1

1It is no secret that performance-enhancing drugs have been used by Olympic athletes for decades. 2According to an article in Forbes magazine, "From the brute steroids the East Germans reportedly used on their Olympians during the Cold War to today's man-made versions of natural human proteins, drugs have been as much a staple of the Games as gold, silver, and bronze" (Herper, par. 4). 3Despite rigorous drug testing, the use of banned performance-enhancing substances has become more widespread than ever. 4The disqualification of athletes from the most recent Olympic Games because of illegal drug use shows that the problem is ongoing.

5It seems apparent that if athletes want to win, they must consider using drugs. 6Dr. Michael Karsten, a Dutch physician who said he had prescribed anabolic steroids to hundreds of world-class athletes, states, "If you are especially gifted, you may win once, but from my experience you can't continue to win without drugs. 7The field is just too filled with drug users" (qtd. in Bamberger and Yaeger 62). 8In fact, some people claim that record-breaking performances of Olympic athletes may be directly due to drugs. 9Charles Yesalis, a Pennsylvania State University professor who has studied the use of drugs in sports, believes "a large percentage" of athletes who have set new records have done so with the help of performance-enhancing drugs. 10"A lot of experts, at least in private, feel that way," he claims (qtd. in Herper, par. 6).

Herper, Matthew. "Olympics: Performance Drugs Outrun Olympics." Forbes 15 Feb. 2002. · Bamberger, Michael, and Don Yaeger. "Over the Edge." Sports Illustrated 14 Apr. 1997: 60–86.
The citation (Herper, par. 4) shows the words come from paragraph 4 of an online article by a writer named Herper. The citation (qtd. in Bamberger and Yaeger 62) means the words were spoken by Dr. Karsten and quoted on page 62 of an article by Bamberger and Yaeger.

Writing technique questions

1. Which sentence states the main idea of the first paragraph?
Answer
Sentence 1: Performance-enhancing drugs have been used by Olympic athletes for decades.
2. What direct quotation supports it? What phrase introduces the quotation?
Answer
The quotation in sentence 2 ("From the brute steroids … gold, silver, and bronze"), introduced by According to an article in Forbes magazine.
3. What is the main idea of the second paragraph? What three direct quotations support it?
Answer
If athletes want to win, they must consider using drugs. Quotations: Dr. Karsten's "If you are especially gifted…"; "a large percentage" (Yesalis); and "A lot of experts, at least in private, feel that way."
4. What verbs introduce the quotations in the second paragraph?
Answer
states (Karsten), believes (Yesalis), claims (Yesalis).
5. Explain the in-text citation at the end of the second paragraph: who spoke the words? Who wrote the article? Is the source printed or online? How can you tell?
Answer
Yesalis spoke the words; Herper wrote the article ("qtd. in Herper"); it is online, because the reference gives a paragraph number ("par. 6") rather than a page number.

Reporting verbs and phrases

To introduce borrowed information (direct quotations, indirect quotations or statistics) use according to or a reporting verb:

assertclaimdeclareinsistmaintainmentionreportsaystatesuggestwrite
1. Position. Reporting verbs can come before, in the middle of, or after the borrowed information. According to usually comes before or after, not in the middle.
One young bicyclist says, "To win in world-class competition, you have to take drugs" (Jones).
"To win in world-class competition," says one young bicyclist, "you have to take drugs" (Jones).
"To win in world-class competition, you have to take drugs," says one young bicyclist (Jones).
According to one young bicyclist, athletes have to take drugs to win (Jones).
Athletes have to take drugs to win, according to one young bicyclist (Jones).
Which position is not normal for "according to"?
2. With or without "as". As one writer says when discussing … , "The human body, of course, doesn't distinguish intentional use from inadvertent exposure" (Kidder, par. 5) or One writer says when discussing …
3. Tense. Any tense: Some critics claim / have claimed that the IOC has been lax… But a past-tense reporting verb may change verbs, pronouns and time expressions in an indirect quotation: Some critics claimed that the IOC had been lax…
4. Name the source. Naming the source with the reporting expression gives authority: The Institute of Global Ethics warns, "The Olympics could well become just another money-drenched media promotion…" (Kidder, par. 7).

Punctuating direct quotations

1. Put quotation marks around information copied word for word. Don't use them for paraphrases, summaries or indirect quotations.
2. Normally put commas and periods inside the closing mark: According to Sports Illustrated, "Eliminating drug use from Olympic sports would be no small challenge." and "Eliminating drug use … no small challenge," according to Sports Illustrated.
  Two exceptions: if you insert only a few quoted words into your own sentence, use no commas (Charles Yesalis believes that "a large percentage" of athletes … (qtd. in Herper, par. 6)); and when you add an in-text citation, the period goes after the closing parenthesis.
3. Capitalize the first word of the quotation as well as the first word of the sentence: Dr. Donald Catlin, director of a drug-testing lab at UCLA, stated, "The sophisticated athlete who wants to take drugs has switched to things we can't test for" (qtd. in Bamberger and Yaeger 62).
4. If you break a quoted sentence in two, enclose both parts in quotation marks, separate the parts with commas, and capitalize only the first word: "The sophisticated athlete who wants to take drugs," stated Dr. Donald Catlin, "has switched to things we can't test for".
5. If you omit words, use an ellipsis (three spaced periods).
6. If you add words, put square brackets around them: "The testers know that the [drug] gurus are smarter than they are."
7. Use single quotation marks for a quotation within a quotation: "My ethical inner voice tells me, 'Don't use drugs,' but my competitive inner voice says, 'You can't win if you don't.'"
8. If a quotation is four lines or longer, don't use quotation marks: introduce it with a colon and indent it one inch from the left margin.

Indirect quotations

In an indirect quotation the speaker's or writer's words are reported without quotation marks, so it is sometimes called reported speech. It uses the same reporting verbs, and that is often added. The tense of the verbs is affected by the tense of the reporting verb.

Compare this model with the second paragraph of the direct-quotation model (same sentence numbers).

Drugs and the Olympic Games 2

5It seems apparent that if athletes want to win, they must consider using drugs. 6Dr. Michael Karsten, a Dutch physician who said he had prescribed anabolic steroids to hundreds of world-class athletes, stated that if [athletes] were especially gifted, [they] might win once, but from his experience [they] couldn't continue to win without drugs. 7He asserted that the field was just too filled with drug users (qtd. in Bamberger and Yaeger 62). 8In fact, some people claim that record-breaking performances of Olympic athletes may be directly due to drugs. 9Charles Yesalis, a Pennsylvania State University professor who has studied the use of drugs in sports, believes that "a large percentage" of athletes who have set new records have done so with the help of performance-enhancing drugs. 10He claims that a lot of experts, at least in private, feel that way (qtd. in Herper, par. 6).

1. Underline the verbs said and stated in sentence 6. What tense are they?
Answer
Simple past.
2. How do the verbs in the indirect quotations compare with the direct version?
Answer
They changed to a past form: are → were, may win → might win, can't continue → couldn't continue.
3. What was added to sentence 7, and how did the verb change?
Answer
that was added (He asserted that…), and is became was.
4. In sentence 6, why are athletes and they in square brackets?
Answer
They replace the original you, which would not fit the new sentence. Added or changed words go in square brackets.
5. Did any words change in sentence 9?
Answer
No: it is the same, because the reporting verb believes is present tense (and the quoted words are kept).

Changing direct quotations to indirect

  1. Omit the quotation marks.
  2. Add the subordinator that (you may omit it if the meaning is clear).
  3. Change the verb tense if necessary, following the sequence of tenses rules.
  4. Change pronouns (and time expressions if necessary) to keep the sense of the original.

Sequence of tenses

If the reporting verb is in a past tense, the verbs in an indirect quotation may change tense. Pronouns (and sometimes time expressions) may change too.

Tense changeDirect quotationIndirect quotation
Simple present → simple pastSusan said, "The exam is at eight o'clock."Susan said (that) the exam was at eight o'clock.
Simple past and present perfect → past perfectShe said, "We didn't have time to eat breakfast."She said (that) they hadn't had time to eat breakfast.
He said, "The exam has just started."He said (that) the exam had just started.
will → would, can → could, may → might, must → had toSam mentioned, "Today I will eat Chinese food, and tomorrow I'll eat French food if I can find a good restaurant."Sam mentioned that today he would eat Chinese food and that tomorrow he'd eat French food if he could find a good restaurant.
Time expressions may change if the meaning requires itThe teacher said, "You must finish the test right now."The teacher said that we had to finish the test right then.
Three exceptions: the tense does not change when…
• the reporting verb is simple present, present perfect or future: He says, "I can finish it today." → He says that he can finish it today.
• the reporting phrase is according to: The lawyer said, "My client is innocent." → According to the lawyer, his client is innocent.
• the information is a fact or general truth: He said, "Water boils at a lower temperature in the mountains." → He said that water boils at a lower temperature in the mountains.

Statistics

Like quotations, statistics are good supporting details, and you must also cite the source. Study the graph, then read the paragraph that uses its data. Notice the reporting expression that gives the source.

World population growth, 1750–2150, in billions. Redrawn from the book's graph; values approximate. Source of the original: United Nations, World Population Prospects, 1998 revision, and estimates by the Population Reference Bureau.

World Population Growth

According to statistics from the Population Reference Bureau, the world's population is increasing at a geometric rate. World population first reached 1 billion back in 1804. It took 123 years for it to reach 2 billion in 1927. By 1960, a period of just 32 years, it had added another billion. Just 15 years later, we were at 4 billion, 12 years later at 5 billion, and 11 years after that at 6 billion. The United Nations has projected an increase to 9 billion by the year 2050. Most of the increase will be in the world's less developed countries ("World" 1).

1. Underline the topic sentence.
Answer
"According to statistics from the Population Reference Bureau, the world's population is increasing at a geometric rate."
2. What is the source of the statistics?
Answer
The Population Reference Bureau (and the United Nations for the 2050 projection).
3. What reporting expression identifies the source?
Answer
According to statistics from the Population Reference Bureau, and The United Nations has projected.

Notice the in-text citation ("World" 1): there is no author, so the first word of the title is used, with the page number.

Write with quotations

Write a short paragraph that develops the topic sentence below. Use the quotations as support, either direct or indirect, add supporting sentences and transition signals, and put an in-text citation after each quotation. First study the example.

Example
Topic sentence: The increased use of computers in business has been accompanied by a costly increase in computer crime.
Point A: Computer criminals cost business a lot of money. "The financial losses to business from computer thefts will exceed $25 billion in 2005."
Point B: Computer criminals steal not only money but also information. "It is not just the money they steal; they steal data, and data is power."
Source: Cybercrime by Meredith Bruce, page 185 (New York: Wexler, 2004).

Completed paragraph

The increased use of computers in business has been accompanied by a costly increase in computer crime. The losses to victims of computer crimes are very high. In her book Cybercrime, author Meredith Bruce claimed that the financial losses to business from computer thefts would exceed $25 billion in 2005 (185). Computer criminals steal not only money but also information. For example, they steal confidential business records, customer lists, and corporate plans. As Bruce stated, "It is not just the money they steal; they steal data, and data is power" (185).
