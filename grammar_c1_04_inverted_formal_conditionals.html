A Condition Without "If"

Four fixed patterns where the verb moves to the front and "if" simply vanishes.

Spot the pattern

Four sentences, none of them starting with "if":

  • "Were I to move abroad, I think I'd miss my family most of all."
  • "Had I known about the strike, I would have left an hour earlier."
  • "Were it not for the traffic, we'd already be home."
  • "Should you need anything else, just ask."

Before you check: in each one, which word has moved to the very front, and what job is it doing?

Check the pattern CLICK
Each sentence is really an "if" sentence with "if" deleted and the verb were, had or should moved in front of the subject instead:
"Were I to move abroad..." = If I were to move abroad...
"Had I known about the strike..." = If I had known about the strike...
"Were it not for the traffic..." = If it weren't for the traffic...
"Should you need anything else..." = If you should need anything else...
Were + subject + to + base verb → a hypothetical action
Had + subject + past participle → a hypothetical past (regret)
   (sometimes TWO "had"s: Had I had more time...)
Were it not for + noun → something is the only thing stopping a result
Should + subject + base verb → a polite possibility (offers, instructions)

The trickiest one: two "had"s

"If I had had more warning, I could have planned differently."
→
"Had I had more warning, I could have planned differently."

The first "had" inverts with "I" — that's the grammar trick. The second "had" is the ordinary past participle of "have", exactly as it was in the plain "if" sentence. It doesn't disappear just because the first one moved.

Other Words for "If"

A set of formal connectors that do a similar job to "if", each with its own shade of meaning.

Spot the pattern: the "only if" group

  • "You can borrow my car, provided you fill it up before you bring it back."
  • "The landlord agreed to the pets, providing we paid an extra deposit."
  • "We'll sign the contract on condition that the price stays fixed for a year." (very formal — contracts, agreements)
  • "As long as you're back by midnight, you can borrow the car." (everyday, spoken)

All four mean roughly the same thing. What's that meaning, and which of the four sounds the most formal?

Check the pattern CLICK
All four mean "if — and only if": one condition has to be met for the rest to be true. On condition that is the most formal (you'd expect it in a contract or a legal letter); provided / providing (that) sit in the middle; as long as is the everyday, spoken version.
provided (that) / providing (that) / on condition that / as long as + clause → "if, and only if"

Spot the pattern: three more useful links

  • "I'll take the eight o'clock train unless the meeting overruns."
  • "But for a last-minute change of plan, we'd have missed the flight entirely." (formal — written or careful speech)
  • "Leave now — otherwise you'll get stuck in rush hour."
  • "Supposing the interview goes badly, would you still take the job if they offered it?"

Each one replaces "if" with something slightly different. What job is each word doing?

Check the pattern CLICK
Unless = "if... not" (one word instead of two).
But for = "if it weren't/hadn't been for" — a shorter, formal cousin of "were it not for".
Otherwise links two separate sentences: do this, or else that will happen. It can't open a clause the way "if" can.
Supposing invites someone to imagine a situation — often at the start of a question.
"If you don't confirm by Friday, we'll release the table."
→
"Unless you confirm by Friday, we'll release the table."

Your Turn to Talk

Time to put several of today's forms to work in one real piece of advice or instruction.

The situation

Imagine you're responsible for someone new — a guest in your home, a new colleague, a new tenant. You need to explain the situation and set some expectations, clearly and politely.

Talk for one minute. Try to cover:

  • What would you do differently if a plan changed? (Were I to...)
  • Is there anything you rely on, or one thing that's the only reason something works? (Were it not for... / but for...)
  • What should they do if a problem comes up? (Should you...)
  • What's your one condition for something? (provided that... / unless...)

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: both mean "if, and only if" — but as long as is the everyday, spoken version ("You can stay as long as you're quiet"), while on condition that is formal and precise, the kind of wording you'd expect in a contract, a rulebook, or a strict written agreement.
