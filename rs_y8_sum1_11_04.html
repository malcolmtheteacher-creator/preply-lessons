Encounter: Christian and Islamic Approaches to Ethics

The Question

Both Christians and Muslims believe that God is the source of morality. But how they understand God's guidance, and how much room they allow for individual choice, differs in important ways. Let's explore their frameworks side by side.

Christian Ethics

Divine Command Theory

God's will determines what is right and wrong. When God commands something, it is good; when God forbids it, it is wrong. The Bible and Church tradition preserve God's moral teachings.

Natural Law

Philosophers like Thomas Aquinas taught that God created the world with a rational order. By using reason, we can discover God's moral design. Certain acts (like killing the innocent) are always wrong because they violate this natural order.

Situation Ethics

Joseph Fletcher argued that Christian ethics is flexible. The Bible's highest law is love. In any situation, the most loving action is the right action — even if it means breaking a rule. Conscience and compassion matter more than rigid rules.

Scripture as Guide

The Bible contains moral teachings — the Ten Commandments, Jesus's teachings on forgiveness and compassion, Paul's letters on virtue. Christians interpret Scripture to guide modern ethical decisions.

Islamic Ethics

Shariah (Divine Law)

Shariah is God's law revealed in the Quran and Hadith (teachings of Prophet Muhammad). It guides every area of life — worship, business, family, justice. Following Shariah is how Muslims obey God and live ethically.

The Five Pillars as Ethics

The Five Pillars are not just religious duties — they are acts of submission to God that shape character and behaviour. Prayer teaches discipline. Almsgiving teaches generosity. Pilgrimage teaches equality and community.

Halal and Haram

Shariah divides actions into categories: halal (permitted and good), haram (forbidden), and makruh (disliked). This clarity helps Muslims make ethical decisions consistently. Avoiding haram protects spiritual purity.

Niyyah (Intention)

A Hadith says "Actions are judged by intentions." Even a good act can be wrong if done with bad motives. Muslims focus on whether the heart is pure and whether the action obeys God.

Comparing Christian and Islamic Ethics

SIMILAR

God as Source of Morality

Both traditions teach that God is the ultimate source of right and wrong. Both see obedience to God as the foundation of ethics. Both reject the Humanist idea that we create morality ourselves through reason alone.

SIMILAR

Compassion and Justice

Both Christianity and Islam emphasise compassion — for the poor, the weak, the stranger. Both demand justice. Jesus taught: "Love your neighbour." The Quran teaches that "God loves the merciful." Both traditions value caring for others.

DIFFERENT

Role of Law

Islam emphasises detailed law (Shariah) as the primary way to understand ethics. Christianity is more flexible — Situation Ethics allows breaking rules if love demands it. Christians argue that legalism misses the spirit of God's law. Muslims argue that clear law prevents confusion and protects justice.

DIFFERENT

Individual Conscience vs Community Guidance

Situation Ethics stresses the individual's conscience — what does love require in this specific situation? Islamic ethics stresses following established law and seeking guidance from scholars. Christians might say "follow your heart"; Muslims might say "follow Shariah and trusted scholars."

DIFFERENT

Flexibility and Change

Christian ethicists debate whether rules can change with context. Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh) allows some interpretation, but the core principles of Shariah are fixed. Muslims see stability in law as protecting the faith; Christians sometimes see flexibility as showing love.

Apply: The Charity Dilemma

The Scenario

You have spare money and want to help people in poverty. But a Christian and a Muslim friend disagree about how much to give and why. The Christian says: "Give what your heart tells you is loving. If you can afford it, be generous." The Muslim says: "Give 2.5% of your wealth (Zakat). That's what God requires. It's not about feeling — it's about obeying God's law."

Evaluate: Which Approach Is Stronger?

Islamic ethics (Shariah) is stronger

It provides clarity and consistency. Everyone knows what is halal and haram. There is no room for confusion or selfishness — you cannot justify breaking the rules by claiming "it felt loving." Shariah protects justice and prevents powerful people from using flexibility to oppress the weak. Clear law is fairer than subjective conscience.

Christian ethics (Situation Ethics) is stronger

Laws are rigid and can cause harm. What if keeping a rule hurts someone? Jesus taught love above all — compassion sometimes requires breaking rules. Individual conscience, guided by prayer and the Spirit, can respond to real human needs. Rules that never change become tools of oppression. Love and flexibility are more like God.

Both traditions need each other

Islamic scholars actually allow some interpretation of Shariah (qiyas, analogy) for new situations. Christian churches do teach rules and expect moral boundaries. The tension between law and love, between community guidance and individual conscience, is healthy. Neither extreme — pure legalism or pure situation ethics — works perfectly.

Key Terms

Divine Command Theory
The idea that God's will determines what is right and wrong. If God commands something, it is good.
Natural Law
The belief that God built moral order into creation, and we can discover it through reason. Some acts are always wrong because they violate nature.
Situation Ethics
The idea that the right action depends on the situation. Love is the highest law. Sometimes breaking a rule is the most loving thing to do.
Shariah
Islamic law based on the Quran and Hadith. It guides all areas of Muslim life and is seen as God's revealed guidance.
Halal
Permitted by Islamic law; lawful and good. Muslims follow halal practices in food, behaviour, and business.
Haram
Forbidden by Islamic law. Acts that are haram are sinful and must be avoided.
Niyyah
Intention. In Islam, actions are judged by their intention. A good act done with a bad motive is not truly good.
Five Pillars
The five core duties of Islam: witnessing faith, prayer, almsgiving, fasting, and pilgrimage. They shape Islamic ethics and character.
