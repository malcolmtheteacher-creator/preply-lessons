220-260 words. Semi-formal discursive essay.

✓ REQUIRED by the Cambridge rubric:

  2. Reach a reasoned conclusion about which is most important overall.
★ USEFUL — but NOT required:

Adding ONE point of your own (an idea NOT in the bullets) is a strong technique we teach because it demonstrates independent thinking and helps you hit the word count. However, Cambridge does not require it — a well-argued essay discussing two bullets with a reasoned verdict can achieve Band 5.

Today's Question

In your English class you have been discussing the role of technology in modern education. Now your teacher has asked you to write an essay on this topic.

Write an essay discussing TWO of the tools below. Explain which one you think has had the most positive effect on students, giving reasons in support of your answer.

  • online courses and lectures
  • educational apps
  • traditional printed textbooks

Some opinions expressed in the discussion:

"Online lectures from top universities are now available to anyone with internet."
"Apps turn learning into a game — students actually want to do them."
"There's still no substitute for a good textbook you can mark up and return to."

Write your essay in 220-260 words in an appropriate style.

Key rule: Discuss exactly TWO of the three bullets — never all three. Develop both in depth. Your opinion in CAE is which of the two bullets matters more, woven through the evaluation and stated in the conclusion. Adding a third "own point" paragraph is a stretch technique for Band 5+ candidates only — it is not what Cambridge asks for, and it carries word-count risk.

Essay Structure — the orthodox shape

This is the structure Cambridge expects. Four paragraphs, two bullets developed in depth, opinion delivered through evaluation and verdict — not through a fourth invented idea.

Introduction

Paraphrase the topic + name the two bullets you'll discuss + hint at your verdict.

50-60 words
Body 1: First bullet

Topic sentence + advantages + concession (limits/qualifier) + example. Develop in depth.

75-85 words
Body 2: Second bullet

Topic sentence + evaluation + explicit comparison with Body 1 + your evaluative angle.

75-85 words
Conclusion

Clear verdict — which of the two bullets matters more, and why. No new arguments.

30-40 words
Where your "opinion" lives: not in a separate fourth paragraph — but woven through the evaluation in Bodies 1 and 2 (concession, comparison, qualifier) and stated cleanly in the conclusion. Which of the two bullets matters more? That is your opinion in CAE.
⭐ STRETCH option (Band 5+ only): a confident candidate who naturally writes short can add a 4th body paragraph (45-55 words) introducing an idea NOT in the bullets — see Tab 6. This is a strategic move, not a Cambridge requirement, and it carries word-count risk. Most candidates do better keeping the four-paragraph spine and developing the two bullets in depth.

Model Essay: Band C1+

Read carefully. Notice how the writer (1) discusses two of the three bullets, (2) adds a fresh point of their own, and (3) delivers a clear verdict. Highlighted phrases are the advanced structures examiners reward.

⭐ Note — this model demonstrates the STRETCH version (with an optional fourth body paragraph adding the writer's own point). For most candidates, the safer orthodox structure is intro + 2 bullets developed in depth + conclusion. Read this model for the language and evaluation moves, but you do not need to copy the four-body structure. See Tab 6 for guidance on when the stretch own-point is worth adding.
INTROFew areas of modern life have been transformed by technology as dramatically as education. With countless tools now competing for students' attention, the question of which innovations deserve priority has become pressing. This essay will examine online courses and educational apps before adding a further consideration of my own.
BODY 1 — ONLINE COURSESIt is clear that online courses have opened learning to audiences who, only a generation ago, would have been shut out by geography or cost. A learner in a remote village can now follow a university lecture delivered from Cambridge or Stanford. However, without self-discipline, completion rates remain stubbornly low, and isolation often undermines genuine understanding.
BODY 2 — APPSEducational apps, by contrast, succeed precisely where traditional study falters: engagement. Short, gamified exercises turn vocabulary drills or maths practice into something students willingly return to. That said, many apps prioritise entertainment over depth, producing learners who can answer quick-fire questions but struggle to sustain a long argument or write extended analytical prose.
BODY 3 — OWN POINTA further factor worth adding is the irreplaceable role of the skilled teacher. No algorithm yet matches a human who can read confusion on a face, reframe an explanation on the spot, or inspire a reluctant student. Schools in Finland, consistently world-leading, have built their success around exactly this human expertise.
CONCLUSIONIn conclusion, although online courses and apps have undeniably expanded access and motivation, it is the trained teacher who remains the decisive influence. Were schools to invest as heavily in staff development as in software, outcomes would improve far more reliably.

Total: 250 words ✓ (within 220-260)

Superlative opener (intro): "Few areas of modern life have been transformed by technology as dramatically as education" — strong, evaluative first sentence instead of the bland "Technology has changed education..."

Emphatic opener (Body 1): "It is clear that..." — asserts a shared starting point before the qualifier lands

Embedded relative clause: "audiences who, only a generation ago, would have been shut out..." — shows range by packing historical context inside a relative clause

Specific-over-vague evidence (Body 1): "delivered from Cambridge or Stanford" — named institutions beat generic "famous universities"

Colon-led topic sentence (Body 2): "succeed precisely where traditional study falters: engagement" — the colon pivots elegantly to the key word

Concessive linker (Body 2): "That said,..." — flips from benefit to limitation in one phrase

Own-point signal (Body 3): "A further factor worth adding is..." — the examiner IMMEDIATELY recognises independent thinking

Tricolon (Body 3): "read confusion on a face, reframe an explanation on the spot, or inspire a reluctant student" — three parallel actions = rhetorical weight

Specific example (Body 3): "Schools in Finland, consistently world-leading" — real evidence lifts the paragraph

Conditional inversion (Conclusion): "Were schools to invest..." — instead of "If schools invested..."

Cleft-pattern (Conclusion): "it is the trained teacher who remains the decisive influence" — puts emphasis exactly where you want it.

Write Body Paragraph 2: Second Given Bullet

Less scaffolding — you lead. Your job: contrast this bullet with Body 1, and hint at which is more effective.

Useful structures for Body 2:

  • Contrast linker: "X, by contrast," / "in contrast," / "conversely,"
  • Colon-led topic sentence: "X succeeds precisely where Y falters: [key word]"
  • Evidence chain: "Short, gamified exercises turn [boring task] into something students willingly return to"
  • Honest limit: "That said, many X prioritise [surface benefit] over [deeper quality], producing learners who can Y but struggle to Z"
  • Comparative reach: "quick-fire questions" vs "extended analytical prose"

⭐ Stretch: Your Own Point (Band 5+ only)

This is the paragraph that lifts the essay to top-band territory. You add a factor NOT in the three bullets — showing independent thinking. Keep it slightly shorter (45-55 words).

What counts as "your own point"?

Any genuine educational factor that isn't "online courses", "educational apps" or "traditional textbooks". Strong options include: the role of the skilled teacher, peer collaboration, hands-on practical work, mentoring, real-world internships, libraries and physical study space, or parental involvement.
