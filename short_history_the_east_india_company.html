The East India Company

A private company with its own army that came to trade in spices and ended up ruling a subcontinent.

Today: you'll tell the story of a business that set out to buy pepper and finished by governing a subcontinent — and you'll master the language of scale and proportion, the phrases English uses to say honestly how big something was: a third of, twice as many, in excess of, accounted for, the equivalent of.

Now — the words for today's story

1. The Company existed only because the Crown said it could: a royal charter, sealed in 1600, gave a few hundred London merchants the legal right to trade in the East.
a charter (n) — an official document from a ruler or state creating a body and granting it particular rights.
2. No other English ship could legally trade east of the Cape of Good Hope, and that monopoly — total, exclusive control of a trade — was the Company's most valuable possession.
a monopoly (n) — complete control of a trade or of the supply of a good, with no competitor allowed in.
3. A single voyage could return several times what it had cost, which made the eastern trade extraordinarily lucrative for those who invested in it.
lucrative (adj) — producing a great deal of money; highly profitable.
4. After 1765 the Company did not merely buy and sell: it collected the land tax of Bengal, and that revenue — money from millions of farmers — paid for its armies.
revenue (n) — income, especially money a government or company receives regularly from taxes or business.
5. Clive won the battle before it was fought by secretly arranging to bribe the Nawab's commander, promising him a throne if his troops stood still.
to bribe (v) — to give money or advantage dishonestly so that someone acts in your favour; (n) a bribe.
6. When the Punjab was beaten, the Company took no payment — it chose to annex the territory outright and rule it directly.
to annex (v) — to take a territory and add it permanently to the lands you govern, usually by force.
7. Critics then and now call the whole system straightforward exploitation: wealth taken out of India by force, tax and rules written in London, with little returned.
exploitation (n) — using people or resources unfairly for your own profit; to exploit (v).
8. What began as a refusal by soldiers to touch the new cartridges spread within weeks into a general insurrection against Company rule in northern India.
an insurrection (n) — a violent uprising against an established authority; a rebellion.
9. A private business with its own army, answering to shareholders in London rather than the millions it governed, struck many even then as dangerously unaccountable.
unaccountable (adj) — not required to answer for one's actions to anyone; free from proper oversight.
10. Stripped of its powers in 1858, the Company was quietly dissolved in 1874, and the greatest corporation in history simply stopped existing.
to dissolve (v) — to bring a company, parliament or organisation to a formal, legal end.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try it from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A charter and a monopoly

On the last day of 1600, Elizabeth I sealed a charter for a few hundred London merchants who called themselves the Governor and Company of Merchants of London trading into the East Indies. It gave them what no amount of private money could buy: a legal monopoly of all English trade east of the Cape of Good Hope. They were a joint-stock company: investors put money into a common pot, shared the risk and divided whatever came back — sensible in a business where perhaps a third of the ships sent out never returned. Nobody in the room imagined an empire. They wanted pepper, cloves and nutmeg, which sold in London for many times what they cost where they grew, making the eastern trade one of the most lucrative gambles open to an Englishman with capital. For its first half-century the Company stayed what its name said it was — a trading firm in rented rooms, dependent on far stronger states, and in excess of six months' sailing from anyone who might have given it an order.

The spice islands, however, were already taken. The Dutch East India Company, founded two years later, was better financed, better organised and more ruthless; it fought for the Moluccas and won. The breaking point came in 1623 at Amboyna, where the Dutch accused a group of English merchants of plotting against them, tortured them and executed them. The episode poisoned relations for a generation and made the point brutally: the English would not hold the spice islands. So the Company turned to the Indian mainland, and stumbled into a far bigger business. India produced cotton cloth of a quality Europe could not match — calicoes and muslins — as well as raw silk, indigo dye and saltpetre, an essential ingredient of gunpowder. Within decades textiles accounted for the great majority of the Company's cargoes, and later tea from China would dominate everything. The Company built fortified trading posts, called factories, at Surat, Madras, Bombay and Calcutta: walled compounds of warehouses, offices and a small garrison.

It is easy to forget how small the Company was in the world it had entered. India then meant the Mughal Empire, one of the richest states on earth, whose economy is generally reckoned to have been the equivalent of a substantial share of world output — some historians put it at around a quarter. The English traded entirely on the emperor's permission, granted through formal orders that had to be negotiated, renewed and paid for. An embassy from James I spent years at the Mughal court securing trading rights, and the ambassador's letters record how little impression his gifts made. The Company's men were petitioners, not rulers: they wore local dress, learned Persian, took local partners, and their factories were licensed, watched and occasionally shut. Its ships were armed and its compounds guarded, but its power on land was negligible. For well over a century, this is the story of foreigners asking politely to do business.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What exactly did the charter of 1600 give the Company, and why did that matter?

2. Why did the Company end up trading on the Indian mainland rather than in the spice islands?

1. A legal monopoly of all English trade east of the Cape of Good Hope, granted on the last day of 1600. No other English merchant could compete, so the profits of a very risky trade went to its shareholders.

2. The Dutch were stronger in the Moluccas and drove the English out, above all after Amboyna in 1623. India offered something better: cotton textiles, silk, indigo, saltpetre and later tea.

Discuss:
  • The Company's advantage was not a better ship or product — it was a piece of paper granting a monopoly. Is a monopoly ever a fair reward for taking a risk nobody else will take?

Part 2 — From trading post to government

The eighteenth century changed everything, and the reason had little to do with Europe. After the death of the emperor Aurangzeb in 1707, Mughal authority fragmented; provincial governors became independent rulers in all but name, and the subcontinent filled with competing states, shifting alliances and constant war. In that world, whoever could put a disciplined army into the field could sell its services or use them. The Company, needing to guard its factories, had begun raising Indian troops — sepoys — drilled in the European manner under British officers. These were not foreign invaders but local men who took the Company's pay, and for most of its history the great majority of its soldiers were Indian. The Company supplied money, muskets, drill and the ability to keep paying. Its French rivals did the same, and the two fought through Indian allies in the south for a generation. Almost by accident, a firm of merchants had acquired a war-fighting machine, and in a fragmenting empire that produced revenue far more reliably than trade ever could.

The turning point is usually dated to June 1757, on a mango field at Plassey in Bengal. Robert Clive faced the army of the Nawab, Siraj-ud-Daulah, with perhaps a tenth of the men. He won in advance, not on the day: through intermediaries he had agreed to bribe the Nawab's leading commander, Mir Jafar, promising him the throne of Bengal if his troops did not fight. They did not, the battle was brief, and Bengal changed hands. What followed mattered more than the fighting. Clive and his colleagues took enormous personal payments from the new Nawab, and Clive went home one of England's richest men. Then, in 1765, after further fighting, the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II granted the Company the diwani — the right to collect the land revenue of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. In law it was an appointment within the Mughal system; in practice a London corporation now held the tax base of perhaps twenty or thirty million people. It was no longer a trader that happened to have soldiers. It was a government.

The consequences came quickly. In 1770 the rains failed in Bengal, and the resulting famine killed on a scale still argued over: contemporaries spoke of something like a third of the population dying, and although modern historians treat that figure with caution, all agree the toll ran into the millions. Through it, revenue collection continued and in some districts was pressed harder, while Company servants were accused of cornering the grain trade. The extraction of wealth was not incidental to the system; it was the system. Meanwhile Company men came home with fortunes so large that a new insult entered the language: the nabobs, vulgar new money buying seats in Parliament. The unease produced Britain's most famous political trial, the impeachment of the first Governor-General, Warren Hastings, whose prosecution by Edmund Burke ran for years before ending in acquittal. Parliament had already begun to act, passing a Regulating Act and then an India Act, because a private company had become too powerful, too rich and too unaccountable to be left alone.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How was the battle of Plassey actually won, and why is it treated as a turning point?

2. What was the diwani of 1765, and what did it turn the Company into?

1. Largely by bribery: Clive secretly agreed to make the Nawab's commander Mir Jafar ruler of Bengal if his troops stood aside. It is the turning point because control of Bengal followed, and with it the shift to territorial power.

2. The right, granted by Shah Alam II, to collect the land revenue of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. It made the Company a government: it taxed millions and paid its armies from the proceeds.

  • The Company's armies were overwhelmingly Indian soldiers serving for pay. Does that make the conquest of India a foreign conquest at all — or something more complicated?

Part 3 — Mutiny, dissolution, and the argument

Once revenue paid for soldiers and soldiers produced revenue, the machine ran on its own. Across the century after Plassey it fought the Anglo-Mysore wars against Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan, the long Anglo-Maratha wars, and finally the Anglo-Sikh wars, after which it chose to annex the Punjab outright. Some states were beaten, some bought, many reduced to dependencies paying for garrisons they had not asked for. At its height the Company's army numbered in excess of a quarter of a million men — by most estimates roughly twice as many soldiers as the British state kept under arms. There was also opium. British tea drinking had created a trade imbalance with China that drained silver out of Britain, and the answer was a drug: opium was grown under Company monopoly in Bengal and Bihar, auctioned in Calcutta and carried to the Chinese coast by private traders, which let the Company profit while keeping its hands technically clean. That trade led towards the Opium Wars. At home, Parliament cut the Company back, ending its Indian and then its China monopoly, until the men who had chartered a trading venture were governing and barely trading.

The end came in 1857. The immediate cause was a rumour among the sepoys that the paper cartridges for the new rifles, which had to be bitten open, were greased with cow and pig fat — offensive to Hindus and Muslims alike. Behind it lay years of grievance: annexations that had swallowed princely states, changes to land tenure that ruined old families, resentment at missionary activity, and the barring of sepoys from the ranks that mattered. Refusal at Meerut in May turned into insurrection across much of northern India, with the aged Mughal emperor in Delhi proclaimed its figurehead. The fighting was savage on both sides, with massacres of civilians and deliberately terrible reprisals. Even the name is contested: the British called it the Indian Mutiny or the Sepoy Rebellion; many Indian historians call it the First War of Independence; others prefer the Uprising of 1857. The result is not contested. The British state decided a company could not be trusted with a subcontinent, and in 1858 the Crown took over directly. Stripped of every function, the Company was formally dissolved in 1874.

The echoes are long and still loud. Crown rule, the Raj, lasted until independence and partition in 1947, and the borders, railways, legal codes and English-language administration it left still shape South Asia. The Company also left an argument that will not settle. To one side, it is the original case study in corporate power without accountability: a private firm that raised armies, waged wars, collected taxes and answered only to shareholders — the lesson being that companies large enough to shape states must be watched. To the other, the debate about exploitation, restitution and reparations for what was taken from India remains genuinely live in politics and scholarship. Then there is the quieter argument about teaching: how much of this belongs in a British classroom, and in whose words. It is a strange afterlife for a firm of pepper merchants. They asked for a licence to buy spices, and four centuries later people are still arguing about what they did with it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Give the immediate cause and two deeper causes of the events of 1857.

2. What happened to the Company in 1858 and in 1874?

1. Immediate: the rumour that the new cartridges, which had to be bitten open, were greased with cow and pig fat. Deeper: any two of — annexations, disruption of landholding, fear of missionary interference, blocked promotion for sepoys.

2. In 1858 the Crown took over the government of India directly, ending Company rule; the Company, left with no functions, was formally dissolved in 1874.

Grammar — expressing scale and proportion

This story cannot be told without numbers, and many of the numbers are uncertain or disputed. English has a set of phrases built for exactly that job: they let you give the size of something, compare it with something else, and stay honest about how sure you are. An army in excess of a quarter of a million. Twice as many soldiers as the Crown. Textiles that accounted for most of the cargo. A third of a population. Revenue the equivalent of a European state's.

How these expressions are built:
• Fractions: a third of / two thirds of / a quarter of + the + noun — "A third of the ships never returned." The verb agrees with the noun after of: a third of the money was… but a third of the ships were…
• Multiples: twice / three times / half + as many (countable) or as much (uncountable) + noun + as — "twice as many soldiers as the British army". Also twice the size of.
• in excess of + a number = more than: "an army in excess of 250,000 men".
• account for + a share of a total: "Textiles accounted for most of the cargo."
• the equivalent of + a noun = equal in value or effect: "revenue the equivalent of a small state's income".
When we use them — and how to stay honest:
• Register: in excess of and accounted for are formal and written; twice as many and a third of work anywhere.
• Hedging a disputed figure: put an approximator in front — something like a third of, roughly twice as many, by most estimates. This is how historians write when the evidence is thin.
• Comparison needs a base: always finish the sentence — "twice as many as the state's own army".
• Fixed prepositions: in excess of, the equivalent of, account for.
