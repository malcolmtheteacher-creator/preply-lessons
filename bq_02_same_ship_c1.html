Is It Still the Same Ship?

If every part is replaced, what is left that makes something the same thing, and what does that say about us?

← All the Big Questions

Today: you'll explain what makes something the same thing over time, and you'll master the structures that let you put the important idea at the front of a sentence.
Warm up — have a go, and don't worry about mistakes. Say whatever comes to mind.
  • Think of something you own, or a place you know, that has been repaired and changed so many times that almost nothing original remains. Do you still call it the same thing?
  • A football club changes every player, its stadium and its owners. Is it the same club? What would have to stay the same for you to say yes?
  • Are you the same person you were at ten? What, if anything, has stayed?

The ideas, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — A ship with no original planks

The Greek writer Plutarch tells of the ship in which the hero Theseus sailed home from Crete. The Athenians kept it for centuries as a monument. As its timbers rotted, they replaced them one by one with new wood, until philosophers began to argue about it: if not a single original plank remained, was it still the same ship?

Nobody had a satisfying answer, and in the seventeenth century Thomas Hobbes made it worse. Suppose, he said, someone collected every plank that had been thrown away and rebuilt the old ship from them. Now there are two ships. The first has the continuous history; the second has the original material. Which one is Theseus's?

It sounds like a children's riddle, but it is not. It asks a question that applies to almost everything: what makes a thing the same thing from one moment to the next?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to the ship of Theseus over the centuries?

2. What did Hobbes add to the puzzle?

1. The Athenians replaced its rotting planks one by one until none of the original wood remained, and then people asked whether it was still the same ship.

2. He imagined someone rebuilding a second ship from the discarded planks, so that one ship has the history and the other has the original material.

Discuss:
  • Hobbes's second ship makes the problem much harder. Before you read on, which of the two would you call the real one, and why?

Part 2 — From ships to people

The puzzle becomes personal when you replace the ship with a person. Much of the material in the body is renewed over a lifetime, yet you do not think you are a different person each decade. The seventeenth-century philosopher John Locke argued that a person's identity lies in memory: you are the same person as the child in the photograph because you can, in principle, remember being them.

In 1984 Derek Parfit pushed the idea to its limit in Reasons and Persons. Imagine a teleporter that scans you, destroys your body and rebuilds an identical one on Mars. Have you travelled, or have you died and been replaced by a copy? Parfit argued that the question matters less than we think. What counts, he said, is psychological continuity: the connected chain of memories, beliefs and intentions. If that chain survives, so does what we care about, even if the matter does not.

Not everyone is persuaded. Critics reply that a perfect copy is still a copy, and that the original has been ended. The disagreement is exactly the one about the ship, with higher stakes.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Locke define personal identity?

2. What did Parfit think matters in the teleporter case?

1. By memory: you are the same person because you can, in principle, remember being your earlier self.

2. Psychological continuity, the connected chain of memories, beliefs and intentions, rather than whether the same matter survives.

Discuss:
  • Would you step into the teleporter? What would your answer show about what you think you are?

Part 3 — Why the answer matters

Nothing here is only about ships. Every institution faces the puzzle. A football club replaces its players, its stadium and its owners; a language replaces its words; a city replaces its buildings. In Japan, the Ise Grand Shrine has been rebuilt on a neighbouring site every twenty years for about thirteen centuries. Is the shrine ancient, or brand new? Its keepers would say both, because what is passed on is the method and the ritual, not the wood.

So there are three main candidates for what makes something the same. The first is the material: the same planks. The second is the form: the same design or function. The third is the history: an unbroken chain of change, each step a small one. Most people feel the pull of all three, and the interesting cases are the ones where they disagree.

That may be why the paradox has lasted so long. A good answer has to say what we are protecting when we repair, restore or remember, and why.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What example shows an institution that is renewed regularly on purpose?

2. What are the three candidates for what makes something the same?

1. The Ise Grand Shrine in Japan, rebuilt every twenty years for about thirteen centuries; what continues is the method and ritual, not the wood.

2. The material, the form (design or function), and the history (an unbroken chain of small changes).

Discuss:
  • Which of the three, material, form or history, matters most for a family home, a friendship and a nation?

Grammar — cleft sentences

When you want a listener to notice the important idea, you can build the sentence around it. The idea goes last, and a short opening prepares for it. English has several neat structures for this. They sound controlled, and they work well in an argument.

Three structures:
• What + clause + is / was … — "What worries me is the copy's memories." "What she wanted was a straight answer."
• It is / was + the important part + that / who + clause — "It was Locke who placed identity in memory." "It is the design that survives."
• The reason (why) + clause + is that … — "The reason why the problem is hard is that every answer has a flaw."
Feel the difference:
• What… puts the focus on a thing or idea and saves it for the end.
• It is… that puts the focus on a person, place, time or reason and holds everything else back.
• The reason why… gives a cause, and the clause after is usually starts with that, not because.

Same or different?

Five situations. Say whether it is the same thing afterwards, and which test you are using: material, form or history. Then reveal.

1. A band replaces every member over forty years but keeps the name, the style and the songs. Is it the same band?
History and form. There was no single moment of replacement; each change was small, and the music continues. Almost nobody insists on the original members.
2. "You cannot step into the same river twice." Is a river the same river from one day to the next?
Form. The water is always different. What stays is the pattern: the course, the banks, the name. This is the case that is usually attributed to Heraclitus.
3. A museum replaces every brick of a famous building after a fire, to the original plans. Is it the same building?
It depends on the test. Same form, new material, broken history. Many people say it is a reconstruction, not the original: the history matters here.
4. A person loses all their memories after an illness but keeps their body and habits. Same person?
Locke and Parfit would hesitate; most people say yes. We usually give weight to the body and character, which suggests memory is not the whole story.
5. A company changes name, owners, location and products. Is it the same company?
Law says yes, ordinary language may say no. Legally a company is a continuing entity; to most people the identity is the purpose and the people, which have gone.

A thought experiment

Imagine this:
  • Hobbes's rebuilt ship sits in one harbour, the repaired ship in another. A court must decide which one owns Theseus's name. What would you ask the court to consider, and why?
  • A restorer replaces one plank a year for a hundred years. At what year, if any, does it stop being the original? What does your answer show about how we use the word "same"?
