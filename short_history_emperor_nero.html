Emperor Nero

The teenage emperor who longed to be an artist — and became history's favourite villain.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of a spectacular rise and fall, and discuss how a reputation is built — and who gets to write it — using narrative tenses and the language of power and scandal.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. At its height, Rome ruled the largest empire the ancient world had ever seen — millions of people, from Britain to Syria, all answering to a single throne.
an empire (n) — a large group of countries or peoples ruled by a single supreme authority.
2. The young king signed whatever was put in front of him, while his old tutor whispered every decision into his ear; everyone at court knew who the real power behind the throne was.
the power behind the throne (phrase) — a person who secretly controls someone who appears to be in charge.
3. As the boy reached for a third slice of cake, his mother caught his wrist to restrain him, and he reluctantly put it back on the plate.
to restrain (v) — to hold someone or something back; to keep under control.
4. Under the new governor's tyranny, villagers were taxed until they starved, jailed without trial, and beaten for the smallest complaint.
tyranny (n) — cruel and unjust use of total power. (A ruler like this is a tyrant.)
5. The whole dinner party was scandalised when the duchess kicked off her shoes and danced barefoot on the table, and the guests gasped behind their hands.
to be scandalised (v) — to be shocked and offended by something considered improper. (noun: a scandal)
6. Rather than admit he had lost the report himself, the manager made the new intern his scapegoat and let the whole office believe she was to blame.
a scapegoat (n) — a person or group unfairly blamed for the faults or problems of others.
7. The crowd turned hostile the moment the referee blew his whistle, booing, jeering and hurling cups onto the pitch.
hostile (adj) — unfriendly and opposed; showing strong dislike or enmity.
8. Around the campfire, grandfather told us the old legend of a giant who slept beneath the mountain — a tale his own grandfather had sworn was true.
a legend (n) — a traditional story widely believed but not provable; often a mix of fact and myth.
9. When the factory cut their wages again, the workers broke into open revolt, marching out of the gates and refusing to lift a single tool.
a revolt (n) — an organised rising against a ruler or authority; a rebellion.
10. With one swipe of the cloth she began to erase the equations from the whiteboard, until not a single number was left.
to erase (v) — to remove or rub out completely, so that no trace remains.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The boy on the throne

In the year 54 AD, a sixteen-year-old boy became master of the Roman world. His name was Nero, and he had not won his crown — his mother had won it for him. Agrippina was one of the most formidable women of the age: ambitious, brilliant and ruthless. She had married the emperor Claudius, persuaded him to adopt her son ahead of his own, and pushed Nero to the front of the line. When Claudius conveniently died, the teenager inherited an empire that stretched from Britain to the deserts of the east.

At first, Agrippina intended to be the power behind the throne, ruling Rome through her son. But Nero was not alone. He had a remarkable tutor, the philosopher Seneca, and a tough, loyal guard commander named Burrus. Between them, these two older men managed for several years to restrain the young emperor and keep him attending to the business of government. Historians often call these early years the "good" years of Nero's reign — a time of fair courts, careful spending and respect for the Senate.

But power inside the imperial palace was a deadly game, and mother and son were soon locked in a struggle for control. In 59 AD it ended in horror: Nero had his own mother killed. With Agrippina gone, the last real check on him began to weaken. Seneca and Burrus could only do so much. Slowly, the talented, well-tutored young ruler started to become something far darker.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Nero come to be emperor at sixteen, and who put him there?

2. Why are the early years of his reign often called the "good" years?

1. His mother Agrippina engineered it — she married the emperor Claudius, had Nero adopted ahead of Claudius's own son, and pushed him to the front of the succession.

2. Because his tutor Seneca and the guard commander Burrus restrained him and ran a period of fair, careful government — before he murdered his mother in 59 AD and slipped free of their influence.

Discuss:
  • A clever boy is handed total power at sixteen, with no one truly able to say "no" to him. What does that kind of power tend to do to a person?

Part 2 — Fire, blame, and the artist-emperor

In July of 64 AD, a fire broke out among the wooden shops near Rome's great racecourse and raced through the crowded city for days. Whole districts were reduced to ash. And here we meet the most famous story ever told about Nero: that he "fiddled while Rome burned" — that he stood by, playing music, enjoying the spectacle. It is a wonderful image, and it is almost certainly false. The fiddle had not even been invented yet. More tellingly, the best evidence suggests Nero was not even in the city when the fire began; he was at his villa down the coast, and hurried back to organise relief and open his gardens to the homeless.

So why the story? Partly because of what Nero did next. As the city was rebuilt, he seized a huge area of the ruins to build himself a vast pleasure-palace, the Domus Aurea or "Golden House" — with gardens, an artificial lake, and walls that glittered with gold and jewels. To ordinary Romans who had lost everything, this looked like an emperor profiting from their misery. And to deflect the rumours that he himself had started the fire, Nero needed a scapegoat. He chose the Christians, then a small and unpopular sect, and had them punished with terrible cruelty.

There was another reason Nero scandalised respectable Rome: he genuinely wanted to be an artist. He sang, played the lyre, wrote poetry and raced chariots — and, astonishingly for an emperor, he performed these things in public, before audiences. To us this might sound harmless, even charming. To the Roman elite it was shameful: emperors were meant to command armies, not chase applause on a stage like a common entertainer. The man who ruled the world seemed to care more about being loved as a performer.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What's wrong with the famous story that Nero "fiddled while Rome burned"?

2. Give two things Nero did that scandalised the Roman elite.

1. The fiddle didn't exist yet, and the evidence suggests Nero wasn't even in Rome when the fire started — he was at a coastal villa and came back to organise relief. The "fiddling" image is later legend, not fact.

2. Any two: building the enormous Golden House on the burnt ruins while Romans were homeless; blaming and cruelly punishing the Christians as scapegoats; and performing in public as a singer, musician and charioteer, which Romans thought beneath an emperor.

Discuss:
  • The "fiddling" story isn't true, yet almost everyone believes it. Why do vivid, simple legends so often beat the more complicated truth?

Part 3 — The fall, and the writing of a villain

In the end, Rome turned on him. Taxes, extravagance, the killing of senators, and the sheer embarrassment of an emperor on a stage all wore away his support. In 68 AD a revolt spread through the provinces; one governor after another declared against him, and finally the Senate condemned Nero as an enemy of the state. Abandoned even by his own guard, and facing arrest and execution, he fled to a villa outside the city and took his own life. According to legend his last words were, "What an artist dies in me!" — vain and theatrical to the very end, though we can't be sure he ever truly said them.

He was just thirty. After his death the Senate carried out what the Romans called damnatio memoriae: his statues were torn down and his name chiselled off monuments, an official attempt to erase him from history. And yet, strikingly, ordinary people scrawled graffiti in his favour — for some Romans, the showman emperor had been a friend of the common people.

Here is the catch that historians keep returning to. Almost everything we know about Nero comes from writers like Tacitus and Suetonius — members of the senatorial class he had bullied, who wrote decades later and were deeply hostile to him. They gave us the monster: the mother-killer, the fire-starter, the fiddling tyrant. Some of it is surely true. But a good deal is rumour, exaggeration and the revenge of his enemies, dressed up as history. Nero may well have been a bad ruler. He was also, perhaps, the first famous victim of a hostile press.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Nero's reign actually end in 68 AD?

2. Why do historians warn us to be careful with Nero's terrible reputation?

1. A revolt spread through the provinces, the Senate declared him an enemy of the state, and — abandoned and facing execution — he fled the city and took his own life, aged about thirty.

2. Because nearly all our sources (Tacitus, Suetonius) were written later by members of the elite who hated him. Much of the "monster" is rumour, exaggeration and the revenge of hostile enemies, so we have to separate fact from legend.

Discuss:
  • His enemies wrote the history, and we still believe it two thousand years later. How much should it worry us that the powerful — and their critics — get to decide how someone is remembered?

The grammar this story runs on: narrative tenses

A life story like this needs three past tenses working together. Get them right and your storytelling sounds effortless; get them wrong and the timeline collapses.

The three tools:
• Past simple — the main events, in sequence: "Nero became emperor," "the fire destroyed the city."
• Past continuous — the background, the longer action in progress: "while Seneca was advising him…"
• Past perfect — a step further back, something that happened before another past event: "by 64 AD his mother had died."

1. Past simple vs past continuous — event against background

Use the past continuous for the longer action already in progress, and the past simple for the shorter event that interrupts or sits inside it.

1. While Seneca and Burrus (guide) the young emperor, Rome quietly (enjoy) some of its best years.

2. The fire (still / burn) when Nero (return) to the city.

3. As the crowd (watch), the emperor calmly (sing) on the public stage.

1. were guiding / was enjoying (two longer actions running in the same period)

2. was still burning / returned (longer action interrupted by a sudden event)

3. was watching / sang (background in progress / the event)

2. Past perfect — the step further back

When you are already telling the past, and you reach back to something earlier still, use had + past participle.

1. By the time the fire broke out, Nero (already / kill) his own mother.

2. The palace he (build) on the ruins shocked the homeless people of Rome.

3. Before he became emperor, Agrippina (remove) every rival from his path.

4. The Senate condemned a man who (lose) the loyalty of his own army.

1. had already killed

2. had built

3. had removed

4. had lost

3. Put them together — narrate a paragraph

Fill each gap with the tense the meaning needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

On the night the fire started, Nero (rest) at his villa by the sea when the news (reach) him. The flames (spread) for days already, and thousands (lose) their homes. He (hurry) back, but the damage (be) done, and the rumours soon (begin).

was resting (background) · reached (event) · had been spreading / had spread (earlier, ongoing) · had lost (earlier event) · hurried (event) · was (event) · began (next event)

Notice how the three tenses layer the time: the long background, the sudden events, and the things that had already happened before.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Nero wanted to be loved as an artist, not feared as an emperor. Was wanting the wrong kind of greatness his real weakness — or just bad luck in who wrote about him?
  2. A simple legend ("he fiddled while Rome burned") has outlived the complicated truth by two thousand years. What does that tell us about how reputations survive?
  3. Today, leaders worry endlessly about their public image. Are we any better than the Romans at separating what a powerful person actually did from the story told about them?
