A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XV of L

The Garden

In which a gentle teacher buys a garden outside the walls of Athens and builds a community where the secret to happiness turns out to be astonishingly simple

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet Epicurus and the philosophy that bears his name — though not the one most people think of. Epicurus founded a school, wrote extensively, and offered a radical answer to the question: what is the highest good? His answer: pleasure. But the pleasure he meant was not what the word implies. It was the absence of pain and fear. This lesson plants the deepest seed in our entire course: a question about forgiveness and guilt that philosophy, for all its power, may never fully answer.

I. Before We Begin

Two questions about pleasure and friendship, before we meet a philosopher who will transform both.

What do you fear most?

Not physical pain or disease. But what do you lie awake worrying about? What would take happiness from you if it came true?

♦ ♦ ♦

A contrast to hold

Diogenes said: Strip away everything. Need nothing. You are free.

Epicurus will say something almost opposite: Seek pleasure, but understand what true pleasure is. Find friends. Build community. The highest good is friendship and the simplest pleasures shared with those you love.

Notice: both philosophers lived simply. Both rejected luxury. But for radically different reasons. Diogenes saw freedom as independence. Epicurus saw happiness as connection. What philosophy calls ataraxia (peace of mind) one philosopher found in solitude; the other found in a garden with friends.

II. The Story — The Man Who Built a Garden

A Birth on Samos

Epicurus was born in 341 BC on the island of Samos. He was not wealthy, but he was educated. His early teachers included followers of Plato and Democritus. The combination shaped him: he inherited Plato's concern for the good life, but Democritus's materialistic philosophy. Everything, Epicurus came to believe, was made of atoms. There was no divine plan, no cosmic purpose. The universe was atoms swerving in the void.

This could have led to despair. Instead, it liberated him. If there is no cosmic purpose, then the only purpose is the one we make. And the purpose he chose was simple: to remove fear and pain from human life.

From Samos to Athens

Epicurus taught in several cities before arriving in Athens around 307 BC. At 34 years old, he did something remarkable: he bought a garden just outside the city walls and founded a school there. Unlike Plato's Academy or Aristotle's Lyceum, Epicurus's Garden was a radical community.

Women were welcome. This was extraordinary. In most philosophical schools, women were excluded or tolerated only as companions. Epicurus welcomed them as genuine students.

Slaves were welcome. His follower Herodotus was a slave, and his philosophy was considered equal to that of free men.

Foreigners were welcome. There was no requirement for Athenian citizenship.

All were welcome. The only requirement was a shared commitment to the philosophical life and the search for happiness through the removal of fear.

The Philosophy: The Tetrapharmakos

Epicurus taught that human suffering comes from four sources of fear. He developed a cure for each. This was his tetrapharmakos — the fourfold medicine.

Fear of the gods must be cast out.
The fear of death must be overcome.
Pain is easy to endure.
What is good is easily obtained. — The Tetrapharmakos, Epicurus

Let's look at each cure:

1. Fear of the Gods Must Be Cast Out

Many people in antiquity were paralyzed by the fear that the gods would punish them. Epicurus did not say the gods don't exist. Rather, he said: the gods are blessed and immortal beings. They exist in perfect peace in the spaces between worlds. They have no concern for human affairs. They do not intervene, do not punish, do not reward. This was radically comforting: you are not being watched. You will not be punished for hidden sins. The universe is indifferent, and that indifference is freedom.

2. Fear of Death Must Be Overcome

Epicurus offered the symmetry argument. Before you were born, you did not exist, and you suffered not at all. After you die, you will not exist, and you will suffer not at all. So why fear a state you have already experienced? Death, when it comes, will not be present to you. You will not feel it. "When death is present, we are not; when we are present, death is not. So death is never present to us."

3. Pain is Easy to Endure

The body can experience pain, yes. But pain is usually acute and passes quickly. Chronic pain, Epicurus observed, tends to be bearable because the mind grows accustomed to it. And there are practices — friendship, philosophy, simple food, rest — that soothe pain. The person who fears pain too much will harm themselves far more than pain ever could.

4. What is Good is Easily Obtained

Here was Epicurus's revolutionary claim about pleasure. The highest goods are free: friendship, conversation, simple food, water, shelter. These are easily obtained. The expensive luxuries that people kill themselves for — fine wines, exotic foods, silks, jewels — these do not increase happiness. After a certain point, they actually decrease it, by creating new fears (fear of losing what you have, fear of looking poor, fear of being judged).

A critical misunderstanding: Later Christians and moralists attacked Epicureanism as hedonistic — devoted to excess pleasure. But this was a profound misreading. Epicurus actually preached a kind of asceticism. He recommended simple foods, not exotic feasts. A simple meal with friends, he said, is the highest pleasure. Fine food eaten alone is empty. Bread and water with genuine friends surpass a feast with those who resent you.

The Philosophical Method

What made Epicurus different from Diogenes was this: Diogenes achieved freedom through radical rejection. He said: cut everything away. But Epicurus achieved freedom through understanding. He wrote extensively — over 300 scrolls — to help people examine their desires and understand which ones were necessary and which were illusions. His students would memorize his key doctrines, not as dogma but as tools for thinking.

The Letter to Menoeceus, preserved by Diogenes Laërtius, is Epicurus at his best:

Cry out as you like, no torture will break our philosophical resolve. But let us always say that we are friends of gods and of human nature. — Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus

The Community and His Death

Epicurus lived in his Garden for the rest of his life, teaching, writing, and sharing meals with his community. He died in 270 BC at 71 years old, in great pain from a kidney stone. According to his student Hermarchus, his final day was one of joy, remembering the conversations and friendship he had shared.

Unlike Diogenes, Epicurus left behind a community that lasted. For centuries after his death, the Garden continued. His words were preserved and transmitted. And though his philosophy was grotesquely misrepresented by later critics, those who read him carefully found something revolutionary: a vision of happiness that was available to everyone, that didn't require wealth or power, and that was built on friendship.

♦ ♦ ♦

What neither Diogenes nor Epicurus solved: There is a silence in the Tetrapharmakos. It addresses four fears. But there is a fifth fear, perhaps the deepest, that neither philosopher explicitly names. What about guilt? What about the person who has genuinely wronged someone they love? What about the weight of that knowledge? The tetrapharmakos tells you: don't fear the gods' punishment — but what if you fear your own conscience? What if you have harmed someone and know it? No cure for that has been offered yet.

III. The Ideas — The Cure and Its Limits

Idea 1: Understanding Pleasure — The Hierarchy of Desires

Pleasure is the highest good, but most people misunderstand what pleasure is.

Epicurus distinguished between three kinds of desires:

Natural and necessary desires: Food when hungry, water when thirsty, shelter from the cold. These create genuine pleasure because they satisfy a real need. They are easily satisfied and should be pursued.

Natural but unnecessary desires: Fancy foods, fine wine, beautiful clothing. These satisfy natural urges (the body's desire for taste, comfort) but go beyond what's necessary. They are legitimate but dangerous because they create constant seeking, comparison, and lack of satisfaction.

Vain desires: Fame, wealth, power, status. These are based on opinion and illusion. They can never be satisfied — there is always more fame to pursue, more wealth to accumulate. And they are entirely unnecessary for happiness. The person enslaved to vain desires has enslaved themselves to an infinite master.

Epicurus recommended: pursue the necessary desires freely and happily. Be cautious with the natural but unnecessary ones. Abandon the vain desires entirely. This is the path to ataraxia — peace of mind.

Idea 2: Friendship as the Highest Good

Friendship is more valuable than pleasure itself.

This is remarkable: Epicurus, who made pleasure the highest good, then qualified it radically by saying that friendship matters more than any individual pleasure. His reasoning: true pleasure comes from simple things (food, rest, conversation). And these simple things are infinitely better when shared with genuine friends.

One of his most famous sayings: "Of all the things that wisdom provides for the blessedness of life, none is more important, more abundant, or more beautiful than friendship."

The Garden was built on this principle. It was not a place of indulgence but of genuine community, where people of all backgrounds gathered to examine life together and find peace.

Idea 3: Atomism with a Swerve — Freedom in a Material Universe

The universe is atoms and void, but atoms swerve. This is how freedom enters the material world.

Epicurus inherited Democritus's atomic theory: everything is made of atoms moving in empty space. But he made a crucial modification. Democritus believed atoms moved according to strict mechanical necessity. Epicurus added the clinamen — the swerve. Atoms sometimes, for no reason, deviate from their path and collide with other atoms, creating new combinations.

This tiny swerve was Epicurus's answer to determinism. In a purely mechanical universe, everything that happens was always going to happen. There is no freedom, no responsibility, no choice. But if atoms can swerve unpredictably, then the future is open. Choice is possible. You are not simply a mechanism playing out a predetermined program.

This is subtle but profound: Epicurus offers a way to be a materialist (everything is atoms) while preserving free will and moral responsibility.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Unresolved Question: The Cure That's Almost Complete

The Tetrapharmakos is brilliant. It removes four sources of suffering. It offers a vision of happiness that is available to everyone, built on friendship and simple pleasure. But notice what it does NOT address:

Think carefully before clicking: If I have genuinely wronged someone — if I have hurt, betrayed, or failed someone I love — can the Tetrapharmakos cure that pain? Can it make the guilt go away?

Epicurus tells me: don't fear the gods' punishment. But I am not afraid of divine punishment. I am afraid of the truth — that I have done wrong. I carry the weight of it.

He tells me: pain is easy to endure. But this is not the pain of the body. This is the pain of conscience.

He tells me: find friendship, simple pleasure, peace of mind. But what if every time I sit with the friend I've wronged, I see the harm in their eyes? What if simple pleasure feels like betrayal?

The Tetrapharmakos cures anxiety, fear, and unnecessary desire. But it does not cure guilt. It does not offer forgiveness. And that absence — that silence where an answer should be — is the deepest unresolved question in philosophy so far.

This is where the course is going. We have asked: what is everything made of? How should we live? How do we achieve freedom? How do we find happiness? But the question that haunts us — that cannot be answered by reason alone, by atoms and void, by friendship and simple food — is: can we be forgiven? Can guilt be lifted? Can we be made clean?

The 5 Images for Location 15

2. A medicine cabinet with four bottles — Each labelled with one of the Tetrapharmakos: "Fear of Gods," "Fear of Death," "Pain," "Vain Desires." Each cure glows softly. This is Epicurus's answer to suffering.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2-3 of these questions to reflect on.

The misunderstanding of Epicureanism

Why was Epicurus so misunderstood by later generations? He preached simplicity, yet his name became synonymous with excess. What does this tell us about how ideas survive through time?

The Tetrapharmakos

Which of the four cures seems most powerful to you? Which seems most incomplete? Why?

The hierarchy of desires

Epicurus says vain desires are endless — there's always more wealth, more fame, more status. Is this true? Have you experienced this trap?

Friendship and pleasure

Epicurus says friendship is the highest good, higher than pleasure itself. But then he says friendship amplifies simple pleasure. Isn't pleasure still primary? Or is he saying something else?

The radical community

Epicurus welcomed women, slaves, and foreigners equally. This was revolutionary. What does his garden tell us about the society he was critiquing?

The fifth fear

What is the fear that the Tetrapharmakos does not address? Have you felt it yourself — the weight of having wronged someone, the desire for forgiveness?
