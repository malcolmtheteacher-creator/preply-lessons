The Great Depression

How the richest decade the world had ever known collapsed into the worst slump in modern history.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of a boom that turned to bust, and discuss how a whole world can lose its confidence at once — using the language of money, hardship and recovery.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The banks called it a boom: factories were busy, wages were rising, and almost everyone believed the good years would simply never end.
a boom (n) — a period of fast economic growth and rising prosperity.
2. Ordinary people poured their savings into wild speculation, gambling on share prices in the hope of getting rich without really working.
speculation (n) — buying something risky in the hope its price will rise and make you a quick profit.
3. In a single terrible week the market crashed, and fortunes that had taken years to build vanished in a matter of hours.
to crash (v) — (of a market) to fall suddenly and very steeply in value.
4. Frightened customers rushed to take out their money, and bank after bank collapsed, unable to give everyone back what they were owed.
to collapse (v) — to fail suddenly and completely; to fall apart.
5. A long, deep economic slump set in, in which trade shrank, businesses closed, and the whole system seemed stuck in the mud for years.
a slump (n) — a long period of serious economic decline and low activity.
6. Across the country, unemployment climbed until almost one worker in four had no job at all and no wage to bring home.
unemployment (n) — the state of having no paid work; the number of people without jobs.
7. With no income, families were thrown into destitution — losing their homes, queuing for free bread, and owning almost nothing.
destitution (n) — extreme poverty; the state of lacking even the basic necessities of life.
8. On the plains a terrible drought killed the crops; for season after season no rain fell, and the dry soil simply blew away on the wind.
a drought (n) — a long period with little or no rain, often ruining crops.
9. A bold new government launched a vast programme of relief, paying for public works so that the jobless could at least earn again.
relief (n) — money, food or work given by the state to help people in hardship.
10. Only slowly, and helped at last by the demands of war, did the economy begin its long recovery back towards health.
a recovery (n) — a return to a normal or healthy condition after a period of difficulty.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The boom that couldn't last

The 1920s in America were a decade of dizzying confidence. Factories hummed, new cars and radios poured off the assembly lines, and for the first time millions of ordinary people felt genuinely modern and prosperous. People called it the Roaring Twenties, and at its glittering heart sat the stock market, where share prices climbed year after year as if pulled up by some invisible hand.

The trouble was that much of this rise rested on speculation rather than real value. Under the loose rules of the day, an investor could buy shares "on margin" — paying as little as a tenth of the price in cash and borrowing the rest. As long as prices kept rising, everyone made money on paper, so more and more people borrowed more and more to buy. By 1929 the amount lent out for share-buying was greater than all the cash circulating in the entire country. It was a tower built on debt, and everyone simply assumed it would keep growing.

Then, in late October 1929, the confidence cracked. On Thursday 24 October, panic selling swept the New York Stock Exchange; the following week, on Monday the 28th and the infamous "Black Tuesday" of the 29th, the market fell off a cliff, with some sixteen million shares dumped in a single frantic day. Billions of dollars of value simply evaporated, and thousands of investors were ruined overnight. The Wall Street Crash had begun, and the long fall would not end until the market had lost roughly nine-tenths of its value.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does it mean to buy shares "on margin," and why did it make the boom so dangerous?

2. What happened on the New York Stock Exchange in late October 1929?

1. It meant paying only a small fraction of a share's price in cash and borrowing the rest. It was dangerous because the whole market was built on borrowed money, so once prices fell, that debt turned a fall into a collapse.

2. The market crashed: waves of panic selling, climaxing on "Black Tuesday" (29 October) with about sixteen million shares sold, wiped out billions in value and ruined thousands of investors.

Discuss:
  • Almost everyone believed the boom would last forever, even as it grew more and more reckless. Why do people so often refuse to see a bubble while they are inside it?

Part 2 — The years of hardship

A stock-market crash need not destroy an economy — but this one tore through a financial system that was already fragile. As share values vanished, frightened customers rushed to pull their savings out of the banks, and the banks, unable to pay everyone at once, began to fail. Thousands of them collapsed in the early 1930s, and with them went the life savings of millions of ordinary families. Money grew scarce, businesses cut back or shut their doors, and the more people lost their jobs, the less they could buy — which threw still more people out of work. A vicious circle had set in, and the country sank into a deep and lasting slump.

At its worst, around 1933, roughly a quarter of American workers had no job at all. With no wages and no welfare state to catch them, families slid into destitution. People queued for hours at soup kitchens and breadlines for a free meal; those who lost their homes built shanty towns out of cardboard, scrap wood and tin on the edges of cities, and named them "Hoovervilles" after the president many blamed for their misery. As if the human disaster were not enough, nature added its own: across the southern plains a years-long drought struck farmland that had been ploughed too hard, and the bare topsoil lifted into vast black dust storms. This "Dust Bowl" drove hundreds of thousands of ruined farmers westward in search of any work they could find.

And the catastrophe did not stay in America. The world's economies were tied together by trade and by loans, and as American money was pulled home, the crisis spread across the Atlantic. By 1931 Germany and Austria were in the grip of their own banking collapse, and in Germany unemployment climbed towards six million. Britain, France and much of the world followed the United States down. By 1932 perhaps thirty million people were out of work worldwide — and in some countries the despair would feed something far darker than poverty.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the banking collapse turn a market crash into a long, deep depression?

2. What were "Hoovervilles" and the "Dust Bowl," and how did the crisis spread beyond America?

1. When banks failed, millions lost their savings and money grew scarce; businesses closed and jobs vanished, so people bought less, which destroyed still more jobs — a vicious circle that deepened into a lasting slump.

2. "Hoovervilles" were shanty towns of cardboard and scrap built by the homeless; the "Dust Bowl" was a drought that turned over-farmed plains into dust storms. The crisis spread through trade and loans, hitting Germany, Austria, Britain and much of the world, with around thirty million unemployed worldwide by 1932.

Discuss:
  • The Depression was not just an economic event but a human one — hunger, lost homes, broken pride. Which of these images stays with you most, and why?

Part 3 — The long road back

In 1933, with the banks shut and a quarter of the workforce idle, a new president took office: Franklin Delano Roosevelt. He had promised Americans "a new deal," and in his first hundred days he moved at astonishing speed — steadying the banks, then launching a flood of programmes aimed at three things he called relief, recovery and reform. The government itself became an employer, paying millions of jobless people to build roads, bridges, dams, schools and parks. Just as importantly, Roosevelt set out to restore something less tangible but just as vital: confidence, telling the frightened nation that "the only thing we have to fear is fear itself."

The New Deal did not cure the Depression on its own. It eased the worst of the suffering and rebuilt a sense of hope, but unemployment stayed painfully high for years, and the economy crept rather than sprang back. The slump had run so deep that no single set of policies could simply lift it out.

What finally ended the Great Depression was, grimly, the approach of another catastrophe. As the world slid towards the Second World War, factories roared back to life to build ships, planes, guns and supplies, and millions of men were drafted into the armed forces. After the United States entered the war at the end of 1941, the jobs returned in their millions and the long recovery was at last complete. The decade had carried a hard lesson out of the ruins: that prosperity built on debt and blind confidence can vanish overnight, and that, when it does, it is ordinary people who pay the price.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the New Deal, and what did it actually achieve — and not achieve?

2. What finally brought the Great Depression to an end?

1. Roosevelt's programme of relief, recovery and reform: it steadied the banks, put millions to work on public projects and restored hope, but it did not end the Depression — unemployment stayed high and recovery was slow.

2. The Second World War: as the world prepared for and entered the war, demand for ships, weapons and supplies revived industry and the armed forces absorbed millions, completing the recovery after the US entered the war in late 1941.

Discuss:
  • It took a world war, not a clever policy, to finally end the Depression. What does that suggest about how hard it really is to pull an economy out of a deep slump?

The grammar this story runs on: the future in the past

This is a story full of people who were sure about a future that never came. To capture that — what someone, in the past, expected to happen next — English uses "the future in the past." It is the perfect tool for the false confidence of the 1920s.

The two main tools:
• would + verb — for a prediction or belief made in the past: "investors were sure prices would keep rising."
• was/were going to + verb — for a plan or expectation held in the past: "everyone thought the good times were going to last forever."
The quiet power of both is dramatic irony: we know the future they were so sure of never arrived.

1. "would" — the belief that proved wrong

Use would + verb for what people in the past predicted or felt certain about.

1. Most investors were convinced that the market (never / fall).

2. The banks promised their customers that the savings (be) completely safe.

3. Few people imagined that the crash (destroy) so many lives.

1. would never fall

2. would be

3. would destroy

2. "was/were going to" — the plan the future broke

Use was/were going to + verb for what people expected or intended — especially when it didn't come true.

1. Everyone thought the good times (last) forever.

2. Many families believed their savings (pay) for a comfortable old age.

3. The farmers were sure the next season (bring) rain at last.

1. were going to last

2. were going to pay

3. was going to bring

3. Put them together — narrate the false dawn

Fill each gap with the future-in-the-past form the meaning needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

In the summer of 1929, no one doubted that prices (keep) climbing. Brokers told nervous clients the boom (go on) for years, and young couples were certain they (be) rich. They had no idea that within months the market (collapse), or that the slump (last) a whole decade.

would keep · was going to go on · were going to be · would collapse · would last

Notice the dramatic irony: every confident prediction here was about to be proved completely wrong.

This is the future-in-the-past — the same backbone you'll use to tell any "they were so sure, and then…" story in the Speak tab.
