My Financial Career

Stephen Leacock (1910) — a young man with fifty-six dollars walks into a bank to open an account, and walks out with it all in his pocket.

Today: you'll tell a very short, very funny story of panic, and use the language of degree and result — too … to, so … that, such … that, enough — to say exactly how nervous someone was and what it made them do.
Before we read:
  • Think of a place or situation where you suddenly lose all your confidence — a bank, a doctor’s office, a passport desk, a job interview. What happens to your voice and your behaviour?
  • Why do formal places with serious people and official forms make some people behave so oddly? What is it about the setting?

Key words — think first, then check

Before we read, let’s unlock the words you’ll need. Think first — the Reveal only confirms.

A. Work it out from the sentence

Read each sentence aloud and say what the bold word means from the rest of the sentence. Then reveal.

1. The interview went badly: I was so rattled by the first question that I forgot my own name.
rattled (adj) — nervous, shaken, unable to think clearly. Example: “The loud question left her completely rattled.”
2. She asked the question in such a timid voice that nobody at the back of the hall could hear a word.
timid (adj) — shy and nervous; easily frightened. (adv: timidly) Example: “He knocked timidly and waited.”
3. “I have something terrible to tell you,” he said in a deep, sepulchral voice, as if he were speaking from inside a tomb.
sepulchral (adj) — gloomy and deep, like a voice from a grave. Example: “The sepulchral silence of the empty church.”
4. In old banks the clerks sat behind a little window called a wicket, and you had to push your money through a slot.
wicket (n) — a small window or opening in a counter, door or gate. In the story, the clerk’s window at the bank.
5. He pushed the letter across the desk with a quick, convulsive movement, as if it were burning his fingers.
convulsive (adj) — sudden, jerky and uncontrolled. Example: “A convulsive laugh shook her shoulders.”
6. After the news she looked ghastly — white as paper, with dark circles under her eyes.
ghastly (adj) — horrible; looking very pale and ill. Example: “He had a ghastly shock.”

B. From the meaning to the word

Which word fits the definition? The first letter is given. Say the word, then reveal.

7. To put money into a bank account. It begins with d.
deposit (v, n) — to put money into an account (or the money you put in). Example: “She deposited her wages every Friday.”
8. The opposite of that: to take money out of your account. It begins with w.
withdraw (v) — to take money out of an account. (n: a withdrawal) Example: “You can withdraw up to two hundred a day.”
9. Acting without caring about the danger or the result — as in “______ with misery, I made a plunge.” It begins with r.
reckless (adj) — careless about danger or consequences. Example: “A reckless decision that cost him his savings.”
10. Very surprised, usually by something you did not expect: “‘Are you drawing it all out again?’ asked the clerk, ______.” It begins with a.
astonished (adj) — extremely surprised. (v: astonish; n: astonishment) Example: “She was astonished to see him.”

The story, in three parts

Stephen Leacock was a professor of economics who wrote some of the funniest short pieces in English. This is the whole of ‘My Financial Career’, in three parts. It is told by a young man who has just had his salary raised — and who is about to make the worst bank deposit in history.

Part 1 — The man who asked to see the manager ‘alone’

When I go into a bank I get rattled. The clerks rattle me; the wickets rattle me; the sight of the money rattles me; everything rattles me.

The moment I cross the threshold of a bank and attempt to transact business there, I become an irresponsible idiot.

I knew this beforehand, but my salary had been raised to fifty dollars a month and I felt that the bank was the only place for it.

So I shambled in and looked timidly round at the clerks. I had an idea that a person about to open an account must needs consult the manager.

I went up to a wicket marked "Accountant." The accountant was a tall, cool devil. The very sight of him rattled me. My voice was sepulchral.

"Can I see the manager?" I said, and added solemnly, "alone." I don't know why I said "alone."

"Certainly," said the accountant, and fetched him.

The manager was a grave, calm man. I held my fifty-six dollars clutched in a crumpled ball in my pocket.

"Are you the manager?" I said. God knows I didn't doubt it.

"Yes," he said.

"Can I see you," I asked, "alone?" I didn't want to say "alone" again, but without it the thing seemed self-evident.

The manager looked at me in some alarm. He felt that I had an awful secret to reveal.

"Come in here," he said, and led the way to a private room. He turned the key in the lock.

"We are safe from interruption here," he said; "sit down."

We both sat down and looked at each other. I found no voice to speak.

"You are one of Pinkerton's men, I presume," he said.

He had gathered from my mysterious manner that I was a detective. I knew what he was thinking, and it made me worse.

"No, not from Pinkerton's," I said, seeming to imply that I came from a rival agency.

"To tell the truth," I went on, as if I had been prompted to lie about it, "I am not a detective at all. I have come to open an account. I intend to keep all my money in this bank."

The manager looked relieved but still serious; he concluded now that I was a son of Baron Rothschild or a young Gould.

"A large account, I suppose," he said.

"Fairly large," I whispered. "I propose to deposit fifty-six dollars now and fifty dollars a month regularly."

The manager got up and opened the door. He called to the accountant.

"Mr. Montgomery," he said unkindly loud, "this gentleman is opening an account, he will deposit fifty-six dollars. Good morning."

I rose.

A big iron door stood open at the side of the room.

"Good morning," I said, and stepped into the safe.

"Come out," said the manager coldly, and showed me the other way.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does the narrator go to the bank, and what does he think he must do first?

2. What does the manager think the narrator is — and what does the narrator do that makes things worse?

1. His salary has been raised to fifty dollars a month, so he feels the bank is the only place for it. He thinks that anyone opening an account must first consult the manager.

2. He thinks he is a detective (‘one of Pinkerton’s men’), then a rich young heir. The narrator makes it worse by seeming to say he is from a rival agency, and then by stepping into the bank’s safe instead of the door.

Discuss:
  • The narrator says ‘alone’ twice — ‘I don’t know why I said alone.’ When people are nervous, they often add something unnecessary or strange. Why is this so funny, and where do you recognise it in real life?

Part 2 — The deposit and the cheque

I went up to the accountant's wicket and poked the ball of money at him with a quick convulsive movement as if I were doing a conjuring trick.

My face was ghastly pale.

"Here," I said, "deposit it." The tone of the words seemed to mean, "Let us do this painful thing while the fit is on us."

He took the money and gave it to another clerk.

He made me write the sum on a slip and sign my name in a book. I no longer knew what I was doing. The bank swam before my eyes.

"Is it deposited?" I asked in a hollow, vibrating voice.

"It is," said the accountant.

"Then I want to draw a cheque."

My idea was to draw out six dollars of it for present use. Someone gave me a chequebook through a wicket and someone else began telling me how to write it out. The people in the bank had the impression that I was an invalid millionaire. I wrote something on the cheque and thrust it in at the clerk. He looked at it.

"What! are you drawing it all out again?" he asked in surprise. Then I realized that I had written fifty-six instead of six. I was too far gone to reason now. I had a feeling that it was impossible to explain the thing. All the clerks had stopped writing to look at me.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does the narrator hand over his money, and how does he sound?

2. What is the mistake on the cheque, and why is the clerk astonished?

1. He pokes the crumpled ball of notes at the clerk with a quick, convulsive movement, as if doing a conjuring trick — pale, with a hollow, vibrating voice, as if the deposit were a painful thing to be done ‘while the fit is on us’.

2. He meant to draw six dollars for present use, but wrote fifty-six — the whole amount. The clerk is astonished that a man has just deposited his money and is already taking it all out again.

Discuss:
  • ‘The people in the bank had the impression that I was an invalid millionaire.’ Why do the staff keep misreading him? What does that tell us about how people interpret odd behaviour in a position of authority?

Part 3 — ‘In fifties’ and ‘In sixes’

Reckless with misery, I made a plunge.

"Yes, the whole thing."

"You withdraw your money from the bank?"

"Every cent of it."

"Are you not going to deposit any more?" said the clerk, astonished.

"Never."

An idiot hope struck me that they might think something had insulted me while I was writing the cheque and that I had changed my mind. I made a wretched attempt to look like a man with a fearfully quick temper.

The clerk prepared to pay the money.

"How will you have it?" he said.

"What?"

"How will you have it?"

"Oh"--I caught his meaning and answered without even trying to think--"in fifties."

He gave me a fifty-dollar bill.

"And the six?" he asked dryly.

"In sixes," I said.

He gave it me and I rushed out.

As the big door swung behind me I caught the echo of a roar of laughter that went up to the ceiling of the bank. Since then I bank no more. I keep my money in cash in my trousers pocket and my savings in silver dollars in a sock.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the narrator say when the clerk asks if he is going to deposit any more? Why?

2. Where does the narrator keep his money now?

1. ‘Never.’ He is too embarrassed to explain the mistake, and has a mad hope that the clerks will think something upset him while he was writing the cheque and that he has changed his mind.

2. In cash in his trouser pocket, with his savings in silver dollars in a sock — he no longer uses banks at all.

Discuss:
  • The whole story is a series of tiny decisions that each make sense in the moment and are ridiculous together. At which point could the narrator have stopped the disaster — and why does he not?

The grammar this story runs on: degree and result

Everything in the story is a matter of degree: so nervous, so far gone, too frightened to explain. Leacock never says ‘he was nervous’ and leaves it there — he tells us how nervous, and what it made him do. That is the work of degree and result structures.

1. too + adjective + to-infinitive; adjective + enough + to-infinitive.
• “I was too far gone to reason now.” • “He was too embarrassed to explain.” • “She wasn’t calm enough to count the money.”
Too means more than is good — the result is that something cannot happen.
2. so + adjective + that-clause; such + (a/an) + adjective + noun + that-clause.
• “I was so rattled that I forgot my name.” • “It was such a strange request that the manager locked the door.”
Use so before an adjective or adverb; use such before a noun phrase. In careful writing you can also invert for emphasis: “So nervous was he that he stepped into the safe.”

Why this story is funnier than it should be

Stephen Leacock (1869–1944) taught economics at McGill University in Montreal and, in his spare time, became the most popular humorist in the English-speaking world. He worked for a time as a bank clerk’s nightmare: a man who understood money in theory and froze in front of it in practice. ‘My Financial Career’ is only a page or two long, and yet it contains a complete comedy: a goal, an obstacle, an escalation, a catastrophe and a punchline.

The comedy works through misunderstanding. The narrator is behaving like a man who is terrified; everyone in the bank sees a man who is dangerous, secret, rich or mad. Each person invents a story for him — detective, millionaire, invalid — and he cannot correct it, because correcting it would take courage he doesn’t have. The reader sees both versions at once. That gap between what he feels and what they see is what makes the story funny, and, underneath, tender.

Look again at the last line. ‘I keep my money in cash in my trousers pocket and my savings in silver dollars in a sock.’ It is a joke — but notice that it is also the worst possible financial advice. The story invites us to laugh at him and to recognise ourselves at the same time.

Go deeper — talk it through

Discuss:
  • The manager and clerks are never unkind. Is anyone to blame for the disaster, or is it simply an accident of temperament? What would a kind bank clerk have done at the cheque?
  • Nervousness is rarely invisible. What do you do to look calm when you aren’t — and does it ever make things worse?
  • Leacock wrote this in 1910. Would the story still work in an online bank, with no manager, no wicket and no clerks? What would the modern equivalent of the panic be?

Task B · Your own moment of panic (4 minutes)

Tell a true (or nearly true) story of a time you were so nervous that you did something silly in a formal place: a bank, an interview, an airport, a classroom, a government office. Build it with the same shape: what you wanted → how you felt → what you did → how people reacted → how it ended.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Opening an account, renting a flat, or applying for a visa — and getting something wrong.
  • Speaking to someone important and saying the opposite of what you meant.
  • A time you agreed to something you didn’t want, because it was too awkward to say no.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Is it better to hide nervousness or to admit it? What would have happened if the narrator had simply said, ‘I’m nervous — I’ve never opened an account before’?
  2. Why do institutions — banks, offices, government departments — so often make ordinary people feel small? Can a service be designed to be less frightening?
  3. Leacock turns embarrassment into comedy. Which of your own embarrassing moments have you been able to laugh about — and how long did it take?
