The Notebooks Too Dangerous to Touch

Marie Curie won two Nobel Prizes, worked from a leaking shed, and left behind laboratory notebooks that will stay radioactive for another thousand years.

Today: you'll follow one of the most extraordinary lives in science — and you'll master the tenses that carry any life story: she was working in a shed when the discovery came; by 1911 she had won two Nobels.

Discuss:

  • What obstacles have been placed in front of scientists because of who they were, rather than what they knew?
  • Is there research so important that the personal risk is worth it? Who should decide?

The vocabulary of this story

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Uranium gave off invisible rays without any energy source — energy pouring silently out of matter itself. Marie named the phenomenon radioactivity.
radioactivity (n) — the spontaneous emission of radiation from unstable atoms. Adjective: radioactive. She coined the word itself — the story starts with naming the invisible.
2. The pitchblende was more radioactive than its uranium could explain. Something unknown was hiding in it — a new element.
an element (n) — a basic substance of chemistry: iron, oxygen, uranium… She found two new ones in a year, and named the first for her occupied homeland.
3. To prove the new element existed, she had to extract it — tonne by tonne, from industrial waste, by hand.
to extract (v) — to remove a substance from the material containing it. Noun: extraction. Four years of stirring produced one tenth of a gram.

B · Which word means…?

Three words: a shed · exposure · a pioneer. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which word means a simple wooden building for storage or rough work?
a shed (n) — the famous laboratory: a leaking former dissection room a visiting chemist compared to "a cross between a stable and a potato cellar". Two Nobel Prizes came out of it.
2. Which word means the state of being unprotected from something harmful?
exposure (n) — exposure to radiation / chemicals / risk. Verb: to be exposed to. Nobody knew the danger; the exposure was total and lifelong.
3. Which word means one of the first people to enter a new field, opening it for others?
a pioneer (n/v) — a pioneer of radiochemistry; to pioneer a technique; pioneering work. She was a pioneer twice over: in the science, and in every institution that had never admitted a woman.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Warsaw's universities were closed to women, so she studied in a secret, illegal network — the "Flying University" — before emigrating. Her education was forbidden, meaning…
  (a) expensive   (b) not allowed by the authorities   (c) incomplete
(b) — forbidden (adj). Poland was then partitioned under Russian rule; higher education for women was banned, so it moved underground and kept moving its address — hence "flying".
2. She refused to patent the radium process, insisting the element belonged to science. She and Pierre gave up a fortune, meaning…
  (a) a large amount of money   (b) a prize   (c) a reputation
(a) — a fortune (n) — radium became, gram for gram, one of the most valuable substances on earth; years later she could not afford a gram of her own element, and American women raised the money to buy her one.

D · Word building & collocation

1. You know to achieve. Build the family: a remarkable ___ (noun) · her list of "firsts" remains ___ (negative adjective: nobody has equalled it).
An achievement · unequalled / unmatched. Her firsts: first woman with a Nobel, first person with two, only person with Nobels in two different sciences, first female professor at the Sorbonne.
2. One verb completes all three: to ___ a prize · to ___ recognition · her work finally ___ her a place in the Academy — no, wait: the Academy rejected her. Which verb, and what does the rejection tell you?
to win a prize / recognition — or more formally to earn. The French Academy of Sciences rejected her by two votes in 1911 — weeks before her second Nobel. Prizes could be won; some doors stayed shut regardless.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — The student who was not allowed to study

Maria Skłodowska was born in Warsaw in 1867, in a Poland that did not officially exist — partitioned between empires, its language suppressed, its universities closed to women. She got her forbidden education anyway, through the "Flying University", an illegal network of classes that met in private apartments and changed address constantly to stay ahead of the police. For years she worked as a governess to pay for her sister's medical studies in Paris — the sisters had made a deal: first Bronia would study while Maria earned, then they would swap. In 1891, aged twenty-four, she finally boarded the train to Paris, enrolled at the Sorbonne as one of a handful of women among thousands of men, and lived in an unheated attic where, one famous winter, she fainted from hunger during her studies. She finished first in her class in physics.

She intended to return to Poland. A Polish university turned her down — a woman — and Paris held other attractions by then: Pierre Curie, a brilliant, unworldly physicist who courted her with a copy of his research papers and the argument that they could share a life "in a scientific dream". They married in 1895; she wore a dark dress she could use afterwards in the laboratory. When she needed a doctoral subject, she chose the strangest result in recent physics: Henri Becquerel had found that uranium salts fogged photographic plates through black paper — invisible rays, coming from nowhere, powered by nothing anyone could name.

Her first great insight was quiet and enormous: measuring precisely, she showed the radiation depended only on the amount of uranium — not its chemistry, not its temperature, not anything done to it. The energy was coming from inside the atoms themselves. She gave the phenomenon its name, radioactivity, and then noticed something stranger: pitchblende, the ore uranium came from, was far more radioactive than its uranium content allowed. Hidden in the ore was something new — and she was going to find it.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What obstacles stood between Maria and an education, and how did she get one anyway?

2. What was her first great insight about the uranium rays?

1. Occupied Poland barred women from university, so she studied illegally in the "Flying University", worked years as a governess to fund her sister first, then reached the Sorbonne at 24 — studying in poverty, and finishing first in physics.

2. That the radiation depended only on the quantity of uranium, unaffected by chemistry or conditions — so the energy came from inside the atom itself. She named the phenomenon radioactivity, then spotted that pitchblende was too radioactive for its uranium: something new was hidden in it.

Part 2 — Four years in the shed

Finding the hidden elements meant separating them from tonnes of ore, and no proper laboratory was offered. The Curies were given a leaking wooden shed — a former dissection room that a visiting chemist described as a cross between a stable and a potato cellar. Into its courtyard came sacks of pitchblende waste from Austrian mines, and for four years Marie did the work of a factory: dissolving, boiling, filtering, crystallising, stirring vats with an iron rod nearly as tall as herself. In 1898 the couple announced two new elements — polonium, named defiantly after her occupied homeland, and radium, "the shining one". But announcing is not proving: chemistry demanded a visible, weighable sample. In 1902, from roughly a tonne of ore, she finally held one tenth of a gram of radium chloride — enough to enter the element in the periodic table beyond argument. The shed, meanwhile, had become quietly magical: the residues on the benches glowed in the dark, and Pierre and Marie would return at night to look at their faintly shining jars — carrying, though they could not know it, the poison in their pockets.

Recognition arrived almost without her. When the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics was being arranged for Becquerel and Pierre, Marie was simply left off — until Pierre, warned by a sympathetic Swedish mathematician, insisted her name be added. She became the first woman to win a Nobel Prize; the committee's president still quoted scripture about man's helpmate in his speech. Fame brought a professorship for Pierre and, for the family, a strange new life in which radium was a sensation — luminous watches, radium tonics, radium beauty creams — while its discoverers, who had refused on principle to patent the extraction process, remained comparatively poor and increasingly, mysteriously, unwell.

Then, on a rainy April afternoon in 1906, Pierre — weakened, and absent-minded as ever — slipped crossing the rue Dauphine and fell under a horse-drawn cart. He died instantly. Marie's diary entries from the weeks after are almost unbearable to read; her response in public was granite. Offered a widow's pension, she refused it. Offered, instead, Pierre's chair at the Sorbonne, she accepted — the first woman ever to teach there — and began her first lecture, before a packed and weeping hall, at the exact sentence of the physics course where Pierre had stopped.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did proving radium's existence actually require, physically?

2. How did she nearly miss the 1903 Nobel, and what happened in 1906?

1. Four years of industrial-scale chemistry by hand in a leaking shed — boiling and stirring tonnes of pitchblende waste — to extract one tenth of a gram of radium chloride from about a tonne of ore.

2. The prize was being arranged for Becquerel and Pierre only; Pierre, tipped off, insisted Marie be included — making her the first female laureate. In 1906 Pierre was killed by a cart in the street; Marie refused a pension, took over his professorship, and resumed his course at the exact sentence where he had stopped.

Discuss:
  • Refusing the patent kept radium free for science and kept its discoverers poor. Was that principle right — and would you have held it?

Part 3 — The scandal, the war, and the price

1911 delivered the cruellest month of her public life and its greatest honour almost simultaneously. The French press discovered her relationship with the married physicist Paul Langevin and detonated: she was attacked as a foreigner, a home-wrecker, and — in the venomous press of the day — worse; a mob gathered outside her house; Langevin fought a duel over the affair (both men, being physicists, fired into the air, or not at all — accounts differ). In the middle of the storm, Stockholm announced her second Nobel Prize, this time in Chemistry, for radium and polonium — and a committee member wrote suggesting she stay away from the ceremony given the scandal. Her reply is one of the great sentences of scientific dignity: the prize, she wrote, was for the discovery of radium and polonium, and there was no connection between her scientific work and the facts of her private life. She went, and collected it. No one else, before or since, has won Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

When war came in 1914, she converted fame into logistics. Reasoning that a wounded soldier X-rayed at the front need not be X-rayed on an operating table hours later, she created mobile radiography cars — "petites Curies" — learned to drive, drove one herself at the front, trained around 150 women as operators, and equipped hundreds of radiological stations. By war's end, over a million wounded men had been X-rayed by her service. She attempted to donate her Nobel medals to the war effort; the Bank of France declined to melt them.

The bill arrived slowly. Decades of unshielded exposure — she had carried test tubes of radium in her pockets and kept a glowing sample by her bed — destroyed her blood, and in 1934 she died of aplastic anaemia, almost certainly caused by her own element. She was buried with Pierre; in 1995 France transferred them both to the Panthéon, making her the first woman honoured there for her own achievements — her coffin lined with lead. And in the Bibliothèque nationale, her laboratory notebooks survive: still radioactive, stored in lead boxes, consultable only by readers who sign a waiver and wear protection. Radium-226 has a half-life of 1,600 years. Her handwriting will outlive every institution that once refused her a place.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What collided in 1911, and how did she answer the suggestion to stay away?

2. What did she build in the war, and what is the meaning of the lead-lined coffin and notebooks?

1. A press scandal over her private life — xenophobic and vicious, with a mob at her house — and, the same weeks, her second Nobel. Advised to stay away, she replied that the prize was for radium and polonium and her private life had no connection to her science. She attended.

2. Mobile X-ray cars she designed, drove and staffed with 150 women she trained — over a million soldiers X-rayed. She died in 1934 of radiation-caused anaemia; her notebooks remain radioactive for centuries and her Panthéon coffin is lined with lead — the achievement and its price, stored together.

Discuss:
  • "No connection between my scientific work and my private life." The press disagreed then, and modern media disagree still. Who is right?

Grammar — narrative tenses for a life story

A biography moves constantly between events, backgrounds and things that had already happened. Three past tenses do all of that work — and telling a life well is knowing which one carries each sentence.

The three tools:
• Past simple — the events, in order: "She arrived in Paris in 1891 and enrolled at the Sorbonne."
• Past continuous — the background, interrupted: "While she was working as a governess, her sister was studying in Paris." · "Pierre was crossing the street when the cart hit him."
• Past perfect — earlier than the story's moment: "By 1902 she had extracted a tenth of a gram." · "The committee had already decided the names when Pierre intervened."
The biography patterns:
• By + date + past perfect — the milestone sentence: "By 1911 she had won two Nobel Prizes."
• When/while + continuous, + simple — the interruption: "While the press was attacking her, Stockholm announced the prize."
• after / before / as soon as + simple or perfect: "After Pierre died, she took his chair."
• Duration in the past: "She stirred those vats for four years." (simple + for, because the period is closed)

The classic error: using the continuous for completed achievements — ✗ "She was winning the Nobel in 1903" → won.

1. Choose the tense

Put the verbs in brackets into the past simple, continuous or perfect.

1. She (study) secretly in Warsaw before she (move) to Paris.

2. Pierre (cross) the rue Dauphine when the cart (strike) him.

3. By the time the scandal broke, she (already / earn) her second Nobel.

1. had studied / moved (or: studied / moved — the perfect sharpens the sequence)   2. was crossing / struck   3. had already earned

2. Build the milestone sentences

Combine each pair into one sentence using the pattern given. Write the full sentence.

1. 1902 arrives. Before that: extraction of the radium sample. (By 1902…) →

2. The war years: she drives an X-ray car; at the same time she trains women operators. (While…) →

3. Pierre dies. Immediately afterwards: she refuses the pension and takes his chair. (After…) →

1. By 1902 she had extracted the radium sample.

2. While she was driving an X-ray car at the front, she was also training women as operators.

3. After Pierre died, she refused the pension and took his chair at the Sorbonne.

3. The obituary paragraph — complete it

Fill each gap with the right tense, then read the whole paragraph aloud as a broadcast obituary.

Marie Curie, who (die) yesterday, (change) science twice before her fortieth birthday. She (grow up) in an occupied country that (forbid) women to study, and she (work) as a governess for years before Paris. While the world (buy) radium face creams, she (refuse) to patent the element she (spend) four years extracting. By her death she (win) two Nobel Prizes, (equip) hundreds of wartime X-ray stations, and (train) a generation of scientists — including a daughter who (soon / win) a Nobel of her own.

died · had changed · grew up / had grown up · forbade · worked / had worked · was buying · refused / had refused · had spent · had won · had equipped · had trained · would soon win

Note the last one — "would soon win" — the future seen from the past, the obituary writer's signature move. (Irène Joliot-Curie's Nobel came in 1935, one year after her mother's death.)

Going deeper

A note on the record: the fainting student, the glowing shed and the duel are documented but legend-polished; the diary after Pierre's death is published and exactly as painful as described; the notebooks really are in lead boxes, and the waiver is real.

What the story actually teaches. First, the economics of exclusion. Every barrier in this life — the closed universities, the omitted Nobel nomination, the rejected Academy seat, the "stay away from Stockholm" letter — was constructed by institutions convinced they were maintaining standards. The cost of that conviction is measurable: how many Curies did the nineteenth century simply not receive? Exclusion never presents itself as waste; it always presents itself as quality control. That is its camouflage, then and now.

Second, the shed as a warning to funders. The most productive laboratory in Europe was a leaking dissection room, which is romantic exactly once. The lesson is not that genius needs poverty — the Curies begged for better and were refused — but that institutions systematically misjudge where the important work is happening, because importance is only visible afterwards. Judge a proposal by its questions, not its address.

Third, the price signature. She named radioactivity, and it killed her; the notebooks glow; the coffin is lined with lead. It is tempting to read this as tragedy, but she did not: knowing the risks late in life, she continued, and her one recorded regret was Pierre. The harder modern question the story leaves is about informed risk — she chose hers unknowingly, then knowingly; the women who later painted radium watch dials chose nothing and were told nothing. The same element, two moral universes — and the difference is simply who knew, and who was allowed to decide.

Talk it through:
  • "Exclusion presents itself as quality control." Test the sentence against any selective institution you know — where is the line between standards and camouflage?
  • The Curies were refused a laboratory while doing the century's best work in a shed. How would you design funding that catches sheds?
  • Her private life was attacked; her reply separated the work from the person. Does that separation hold today — for scientists, artists, executives?
  • Series thread: Semmelweis was destroyed by his institutions, Snow ignored by his, Curie obstructed by hers — and all three are now their institutions' proudest monuments. What does that pattern predict about today's outsiders?
