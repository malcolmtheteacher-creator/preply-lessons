The Three Strangers

Hardy's story of a christening party on a wet March night, three knocks at the door, and a song that nobody should be singing.

Today: you'll sit by the fire in a shepherd's cottage while three strangers arrive out of the rain, and work out — before the guests do — who each of them really is. Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. He had walked three miles in the rain without a coat, and stood in the doorway with water running from his hat, his sleeves and his boots. He was absolutely drenched.
drenched (adj) — completely wet. Also: soaked, soaked to the skin, wet through. Figuratively: a garden drenched in sunlight.
2. Twenty neighbours, a fiddle, a blazing fire, plenty to drink, and dancing until the floor shook — it was a warm and convivial evening.
convivial (adj) — friendly, lively and enjoyable, usually with food and drink: a convivial atmosphere, a convivial host. Slightly formal; from the Latin for "feast".
3. He kept his hat low over his face, glanced quickly at each new arrival, and then looked away. There was something furtive about him.
furtive (adj) — secret and nervous, as if trying not to be noticed: a furtive glance, furtive movements. It suggests guilt. Near neighbours: shifty, stealthy.

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: fugitive · gallows · reprieve. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means a person who is running away, especially from the police or from prison?
a fugitive (n) — a fugitive from justice. Related: to flee, flight.
2. Which word means the wooden frame from which criminals were hanged?
the gallows (n) — singular, despite the -s. It gives us gallows humour = jokes about terrible situations, which is exactly what the hangman's song is.
3. Which word means an official order to stop or delay a punishment — and, informally, any welcome delay?
a reprieve (n, also verb) — a last-minute reprieve, the prisoner was reprieved. Everyday use: "The exam's been moved to next week — a reprieve!"

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Mrs Fennel was a thrifty woman; she watched every mug of mead that was poured. Thrifty means…
  (a) careful with money and resources   (b) rude to guests   (c) fond of alcohol
(a) — thrifty (adj) is positive or neutral; mean or stingy are negative. A thrift shop (AmE) is a charity shop (BrE).
2. "The man they were going to hang tomorrow has escaped. He's still at large." To be at large is to be…
  (a) very big and strong   (b) free, not yet caught   (c) hiding in a large crowd
(b) — at large = (of a dangerous person or animal) free and not captured. A second meaning: society at large = society in general.

D · Word building & collocation

1. A picture is hung on the wall. What do we say about a man executed with a rope: he was hung or hanged? And what do we call the man whose job it was?
For execution, the standard past form is hanged: he was hanged in 1825. For everything else, hung. The man is the hangman or, formally, the executioner. A crime punished by death was a hanging offence — still used jokingly: "Being late isn't a hanging offence."
2. The second stranger answers a simple question with a song that hides the answer like a puzzle. He is speaking in ___. Complete it — and say what a report riddled with errors means.
to speak in riddles = to talk in a deliberately mysterious way. riddled with (from an old word for a sieve) = full of holes, or full of something bad: riddled with errors, riddled with corruption.

  • Sort today's words into words about the party, words about the law, and words about people's behaviour.

The story, in three parts

Hardy's story, retold and shortened, with the hangman's song in Hardy's own words. Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A party at Higher Crowstairs, and a knock

Among the high, grassy downs of Wessex there stood, fifty years ago, a lonely cottage called Higher Crowstairs, three miles from the county town of Casterbridge. On the evening of the twenty-eighth of March the rain came down across the hills like arrows, and the sheep stood with their backs to it. But inside, Shepherd Fennel was giving a party. His second daughter had been christened that day, and nineteen neighbours were crowded round a blazing fire while a boy of twelve played the fiddle. Mrs Fennel, a thrifty woman, had worried all week: if the guests danced too long they would drink too much, and if they talked too long they would eat too much. Her old, strong mead she watched with special care.

At about eight o'clock, between two dances, there came a knock. A tall, thin man of about forty stood at the door, drenched, his hat pulled low. Might he come in and rest out of the rain? "Of course," said the shepherd, and the guests made room for him in the chimney corner, the warmest seat in the house. He was pleasant enough, but he answered questions about himself in very few words. He was a wheelwright, he said, on his way somewhere. He took out a pipe and asked if anyone could spare some tobacco. "A pipe but no tobacco?" said the shepherd. "I dropped it on the way," said the man, and the subject was changed.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is there a party at Higher Crowstairs, and what is Mrs Fennel worried about?

2. What is slightly strange about the first stranger?

1. To celebrate the christening of the Fennels' second daughter. Mrs Fennel is thrifty and worries that the guests will drink or eat too much — especially her old mead.

2. He says very little about himself, keeps his hat low, and has a pipe but no tobacco.

Discuss:
  • Nobody at the party questions the stranger very hard. Is that admirable country hospitality, or carelessness? Would it happen today?

Part 2 — The second stranger sings

Soon there was another knock. The second stranger was a short, heavy man in a grey suit, cheerful and loud. He too was going to Casterbridge, and he sat down in the chimney corner beside the first stranger, so close that the first man could not have got out if he had wanted to. He drank the mug of mead that was passed to him, and then another. Mrs Fennel watched in silent despair. "And what is your trade, sir?" someone asked. "I'll sing it to you," he said:

O my trade it is the rarest one,
  Simple shepherds all —
My trade is a sight to see;
For my customers I tie, and take them up on high,
  And waft 'em to a far countree!

The room went very quiet. Only one person joined in the chorus: the first stranger, in a low, strong voice. Then, one by one, the guests understood. This was the hangman. There was to be a hanging at Casterbridge gaol in the morning — a poor watchmaker called Timothy Summers, who had stolen a sheep in broad daylight because his family was starving. Everyone had said it was a hard sentence. And here was the man who would carry it out, warming his feet at their fire and singing about it.

He was halfway through the next verse when there was a third knock. A small, nervous man stood in the doorway and asked the way to a village. Then his eyes fell on the two strangers in the chimney corner. His face went white. His knees shook. He stared for a second, turned, and ran out into the dark.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does the second stranger reveal his job, and what is he going to do tomorrow?

2. What does the third stranger do when he sees the two men by the fire?

1. He sings about tying up his "customers" and taking them "up on high" — he is the hangman, and tomorrow he will hang Timothy Summers, a watchmaker who stole a sheep to feed his starving family.

2. He goes pale, trembles, stares at them, and runs out into the night without a word.

Discuss:
  • The first stranger joins in the hangman's chorus — the only guest who does. Why might a man sing loudly in a moment like this? Have you guessed who he is?

Part 3 — The wrong man

Before anyone could speak, there came the boom of a gun from the direction of Casterbridge. "Be jiggered!" cried the hangman. "That's the gaol gun. A prisoner has escaped — and that's my man!" Every head turned to the open door. The third stranger! Who else would turn white at the sight of the hangman and run? The constable, who was among the guests, was persuaded, rather against his will, to lead a search, and in the confusion nobody thought to keep an eye on the quiet man from the chimney corner.

They found the little man standing under a tree on the hillside and marched him back to the cottage, where a magistrate and two officers from the gaol had now arrived. "This isn't the man," said the officers. "Not a bit like him." "Then why did you run?" asked the magistrate. The prisoner, said the little man, was his brother. He had walked all day to say goodbye to him before the hanging — and when the cottage door opened he had seen his brother, whom he believed to be in the condemned cell, sitting by the fire, hemmed in by the very man who had come to hang him, and singing his own execution song to save appearances. His brother had given him one look that said: Don't speak, or I'm a dead man. And so he had run.

"Then the man in the corner was the prisoner!" said the shepherd. "A wheelwright, he said." "A watchmaker," said the brother. "He meant the wheels of clocks." Next morning a search was made, but somehow it was not a very energetic one. The people of the district had heard about the starving family and the stolen sheep. He was never found. But the story of the night when three strangers came to Higher Crowstairs is told there to this day — and the baby whose christening it was is now an old woman.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does everyone assume the third stranger is the escaped prisoner — and who is he really?

2. Who was the first stranger, and why was the search for him "not a very energetic one"?

1. Because he turned pale and ran when he saw the hangman, just before the gaol gun announced an escape. He was the prisoner's brother, who had come to say goodbye.

2. The escaped prisoner, Timothy Summers, sitting beside his own hangman. Local people sympathised with a man sentenced to death for stealing a sheep to feed his family, so nobody tried very hard to catch him.

Discuss:
  • Go back through the story. Which clues to the first stranger's identity did you notice, and which did you miss? (Think: the pipe, the trade, the chorus, the seat by the fire.)

Grammar — question tags & reply questions

Everyone at Higher Crowstairs wants to know who the strangers are — without being rude. In English, the politest way to ask is often not to ask at all, but to make a statement and add a tiny question at the end: "You're not from round here, are you?"

How question tags work:
• Positive statement → negative tag; negative statement → positive tag: "You're a wheelwright, aren't you?" "You haven't got any tobacco, have you?"
• Use the same auxiliary — or do / does / did if there isn't one: "He sings well, doesn't he?" "They found him, didn't they?"
• Intonation is meaning. Falling tone ↘ = "I'm sure, please agree": "Terrible night, isn't it ↘". Rising tone ↗ = a real question: "You're going to Casterbridge, aren't you ↗".
• Same-way tags (positive + positive) signal surprise or suspicion: "So you've got a pipe but no tobacco, have you?"
The tricky tags (C1) & reply questions.
• I'm right, aren't I? · Let's dance, shall we? · Pass the mead, will you? · Nobody saw him, did they? · Nothing happened, did it? — nobody/nothing are negative, so the tag is positive; nobody takes they.
• Reply questions show interest or surprise and keep a conversation going: "I'm going to Casterbridge." — "Are you?" "The prisoner has escaped." — "Has he?"
In real conversation, these little questions do the work of listening. Without them, English speakers can sound cold or uninterested.

Going deeper

Thomas Hardy published "The Three Strangers" in Longman's Magazine in 1883 and collected it in Wessex Tales in 1888. "Wessex" was his name for the south-west of England, mainly his native Dorset, and "Casterbridge" is Dorchester. The story is set "fifty years ago", around the 1820s, when stealing a sheep could still be punished by death in England; that penalty was abolished in 1832. As a boy of sixteen, Hardy watched a public hanging in Dorchester, and he never forgot it.

The craft. The structure is a fairy-tale pattern — three knocks, three strangers — and Hardy uses our expectations against us. We assume the third stranger matters most, because in stories the third one always does; in fact the key figure has been sitting by the fire since the first page. Hardy plays fair: every clue is there — the pipe with no tobacco, the vague answers, the "wheelwright" (a watchmaker works with wheels too), the shadowy chimney corner, and above all the chorus he sings so loudly. The great scene is the song: the hangman singing cheerfully about his work, shoulder to shoulder with the man he has come to kill, who sings along to stay alive. It is comedy and horror at once. Then the ending opens out: a search that is "not very energetic" is a whole community's quiet verdict on an unjust law. And the final line — the baby is now an old woman — turns one night into local legend, told and retold, which is exactly how stories like this survive.
Talk it through:
  • Hardy follows the "rule of three", then breaks it. Where else have you seen stories use three — three brothers, three wishes, three tests? Why does the pattern feel so natural?
  • The first stranger joins in the hangman's song. Is that courage, intelligence, or desperation? What would you have done in his seat?
  • The villagers don't try hard to catch him. Is that justice, or a community ignoring the law when it suits them?
  • Why does Hardy end with the baby grown old, rather than with the escaped man? What does the final sentence do to the whole story?

Task A · Narrate the whole story (4 minutes)

Tell The Three Strangers as if to someone who has never read it, and keep the secret of the first stranger until the very end, as Hardy does. Move through the arc: the lonely cottage, the rain, the christening party → knock one: the thin man and the empty pipe → knock two: the hangman, the mead, the song → knock three: the pale man who runs → the gaol gun and the search → "this isn't the man" → the brother's story → the search nobody wanted to win.

Include at least three question tags in dialogue (one a same-way tag of suspicion), one reply question, and at least five key words (drenched, convivial, furtive, fugitive, gallows, reprieve, thrifty, at large, hanged, speak in riddles).

Task B · Were the villagers right? (4 minutes)

Build an argument. One view: the law is the law; a community that decides for itself which criminals to catch has no law at all. Another: a law that hangs a starving man for a sheep has lost its authority, and the villagers' half-hearted search was the most just thing that happened that night. Take a side, give three reasons, deal with the strongest objection, and say what Hardy himself seems to think.

Then argue the opposite for one minute — the harder direction is where the language comes from.

Task C · Strangers, trust and hiding in plain sight (4 minutes)

The story is about who we trust, and how easily we miss what's in front of us. Talk about it in today's world.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Role-play: your partner is a stranger who arrives at your party. Find out who they really are in ninety seconds using only tags and reply questions — no direct questions allowed.
  • Hospitality to strangers: is it disappearing? Compare your grandparents' generation with yours.
  • A real case of someone "hiding in plain sight" — a spy, an impostor, a fugitive who lived openly for years. Why weren't they noticed?
  • Gallows humour: why do people joke about death, danger or disaster? When is it healthy and when is it cruel?
  • A punishment that seems far too harsh today. Choose one from history and explain how society changed its mind.
  • A story that is told again and again in your family or town. Tell it — and say why you think it has survived.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The brother ran away and nearly got himself arrested — but by running, he saved his brother's life. Was it the bravest thing in the story, or simply panic?
  2. The hangman is cheerful, friendly and fond of a drink. Does that make him more or less disturbing than a cold, cruel executioner?
  3. Hardy says the story is still told at Higher Crowstairs. Why do communities keep stories like this alive? What do they teach?
  4. Before you go — from memory: ask the first stranger three questions using tags — one rising, one falling, and one same-way tag of suspicion — and explain why the tag after nobody is positive.
