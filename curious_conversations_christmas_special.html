How the World Celebrates Christmas

Over 2 billion people worldwide celebrate Christmas—but they do it in wildly different ways

What do you picture when you think of Christmas? Snow, turkey, Santa Claus, Christmas trees? That's the Anglo-American version, but it's just one of hundreds of traditions around the world.

Let's take a global tour of how different cultures celebrate December 25th (and sometimes January 7th!):

🇯🇵 Japan: KFC Christmas

In Japan, where only 1% of the population is Christian, Christmas is a romantic holiday similar to Valentine's Day. But the most famous tradition? Eating KFC. In 1974, KFC launched a "Christmas Chicken" marketing campaign that became so successful that families now order their Christmas KFC weeks in advance. On Christmas Day, 3.6 million Japanese families eat KFC—that's about 3% of the entire population!

🇦🇹 Austria & Germany: Krampus the Christmas Demon

While Santa brings gifts to good children, in Alpine regions, Krampus punishes the bad ones. This horned, demonic creature appears in parades on December 5th (Krampusnacht), chasing people through the streets with chains and bells. It's terrifying and festive at the same time. The tradition dates back to pre-Christian winter solstice celebrations.

🇻🇪 Venezuela: Roller Skating to Church

In Caracas, Venezuela, there's a tradition of roller skating to early morning Christmas mass. Roads are closed to cars so families can skate to church together. Why? Nobody knows the origin, but it's been a beloved tradition for decades. After mass, people return home for tamales and hallacas (traditional Christmas food).

🇳🇴 Norway: Hiding the Brooms

On Christmas Eve, Norwegians hide all their brooms. Why? According to folklore, Christmas Eve is when witches and evil spirits come out to steal brooms for flying. So families hide them to be safe. Some people still do this "just in case"—better safe than sorry!

🇲🇽 Mexico: Las Posadas

From December 16-24, Mexican communities reenact Mary and Joseph's search for shelter in a tradition called Las Posadas. Each night, groups go house to house asking for lodging, being turned away until they reach the designated host house. Then everyone celebrates with food, piñatas, and singing. It's Christmas spread over nine days!

🇺🇦 Ukraine: Spider Web Decorations

Ukrainians decorate their Christmas trees with artificial spider webs (called pavuchky). The tradition comes from a folk tale about a poor widow who couldn't afford decorations. On Christmas morning, spiders had covered her tree in beautiful webs that turned to silver in the sunlight. Finding a spider web on Christmas is considered good luck!

🇮🇸 Iceland: Thirteen Yule Lads

Iceland doesn't have one Santa—they have thirteen Yule Lads (Jólasveinar). Starting on December 12, one arrives each night until Christmas. Each has a different personality: Door-Slammer, Spoon-Licker, Sausage-Swiper. Children leave shoes in their windows, and good kids get treats while naughty ones get rotten potatoes!

🇵🇭 Philippines: Giant Lantern Festival

The Philippines has the longest Christmas season in the world—starting in September! The highlight is the Giant Lantern Festival in San Fernando, where enormous, elaborate lanterns (parols) compete in displays of light and color. Some are over 20 feet tall with thousands of lightbulbs programmed to create intricate patterns.

🇪🇹 Ethiopia: January 7th Christmas

Ethiopian Orthodox Christians celebrate Ganna on January 7th (following the old Julian calendar). The celebration includes an all-night church service where people wear traditional white robes, followed by a game called ganna (similar to hockey) that gives the holiday its name.

Food Traditions Around the World:

  • UK: Christmas pudding set on fire with brandy
  • Italy: Seven-fish feast on Christmas Eve (Feast of the Seven Fishes)
  • Australia: Beach barbecues (it's summer!)
  • Germany: Stollen (fruit bread) and gingerbread
  • Sweden: Julbord buffet with pickled herring
  • Poland: 12-course meatless Christmas Eve dinner

Talking About Celebrations

Click each expression to see how it's used

1. "Get into the spirit (of)"
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: To start feeling excited and enthusiastic about something, especially a holiday or celebration. To adopt the mood or feeling of an occasion.
Example:
"I wasn't really feeling Christmas this year, but once I saw the giant lantern festival in the Philippines, I really got into the spirit!"
NOW YOU TRY:
What helps you get into the spirit of celebrations or holidays? What tradition or activity makes you feel festive?
2. "Go all out"
Formality: Informal/Neutral
Meaning: To do something with maximum effort or enthusiasm; to not hold back. Often used for celebrations, decorations, or special events.
Example:
"My neighbors really go all out for Christmas—they have so many lights on their house that you can see it from space! The electricity bill must be insane."
NOW YOU TRY:
Do you know someone who goes all out for holidays or celebrations? Describe what they do. Or do YOU go all out for anything?
3. "Keep up appearances"
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: To maintain a tradition or behavior to seem normal or proper, even if you don't feel like it. Often done to meet social expectations.
Example:
"Honestly, I don't enjoy big Christmas dinners, but I keep up appearances for my family. They'd be heartbroken if I didn't show up and pretend to love the turkey."
NOW YOU TRY:
Is there a tradition or holiday activity you do just to keep up appearances? Why do you continue doing it?

Family & Traditions

4. "Make a big deal out of"
Formality: Informal/Neutral
Meaning: To treat something as very important or significant; to give something a lot of attention and celebration. Can be positive or slightly negative (making something more important than it needs to be).
Example:
"In the Philippines, they make a big deal out of Christmas—the celebrations start in September and last four months! That's serious commitment."
NOW YOU TRY:
What does your family or culture make a big deal out of? Is there something others think is minor but you celebrate extensively?
5. "Family tradition"
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: A custom, activity, or ritual that a family does regularly, often passed down through generations. Creates family identity and continuity.
Example:
"It's a family tradition in Norway to hide all the brooms on Christmas Eve. My grandmother did it, my mother does it, and I still do it even though I don't actually believe witches will steal them!"
NOW YOU TRY:
Describe a family tradition from your childhood. Do you still practice it? Will you pass it on to the next generation?

All 5 Expressions:

  • "Get into the spirit" — feel excited about celebration
  • "Go all out" — do something with maximum effort
  • "Keep up appearances" — maintain tradition for social reasons
  • "Make a big deal out of" — treat as very important
  • "Family tradition" — custom passed through generations

This Holiday Season:

Pay attention to how people around you celebrate. Notice what traditions people "keep up appearances" for versus what they genuinely enjoy. Ask people about their family traditions—everyone has interesting stories!

Remember: There's no "right" way to celebrate holidays. Whether you go all out with decorations or quietly enjoy time with family, whether you follow traditions or create new ones—what matters is finding meaning in the season for yourself.

🌍 Global Holiday Wisdom:
