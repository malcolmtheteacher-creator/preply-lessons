Reporting Something Lost

It's out there somewhere, and someone kind might just have it. Time to describe it well, keep your nerve — and keep hoping.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

You realise it about a mile too late: your rucksack isn't on your shoulder. You had it on the train — you're almost sure you had it on the train — and now you don't. Inside it, among the ordinary things, is the one thing you'd hate to lose: a small camera, still full of photos from a trip you can never take again.

So you've come to the station's lost-property office: a little window at the end of a corridor, behind it shelves of umbrellas, single gloves, forgotten teddy bears and one very sad accordion. Somewhere in this building — or somewhere on a train pulling further away by the minute — is your bag. Whether it comes back to you may depend on how clearly you can describe it, how patient you can be, and how much you can make a busy stranger care.

Behind the window: a clerk who has reunited thousands of people with their things, and gently disappointed thousands more. Unflappable, methodical, kind underneath — and quietly rooting for a happy ending, if you give them one to root for.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. What's the most precious thing you've ever lost — and did it ever find its way back? How did losing it feel, beyond the money?
  2. If you found a stranger's bag on a train — phone inside, photos, a little cash — what would you do with it, honestly, and what does that say about you?

Who's who

You
The Owner 🎒

Anxious but holding it together. You need to describe your bag precisely, answer awkward questions honestly, and stay hopeful without becoming a nuisance.

The other voice
The Lost-Property Clerk 🔦

Calm, thorough, seen every kind of loss. Won't promise what they can't deliver — but will move heaven and earth for someone who's polite and clear.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the careful describing, the honest hedging, and the way hope is kept alive right to the end.

ClerkAfternoon. You've got the look of someone who's lost something — how can I help?
YouI'm afraid you're right. I think I may have left my rucksack on the train — the 4:15 down from Edinburgh. I only realised once I was out on the concourse, by which point the train had gone.
ClerkRight, let's see what we can do. These things do come back more often than you'd think. Can you describe the bag for me?
YouOf course. It's a smallish canvas rucksack, dark green, quite worn — a bit battered, honestly. There's a hand-sewn patch of a mountain on the front flap, so you really can't mistake it for anyone else's.
ClerkA mountain patch — that's helpful, that's the sort of thing that jogs a memory. And roughly what size are we talking?
YouOh, about the size of a large shoebox, I suppose — big enough for a laptop, but I was only carrying it half full.
ClerkAnd can you tell me what was inside? It helps us match it, and it helps rule out anyone claiming it who shouldn't.
YouA jumper, a paperback, the usual bits — and a small silver camera. That's the one that matters, to be honest. It's of no real value to anyone but me, but it's got photos on it I can't replace. More sentimental than anything.
ClerkUnderstood — and I'll make a note of that camera, because that's your proof it's yours. Now, we've had a few bags handed in off the Edinburgh services today. Let me have a quick look out the back — bear with me.
ClerkRight. Two rucksacks back here, but neither's green and neither's got your mountain. I'm sorry to say it's not turned up just yet.
YouAh. Well, it was worth a try. I don't suppose there's any chance more come in later on — does anything still trickle through from that train?
ClerkIt does, as it happens. The cleaners work the carriages at the far end of the line, so things from the 4:15 can reach us a good day or two later. It's early days yet — I wouldn't give up hope.
YouThat's reassuring, thank you. In that case, would you mind taking my number, so you can let me know the moment anything surfaces?
ClerkAbsolutely — pop your name and number on this slip, with a line about the camera, and we'll ring you the instant a green bag with a mountain walks through that door.
YouYou're very kind. I'll keep my fingers crossed — and I'll pop back tomorrow to check, if that's alright. Thank you ever so much for looking.
ClerkDo. And chin up — you'd be amazed what finds its way home in the end. Stranger things have come back through this window than a green rucksack.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The clerk asks what was inside the bag for two separate reasons. What are they — and why does knowing about the camera matter later?

2. When the bag isn't out the back, the owner doesn't just leave. What one question do they ask that keeps the whole thing alive — and what hopeful fact does it unlock about the 4:15?

3. The owner describes the bag as "of no real value to anyone but me." Why choose those words rather than simply calling the camera expensive?

1. First, the contents help the clerk match the bag to one on the shelf; second, they help rule out anyone falsely claiming it. The camera does double duty — it's the detail that later proves the bag is genuinely the owner's, so the clerk makes a special note of it.
2. "Is there any chance more come in later — does anything still trickle through from that train?" It unlocks the fact that cleaners work the carriages at the far end of the line, so items from the 4:15 can arrive a day or two later. The loss isn't final; it's "early days".
3. Calling it expensive might tempt a dishonest finder and makes the owner sound worried about money. "Of no real value to anyone but me" quietly signals it's precious for personal reasons, invites sympathy, and makes the clerk want to help a person — not process a valuable.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the moment something's gone missing — clear, calm and quietly persuasive, at this window and any other. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🎒 Reporting the loss

Phrase 1

You're fairly sure you left your bag on the train — but not certain, and you don't want to sound as though you're accusing anyone. Which opener reports the loss while leaving room for doubt?

A. "Someone's taken my rucksack off the train."
B. "Where's my rucksack? It was on the train."
C. "I think I may have left my rucksack on the train."
C — "I think I may have left my rucksack on the train."
"I think I may have…" owns the mistake gently and blames nobody. It's calm, honest and instantly gets the clerk on your side — where "someone's taken" would start a very different, colder conversation.
Phrase 2

You want to ask, hopefully but without demanding, whether your bag has been brought in — the classic English way of fishing for good news while bracing for bad. Complete the opener: "I ___ ___ anyone's handed in a green rucksack…?"

"I don't suppose anyone's handed in a green rucksack…?"
"I don't suppose…" politely expects a no — which makes a no painless to give and a yes a lovely surprise. It's the softest way in English to hope out loud.

🔍 Describing it well

Phrase 3

The clerk asks how big the bag is. You've no exact measurements, so you reach for a familiar object to give them a picture. Complete the frame: "Oh, ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ a large shoebox, I suppose."

"Oh, about the size of a large shoebox, I suppose."
"About the size of + everyday object" is how native speakers describe dimensions without a tape measure. Reach for the nearest shared thing: "the size of a paperback", "about as long as your forearm".
Phrase 4

Every bag looks alike on a shelf — so you point to the ONE feature that makes yours unmistakable. Put the pieces in order:

on the front,you reallythere's a mountain patchcan't miss it
"There's a mountain patch on the front, you really can't miss it."
Lead with the distinguishing detail, then reassure them it's obvious: "you really can't miss it", "you couldn't mistake it for anyone else's". One vivid feature beats five vague ones.
Phrase 5

The thing that truly matters is the camera — precious to you, worthless to a stranger. You want the clerk to grasp why it matters without sounding like you're flashing money around. What do you say?

"It's of no real value to anyone but me — more sentimental than anything."
"Of no value to anyone but me" and "more sentimental than monetary" turn your loss into a human story, not a price tag. It wins sympathy and, quietly, discourages a dishonest finder.

❓ Answering the tricky questions

Phrase 6

The clerk asks exactly where you last had the bag. You're not 100% sure of the moment, and it's better to admit that than to guess wrongly. Which answer is honest AND still useful?

A. "I had it on the train, definitely, one hundred percent."
B. "I couldn't say for certain, but the last time I definitely had it was boarding at Edinburgh."
C. "I don't know, could be anywhere."
B — "I couldn't say for certain, but the last time I definitely had it was boarding at Edinburgh."
"I couldn't say for certain, but…" is honest hedging: it admits doubt yet still hands over the one solid fact ("the last time I definitely had it was…"), which is exactly what a search needs.
Phrase 7

The clerk disappears out the back to check the shelves and asks you to wait. You want to sound patient and easy, not like a clock-watcher. What's the graceful thing to say?

"Take your time — no rush at all."
Giving a busy person permission to be slow is oddly disarming. It signals you're reasonable and grateful — the kind of person they'll go the extra mile for. Cousins: "Whenever you get a chance", "No hurry."

🤞 Keeping hope alive — and the door open

Phrase 8

The bag isn't there yet, but things still arrive. You want them to contact you the moment it does. Which request is warmer and more likely to be honoured?

A. "Take my number and call me if you find it."
B. "Would you mind taking my number, so you can let me know the moment anything surfaces?"
B — "Would you mind taking my number, so you can let me know the moment anything surfaces?"
"Would you mind + -ing" treats the favour as theirs to give, and "the moment anything surfaces" quietly asks for promptness without nagging. A is an instruction; B is a request someone actually wants to grant.
Phrase 9

Nothing's certain, but you refuse to give up. You want a warm, hopeful line that says "I'm still counting on a happy ending" without a trace of gloom. What do you say?

"I'll keep my fingers crossed."
Light, human and hopeful — it keeps the mood up and hands the clerk a shared little wish. Relatives: "Fingers crossed something turns up", "Here's hoping", "You never know."
Phrase 10

You want to thank the clerk AND signal you'll be back to check — leaving the door open without being a pest. Which closing line does both, warmly?

A. "Alright, thanks. Bye."
B. "I'll pop back tomorrow to check, if that's alright — thank you ever so much for looking."
C. "I expect you to have found it by tomorrow."
B — "I'll pop back tomorrow to check, if that's alright — thank you ever so much for looking."
"…if that's alright" asks permission rather than announcing a demand; "thank you ever so much for looking" thanks them for the effort, not just the result. Warm, respectful, and it keeps the search alive.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole visit out loud using all ten, in order — report it → describe it → answer the questions → keep hope alive → a grateful goodbye. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

🕯️ The last train home

One more round — no script, no safety net. It's late, the office shuts in ten minutes, and this is the last train home. The clerk has just told you a green rucksack was handed in this morning — but it's already been sent to the central depot across town, which is now closed until Monday, and you fly out tomorrow at dawn. Start the timer and talk your way through it: can they call the depot before it locks up? Could a colleague hold it for you? Can they at least confirm it's yours by describing what's inside — and put your camera safely aside? Explore every angle, stay warm and reasonable throughout. One rule: the moment you turn sharp or start demanding, the shutters come down — and so does your last chance before the flight.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1872, a ship called the Mary Celeste was found drifting in the Atlantic — sails set, cargo intact, half-eaten breakfast on the table — and not a soul on board. The crew had simply vanished, and no lost-property office in the world has ever handed them back.

A century and a half later, here you are at a little window, describing a green rucksack, hoping the sea of a railway will give up what it swallowed. Some things trickle back a day or two late. Some, like ten people off a ghost ship, never do.

The difference between a mystery and a happy ending is often just this: a good description, a patient wait, and someone kind at the other end of the search.

Two bigger questions

  1. The Mary Celeste is famous precisely because it was never explained — we love an unsolved disappearance. Why do open-ended mysteries grip us more than tidy answers ever could?
  2. When something is lost for good — a bag, a ship's crew, a person — we often invent a story to fill the silence. Is that comforting or dangerous? What does it cost us to admit we simply don't know what happened?
