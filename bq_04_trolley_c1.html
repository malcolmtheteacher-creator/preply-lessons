The ideas, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The switch

In 1967 the British philosopher Philippa Foot described a puzzle that has since been named the trolley problem. A runaway tram is heading for five people tied to the track. You are standing next to a lever. If you pull it, the tram will switch to a side track where it will kill one person instead. Do you pull it?

Most people say yes. In surveys across many countries, a large majority agree that it is better to save five than one. This is the logic of utilitarianism, the view, developed by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, that the right action is the one that produces the greatest good for the greatest number. By that measure, the choice is easy.

Foot herself was less interested in the answer than in what came next. Because if the numbers decide, nothing else should matter. And as the next version shows, something else clearly does.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Describe the original trolley problem.

2. What do most people say, and which theory does that fit?

1. A runaway tram will kill five people unless you pull a lever to send it to a side track, where it will kill one.

2. Most people pull the lever; this fits utilitarianism, the view that the best action produces the greatest good for the greatest number.

Discuss:
  • If you would pull the lever, what is the strongest argument that you are wrong?

Part 2 — The footbridge

In 1976 the American philosopher Judith Jarvis Thomson offered a second version. This time you are standing on a footbridge above the track, next to a very large stranger. The tram is still heading for five people. The only way to stop it is to push the man off the bridge. His body would stop the tram; he would die; the five would live.

The numbers have not changed: five against one. Yet almost everyone says no. Why? One answer comes from Immanuel Kant, who argued that we must never treat a person merely as a means to an end. In the footbridge case, the man is used. In the switch case, his death is a side effect, not the method. Aquinas had made a similar distinction centuries earlier, now known as the doctrine of double effect.

Psychologists have added another suggestion. In brain-imaging studies by Joshua Greene and colleagues, personal dilemmas such as pushing seemed to engage emotional regions more strongly than impersonal ones such as pulling a lever. If that is right, the difference we feel may be part moral principle and part gut reaction, and philosophers still argue about which one deserves our trust.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What changes between the switch and the footbridge?

2. What explanation does Kant's idea give?

1. Nothing in the numbers; but you must push a man with your own hands, and his body is the method of stopping the tram.

2. That people must not be used merely as a means; in the footbridge case the man is used, whereas in the switch case his death is a side effect.

Discuss:
  • Is a gut reaction a good guide to morality, or a bias that philosophy should correct?

Part 3 — When the trolley is a car

These puzzles used to be seminar games. Now they are engineering problems. A self-driving car may one day have to choose between swerving and staying on course when a crash cannot be avoided. Someone must decide, in advance, what it will do.

In 2018 researchers at MIT published the results of the Moral Machine, an online game in which people chose how a car should act. Tens of millions of decisions came in from people in more than two hundred countries and territories. Some preferences were widely shared: save more lives rather than fewer, and humans rather than animals. Others varied from region to region, such as how much weight to give to age or social status.

That raises a new kind of question. It may be possible to find out what people say they want, but should a machine be built to follow it? Or should some principles be fixed in advance, whatever a majority happens to prefer?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the Moral Machine?

2. What did the results show?

1. An online game from MIT in which people decided how a self-driving car should act in unavoidable crashes.

2. Tens of millions of decisions from over two hundred countries: some preferences were widely shared, while others varied by region.

Discuss:
  • Who should decide how a self-driving car behaves in a crash: engineers, governments, the public or the owner?

Grammar — past modals of judgement

When we judge what someone did, we usually do it in the past, and English uses modal verbs plus have + past participle to say whether it was wrong, possible or unnecessary. These structures are the language of blame, excuse and regret.

Three structures:
• should have / ought to have + past participle — criticism or regret about something that did not happen: "She should have reported it." "We ought to have asked." The negative, shouldn't have, criticises something that did happen.
• could have / might have + past participle — something was possible but did not happen: "He could have saved them." "The bridge might have collapsed."
• needn't have + past participle — you did something that turned out to be unnecessary: "You needn't have brought food."
Feel the difference:
• should have expresses judgement, a standard that was not met.
• could have expresses possibility, with no blame attached.
• needn't have expresses wasted effort, and the action did happen. Compare didn't need to, which does not tell you whether it happened.

Switch or push?

Five versions of the dilemma. Say what you would do and which principle you are using, then reveal.

1. You pull a lever and divert a tram from five people onto a track with one worker. Do you pull it?
Utilitarian reasoning. Most people say yes: five lives against one, and the death is a side effect.
2. You push a large stranger off a bridge to stop the tram. Do you push?
Kant's means principle. Almost everyone says no, though the numbers are the same: you would be using a person as a tool.
3. The side track loops round to the five, and it is the one man's body that stops the tram. Do you pull the lever?
The loop case. This is the hard one: he is used, as in the footbridge, but you do not touch him. People are often divided, which suggests that intention and contact both count.
4. A surgeon can save five dying patients by taking the organs of one healthy visitor. Should she?
Rights. Nearly everyone says no, and not only because of consequences: it suggests that we believe in individual rights that cannot be traded for numbers.
5. A self-driving car must choose between swerving into one pedestrian and hitting five. Should it swerve?
Who decides. The structure resembles the switch, but now the choice is built into a product, and the question becomes whether a company or a law should decide in advance.
