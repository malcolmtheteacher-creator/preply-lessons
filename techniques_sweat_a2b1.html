A. Guess from the sentence

Read the sentence. What does the bold word mean? Then reveal.

1. Delia washed clothes for rich families. Every week she cleaned their shirts and sheets in a big tub of water and soap.
to wash (v) — to clean something with water and soap. a washerwoman = a woman who washes clothes for money.
2. In the hot sun, sweat ran down her face and her arms as she worked.
sweat (n) — the water that comes out of your skin when you are hot or working hard. (v: to sweat)
3. A long snake lay in the box. It was six feet long, and it made a dry rattling sound when it moved.
a snake (n) — a long, thin animal with no legs; some snakes are poisonous.
4. She was frightened. Her hands shook and she could not speak.
frightened (adj) — very afraid.

5. Shirts, dresses, trousers — the things you wear. It begins with c.

The story, in three parts

This is a famous American story, told here in simple English. In the original, the people speak in the English of Black Florida a hundred years ago. It is a hard story about a cruel marriage. Read each part, then try the questions from memory.

Part 1 — Sunday night

Delia Jones lives in a small town in Florida. She is a washerwoman: she washes clothes for white families. Every Saturday she collects the dirty clothes with her pony and cart. Every Sunday night, after church, she sorts them, so that she can start early on Monday. She has to. It is her only money.

Tonight she is on the kitchen floor with the clothes, singing quietly. Her husband, Sykes, is out with her pony and cart. Suddenly something long and black falls on her shoulders and slides to the floor. A snake! For a minute she cannot move. Then she sees: it is only Sykes's whip. Sykes is standing in the door, laughing. He knows she is frightened of snakes. That is why he did it.

Then he kicks the clean clothes across the floor. "I told you: no white people's clothes in my house! You go to church and then you work on Sunday. You are a hypocrite!"

And for the first time in fifteen years, Delia stands up to him. She is small and thin, and he is big. "Fifteen years I have been married to you, and fifteen years I have washed clothes. Sweat, sweat, sweat! My sweat paid for this house — not you!" She picks up the heavy iron pan from the stove. Sykes is surprised. For once, he does not hit her. He says he hates thin women, and he goes out to his girlfriend, Bertha.

In bed, Delia remembers fifteen years: the first time he hit her, two months after the wedding; the money he spent in the city; the love she brought, and the nothing he brought. It is too late for love now. But she has her little house, and her flowers. "One day," she says, "Sykes will get what he gives."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is Delia's job, and why does she work on Sunday night?

2. What does Delia do for the first time in fifteen years?

1. She washes clothes for white families. She sorts them on Sunday night so she can start early on Monday. It is her only money.

2. She stands up to Sykes. She tells him her sweat paid for the house, and she picks up the iron pan. He does not hit her.

Discuss:
  • Sykes drops the whip on her because he knows she is afraid of snakes. What kind of person does that?

Part 2 — The men on the porch

It is a hot Saturday in July. The men of the town are sitting on the porch of Joe Clarke's shop. It is too hot to talk. Then Delia drives past with her pony and cart full of clean clothes.

"There goes Delia," one man says. "Rain or sun, every Saturday, she carries those clothes." "She has to, if she wants to eat," says another. "Sykes does nothing." "She was pretty when he married her. He has beaten her enough to kill three women."

They talk about Bertha, Sykes's big, loud girlfriend from another town. Joe Clarke says: "No law can make a man good if he isn't good inside. Some men take a wife like a piece of sugar cane. They squeeze out all the sweetness, and then they throw her away." The men say they should take Sykes to the swamp and beat him. But it is too hot. They buy a watermelon instead.

Then Sykes and Bertha arrive. Sykes buys Bertha soda and sweets, loudly, so that Delia can see. "This is my town," he tells Bertha. "You can have it. And you can have that little house, when I get that woman out of it."

Now Delia and Sykes fight all the time. Bertha even comes to Delia's gate to call Sykes out. Two or three times Delia tries to be friendly. Sykes only laughs.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What do the men on the porch think of Sykes?

2. What does Sykes promise Bertha?

1. That he is worthless, that he does nothing, and that he has beaten Delia terribly. They say they should beat him — but they buy a watermelon instead.

2. That she can have the town — and Delia's little house, when he gets Delia out of it.

Discuss:
  • The men know everything about Sykes and do nothing. Why?

Part 3 — The snake

August. It is so hot that dogs go mad. One day Delia comes home and finds Sykes at the kitchen door with a wooden box. "Look in the box, Delia. I brought you something." Inside is a rattlesnake, six feet long. Delia nearly faints. "Take it away! Please! Kill it!" Sykes laughs. "No. It stays. I know how to handle snakes."

The box stays by the kitchen door. After a few days the snake wakes up. It rattles every time Delia walks past. One day she sees its white teeth in the wire. This time she does not run. She stands and looks at it, full of anger.

That night she says to Sykes: "Take that snake away. You starved me, and I took it. You beat me, and I took it. But this has killed everything inside me." Sykes says the snake stays. Delia stands up. "I hate you, Sykes. I hate you as much as I loved you. Go to your woman. But leave me and my house alone. And if you hit me again, I will go to the police." Sykes shouts terrible things about her body — and leaves.

On Sunday Delia goes to church and comes home singing. The snake's box is quiet. Maybe he took it away! Maybe he is sorry! She carries the lamp into the bedroom to sort the clothes in the big basket by the bed. She opens the basket. The snake is inside.

She runs, and the lamp goes out. She climbs into the hay barn and lies there for an hour, shaking. Then she becomes calm. "I did the best I could," she says. "If things are not right, it is not my fault."

At dawn, Sykes comes home. He breaks the empty box and goes into the dark house. He has no matches — he left them at Bertha's. Delia sits under the bedroom window. Inside, the snake begins to rattle. Sykes cannot see where it is. He jumps onto the bed — and the snake bites him. He screams. Then he calls her name, again and again: "Delia! Delia!"

Delia cannot move. The sun comes up. At last she goes to the door. Sykes is on the floor, his neck swollen, one eye open, full of hope. He sees her. He sees the tubs and the lamp — and he knows that she was at home all night. Delia cannot bear it. The doctor is too far away. She walks to the tree in the garden and waits there in the heat, while inside the house, her husband dies.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where does Delia find the snake on Sunday?

2. What happens to Sykes, and what does Delia do?

1. In the big clothes basket beside the bed. Sykes put it there.

2. The snake bites him in the dark. He calls Delia's name for hours. She does not help him — she waits under a tree in the garden while he dies.

Discuss:
  • Delia does not help Sykes. Is she right or wrong?

The grammar this story runs on: have to and must

Delia's life is duty: she has to wash, she has to work on Sunday, she must deliver on Saturday. English has two main ways to talk about things you are obliged to do.

have to — a duty from outside (the job, the rules, the situation).
• "She has to collect the clothes on Saturday." • "I have to get up at six."
• Past: "She had to work all night." • Question: "Do you have to work on Sunday?"
must — a duty you feel strongly, or a strong order.
• "I must finish this today." • "You must take that snake away!"
• must has no past form — use had to.
Careful with the negatives — they mean different things!
• don't have to = it is not necessary: "On Sunday she doesn't have to deliver clothes."
• mustn't = it is forbidden: "You mustn't touch the snake."

Why people still read this story

Zora Neale Hurston (1891–1960) grew up in Eatonville, Florida, a town run by Black people. As a child she sat under the porch of the shop and listened to the men talk. She wrote 'Sweat' in 1926. She died poor and forgotten — and then, in the 1970s, people found her books again. Today she is one of the most famous American writers.

The story is like a story from the Bible. Delia works and prays. Sykes brings a snake — 'the old devil' — into the house. And the snake bites Sykes, not Delia. He gets what he gives. But Hurston does not make it simple. Delia does not kill him. She does not save him. She waits under a tree.

Look at the end. Sykes sees the tubs and the lamp. He knows Delia was at home all night. Why does Hurston want him to know?
