Your Mother's Advice

Sunday lunch at your mum's. You mentioned that work has been hard, and by the time the plates are cleared she has redrafted your CV, rearranged your flat and taken a view on your son's reading. She means every bit of it kindly. You'd still like her to stop.

The Scene 🎬

It's two o'clock on a Sunday and you're at your mum's kitchen table, where you have sat roughly a thousand times before. There's a crumble in the oven. There is also, as there always is, a running commentary: what you should do about work, what you should have done about the flat, what Karen's boy did and how well it turned out for him, and whether your son is reading enough for his age.

None of it is cruel. That's the difficult part. Sylvia isn't scoring points — she genuinely believes she is helping, because for about thirty years helping was the job, and nobody ever came in and told her the job had finished. So she keeps doing it, and you keep going quiet, and lately you've noticed you've started leaving things out when you talk to her, just to save yourself the fixing.

That's the bit that's actually bothering you. Not the advice — the editing. So today, in the twenty minutes before the crumble comes out, you're going to say something. Not a row. Not a list of everything she's ever done. Just one honest ask, made warmly enough that she can hear it.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Who in your life gives you advice you didn't ask for? Do you argue with it, nod along and ignore it, or have you simply stopped telling them things?
  2. When you tell someone your problem, what are you usually after — a solution, or a witness? Be honest about which one you actually want, and whether you ever say so.

Who's who

You
The Grown-Up Child 🍽️

Forty-odd years old, running your own life perfectly well, and still somehow eleven at this table. You want one thing to change, you want to say it once, and you'd like to stay for pudding afterwards.

The other voice
Sylvia, your mum 👩‍🍳

Warm, quick, and certain. She has an opinion on your job, your flat, your cooking and how you're raising your son, and she shares all of them — not to win, but because sitting on a good idea while someone she loves struggles feels to her like doing nothing at all.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how the ask is built — the warmth that goes in before the complaint, the evidence that can't be argued with, and the moment the ask gets small enough to actually agree to. The words matter far less than the order they arrive in.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Notice that neither of them is wrong, and that it very nearly goes off the rails twice.

Sylvia…and honestly, if you sent it to Karen's boy, he'd have you in somewhere by Christmas, he knows everyone. Is that all you're having? There's loads more in the pan.
YouI'm alright, honestly. Mum — can I say something, and you're probably not going to like it?
SylviaOh. Right. Well, go on then.
YouIt's — no, hang on, let me do this properly or I'll make a mess of it. It's the advice. And I know you only do it because you care, I do know that, I'm not — but you've done it about four times since I got here, and I've counted, which probably tells you something.
SylviaFour? Since two o'clock? What four? The oven doesn't count, that's not advice, that's a fact, the shelf's in the wrong place and everything's burning on top. And anyway, you're the one who said work was hard. You told me. What am I meant to do, sit here saying nothing?
YouI wasn't asking. I was just telling you what's going on.
Sylvia…Well. That's me told, isn't it. But if I can see the answer sat right there, and you're miserable — I'm not going to just sit on it, am I. That's not me.
YouNo, I know. Sorry, that came out a bit — look. Could you just listen for a bit, without fixing it? That's genuinely all I'd want.
SylviaAnd do what instead? Sit there nodding? Going "oh dear"?
YouHonestly, "that sounds hard" would do. That's all I'm after. And then I'd go home feeling better instead of feeling like something that wants sorting out.
SylviaSomething that wants sorting out. Charming. That's — mm. That's not fair, but it's not miles off either, I suppose.
YouAnd when I want your opinion, I promise I'll ask for it. I do ask. The boiler, in February — I rang you about the boiler and you were right.
SylviaI was right about the boiler. Your gran was like this with me, you know. Every single Sunday. I swore blind I'd never — well. Anyway.
YouIt's just, it makes me tell you less, and I don't want that. There's stuff I don't mention now, because I know how it'll go, and then you say I never tell you anything, and round we go. And I'd rather tell you things than start editing what I say.
Sylvia…Right. Okay. I'll try. I'll be terrible at it, mind, you know I will, you'll have to say.
YouYou'll be alright. And Mum — nothing's changed, I'm not having a go at you. I'm still sat here, I'm not going anywhere, and I would quite like some of that crumble.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Instead of "you're always doing this", the caller uses a number. Why is a number so much harder for Sylvia to argue with — and what does she do with it anyway?

2. Sylvia's strongest defence is that she was answering something. What was it, and what's the one-line reply that takes the ground out from under it?

3. The complaint stops being about irritation and turns into something Sylvia actually minds losing. What is it — and why does that argument work when "it annoys me" wouldn't have?

1. "Always" is an opinion, so she can simply disagree with it, whereas four times since two o'clock is a count of things that happened in front of both of them. What she does is argue the number down rather than the point — the oven doesn't count, that's a fact, not advice — and notice that even while she's arguing, she has accepted the other three.
2. She points out that they were the one who said work was hard, so what is she supposed to do, say nothing? The reply doesn't deny it: it says the words were never a request in the first place, which reframes the whole thing rather than fighting her on whether she was right.
3. It's that she's being told less — things get left out, because it's easier than being fixed. "It annoys me" is a complaint about the child's feelings, which Sylvia can weigh against her own good intentions and win. Being told less is a loss to her, and it's already happening, so there's nothing to weigh.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten lines for asking someone who loves you to do less of something. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is what makes it stick, and reading the answer first only makes it feel obvious.

Say it like you mean it. Every line here can be said two ways. Said quickly, with the pitch rising at the end, it becomes an accusation and you'll be arguing within a minute. Said slowly, a bit lower, with the warmth left in and your eyes still on them, the same words become a request. The difference isn't the vocabulary — it's the speed and the pitch, so slow down more than feels natural and let the last word drop rather than rise.

🚪 Opening the door

Phrase 1

You're about to raise something awkward with someone who won't enjoy hearing it. You want them curious rather than defensive. Which opener gives you the best chance of a fair hearing?

A. "We need to have a conversation about your behaviour."
B. "Don't take this the wrong way, but you're impossible."
C. "Can I say something, and you're probably not going to like it?"
C — "Can I say something, and you're probably not going to like it?"
A sounds like a manager about to begin a formal process, and B tells them how to feel and then insults them in the same breath, which more or less guarantees a row. C works because it does two jobs at once: it asks permission, so they're allowed to say when, and it warns them honestly that this won't be comfortable, which means they aren't ambushed and can brace themselves rather than bite back.

🧾 Naming it without starting a row

Phrase 2

Before the complaint lands, give them credit for the motive behind it — because they aren't being unkind, they're being unhelpful, and those need saying differently. Put the pieces in order:

becauseI knowyou care.do ityou only
"I know you only do it because you care."
The word doing the work is "only" — it says the caring is the whole reason, with nothing worse hiding underneath it, so they don't have to defend themselves before you've even finished. Say this first and the complaint that follows arrives as information rather than as an attack; leave it out and they'll spend the rest of the conversation proving they meant well.
Phrase 3

Now the evidence. You want something they can't wave away as your mood or your memory. Which of these is the hardest to argue with?

A. "You're always giving me advice."
B. "You never listen — you just tell me what to do."
C. "You've done it about four times since I got here, and I've counted, which probably tells you something."
C — "You've done it about four times since I got here, and I've counted, which probably tells you something."
"Always" and "never" are opinions dressed up as facts, so A and B can be beaten by a single exception and usually are. C gives a number from the last two hours, which they were present for, and then does something cleverer: "I've counted" admits how much this has been on your mind, and "which probably tells you something" hands them the conclusion instead of stating it, so they reach it themselves. Note the softening "about" — you're not building a legal case, you're telling them it's got to you.

🙅 Saying what you actually want

Phrase 4

They've answered a question you never asked, and now they're pointing out that you brought it up in the first place. Two opposite verbs, one sentence — complete it: "I wasn't ___, I was just ___ you what's going on."

"I wasn't asking, I was just telling you what's going on."
This doesn't argue about whether their advice was good, which is a fight you can't win and don't need — it corrects what your words were for. Sharing something and requesting help look identical from the outside, so people who love you guess, and they usually guess wrong in the direction of helping. Be warned: said flatly this one stings, so it wants a gentle voice and often an apology just behind it.
Phrase 5

Here's the actual ask, and it needs to be small enough to say yes to. Not "stop giving me advice for ever" — just this, just now. Put the pieces in order:

withoutCould youfixing it?for a bit,just listen
"Could you just listen for a bit, without fixing it?"
To "fix" a problem here means to solve it, and "without fixing it" names the exact habit while leaving the person alone, which is why it doesn't wound. Every other word is there to shrink the request: "could you" rather than "stop", "just" rather than "only ever", "for a bit" rather than "from now on". A small ask gets agreed to; a large one gets negotiated.
Phrase 6

They're worried that if they stop advising, they stop being any use to you at all. Reassure them that the door isn't shut — complete it: "When I want your opinion, I promise I'll ___ ___ ___."

"When I want your opinion, I promise I'll ask for it."
Notice it's "when", not "if", which quietly promises that the asking will happen rather than merely allowing that it might. What you're doing is trading: they give up guessing when you need help, and in return you take on the job of saying so. That's a fair swap and it sounds like one — and it only holds if you then actually ask them something, which is why it helps to point at a time you already did.
Phrase 7

They push back, a bit hurt: "And do what instead? Sit there nodding?" They genuinely can't picture what listening without helping would even look like. In your own words, tell them how little you need — and name the actual words that would be enough. Say it out loud first, then reveal.

e.g. "Honestly, 'that sounds hard' would do. That's all I'm after."
What makes this land is that you hand them the script — three words, which is so little that refusing looks absurd, and it answers the real fear underneath their question, which is that they'd be sitting there useless. "That's all I'm after" means that's the only thing I want, and its job is to close the subject: no more is coming, this was the whole request. If you can quote the sentence you want to hear, quote it.

💛 Why it matters

Phrase 8

Stop talking about how it makes you feel and name the thing they're losing, which is already happening whether they like it or not. Complete it: "It makes me tell you ___, and I don't want that."

"It makes me tell you less, and I don't want that."
"It annoys me" is a complaint they can weigh against their good intentions, and good intentions usually win. This isn't a complaint at all — it's a consequence, and it's a consequence they mind, because being told things is most of what they want from you. The second half, "and I don't want that", is what keeps it from sounding like a threat: you're not withdrawing, you're reporting something you're both losing.
Phrase 9

Explain why you're saying any of this out loud rather than quietly going a bit vaguer with them every year. Put the pieces in order:

what I say.I'd ratherthan starteditingtell you things
"I'd rather tell you things than start editing what I say."
To "edit" what you say is to cut and shape it before it leaves your mouth, so that what they hear is a tidied version of your life with the difficult parts taken out. That's the honest reason the conversation is happening: the alternative to this awkward twenty minutes isn't peace, it's a slow drift into telling them nothing much. "I'd rather" makes it a choice you're making in their favour, which is exactly what it is.

🤝 Landing it

Phrase 10

They've gone quiet, and they're wondering whether this was a small request or the beginning of losing you. Put that right — complete it: "Nothing's changed — I'm not ___ ___ ___ at you."

"Nothing's changed — I'm not having a go at you."
To "have a go at" someone is British English for telling them off or attacking them, and it's worth saying you aren't, because from the inside that's precisely what it can feel like. "Nothing's changed" is the important half: it says the relationship is exactly where it was this morning and that one thing being different doesn't put anything else at risk. Say it while you're still in the room, still eating, still staying — the ordinariness is doing as much work as the sentence.

🔑 Make them yours

These lines aren't only for mothers. They work for anyone who helps too hard — a partner, a friend, a colleague who redrafts everything you send. Pin three of them to a real person in your own life, so you'll reach for them when it happens rather than three days afterwards. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"Can I say something, and you're probably not going to like it?" — who would you need this opener for, and what's the awkward thing behind it?

"Could you just listen for a bit, without fixing it?" — make the ask small enough that they could say yes today. What exactly are you asking them to do less of?

"It makes me tell you less, and I don't want that." — what does their helping actually cost them, in your case?

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your kitchen table. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer — nobody gets thirty seconds to compose a sentence with their mother sitting opposite them.

Swap sides, and this is the harder half. You're the mother. You've helped this person their whole life, it's how you show love, and they've just told you it isn't landing. Be hurt — that's honest — but hear it. Taking this well is a real skill, and most people never manage it.

The Twist

😬 She says yes — and then you need her

One more round, no script. This time Sylvia agrees straight away, and rather too well: "Fine. Understood. I'll say nothing." And then she does exactly that — she goes quiet, and starts checking herself mid-sentence, and it's horrible. Meanwhile you remember why you actually came: there's the money for the deposit, and Ben's school place, and you genuinely wanted to know what she thought. Now asking her feels like proving her right, and not asking her means you've traded her advice for her silence and lost the good half. Start the timer and get through it. Can you ask her properly, out loud, without handing back everything you just agreed — and without pretending the last ten minutes didn't happen? One rule: "never mind, it doesn't matter" counts as losing.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I gave her credit for meaning well before I said what was wrong.
  • My evidence was something that happened, not "you always do this".
  • My ask was small enough that a reasonable person could say yes today.
  • I said what she loses by it — not just how it makes me feel.
  • She knew, by the end, that she hadn't lost anything and I wasn't leaving.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Advice is often love in the wrong shape. Someone can see you struggling, they can see a way through, and sitting still while you work it out yourself feels to them like watching and doing nothing — so they hand you the answer, and you hear something else entirely, which is that they don't think you'd have got there.

With parents it's worse, because for years they were right and it was their job to be. Nobody sends a letter saying the job has ended. The child grows up somewhere else, out of sight, and turns up at Sunday lunch fully formed, and the mother across the table still has thirty years of habit and no new instructions.

Which is why saying it, once, plainly, is a kindness rather than a betrayal. The alternative isn't peace — it's the slow editing, the shorter answers, the fine thanks, until you're both perfectly polite and neither of you knows anything. Better an awkward twenty minutes before the crumble.

Two bigger questions

  1. In your family, and in your culture, is unasked-for advice a sign of love or a sign of disrespect? Would this conversation even be sayable out loud where you grew up — and if not, what do people do instead?
  2. Sylvia says her own mother was exactly like this, and that she swore she'd never be. Why is it so much easier to see the habit in the person above you than in yourself — and what have you already caught yourself doing to someone younger?
