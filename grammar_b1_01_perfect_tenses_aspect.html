Getting Things Done: "I've tidied it"

For a finished job — the action is over, and the result is what matters now.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday updates:

  • "I've tidied my desk — much better now."
  • "She's finished the report early."
  • "We've fixed the printer, at last."

Before you check: what two ingredients appear in every bold phrase?

Check the pattern CLICK
have / has + past participle (tidied, finished, fixed). No time word is needed — it's about the result you can see or feel right now.

A fact in the past vs a result now

"I washed the car yesterday."
→
"I've washed the car — look, it's clean!"

Both sentences are correct English. The first is just a fact about yesterday. The second connects the action to a result you can point to right now.

How Long Have You Been...?

For an activity or a duration — something that started in the past and is still going, or has just stopped.

Spot the pattern

  • "I've been learning Spanish for two years."
  • "She's been working from home since March."
  • "How long have you been waiting?" — "About ten minutes."

What's the structure this time? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
have / has + been + verb-ing (learning, working, waiting). The focus is the activity itself and how long it's lasted — not a finished result.

for + a length of time ("for two years", "for ten minutes")

Result or Activity?

Same present perfect family, two different jobs. And a few verbs that only ever do one of them.

Spot the pattern

  • "I've read that book." vs "I've been reading that book all week."
  • "He's eaten." vs "He's been eating my snacks again!"
  • "I've known her since school." (there's no continuous version of this one)

What's different in meaning between the first two pairs? And why do you think the third pair only has one form? Say your ideas before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
The simple form (I've read, he's eaten) points to the finished result. The continuous form (I've been reading, he's been eating) points to the activity itself — sometimes still going, sometimes just stopped, and it can even sound a bit annoyed ("he's been eating my snacks again!"). Know describes a state, not an action, so it doesn't take the continuous form. Other state verbs behave the same way: like, love, want, believe, own, need, understand.
have / has + past participle → a finished result
have / has + been + verb-ing → an activity or duration
(state verbs like know, like, want, need don't usually take the continuous form)
