METAR & TAF — the report and the forecast

You already read the METAR — the weather now. Its partner is the TAF (Terminal Aerodrome Forecast) — the weather to come, usually for the next 24 to 30 hours. A pilot plans with the TAF and lands on the METAR.

The four words that run a TAF:
FM (from) — a quick, lasting change from a stated time.
BECMG (becoming) — a gradual change over a period.
TEMPO (temporary) — short-lived changes, each lasting under an hour.
PROB30 / PROB40 — a 30% or 40% chance of the condition.

Read this forecast aloud, then say what it means

TAF EGLL 121100Z 1212/1318 22010KT 9999 SCT035 BECMG 1214/1216 24015G25KT TEMPO 1216/1220 6000 SHRA PROB30 1218/1220 3000 TSRA
Roughly: “Heathrow forecast, issued at eleven hundred on the twelfth, valid from midday on the twelfth to eighteen hundred on the thirteenth. Wind two-two-zero degrees, one-zero knots; visibility ten kilometres or more; scattered cloud at three thousand five hundred feet. Becoming, between fourteen and sixteen hundred, wind two-four-zero degrees, one-five knots, gusting two-five. Temporarily, between sixteen and twenty hundred, visibility six thousand metres in rain showers. Thirty per cent probability, between eighteen and twenty hundred, of visibility three thousand metres in a thunderstorm with rain.”

01:00
Roughly: “Wind two-zero-zero degrees, one-two knots; visibility good; broken cloud at two thousand feet. Becoming, late evening, wind lighter from two-four-zero at six knots, visibility dropping to three thousand metres in mist, cloud broken at four hundred feet. More words of your world

The vocabulary that separates a Level 4 from a Level 5 — precise words for the conditions that actually threaten aircraft. Tap each card to check yourself.

mistReduced visibility, but still 1 km or more (code BR).
fogThick — visibility below 1 km (code FG). Freezing fog is worse still.
hazeFine dust or smoke reducing visibility (code HZ) — dry, not wet.
turbulenceRough, bumpy air that shakes the aircraft.
icingIce forming on the aircraft — it adds weight and spoils the wings.
a squallA sudden, sharp increase in wind for a short time.
a microburstA violent, localised downward blast of air — extremely dangerous near the ground.
towering cumulus (TCU)A tall, growing cloud — a thunderstorm in the making.
embeddedHidden inside other cloud — “embedded cumulonimbus” the pilot can't see coming.
a downpourA sudden burst of very heavy rain.
to set inTo arrive and stay: “the fog has set in.”
to lift / to clearWeather getting better: “the fog is starting to lift.”
Visibility falls to six hundred metres in a grey, wet murk. Is that mist or fog?

On the radio — standard words

Radio English uses a small set of fixed words so that nothing is misheard. Using them correctly is exactly the kind of clear, confident interaction the examiner rewards. Tap to check yourself.

RogerI have received all of your last message. (Not “yes”.)
WilcoI understand and will comply.
AffirmYes.
NegativeNo / that is not correct / not permitted.
StandbyWait and I will call you.
Say againRepeat all, or part, of your message.
Read backRepeat this message back to me exactly.
ConfirmIs this correct? / I request confirmation of…
CorrectionI made an error; the correct version is…
How do you read?How clear is my transmission? (Answer 1–5.)

The unexpected — more Part 2

The heart of the test, again, with harder situations. Remember the three moves: describe clearly, advise, and clarify if you're unsure — and never go silent.

Situation 1 — the pressure is falling

Over the last hour the pressure has fallen sharply and the wind has begun to back and strengthen — a cold front is approaching fast. Brief the tower on what is happening and what to expect. One minute.

01:00
Something like: “Tower, be advised the pressure is falling rapidly and the wind is backing and strengthening — a cold front is approaching from the west. Expect it to arrive within the hour, bringing a sharp wind shift, heavy rain, possible thunderstorms and a risk of wind shear. I'd recommend warning inbound traffic and standing by for a rapid change. I'll issue an updated report shortly.”
Situation 2 — it's freezing

The temperature has dropped below zero, freezing fog is forming and the runway is starting to ice over. Report the conditions and their danger to the tower.

01:00
Something like: “Tower, temperature is now minus two and falling. Freezing fog is forming, so visibility is dropping and ice is building on the runway and on parked aircraft. Braking action is likely to be poor, and aircraft will need de-icing before departure. I'd recommend a runway inspection and a check of the braking action as soon as possible.”
Situation 3 — a pilot reports wind shear

An aircraft on final approach reports “a sudden loss of airspeed at three hundred feet — possible wind shear.” Confirm you understood, and pass a warning to the other inbound traffic.

00:45
Something like: “Roger, wind shear reported on final at three hundred feet, sudden loss of airspeed. All stations, be advised: wind shear reported on the approach to runway two-seven. Exercise caution and be prepared to go around.” (Confirm what you heard, then warn everyone clearly.)
Situation 4 — the sensor fails

Your automatic weather sensor has failed and is giving no readings. You must switch to a manual observation. Tell the tower what has happened and give them a manual report of the current conditions.

01:00
Something like: “Tower, be advised the automatic weather station has failed — I am now providing manual observations. Estimating the wind at two-two-zero degrees, one-five knots; visibility approximately six kilometres; cloud broken at one thousand five hundred feet; temperature one-two. I'll update you every few minutes until the system is back.” (Say clearly that the readings are manual and estimated.)

Part 3 — describe the picture

Describe this picture (about 90 seconds)

Start wide (“This is an airfield early in the morning, in thick fog…”), then the detail (the faint runway, the limp windsock, the dim sun, the low visibility), then what it means for flying.

01:30
Useful words: thick fog / very low visibility / the runway disappears into the murk / the windsock is hanging limp, so the wind is light / a pale sun trying to break through / conditions below landing minimums / flights likely to be delayed or diverted.

Then discuss (2 minutes)

Choose one and speak for two minutes:
  • Should an airport close automatically in bad weather, or should a human always make the final decision? Why?
  • Is aviation weather becoming more extreme? What changes have you noticed yourself?
  • Some people say pilots and controllers rely too much on automation. Do you agree?

Before you finish — be honest with yourself

  • I said every number digit by digit, with clear stress.
  • I used the standard radio words correctly (“Correction”, “Say again”, “Read back”).
  • I described the change over time with the right tenses (falling, becoming, will drop).
  • When I lost a word, I explained it another way instead of stopping.
  • I finished my consonants cleanly — no extra vowel on the end.
Any box you couldn't tick is your target for next time — and it's exactly what the examiner is listening for.
