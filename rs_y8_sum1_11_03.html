YEAR 8 · UNIT 5: ETHICS

Humanist Ethics — Reason and Empathy

How do we know what's right without religion?

Can We Be Ethical Without Religion?

About 4 million people in the UK are non-religious. They're not religious, but they're still ethical people. How do they decide what's right and wrong? Humanism offers one answer.

Question 1: What is Humanism?

🔒Think about non-religious ethics!

Answer: Humanism is a worldview that emphasises reason, science, and human wellbeing as the basis for ethics. It doesn't rely on religious authority or belief in the divine.

Question 2: Do you need religion to be moral?

🔒Think about this carefully!

Answer: Many would say no. Non-religious people are often deeply ethical. Religion can support ethics, but it's not necessary for them. Reason and empathy can do the work.

Meeting Humanist Ethics

No Divine Command, But Natural Morality

Humanists don't believe ethics comes from God. Instead, they argue that ethics emerges naturally from human nature and reason. We naturally want to reduce suffering and promote wellbeing. That's where ethics comes from.

The Golden Rule Across Cultures

A remarkable fact: virtually every culture and religion has a version of the Golden Rule: "Treat others as you would like to be treated." This isn't unique to religion. It's a natural ethical principle humans discover everywhere.

The Golden Rule in different traditions:
  • Christianity: "Do unto others as you would have them do unto you"
  • Islam: "None of you believes until he wishes for his brother what he wishes for himself"
  • Judaism: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow"
  • Buddhism: "Treat not others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful"
  • Humanism: "Act only in accordance with that maxim whereby you can, at the same time, will that it should become a universal law"

Utilitarianism: The Greatest Good

Many Humanists embrace utilitarianism—a philosophy that ethics is about maximising wellbeing and minimising suffering for the most people. This is a consequentialist approach that doesn't require any religious belief.

Key thinkers: Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill developed utilitarianism in the 19th century as a rational, non-religious approach to ethics.

Empathy as a Foundation

Modern humanists emphasise empathy—the ability to understand and share others' feelings. Through empathy, we naturally care about reducing others' suffering. This is the foundation of humanist ethics.

Peter Singer's Expanding Circle

Philosopher Peter Singer argues that ethics is about expanding our circle of concern. We start caring about our family, then our community, then our nation, then all humanity, then animals. The ethical life is one of growing empathy for all sentient beings.

Key insight: For humanists, you don't need to believe in God to be ethical. Reason, empathy, and the desire to reduce suffering are enough.

Exploring Humanist Approaches

Science and Ethics

Humanists trust science to help us understand ethics. Neuroscience shows us that humans are naturally empathetic. Psychology shows us what makes people happy and unhappy. Ethics should be based on this understanding, not on ancient texts or faith.

Reason and Dialogue

When people disagree about ethics, humanists favour dialogue and reason. We should discuss our values openly and try to find common ground. We shouldn't appeal to religious authority—instead, we should appeal to shared human values and reason.

Secular Society

Humanists believe that society should be based on secular principles—not religious ones. Laws should be justified by reason and evidence, not by religious belief. Everyone's freedom of conscience should be protected, including the freedom not to believe in God.

Question 3: Can a secular society still be ethical?

🔒Think about this question!

Answer: Humanists say yes. Many secular societies (like Norway, Denmark, Japan) have low crime, high happiness, and strong ethical cultures. Ethics doesn't require religion.

Applying Humanist Ethics

Scenario: Assisted Dying

A person is terminally ill and in terrible pain. They ask for help ending their life. A religious ethicist might say it's against God's will. A humanist would say: the person's suffering matters more than religious rules. Their wellbeing and autonomy (their right to choose) matter.

Question 4: Is this utilitarian thinking?

🔒Think about outcomes!

Answer: Yes. The utilitarian calculus weighs the person's suffering against other considerations. If helping them die reduces their suffering more than keeping them alive, the ethical choice is to help.

Evaluating Humanist Ethics

Strengths

  • Based on reason and evidence, not faith
  • Focuses on human wellbeing and happiness in this life
  • Values freedom of conscience and secular equality
  • Can dialogue with all worldviews since it appeals to shared human values

Challenges

  • How do you measure and compare different wellbeings? Whose wellbeing counts?
  • Can utilitarian calculations justify harming minorities for the greater good?
  • Does empathy give us enough guidance in complex dilemmas?
  • Religious people argue that secular ethics lacks the depth of religious tradition
The conversation continues: Humanist and religious ethicists disagree about many things, but both can work toward wellbeing, justice, and compassion.

Consolidating Your Understanding

Key Ideas

  • Humanism offers a secular approach to ethics based on reason and empathy
  • The Golden Rule appears across all cultures, suggesting universal ethical principles
  • Utilitarianism—maximising wellbeing—is a common humanist approach
  • Humanists believe science and dialogue can guide ethical decisions

Key Vocabulary

  • Humanism: A worldview based on reason, science, and human wellbeing
  • Utilitarianism: Ethics based on maximising wellbeing and minimising suffering
  • The Golden Rule: Treating others as you'd want to be treated
  • Empathy: Understanding and sharing others' feelings
