Encounter: What Is Humanism?

A Non-Religious Worldview

Humanism is a philosophical worldview based on reason, evidence, and empathy — not on belief in God or religion. Humanists argue that we don't need divine authority to be moral or to live meaningful lives. Instead, morality comes from understanding human wellbeing and using scientific thinking to solve problems.

The Core Ideas of Humanist Ethics

No Divine Authority Needed

Religious people say "God told us how to behave." Humanists say we can work out right and wrong ourselves through reason and evidence, without needing a god to tell us. Morality is human-made, not divinely revealed.

The Golden Rule Everywhere

Every major culture — religious and non-religious — teaches the Golden Rule: treat others as you want to be treated. If we all follow this, we can be good without religion. The rule proves morality is universal human wisdom, not unique to religion.

Human Wellbeing as the Measure

Humanists ask: "What promotes human flourishing and reduces suffering?" Right actions are those that increase wellbeing for people. Wrong actions cause harm. This is practical ethics focused on real human life, not heavenly rewards or divine punishment.

Scientific Thinking for Ethics

Humanists apply scientific method to ethics. We test ideas against evidence. We look at what actually reduces poverty, disease, and conflict. We don't need faith — we need data and careful reasoning about what works.

Key Humanist Thinkers

Peter Singer

Peter Singer is a modern humanist philosopher who argues for expanding our circle of moral concern. He challenges the assumption that only humans matter morally. He asks: why can we harm animals for food or entertainment when they can suffer just like we can? He also founded the Effective Altruism movement — the idea that we should use evidence and reason to do the most good with our resources.

The Amsterdam Declaration

Written in 2002, the Amsterdam Declaration is the humanist manifesto. It states that humanists are committed to freedom, reason, and compassion. It says humanism is a philosophy based on human dignity, equality, and the responsibility to improve life for all people — without needing religious beliefs.

Comparing Religious and Humanist Ethics

Religious Ethics

Source: God, sacred texts, religious authority. Motivation: Obedience, fear of punishment, hope for reward. Example: "Don't lie because God commands it."

Humanist Ethics

Source: Reason, evidence, empathy. Motivation: Care for human wellbeing, desire to reduce suffering. Example: "Don't lie because it damages trust and harms people."

Key insight: Both religious and humanist ethics often reach the same conclusions (don't harm people, be honest, care for the vulnerable), but they give different reasons. Humanists argue that the reason doesn't matter — what matters is the outcome: people living good, flourishing lives.

The Big Question

Is God necessary for morality? Religious people might say yes — if there's no God, how can anything be truly right or wrong? But humanists say no — we can figure out right and wrong through reason and care for each other. What do you think?

Evaluate: Is Religion Necessary for Morality?

Religion is necessary for strong morality

Fear of God's punishment and hope for divine reward are powerful motivators. Without belief in an all-knowing judge, some people might cheat or harm others. Religious communities also provide support networks that encourage good behaviour. Millions of people find meaning and morality through faith. If you remove that foundation, you risk moral collapse.

Religion is not necessary for morality

Studies show humanists and atheists are just as moral as religious people. We don't need fear of punishment to be good — empathy and reason are enough. The Golden Rule appears in every culture, religious and non-religious, showing morality is universal human wisdom. Scientific ethics can tackle real-world problems using evidence. Religious motivation can actually cause harm — justifying violence "in God's name" or oppressing people who don't share the faith.

Different people need different frameworks

Some people genuinely find that faith gives them meaning and guides their ethics. Others find the same meaning through reason and human connection. Neither approach is superior — what matters is that people live ethically and treat others well. The world needs both religious and humanist voices. Rather than argue about whether God is necessary, we should focus on shared values: reducing suffering, promoting justice, and respecting human dignity.

Key Terms

Humanism
A worldview based on reason, evidence, and empathy that doesn't require belief in God or religion. Humanists believe morality comes from human wellbeing and scientific thinking.
Secular
Not religious; separate from religion. Secular ethics are based on reason and evidence rather than faith or divine commands.
Empathy
The ability to understand and share the feelings of others; to imagine what someone else is experiencing and care about their wellbeing.
The Golden Rule
The ethical principle to treat others as you want to be treated. It appears across all major religions and cultures.
Reason
The ability to think logically and rationally; to work out right and wrong through argument and evidence rather than faith or emotion.
Peter Singer
A modern humanist philosopher who argues for expanding moral concern to animals and founded the Effective Altruism movement.
Effective Altruism
A movement that uses evidence and reason to work out how to do the most good for the most people with available resources.
Atheism
The belief that God does not exist. An atheist does not believe in any god or gods, but can still be moral using reason and empathy.
