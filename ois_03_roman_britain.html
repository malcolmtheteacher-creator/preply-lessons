Bridge to Today

Caesar came and went. But 100 years later, the Romans returned — and this time they stayed for nearly 400 years. They built roads, towns, walls, and a civilisation. They brought the Christian faith. And in the end, they left, and Britain had to learn to defend itself.

Today we follow H.E. Marshall through the glory and the end of Roman Britain.

II. Building the Wall — Roman Britain in Prosperity

The Conquest: 43 AD — Emperor Claudius & Agricola

The Romans under Emperor Claudius invaded Britain in 43 AD with 40,000 troops. They did not leave quickly. Over the next 400 years, they transformed the island.

One of the greatest Roman governors was Julius Agricola. Marshall describes him:

When Vespasian was Emperor, he sent a man to govern Britain. This man was called Julius Agricola. He was a very clever soldier and a very wise man. He not only took away many of the heavy taxes which the Romans had made the British pay, but he built schools and had the people taught to read and write. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter VI
The historian's note: Agricola was indeed a remarkable governor (78-84 AD). He pushed the frontier north into Scotland, won a great victory at the battle of Mons Graupius in 84 AD, and brought Romanisation to Britain more thoroughly than any governor before or after. Archaeological evidence shows he built roads, forts, and civilian towns. His biography was written by his son-in-law Tacitus, so we know far more about him than most Roman officials.

Roman Roads, Towns & Civilisation

Marshall was fascinated by how the Romans transformed British life:

Before the Romans came, the Britons had been very ignorant and wild. But now the Romans taught them many things. They taught them how to make good roads, how to build grand houses instead of little huts, and how to read and write. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter VI
The historian's note: Marshall's picture of the pre-Roman Britons as "very ignorant and wild" reflects Victorian prejudice more than archaeological reality. However, the Romans did introduce substantial infrastructure: over 3,000 miles of paved roads, fortified towns like Londinium (London), aqueducts bringing fresh water, and public buildings. But the Britons were not blank slates — they had skilled metalwork, established trade routes, and a sophisticated legal system. Roman rule brought a particular type of urbanisation and writing, not civilisation from scratch.

Hadrian's Wall: 122 AD

Emperor Hadrian came to Britain around 122 AD and found the northern frontier troublesome. The Picts and Caledonian tribes had never been conquered. So Hadrian ordered the building of a wall across the island.

Emperor Hadrian came to Britain, and he found the wild people of the north very troublesome, so he built a wall across Britain from the Tyne to the Solway. This wall which Hadrian built is still called by his name, and is still to be seen to this day. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter VI

The wall was begun in 122 AD and stretched 73 miles (now 80 km) from the Solway Firth in the west to the River Tyne in the east. It was not merely a military barrier but a statement of power — a line saying: "This far Rome rules, and no further."

The historian's note: Hadrian's Wall was extraordinary for its time: built entirely of stone, 15 feet high in most places, with forts every mile and larger garrisons at intervals. It took seven years to build (122-130 AD), involving thousands of soldiers and British labourers. However, it was not the "barbarians outside, Romans inside" frontier it appears. Trade continued across it, and many soldiers' families lived in the civilian settlements on the north side. The wall's purpose was as much about controlling and taxing movement as about blocking invasion. The Picts never mounted a full invasion across it.

Roman Towns in Britain (that still exist)

Londinium (London) — the capital, with a port on the Thames

Aquae Sulis (Bath) — named for its hot springs, a spa town where the wealthy took healing baths

Eboracum (York) — a legionary fortress and garrison town in the north

Calleva Atrebatum (Silchester) — a planned Roman town with a regular grid of streets

All of these settlements had paved roads, public buildings, shops, temples, and bathhouses. The Romans brought to Britain not just military power but urban life.

III. The Martyr & the Departure — The End of Roman Britain

St. Alban: The First British Christian Martyr

Around the year 305 AD, Christianity came to Britain. And with it came persecution. Marshall tells the story of St. Alban, the first British martyr.

The first Christian martyr in Britain was called Alban, and he lived in the town called Verulamium. When soldiers came to kill the Christians, a holy man called Amphibalus came to hide in Alban's house. Alban hid him and talked to him every day, and Amphibalus told him all the story of Christ. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter VII

Alban was converted to Christianity by Amphibalus. When soldiers came looking for the priest, Alban put on Amphibalus's robes and gave himself up instead.

When the soldiers came for Amphibalus, Alban dressed himself in the priest's clothes, so that they might catch him instead and let Amphibalus go free. The soldiers were deceived, and Amphibalus escaped. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter VII

Alban was brought before the Roman Governor and commanded to deny his faith. He refused.

The Governor asked Alban what god he worshipped. "I worship the true and living God who created all things," Alban answered. The Governor ordered him to sacrifice to the Roman gods, but Alban refused. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter VII

For his refusal, Alban was sentenced to death. He was led up a hill and beheaded with the sword. The town changed its name from Verulamium to St. Albans, in his honour.

The historian's note: St. Alban is a legendary figure — the story comes from much later Christian sources (4th-6th centuries) and may be partly mythical. However, there is likely a historical core: a Christian British martyr executed by Roman authorities. The persecution of Christians in Britain was real, particularly after 303 AD when the Emperor Diocletian ordered a general persecution. The town of Verulamium was a major Roman settlement (now St. Albans), and the naming of the town after a martyr is a pattern we see in early Christian Britain. Whether the details of Alban's story are historical or hagiographic, his legend marks the moment when Christianity, which would eventually transform Britain, first took root here.

The Weakening of Rome: 300-400 AD

By the year 300 AD, the Roman Empire was cracking. The legions that had once seemed invincible were needed elsewhere. Germanic tribes were pressing the Rhine frontier. The Goths threatened the Danube. Rome could no longer spare troops for Britain.

Around 370 AD, the Saxon Shore forts were built along the east coast to guard against Germanic and Saxon raiders. But Rome itself was in trouble. In 410 AD, something unthinkable happened.

410 AD: Rome Abandons Britain

The Visigothic King Alaric attacked Rome itself. Emperor Honorius, desperate to save Italy, withdrew the last Roman legions from Britain. For the first time in nearly 400 years, Britain was on its own.

Nearly five hundred years after Julius Caesar's first coming, the Romans gave up and left Britain altogether. The soldiers sailed away, and the people were left to defend themselves. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter VIII
The historian's note: The historical moment was even more stark than Marshall suggests. In 410 AD, the Roman Governor of Britain and the Roman military units formally ended their administration. Emperor Honorius wrote that Britain could no longer expect help from Rome. Archaeological evidence shows a sharp break: Roman pottery stops being imported, the military infrastructure is abandoned, trade collapses. However, "Roman" Britain didn't simply vanish — many Romano-British people remained, towns continued (though declining), and some Roman administrative structures lingered for decades. The withdrawal was the beginning of a long transformation, not an instant collapse. Christianity remained strong. But the age of Roman roads, legions, and imperial rule was over.

The Legacy of Roman Britain

What did Rome leave behind? Roads — many of which are still used today. Towns and cities — London, Manchester, Lancaster all began as Roman forts. The English language — full of Latin words: window, street, kitchen, wine, cheese. The idea of writing and law. And, most importantly, Christianity, which the Romans had finally allowed to flourish, and which would guide Britain through the chaos to come.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these discussion questions to reflect on.

What did the Romans really bring?

Marshall calls the Britons "very ignorant and wild," but archaeology shows they were already skilled farmers and artists. What does it mean when one culture tells the story of "civilising" another?

Why build Hadrian's Wall?

The Romans never conquered Scotland. So they built a wall. But what kind of barrier is a wall? Who does it keep out, and who does it keep in?

The sacrifice of St. Alban

Alban dressed up as a priest to save another man, then died rather than deny his faith. What does that story tell us about the arrival of Christianity in Britain?

400 years, then gone

Rome ruled Britain for nearly four centuries. Then, in 410 AD, it was over. What happens to a society that depends on a distant empire for 400 years, then is suddenly left alone?

Roads that lasted

Many Roman roads in Britain are still used today. Why do some things from the past last for millennia, while empires fall?

The legacy question

Which was more important for Britain's future: Roman roads, Roman Christianity, or the fact that Rome left, forcing the Britons to stand on their own?
