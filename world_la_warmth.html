Warm, Loud and Touchy?

Latin Americans are imagined as warm, loud and very physical — kisses, hugs and standing close. The reality varies by country, by city and by situation.

The stereotype: "Latin Americans are all warm, loud and touchy." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Kisses and hugs

In much of Latin America, friends — and often new acquaintances — greet each other with a kiss on the cheek. In Argentina and Uruguay, one kiss is common, even between male friends. In Brazil, the number varies by region, from one to three. Hugs, called "abrazos", are common between friends, and in Mexico an "abrazo" with a pat on the back is a classic greeting between men.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many kisses are common in Argentina?

2. What is an abrazo?

1. One.

2. A hug or embrace.

Discuss:
  • Would you feel comfortable kissing a new acquaintance?

Part 2 — Distance and volume

In the 2017 study of personal space across 42 countries led by Agnieszka Sorokowska, Argentinians preferred the smallest distance from strangers of all the countries studied — much smaller than most Europeans. In many Latin American conversations, overlapping talk and interruptions can show interest and enthusiasm rather than rudeness. However, more reserved styles are also common, for example in parts of the Andes.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the 2017 study find about Argentinians?

2. What can overlapping talk show?

1. They preferred the smallest distance from strangers.

2. Interest and enthusiasm.

Discuss:
  • Is interrupting always rude?

Part 3 — Not one style

Business greetings are usually more formal than social ones: a handshake is safer for a first meeting, and titles such as "Licenciado" or "Doctora" are important in some countries. Highland Bolivians or Peruvians may be quieter and more reserved than people from coastal Brazil or the Caribbean. Many Latin Americans themselves notice differences between their countries — and find foreigners colder than they are.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is safer for a first business meeting?

2. Which titles can matter?

1. A handshake.

2. Licenciado, Doctora.

Discuss:
  • Do you adapt your greeting to the situation?

Grammar — Comparatives & Superlatives

Cultural differences are comparisons. To compare fairly and precisely, English uses comparatives and superlatives — with modifiers.

How it works:
• -er / more … than: "Greetings are warmer in the south."
• (not) as … as: "Finns are not as touchy as Italians."
• modifiers: much, far, a bit, slightly + comparative.

Going deeper

The "warm and touchy" stereotype comes from real differences in greetings and personal space, often noticed by visitors from cooler-contact cultures. It turns into a cliché when it is applied to everyone — or when "warm" slides into ideas of being emotional or unprofessional, which are unfair and inaccurate.

Worth noticing: Use "much/far/a bit" before comparatives, "the" before superlatives, "not as… as" for unequal comparisons.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Mirror and adapt. In Latin America, follow your host's lead: if they lean in for a kiss, accept one (usually right cheek); in business, start with a handshake and use titles. Don't step back if someone stands closer than you're used to — it's not aggression.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Why do greetings differ so much?
  • Is warmth cultural or personal?
  • How will you greet people next time?
