The story, in three parts

Part 1 — Why they were built

The three great pyramids of Giza were raised about 4,500 years ago, during the age Egyptologists call the Old Kingdom. To understand why so much effort was poured into them, you have to understand what the Egyptians believed. The pharaoh was not merely a king; he was thought to be a living god, the link between his people and the heavens. When he died, it was believed that he did not simply vanish. He passed into the afterlife — and if his body and his soul were properly protected, he would go on watching over Egypt forever.

For that, the corpse had to survive. So the royal body was embalmed with great care, dried and wrapped over many weeks, and then it had to be sealed somewhere no flood, no sand and no thief could ever reach. The pyramid was the answer: a vast, sacred tomb built to last for eternity, its shape perhaps meant to echo the rays of the sun on which the king would climb to the sky.

The greatest of the three was commissioned by the pharaoh Khufu around 2560 BC. When it was finished, it stood 146 metres high and would remain the tallest structure made by human hands for nearly four thousand years. Two more pyramids were added by Khufu's son Khafre and his grandson Menkaure, and around them a whole city of temples, causeways and smaller tombs was laid out on the desert plateau.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the ancient Egyptians believe about the pharaoh and about death?

2. Who commissioned the greatest of the three pyramids, and what record did it hold?

1. The pharaoh was believed to be a living god; when he died his body had to be preserved and protected so his soul could pass safely into the afterlife and keep watching over Egypt.

2. It was commissioned by the pharaoh Khufu (around 2560 BC); at 146 metres it was the tallest human-made structure on Earth for almost four thousand years.

Discuss:
  • A whole society organised its resources around what would happen after one man died. What does that tell you about how the ancient Egyptians saw death?

Part 2 — How they were built

How were they built? For centuries the honest answer was: nobody was quite sure. The Egyptians had no iron tools, no pulleys and no wheels for transport, yet the Great Pyramid alone contains well over two million blocks of stone, some weighing more than fifty tonnes. For a long time it was imagined that the work was done by armies of slaves driven under the whip. That picture, we now know, is mostly wrong.

The pyramids were built by a paid, organised workforce — skilled craftsmen and gangs of ordinary Egyptians who were housed, fed and cared for near the site. A workers' town has been excavated close to the pyramids, and in it were found bakeries, breweries and even the tombs of workers who died on the job, buried with honour in the shadow of the monument they had raised. The great limestone blocks were quarried nearby, while the finer white casing stone was floated down the Nile by boat and the granite for the inner chambers was brought over a thousand kilometres from the south.

On site, the blocks were most likely hauled up long ramps of mud brick and rubble, dragged on wooden sledges over sand that was wetted to reduce the friction. Everything was measured with extraordinary care: the base is almost perfectly level, and the four sides are aligned to the points of the compass to within a fraction of a degree. It is a feat of organisation as much as of muscle — tens of thousands of people, fed and directed for two decades, to build a single tomb.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What has the workers' town near Giza revealed about who actually built the pyramids?

2. How were the huge stone blocks most likely moved and raised into place?

1. That they were built not by slaves but by a paid, organised workforce who were housed, fed and cared for, with bakeries, breweries and even honoured graves for workers who died.

2. The blocks were quarried nearby (or shipped down the Nile), dragged on wooden sledges over wetted sand, and hauled up long ramps of mud brick and rubble.

Discuss:
  • For centuries people assumed the pyramids must have been built by slaves. Why do you think that idea took such a strong hold, and why might it matter that it was wrong?

Part 3 — What became of them

The pyramids were designed to keep their treasures safe forever, and in this one aim they failed almost completely. Within a few generations, the burial chambers were broken into and plundered. The gold, the jewellery and the sacred objects that had been sealed inside with the king were carried off by robbers, some of whom may have been the very workers who knew the passages best. By the time archaeologists entered the great chambers in the modern age, they were bare.

Time stripped the outside too. The Great Pyramid was once encased in a smooth, gleaming skin of white limestone, its point crowned by a capstone, so that in the sun it must have blazed like a mountain of light. Over the centuries much of that casing was quarried away and used to build the city of Cairo, leaving the rough, stepped core we see today. The great stone Sphinx that guards the site lost its nose to the ages, and the desert crept steadily closer.

And yet they endured. When the Greek writers drew up their list of the Seven Wonders of the ancient world, the pyramids were placed at the very top — and they are the only wonder still standing. They have been studied, measured, argued over and marvelled at for more than four thousand years. Empires have risen and fallen around them; the pyramids have simply remained, the oldest surviving monument to a civilisation's belief that death could be defeated.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to the treasures the pyramids were built to protect?

2. Why does the Great Pyramid look rough and stepped today when it did not originally?

1. Within a few generations the chambers were broken into and plundered; the gold and sacred objects sealed inside were stolen, and the chambers were bare by the modern age.

2. Its smooth white limestone casing was quarried away over the centuries and reused to build Cairo, leaving only the rough stepped core.

Discuss:
  • The pyramids failed at their one real purpose — protecting the king's body and treasure — yet we call them a triumph. Can something be a failure and a masterpiece at the same time?

Grammar — the passive voice

Notice how the whole story of the pyramids is told: the pyramids were built, the stones were dragged, the tombs were plundered. We rarely name the individual workers, because we don't know them and, honestly, it isn't the point. When what happened matters more than exactly who did it, English reaches for the passive voice.

How it's formed: the correct tense of be + the past participle of the main verb.
• Present simple: "The pyramids are visited by millions every year."
• Past simple: "The Great Pyramid was built around 2560 BC."
• Present perfect: "A workers' town has been excavated nearby."
• Modal: "The blocks must have been dragged up ramps."
Why we choose it:
• The doer is unknown or unimportant: "The tombs were plundered." (we don't know exactly who by)
• We want to focus on the action or the object, not the doer: "The Great Pyramid was finished in about twenty years."
• To add the doer, use by: "The pyramids were built by a paid workforce." Leave by… out when it doesn't matter.

1. Put it in the passive

Rewrite the underlined part in the passive voice, in the tense given. Write only the passive verb phrase.

1. Workers dragged the blocks up the ramp. → The blocks up the ramp. (past simple)

2. Robbers have stolen the treasure. → The treasure . (present perfect)

3. Millions of tourists visit the pyramids each year. → The pyramids by millions each year. (present simple)

2. has been stolen (has been + past participle)
