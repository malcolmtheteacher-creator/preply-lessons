The Neighbour's Noise

Three nights of bass through the floorboards, and a door you have never once knocked on. The music is the easy part. The hard part is that you'll be standing next to this man at the bins every Tuesday for the next five years.

The Scene 🎬

It isn't loud, exactly. That's almost the worst of it. If it were loud you'd have rung someone by now. Instead it's a low thud coming up through the floorboards — you can't hear the tune, only the shape of it — and it has been going until about two in the morning, three nights this week. You have lain there in the dark composing furious speeches you will never give, and then got up at seven feeling like a wrung-out cloth.

So this evening, before you can talk yourself out of it, you go down a flight of stairs and knock on a door you've lived above for two years without ever touching. And there he is: Tomasz. Faintly grey around the edges, holding a mug, blinking at you. He has no idea. None. You can see it land on his face in real time, and honestly, it's a bit awful to watch.

Here's the thing. You could win this. You could recite the times and the dates and the number of hours' sleep and leave him in no doubt. And then you'd have to nod at him by the bins every week until one of you moves out. Winning isn't the goal. Sleeping and still being able to say hello — that's the goal, and it's much harder.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Have you ever put up with something for weeks rather than mention it to someone once? What was it, and what were you actually afraid would happen if you spoke?
  2. Think of the neighbours you've had. Which ones did you know by name — and does knowing someone's name make it easier to complain to them, or much harder?

Who's who

You
The One Who Hasn't Slept 🥱

Exhausted, mildly furious at three in the morning, and now — face to face with an actual person holding a mug — mostly just embarrassed. You want the music down. You also want, quite badly, not to become the neighbour everyone avoids.

The other voice
Tomasz, Downstairs 🎧

Friendly, a bit hungover, and genuinely had no clue any of it travelled. The moment he understands, he's mortified — and a mortified person is only one bad sentence away from getting defensive instead.

First: read the conversation in the next tab, and watch what You keeps doing — at every single turn, You leaves Tomasz a way out of the embarrassment. A reason it isn't his fault. A door marked you're not a bad person. That's not politeness for its own sake; that's the reason he ends up agreeing instead of digging in. Count the exits.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. One of these two knocked on a door to make a complaint. The other one is the one who did something wrong. So why is the person complaining apologising more than the person who kept them awake — and what does all that apologising actually buy?

2. Nobody tells Tomasz what's causing the noise. He works it out himself, out loud, halfway through a sentence. What is it — and why does it matter that he found it rather than being told?

3. Right at the end, You offers Tomasz something that isn't about the music at all. What is it, and why is that the line that means they can still say hello on the stairs?

1. The apologies are the exits. Every "sorry", every "it's fine", every "I'm not asking you to stop" tells Tomasz that he isn't being put on trial, which means he never has to defend himself — and someone who isn't defending himself is free to just agree. If You had arrived with the times and the dates and none of the softening, Tomasz would have spent the whole conversation protecting his own dignity rather than solving the problem.
2. The speaker is standing directly on the floorboards, so the bass goes straight into the structure of the building. It matters because it's his discovery: he says it himself, twice, working it out as he goes. A fix you find yourself is one you actually carry out, whereas being told what you've done wrong invites you to argue about it.
3. You tells him where you live and invites him to complain about you — bang on the ceiling, come and knock. That turns two strangers with a grievance between them into two neighbours with an arrangement, and it quietly says that neither of you is the problem here; the building is.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for complaining to someone you can't get away from — a neighbour, a colleague, a brother-in-law. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. All ten of these die if you say them briskly, because brisk sounds rehearsed, and rehearsed sounds like a complaint that's been building for a fortnight — which it has, but he mustn't hear that. Let yourself be a bit awkward. Trail off. Let the sentences come out slightly wrong. Doorstep English is nobody's best English, and a person who sounds like they'd rather not be doing this is much easier to say yes to than a person who sounds prepared.

🚪 Knocking without starting a war

Phrase 1

The door opens. You have about two seconds to signal that you know exactly how this looks, and that you're not enjoying it either. Which opener does that?

A. "I've come about the noise."
B. "Sorry to be that neighbour."
C. "Do you have any idea what time you were up until?"
B — "Sorry to be that neighbour."
"That neighbour" is the one everybody has a story about: net curtains, notes in the hallway, complaints about the recycling. By naming him and apologising for resembling him, you show you know the role you've just walked into and you don't want it — which tells the person at the door, before you've said anything else, that this isn't who you are. A goes straight in with a charge, and C is an accusation wearing a question mark.
Phrase 2

You can feel that whatever comes next is going to sound heavier than you want it to, so you say so in advance. Put the pieces in order:

than I mean it.going to soundThis is probablyworse
"This is probably going to sound worse than I mean it."
It's a warning label you stick on your own sentence before you say it, which lets the other person hear the complaint at a lower volume than the words alone would carry. "Probably" is doing quiet work in there too — it hedges, so you're not even confident about your own warning, and that little wobble is what stops the whole thing sounding managed.

🗣️ Saying the thing, plainly

Phrase 3

Now you have to actually say it. You've got three versions in your head at two in the morning. Which one gets you what you want?

A. "The music from your flat has been audible until approximately two a.m. on three separate nights this week."
B. "It's just the music, the last few nights — I've not really slept."
C. "You might want to think about keeping it down a bit sometimes, maybe."
B — "It's just the music, the last few nights — I've not really slept."
A is a charge sheet — the dates, the times, the word "separate" — and it tells him you've been keeping a file on him, which is frightening. C is so hedged he can nod, agree, and change nothing, because you've never said what's wrong. B is the only one that does both jobs at once: it says the fact ("the music"), and it says the cost ("I've not really slept"), and the cost is the part that moves people. Nobody changes their evening because of a decibel. They change it because you're tired.
Phrase 4

He's gone pale. He now thinks you're here to end his social life. Take that off the table — put the pieces in order:

having people over.I'm not asking youHonestly.to stop
"I'm not asking you to stop having people over. Honestly."
Saying what you're not asking for is often more useful than saying what you are, because the thing he's braced for is always bigger than the thing you actually want. "Have people over" means to invite friends to your home — it's the ordinary, friendly phrasing, and choosing it rather than something like "your gatherings" keeps his life sounding normal rather than like a problem to be managed. The "Honestly" on its own afterwards is what makes it believable, because it's the bit you'd say if you meant it.

🤲 Making it easy for them to say yes

Phrase 5

Time to name a time — but as a question he could still say no to, not a rule you've brought down with you. Complete it with one informal adjective, built from the verb do plus -able: "Would midnight be ___, do you think?"

"Would midnight be doable, do you think?"
"Doable" means possible, manageable, something a person could realistically pull off — and it's casual in a way that "acceptable" or "possible" aren't, so it lands as a favour being asked rather than a term being set. Then "do you think" hands him the last word. He can still say no, at least in theory, which is exactly why he says yes.
Phrase 6

You've suggested a time. Now give him room to suggest a different one. Which line genuinely hands him the decision, rather than just checking he agrees with yours?

A. "So midnight, then?"
B. "What would work for you?"
C. "Is midnight okay or not?"
B — "What would work for you?"
A and C both look like questions but they only offer him your answer, with a yes-or-no attached; B is an open question, so the sentence is genuinely his to finish. It costs you almost nothing — he'll usually land on something close to what you suggested anyway — and it buys you a man who chose the arrangement rather than one who was handed it.

🪟 Giving them the exit

Phrase 7

Leave him a version of events where he isn't a bad neighbour at all, just someone who had a couple of unusual nights. Complete the chunk: "If it's a ___-___, forget I said anything."

"If it's a one-off, forget I said anything."
A "one-off" is something that happens once and isn't going to become a habit — a one-off payment, a one-off mistake — so offering him that word is offering him an identity he can accept, because "I had a couple of late ones" is a thing a reasonable person can be, while "I'm the man who keeps the building awake" isn't. And "forget I said anything" is the exit itself: you've handed him permission to make the whole conversation disappear, which is precisely why he won't need to.
Phrase 8

Now turn the whole thing round. In your own words, invite him to picture himself standing where you're standing — and get him to admit he'd have knocked too. Say it out loud before you reveal.

e.g. "You'd tell me if I was doing it to you, wouldn't you?"
Two things are happening here. The imagined swap puts him on your side of the door for a second, so complaining stops being an attack and becomes the normal thing neighbours do. And the tag question — "wouldn't you?" — asks him to agree out loud, which means by the time he's answered, he has agreed that knocking was fair. He can't be annoyed with you for doing the thing he's just said he'd do.

🪜 Ending on the stairs, not in court

Phrase 9

Before you go, make the arrangement run both ways — tell him where to find you if you're ever the one keeping him up. Complete the chunk: "Right — well, I'm in number four if I'm ever the ___."

"Right — well, I'm in number four if I'm ever the problem."
Up to this point you've been the one with the grievance and he's been the one in the wrong, which is an uncomfortable shape for two people who share a staircase. This line dissolves it, because by inviting him to complain about you, you've made it a mutual arrangement rather than a verdict — and it gives him something to do with all that embarrassment, which is to imagine a future in which it's your turn.
Phrase 10

Last line before the door shuts. You want him to go back inside feeling like he's just met a decent person, not like he's just been told off. Which one does that?

A. "So we're agreed, then. Midnight."
B. "Anyway. Sorry again. It's genuinely fine."
C. "Well, let's hope I don't have to come back down."
B — "Anyway. Sorry again. It's genuinely fine."
A closes it like a meeting, which reminds him it was one. C is a threat with a smile on it, and he will remember that line every time he sees you. B does something slightly odd and very English: it apologises for the complaint, refuses to make anything of it, and lets him off — and "anyway" is the signal that the subject is now closed and neither of you has to keep standing here. It's the difference between an ending and a verdict.

The Twist

😬 Friday

He said sorry. Of course he did — he was lovely about it. He moved the speaker. And then on Friday it starts again at half eleven and it's still going at one, and you're lying there in the dark knowing you have to go down those stairs a second time. Here's the problem: everything that worked last week is gone. You can't say "sorry to be that neighbour" — you already are that neighbour now, twice over. You can't offer him the one-off; you used it. And "forget I said anything" has been rather comprehensively disproved. Start the timer and knock anyway. What's left when the warm opening has been spent? One rule: you may not use a single phrase you used last week, and the moment you get sarcastic — "so, midnight, was it?" — you've lost him, and you've lost the bins for five years.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said what the problem was and what it had cost me — no list of dates, no vague hinting.
  • I took the big scary thing off the table by saying what I wasn't asking for.
  • I asked rather than told, and he could have said no to me.
  • I left him at least one way of not being the villain in this.
  • I'd be happy to run into him at the bins tomorrow — and so would he.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

A block of flats is a strange arrangement when you think about it: dozens of people sleeping within a few feet of each other, sharing floors and walls and a bin store, and knowing more or less nothing about one another. We hear each other's showers and arguments and terrible taste in television, and we pass on the stairs and say "alright?" and mean nothing by it, and that's the deal.

Which is why we'll do almost anything rather than knock. We'll lie awake for a month, draft the note, not leave the note, mention it to everyone except the one person who could fix it — because knocking makes it real, and once it's real you can't un-know each other. A stranger you resent is easier to live above than a neighbour you've had a scene with.

And yet the whole thing runs on people occasionally being brave enough to go down a flight of stairs and be a bit awkward for ninety seconds. Nobody writes about it and nobody thanks you for it, but that small, embarrassed, badly-worded diplomacy is roughly what makes a city liveable rather than just crowded.

Two bigger questions

  1. Why is it so much easier to complain to a company than to a person — to spend forty minutes on hold rather than ninety seconds on a doorstep? What are we actually protecting when we avoid the person?
  2. Some cultures knock; some leave a note; some go straight to the landlord or the council; some simply endure it. What's the normal move where you're from — and what does that choice say about how that place thinks people should live near each other?
