Politics Around the World: Myth vs How It Really Works

8 lessons on how different countries run themselves — each one opens with a common belief and finds out how the system really works.

Kings who do not rule, presidents who cut ribbons, countries where you can be fined for not voting: politics looks very different from one country to the next. Start with Where Does Power Sit?, then work through the topics. Each lesson asks three questions — what is the common belief, how does the system really work, and why does the belief exist? — and finishes with practical tips for reading and talking about politics across cultures.

These lessons explain systems and the language of debate. They do not tell you which party, leader or policy is right. When opinions differ, we look at how people argue about it, so that you can join a discussion with confidence and respect.

1Where Does Power Sit?
Systems of government
Crowns, presidents and prime ministers: three very different ideas of power. Before comparing countries, it helps to know who actually decides.
The stereotype: “A country with a king or queen is ruled by the king or queen.”

Leaders, parties and the rules that shape who governs.

2Prime Minister or President?
Leaders and elections
Voters in the USA choose a president. Voters in the UK choose MPs, and the prime minister comes from that. What difference does it make?
The stereotype: “In America people elect the leader; in Britain they don't.”

3Two Parties or Many?
Parties and coalitions
In the USA the choice is usually between two parties. In Germany and the Netherlands, no single party usually wins, so parties must form a coalition.
The stereotype: “Elections are always a choice between two big parties.”

6Are the Nordics Socialist?
Welfare states and markets
Nordic countries are known for high taxes and generous public services. But are they socialist, or just wealthy market economies with a big welfare state?
The stereotype: “The Nordic countries are socialist.”

Votes, referendums and the duty to turn up.

4Should Everyone Vote on Everything?
Direct democracy
Swiss voters are asked to decide on specific proposals several times a year. Is direct democracy the ideal, or a risky shortcut?
The stereotype: “Switzerland is a perfect democracy: the people decide everything.”

5Should Voting Be Compulsory?
Turnout and the duty to vote
Some countries treat voting as a right. Others treat it as a duty. Australia, Brazil and Mexico show three different ways of making that duty real.
The stereotype: “Voting is a free choice everywhere — nobody can make you vote.”

💬 How people argue2

Debate styles and the news that frames them.

7How Do Politicians Argue?
Debate styles
British MPs can shout across the chamber. US candidates debate by the clock. Elsewhere, much of the work is done in committees and behind closed doors.
The stereotype: “Politicians everywhere argue the same way: loudly.”

8Who Tells Us the News?
Media and framing
Public broadcasters, commercial channels and social media feeds all tell the news differently. Who pays for the story changes how it is told.
The stereotype: “The news is just the facts, and every country gets the same ones.”
