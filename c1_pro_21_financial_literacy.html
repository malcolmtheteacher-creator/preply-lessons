Which company is in the most danger?

Three small companies. Read the facts, decide, and explain your choice before you reveal the answer.

Company A

  • Profit last year: $400,000
  • Cash in the bank: $150,000
  • Spending more cash than it receives: $50,000 a month
  • Customers pay after 90 days

Company B

  • Loss last year: $200,000
  • Cash in the bank: $900,000
  • Cash is growing every month
  • Customers pay a year in advance

Company C

  • Profit last year: $100,000
  • Cash in the bank: $300,000
  • Cash is stable
  • Customers pay after 30 days
Company A. It is the only one with a healthy profit, and it is the closest to disaster. With $150,000 and a $50,000 monthly cash outflow, it has about three months left. Its profit is real on paper, but the money is stuck with customers who pay slowly. Company B shows a loss and is perfectly safe, because customers pay in advance. Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact.

The vocabulary of the boardroom

Eight terms, in two rounds of four. Say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. For every $100 of sales, the product itself costs $40 to make or deliver, so $60 is left to pay for everything else. The business keeps a 60% gross margin.
gross margin (n): (revenue minus direct costs) ÷ revenue. It tells you whether the product itself makes money, before rent, salaries and marketing. A margin falling from 80% to 55% is a warning about price or cost.
2. The company is spending $200,000 more cash than it receives each month. Its burn rate is $200,000.
burn rate (n): how fast a company uses up its cash. To burn cash; a high-burn start-up. It matters most for businesses that are not yet profitable.
3. With $800,000 in the bank and a burn rate of $200,000 a month, the company has a runway of four months.
runway (n): cash ÷ burn rate, which is how long the company can survive before it runs out of money. We have 18 months of runway; we're running out of runway.
4. Customers have received the goods and been sent an invoice, but they haven't paid yet. That money, owed to the company, sits on the balance sheet as receivables.
receivables (n, plural): money customers owe you. It counts as sales in the profit figure but is not yet cash. Rising receivables can mean customers are paying more slowly. The opposite is payables: money you owe to suppliers.

B · Which term means…?

Four terms: EBITDA · depreciation · leverage · overhead. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means the gradual write-down of an asset's value over its useful life, recorded as a cost but with no cash leaving the company?
depreciation (n): a delivery van is depreciated over five years. A non-cash expense, which is one reason profit and cash differ.
2. Which term means using borrowed money to finance the business, which increases both the possible returns and the risk?
leverage (n): a highly leveraged company; to leverage debt. It is measured with the debt-to-equity ratio.
3. Which term means profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, often used to compare the operating performance of different companies?
EBITDA (n, said "ee-bit-dah"): it strips out financing and accounting choices. Popular in acquisitions, and criticised because it ignores the real cost of the equipment a business must keep replacing.
4. Which term means the running costs that don't produce revenue directly: rent, insurance, utilities, administration?
overhead (n, often plural in US English "overhead costs"): to cut overheads; high overheads. Cutting it helps margins but can damage the business if you cut too deep.

C · Cover and explain

Only the term is shown. Explain it to someone who has never worked in finance (one sentence each), then reveal.

runway  ·  receivables  ·  gross margin  ·  leverage

runway: how many months of cash are left at the current burn rate.

receivables: money customers owe, counted as sales but not yet received.

gross margin: what's left from each sale after the direct cost of the product.

leverage: using borrowed money, which raises both returns and risk.

Talking about numbers

Knowing the terms is half of it. The other half is describing change clearly. Two small sets to learn, each followed by practice.

Set 1 · Describing change

Costs have outpaced revenue. (grown faster than)

Sales levelled off at around $3 million. (stopped rising or falling)

Margins have been squeezed by rising costs. (reduced under pressure)

Set 2 · Comparing periods

Revenue is up 12% year on year. (compared with the same period last year)

Cash fell from $5.0 million to $0.8 million over two years.

Expenses rose by 56%, while revenue grew by 75%.

Write it down, then say it: three concerns, each with a question.

1 · The gross margin is collapsing. It fell from 80% to 55% while revenue grew. "What is driving the fall in gross margin? Are we discounting to win customers, or is it costing more to deliver?"

2 · Costs have outpaced income. Over two years revenue rose roughly 2.1 times, while operating expenses rose roughly 2.3 times. In Year 3 gross profit ($2.31M) covers little more than half of operating expenses ($4.1M). "At what level of revenue does this model break even, and how realistic is that?"

3 · Receivables are growing far faster than revenue. Money owed by customers rose from $0.3M to $2.0M, more than six times, while revenue only doubled. Part of the "sales" has not been collected. "Why are customers paying more slowly? How much of the receivables is overdue, and are we at risk of bad debts?"

4 · The runway is about four months. $0.8M in the bank with $2.4M a year of cash outflow is $0.2M a month. "What is the plan if we can't raise money before the cash runs out?"

From earlier lessons

1. What are three phrases you can use to politely disagree with someone in a meeting?
For example: "I see where you're coming from, but…"; "I'm not entirely convinced that…"; "With respect, the figures suggest otherwise."
2. What is the difference between although and whereas?
although introduces a surprising contrast with the main idea ("Although sales rose, cash fell"). whereas compares two parallel things ("Sales rose, whereas costs fell").
3. Give two ways to signal that you're about to change the topic in a presentation.
For example: "Turning now to…"; "That brings me to…"; "Let me move on to…"

From today, closed book

Cover the answers. Say each one, then reveal.

1. Because profit includes sales that haven't been paid for yet, and non-cash items such as depreciation, while bills must be paid in cash.
