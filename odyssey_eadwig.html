The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Eadwig's coronation at Kingston was followed by a great feast.
a coronation (n) — the ceremony at which a king or queen is crowned.
2. During the feast the young king chose to withdraw from the hall.
to withdraw (v) — to leave a place or group.
3. What happened next became a scandal repeated for centuries.
a scandal (n) — an event that causes public outrage or shock.
4. Soon afterwards, Eadwig decided to banish Dunstan from the kingdom.
to banish (v) — to send someone away as a punishment.
5. Archbishop Oda had the king's marriage annulled because the couple were too closely related.
to annul (v) — to declare officially that something (e.g. a marriage) is not valid.
6. The surviving accounts were written by monks hostile to Eadwig.
hostile (adj) — unfriendly; opposed.
7. In 957 the nobles of Mercia and Northumbria decided to divide the kingdom by choosing Edgar as their king.
to divide (v) — to split into parts.
8. Eadwig was extremely generous with land, issuing many charters.
generous (adj) — giving more than is expected.
9. His reputation has been shaped almost entirely by his enemies.
a reputation (n) — what people generally think about someone.
10. When he died in 959, his brother was able to reunite England.
to reunite (v) — to bring together again.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The scandal at the feast

Eadwig, a son of Edmund I, became king in 955 when he was only about fifteen. At his coronation feast, according to a biography of Dunstan written some forty years later, the young king left the hall and went to be with a noblewoman, Æthelgifu, and her daughter Ælfgifu. Dunstan and a bishop were sent to fetch him; they found the crown thrown on the floor, and Dunstan dragged the king back to the feast.

The monk who wrote this said that Eadwig had behaved shamefully. But we only have Dunstan's side. Ælfgifu became Eadwig's wife — and the marriage was later annulled by Archbishop Oda, because the couple were too closely related.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the story say happened at the coronation feast?

2. Why was the marriage annulled?

1. Eadwig left the hall to be with two noblewomen, and Dunstan dragged him back.

2. The church said Eadwig and Ælfgifu were too closely related.

Discuss:
  • Why do we need to be careful when the only source is written by the hero's enemy?

Part 2 — Dunstan banished

Soon after the coronation, Dunstan was banished and went to Flanders. Eadwig also gave away great amounts of land: more charters survive from his first full year than from almost any other year of the age. Some historians think he was buying support; others think he was simply rewarding his own friends instead of his uncle's old advisers.

Either way, many powerful nobles were unhappy.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did Dunstan go?

2. What is unusual about Eadwig's charters?

1. Into exile in Flanders.

2. So many survive — he gave away a large amount of land in a short time.

Discuss:
  • Is giving away lots of rewards a smart way to win friends — or a dangerous one?

Part 3 — A kingdom divided

In 957 the nobles north of the Thames — in Mercia and Northumbria — chose Eadwig's younger brother Edgar as their king. Eadwig kept Wessex and Kent. Edgar called Dunstan back from exile.

Eadwig died in 959, aged only about nineteen; the cause is unknown. Edgar reunited the kingdom. Because the monks who wrote the history favoured Dunstan and Edgar, Eadwig was remembered as a foolish boy — a reputation that may be unfair.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How was the kingdom divided in 957?

2. Why might Eadwig's reputation be unfair?

1. Mercia and Northumbria chose Edgar; Eadwig kept Wessex and Kent.

2. The writers were monks who supported his opponents, Dunstan and Edgar.

Discuss:
  • Can a young person be fairly judged on their actions at fifteen?

Grammar — Reported Speech

Much of what we know about the past comes from chroniclers reporting what people said. Reported speech moves the tense back and changes the pronouns.

How it works:
• "I will return," he said → He said that he would return.
• "We have lost," she said → She said that they had lost.
• Reporting verbs: claimed, promised, warned, insisted, denied.

2. Dunstan said, "I will bring him back." → Dunstan said that he him back.

3. Oda declared, "This marriage is not lawful." → Oda declared that the marriage lawful.

Going deeper

Eadwig is a test case in reading sources. Almost everything colourful we "know" about him comes from writers who loved Dunstan and Edgar. Modern historians see a teenage king trying to escape the control of his family's old advisers, and failing.

Worth noticing: reported speech often hides opinion: words like claimed and denied tell us whether the writer believes the speaker.
Learning from the past — for today: Before you share a story about someone, ask who first told it — and what they gained. Reputations online can be built by one hostile account. Check a second source before you judge.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Would Eadwig's reputation be different if his friends had written the history?
  • Was splitting the kingdom wise or dangerous?
  • How much should we forgive the mistakes of the young?
