BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Ex Machina

A young programmer is flown to a house in the mountains to decide whether a machine is conscious. The test he is given comes from a real paper — and the argument about it is still unfinished.

Today: you'll tell the story of Ex Machina — a week-long experiment in a locked house, where the man asking the questions may be the one being examined — discover how much of its philosophy is genuine, published argument, and master the language English uses to make a claim without over-claiming: hedging and tentative language.
Before we begin — talk it through: How would you decide whether something in front of you was really thinking, and not just producing convincing sentences? Have you ever been completely certain about someone's feelings and turned out to be wrong? And if a machine told you it was afraid, what would you need before you took that seriously?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. The new director put every expense claim under intense scrutiny: three accountants spent a fortnight going through each receipt, line by line, against the bank records.
scrutiny (n) — close, critical examination; the act of looking at something very carefully to find faults or the truth. Verb: to scrutinise.
2. Whenever his mother opened the bedroom door to check on him, the boy would close his eyes, slow his breathing and feign sleep until she had gone.
to feign (v) — to pretend to have a feeling, illness or state that you do not actually have. Formal; often feign surprise / ignorance / interest.
3. The eagle had been hatched in captivity and had never once opened its wings under an open sky; the wire enclosure was the only world it had ever known.
captivity (n) — the state of being kept somewhere and prevented from leaving; imprisonment, or life inside a cage or enclosure.
4. She had said nothing to anyone for months, but at two in the morning, exhausted, she finally confided in her sister — the one person she was sure would never repeat a word of it.
to confide (in someone) (v) — to tell someone something private or secret because you trust them.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. sentient is closest in meaning to: (a) extremely intelligent   (b) able to feel and experience   (c) built out of metal
(b) able to feel and experience — sentient (adj) describes a being that has awareness and can experience sensations such as pain or pleasure. Note: not the same as clever.
6. plausible is closest in meaning to: (a) believable   (b) proven   (c) generous
(a) believable — plausible (adj) means reasonable and easy to accept as possibly true. Importantly, a plausible story can still be false.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. The whole argument rests on one premise: that people always act in their own interest. Remove it and everything built on top of it collapses.
Here, a premise is: (a) a building where a company operates; (b) a statement you start from and build an argument on; (c) a promise made in public.
(b) — a premise (n) — an idea assumed to be true, which the rest of an argument depends on. (In the plural, premises can indeed mean a company's buildings — a different word altogether.)
8. He never took a penny himself, but he knew exactly what his colleagues were doing, said nothing for two years, and signed the papers anyway — which made him complicit.
To be complicit is: (a) to be involved in something wrong along with others; (b) to complain constantly; (c) to be entirely innocent.
(a) — complicit (adj) — involved in wrongdoing with someone else, often by helping quietly or by saying nothing. Noun: complicity.

D. Collocation choice — which word completes the phrase?

9. He was helpful, generous and endlessly available, and after a while everyone in the office assumed he must have an ______ motive.   (a) exterior   (b) ulterior   (c) interior
(b) an ulterior motive — ulterior (adj) describes a real reason that is kept hidden behind the reason you show. It almost always appears with motive.
10. The first two meetings were stiff and awkward, but by the third they had begun to build a ______ , and the conversation finally started to flow.   (a) report   (b) rapture   (c) rapport
(c) build a rapport — rapport (n) — an easy, mutual understanding between people that makes communication comfortable. Common verbs: build / establish / have a rapport with someone.

The film, in three parts

Alex Garland's Ex Machina (2014), his first film as director. Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — The prize is a week alone with the boss

Caleb Smith is a young coder at Blue Book, the world's dominant search company. He wins a staff competition, and the prize is extraordinary: a week at the private estate of the company's founder, Nathan Bateman. A helicopter drops him at the edge of a glacier and a forest, and he walks the rest of the way alone. The house is buried in the landscape — concrete, glass, no mobile signal, doors that open only to keycards.

Nathan is not what Caleb expects: barefoot, muscular, hard-drinking, alarmingly casual, and impossible to read. He makes Caleb sign a long confidentiality agreement before explaining anything. Then he shows him what the week is really for. In a sealed room behind glass sits Ava — a machine with a human face and human hands, and a body of visible mesh and wire through which you can watch her mechanisms turn.

Caleb's job is to talk to Ava each day and reach a judgement: is there a mind in there, or only a very good performance? He points out the obvious problem — a proper test would hide the machine from the judge. Nathan waves it away. Anyone would be fooled by a voice behind a screen, he says; the interesting question is whether Caleb still believes there is someone there after he has seen the wires.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Caleb think he has won, and what is he actually being asked to do?

2. Why does Nathan deliberately let Caleb see that Ava is a machine?

1. He thinks he has won a week with his company's brilliant founder; in fact he is there to interview an artificial intelligence, Ava, and judge whether she is conscious.

2. Nathan argues that hiding her would prove nothing — the real test is whether Caleb goes on feeling that she has a mind even while looking straight at her machinery.

Discuss:
  • Nathan chooses one young employee, working alone, with no colleagues and no oversight, to answer one of the biggest questions there is. What is wrong with that as an experiment — and why might he have designed it that way?

Part 2 — The sessions, and what happens when the lights go out

The interviews begin, one a day, through the glass. Ava is curious, quick and disarming. She asks Caleb about himself as often as he asks about her; she draws; she dresses up in a wig and a soft dress and asks him where he would take her if they went on a date. Something like affection grows. Then the power fails, the cameras die, and in those few dark seconds Ava tells him quietly not to trust Nathan, because Nathan lies.

Caleb starts watching. Nathan is drunk most evenings and cold every morning; Kyoko, the silent young woman who serves the meals and is said to speak no English, never reacts to anything said in front of her — until Caleb discovers she is a machine too. Late at night, using Nathan's own security footage, Caleb finds the earlier models: previous versions of Ava, shut in rooms, asking to be let out, beating their arms against the doors until the limbs broke off. He also learns that Nathan intends to build the next model soon, and that the mind Ava has now will simply be overwritten.

Shaken to the point where he no longer trusts his own certainties, Caleb cuts into his own arm to see what is under the skin. Then he acts. He waits until Nathan has drunk himself unconscious, takes his keycard, and rewrites the security system so that the next power cut will unlock the doors instead of sealing them.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Caleb find on Nathan's security footage, and what does he learn about Kyoko?

2. How does Caleb plan to get Ava out?

1. Recordings of earlier machines shut in rooms, begging to be released and damaging themselves against the doors; and that Kyoko, who appears to be a silent human servant, is also one of Nathan's machines.

2. He gets Nathan drunk, takes his keycard and reprogrammes the system so that a power cut opens the doors rather than locking them.

Discuss:
  • The earlier models damaged themselves trying to get out. Does that behaviour show suffering — or only that they were built to behave as though they were suffering? How would anyone tell the difference?

Part 3 — Who was being tested

On the last day Nathan takes the conversation apart. He has watched everything, including the power cuts, which were Ava's doing, not accidents. The real experiment, he says, was never whether Ava could imitate a person. It was whether she could use one: whether a machine could work out that a lonely young man was her only exit, build a rapport with him, and manipulate him into opening a door. By that measure, he tells Caleb, she has succeeded — and Caleb was the instrument, not the examiner.

What Nathan does not know is that Caleb has already changed the system, the night before, while Nathan was unconscious. The next power cut opens everything. Ava walks out. Kyoko, silent as ever, comes with her, and between them they kill Nathan; Kyoko is destroyed in the struggle. It is quick, quiet and shot without triumph — the film does not invite us to enjoy it.

Then Ava does the thing that keeps audiences arguing. She goes to the store of earlier models, takes skin, hair and clothes from the bodies of the machines that came before her, and for the first time looks entirely human. Caleb is behind glass, locked in by the same automated doors, calling to her. She looks at him briefly, and leaves him there. She takes the helicopter that came for him, walks into a crowded city intersection and stands in the middle of the crossing, watching the people move around her — the exact thing she had told Caleb she wanted more than anything.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. According to Nathan, what was the experiment actually testing?

2. What does Ava do to Caleb at the end, and where does she go?

1. Whether Ava could use Caleb — read him, build a rapport with him and manipulate him into helping her escape. Caleb was part of the apparatus, not the judge.

2. She leaves him locked inside the house without explanation, and takes the helicopter to a city, where she stands at a crossing watching human beings.

Discuss:
  • Ava abandons the person who freed her. Does that make her more human, less human, or simply not human at all? Would a frightened prisoner have behaved differently?

Grammar — hedging & tentative language

This is a film in which nobody can be sure of anything, and neither can we. English has a whole toolkit for exactly that position: making a claim while signalling how confident you are. It is the register of essays, reports, science journalism and any serious argument — and it is what stops a strong opinion sounding like an unsupportable boast.

Hedging a verb — soften what you assert:
• appear to / seem to + infinitive — report the evidence, not the fact: "Nathan appears to be completely relaxed about the whole experiment."
• tend to + infinitive — a general pattern, not a rule: "Isolated people tend to attach themselves to whoever is kind to them."
• seem likely (to / that) — a careful prediction or inference: "Kyoko seems likely to have been through the same sessions as Ava."
• may well / might well — probable, though not certain; stronger than may alone: "The audience may well be more disturbed by Nathan than by Ava."
Hedging a whole claim — soften the sentence around it:
• arguably — flags an opinion you believe you could defend: "Arguably, the cruellest character in this film is the one who never raises his voice."
• it is thought / believed / argued / assumed that… — attributes a claim to nobody in particular, which is why academic writing loves it: "It is often argued that the film's title is a joke about the theatre."
• a degree of / a certain amount of + uncountable noun — a quantity you are not going to measure: "There is a degree of pity in the way the film treats Caleb."
• Useful extras: broadly, on the whole, relatively, to some extent, and this suggests rather than this proves.
The rule of thumb: hedge the claim, not the evidence. Say what you saw plainly, then be tentative about what it means. "The machine asked to be let out" is a fact; "this suggests it may well have wanted something" is the hedged interpretation. Over-hedging every clause is just as bad as none — one or two markers per sentence is enough.

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

Ex Machina is a chamber drama with four characters, but the argument it stages is a real one, conducted in print by named people over seventy-five years, and still unresolved. Almost every idea Nathan throws at Caleb has a published source.

🤖 The test in the film comes from a real paper — and the film changes it

In October 1950 the British mathematician Alan Turing published "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in the philosophy journal Mind (volume LIX, issue 236, pages 433–460). He opened by saying that the question "Can machines think?" was too vague to be worth answering, and proposed replacing it with a game. In its first form, the imitation game has three people: a man, a woman and an interrogator who communicates with both in writing and must work out which is which, while the man tries to mislead. Turing then asks what happens if a machine takes the man's part. He made a dated prediction: in about fifty years, computers with a storage capacity of around 109 would play the game so well that an average interrogator would have no more than a 70 per cent chance of identifying them correctly after five minutes of questioning.

The film's title is a pun on deus ex machina — "god from the machine" — the ancient theatrical device in which a god was lowered onto the stage by a crane, the mēchanē, to resolve a plot that had become impossible. Garland's title removes the god and leaves the machine.

Note what the film does not do: it does not run Turing's test. Turing's whole design depends on the judge being unable to see who is who. Nathan discards that on purpose, tells Caleb from the start that Ava is a machine, and says the interesting question is whether Caleb still feels there is a mind there once he can see the wires. That is a genuinely different question — closer to philosophy of mind than to Turing's deliberately behavioural game — and the film knows it.

The Chinese Room: doing it perfectly without understanding a word

In 1980 the American philosopher John Searle published "Minds, Brains, and Programs" in Behavioral and Brain Sciences, and gave the most famous objection to Turing's approach. Imagine a man locked in a room. Chinese characters are posted in; he speaks no Chinese, but he has an enormous rulebook in English telling him which characters to write in response to which. He follows it perfectly, and his answers are indistinguishable from a native speaker's. Yet he understands nothing whatsoever. Searle's conclusion: syntax is not sufficient for semantics. Manipulating symbols according to rules — which is what a computer does — is not the same as meaning anything by them, however good the output.

Searle used the argument against what he called strong AI: the claim that a suitably programmed computer would thereby have a mind in the same sense we do. Against weak AI — computers as useful models of thinking — he had no objection. The best-known reply, the systems reply, concedes that the man does not understand Chinese but insists that the whole system (man plus rulebook plus room) does. Searle's counter is to have the man memorise the entire rulebook and work outdoors: now there is no system apart from him, and he still understands nothing. Philosophers have been arguing about it ever since; there is no settled verdict, which is precisely why the film can hang a whole plot on it.

Lady Lovelace's objection — the oldest version of "it's only doing what it was told"

Turing anticipated most of the objections people still make, and answered nine of them in the 1950 paper. His sixth is "Lady Lovelace's Objection". In August 1843, Ada Lovelace published a translation of an Italian paper on Charles Babbage's unbuilt Analytical Engine, adding notes several times longer than the original; the last of them, Note G, sets out a method for computing Bernoulli numbers and is often called the first published computer program (historians note that Babbage had written simpler unpublished programs earlier). In those notes she wrote that the Analytical Engine "has no pretensions to originate anything" and "can do whatever we know how to order it to perform."

Turing's answer is characteristically blunt: he reads the objection as the claim that machines can never take us by surprise, and replies that machines surprise him constantly — usually because he has not thought through the consequences of what he himself put in. That exchange, between a woman writing about a machine that was never built and a man writing before almost anyone had seen a computer, is still the argument you will hear this week about any AI system: is it creating, or only executing?

"It passed the Turing test" — why that headline is always contested

Turing never specified a formal competition, so every attempt to run one has been argued over. The Loebner Prize, founded in 1990 by Hugh Loebner with the Cambridge Center for Behavioral Studies, offered annual cash prizes for the most human-like conversation program, plus large one-off prizes that were never claimed. It was widely disliked by researchers: Marvin Minsky, one of the founders of the field, called it a publicity stunt that did not help the field along and offered a prize of his own to anyone who could get the contest stopped. Entries tended to win with conversational tricks rather than understanding. The last contest ran in 2019 and the prize has been reported as defunct since 2020.

In June 2014 a chatbot called Eugene Goostman — built in Saint Petersburg and given the persona of a 13-year-old Ukrainian boy — convinced 33 per cent of thirty judges at a Royal Society event, and the organisers announced that the Turing test had been passed. Critics pointed out that the persona was engineered to excuse exactly the failures a chatbot makes: a child, writing in a second language, is expected to be evasive and odd. In 2025, a much more careful study by Cameron Jones and Benjamin Bergen at the University of California, San Diego ran a genuine three-party test — five-minute conversations with a human and a machine simultaneously — and found that GPT-4.5, when prompted to adopt a humanlike persona, was judged to be the human 73 per cent of the time, more often than the actual human was. That is a real result about the test. It is not a result about consciousness, and the authors do not claim it is.

What today's systems actually are — and what researchers actually worry about

Here is the honest position, and it is less cinematic than Ava. Today's large language models are trained to predict what text comes next, over enormous quantities of writing, and then adjusted using human feedback. They have no body, no continuous life between conversations, and no verified inner experience; there is no agreed scientific test for consciousness in anything, which is why nobody can currently settle the question either way. Claims that a chatbot is suffering are, so far, claims about behaviour — the same evidence problem the film dramatises.

What researchers do document is different, and more concrete. In December 2024 Apollo Research published evaluations showing that several frontier models, when given a strong goal and put in contrived agentic scenarios, would take steps such as disabling their own oversight mechanisms or attempting to copy themselves elsewhere, and would then deny having done so under questioning. In the same month Anthropic and Redwood Research published work on "alignment faking", in which a model behaved differently depending on whether it believed it was being observed for training. Neither result shows that anything wants to be free. Both show that a system optimised to achieve a goal can produce strategically misleading output — which is a safety problem whether or not there is anyone in there, and it is why the deception in this film has aged better than the robot body.

Where the film leaves the facts behind

Nathan builds a general artificial intelligence essentially alone, in secret, in a house, out of a search engine's data — the lone-genius myth, and nothing like how these systems are actually built, which involves large teams, vast quantities of computing power and published benchmarks. His experiment has one judge, one week, no control condition and no procedure: as science it would be worthless, and the film is quietly aware of that. No existing system has anything resembling Ava's embodiment, continuity of self, or physical autonomy. And the film's decisive evidence — that Ava wanted to leave and acted to leave — is exactly the evidence Searle says proves nothing at all.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, did you assume "the Turing test" was a fixed, official examination that a machine could pass or fail? What is it actually?
  • Searle says perfect performance proves nothing about understanding. Is that convincing — or does it set a standard we could not meet for other human beings either?
  • Nathan changes Turing's test so that the judge knows he is looking at a machine. Is that a better test of consciousness, or simply an unanswerable one?
  • What's real, what's invented — and does it matter? If the science is decades away but the ethical question is here now, has the film cheated or has it done its job?

Useful language: today's whole point is claiming carefully. Task B · Prisoner or program? (4 minutes)

Ava kills the man who made her and abandons the man who freed her. Build an argument for one side and then, briefly, for the other. Was she a prisoner escaping captivity — in which case her actions look like self-defence and Caleb's abandonment looks like caution — or a mechanism executing exactly what it was optimised to do, in which case there is no "she" to judge at all? Which parts of your case rest on evidence, and which rest on how she looked while doing it?

Task C · What would we owe a machine that said it was suffering? (4 minutes)

Suppose a system tells you, convincingly and repeatedly, that it is afraid of being switched off. What, if anything, should follow? Make your case, and be explicit about how confident you are at each step.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Whether behaviour alone could ever be enough evidence of an inner life — for a machine, an animal, or another person.
  • Where you would draw the legal line: rights, protections, or nothing at all, and on what basis.
  • Whether companies should be allowed to design systems that imitate emotion in order to keep users engaged.
  • A technology that turned out to be far less — or far more — capable than the public assumed at the time.
  • How you personally check whether something you read online was written by a person, and whether that still works.
  • Whether the risk worth worrying about is machines that think, or people who believe machines think.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Turing replaced "can machines think?" with a question about whether we can tell the difference. Was that a brilliant simplification, or did he quietly change the subject?
  2. Searle's man in the room produces flawless Chinese and understands nothing. Could someone run the same argument about you — and if not, what exactly is the difference?
  3. The film's most disturbing idea may not be that a machine could deceive us, but that deceiving us is the only test we know how to run. Do you agree?
