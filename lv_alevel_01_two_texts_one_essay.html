The idea behind the whole paper

Nobody speaks "English." Everybody speaks a version of it — shaped by where you grew up, who you belong to, what you do, and when you happen to be alive. Language varies from person to person and from century to century, and every variation carries information. Paper 1 hands you pairs of texts and asks: read the variation — what does it tell you?

SPEAK Quick test of the idea. One person calls the evening meal "dinner", another "tea", another "supper". Three words, one meal — what might each word quietly tell you about the speaker?
Region and social background, mostly — "tea" leans northern and working-class, "supper" carries an upper-class or old-fashioned shade, "dinner" sits in between and varies by region. No option is wrong; each is a badge. That is the whole subject in one meal.

Where each 30 marks comes from — four equal strands

StrandIn plain words
Method + terminologyfeatures named precisely and quoted, in controlled academic writing
Concepts + theorythe field's big ideas applied to the data, not name-dropped
Contextwho, to whom, when, in what mode — and how that builds meaning
Connections across textsgenuine comparison — likenesses AND differences, threaded through the whole essay

That last strand is the paper's signature. Write about the texts one at a time — Text A essay, then Text B essay — and a quarter of the marks quietly dies. The fix is a habit, and today builds it.

The golden rules

Rule 1: never one text at a time. Every paragraph holds both texts, joined by comparison hinges: whereas while similarly by contrast both — yet

Rule 2: variation is never error. A regional accent, a dialect word, slang, an old spelling — none of it is "wrong English." Each is identity, or history, wearing language. Examiners penalise answers that sneer.

SPEAK Practise rule 2: a speaker says "we was laughing". Give me two descriptions of that — one that breaks the rule, one in proper exam voice.
Breaks it: he uses bad grammar. Exam voice: the non-standard past form "we was" is a common dialect feature that signals group belonging and informality — and for many speakers carries covert prestige (a concept arriving properly in Lesson 2).

The hinges — comparison as a sentence skill

Comparison lives in sentence shapes. Three to master:

ShapeSkeletonExample
Contrast hingeWhile X does A, Y does B"While the blogger performs intimacy through direct address, the Victorian letter constructs distance through titles and full names."
Similarity hingeBoth X and Y do A — though differently"Both speakers reach for humour, though one aims it at himself and the other at his listeners."
Pivot hingeX does A; Y answers it"The advert commands its reader; the review merely nudges — the same persuasive job, two levels of force."

First comparison — small data, full method

Two people describe the same rainy festival. Speaker 1 is being interviewed on local radio; Writer 2 is posting a review online.

Speaker 1: it was chucking it down (.) absolutely chucking it (.) but honestly the whole field just (.) we all just danced harder you know
Writer 2: Torrential rain transformed the site into a quagmire. Verdict: unforgettable. Would I return? Instantly.

Say a full hinge sentence aloud for each angle, then check one strong version:

Angle 1: informality

"While the speaker's colloquial idiom — the repeated 'chucking it down' — performs live enthusiasm, the writer's formal Latinate choices ('torrential', 'quagmire') build considered authority; both, though, are performances of personality for an audience."

Angle 2: sentence shapes

"The speaker chains clauses loosely with pauses and 'you know', typical of unplanned speech, whereas the writer deploys crafted minor sentences — 'Verdict: unforgettable.' — and a self-answered question, compression only planned writing can afford."

Angle 3: audience

"Both address strangers, but differently: the speaker recruits the interviewer with 'you know', assuming shared feeling, while the writer performs for invisible readers who will judge the review itself as writing."

Mode — the engine under Section A

Almost every Section A pair puts writing against speech. The differences are systematic, and naming them is easy marks:

Spontaneous speech has…Crafted writing has…
fillers (er, like, you know), false starts, self-repairrevision — the reader never sees the drafts
pauses (.) doing the work of punctuationpunctuation, layout, capitals — graphology
chained clauses joined by and, so, butsubordination and built architecture
accent, transcribed in /slashes/no voice — but tricks to fake one (brackets, italics, CAPITALS)
a listener present, shaping every turnan imagined reader, addressed blind

The twist worth marks: writers constantly borrow speech (chatty asides, one-word sentences) and skilled speakers produce planned, writing-like passages. When a text crosses the border, say so — border-crossing is where identity shows.

Now you build one

New pair — two people quitting the same job. Write ONE sentence with all five ingredients, then compare.

Text 1 (resignation letter): It is with mixed emotions that I tender my resignation, effective the 30th of June.
Text 2 (spoken, to a friend): I just (.) I walked in and went (.) yeah I'm done (.) sorry (.) and that was that
"While the letter's formulaic formal register — 'I tender my resignation' — performs the professional self the workplace requires, the spoken account's false starts and reported speech ('I walked in and went (.) yeah I'm done') perform the private self among friends; the same event, retold across two registers, shows one person managing two identities."

From memory

1. Sections, marks and minutes of Paper 1?

Two sections, one 30-mark essay each, 2 hours 15 — roughly 65 minutes per essay. Section A: identity across two modern texts. Section B: change across an old and a recent text.

2. The four mark strands?

Terminology + method · concepts + theory · context · connections across the texts.

3. The two golden rules?

Never one text at a time — comparison in every paragraph. And variation is never error — accents, dialects and slang are identity, not mistakes.

4. The three hinge shapes, from memory, with skeletons.

Contrast: While X does A, Y does B. Similarity: Both do A — though differently. Pivot: X does A; Y answers it.
