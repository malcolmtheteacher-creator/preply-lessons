🔍 The EQ Puzzle

Look at these two reactions to the same situation:

Situation: A colleague takes credit for your idea in a meeting.
Person A:
"You always steal my ideas! You're so dishonest. You should be ashamed of yourself."
Person B:
"I feel frustrated because that idea was important to me. I'd like us to find a way to give credit where it's due."
What's the difference between these two responses? Which person would you rather work with? Why?
Person A uses blaming language ("You always..."), commands ("You should..."), and character attacks ("You're dishonest"). This escalates conflict.

Person B labels their own feeling ("I feel frustrated"), takes responsibility for it, and suggests a solution. This is the language of high emotional intelligence — direct, honest, and constructive.

💬 Two approaches to emotions

There's a pattern here. When we're emotional, most of us do one of two things:

❌ Indirect / Blaming
"You're driving like a maniac!"
"You never listen to me."
"This meeting is a waste of time."
✅ Direct / Emotionally intelligent
"I'm scared. Could you slow down?"
"I feel unheard right now."
"I'm frustrated — I'd like to focus."

The emotionally intelligent version does three things: names the feeling, owns it, and moves toward a solution.

Think of a recent moment when you (or someone you know) reacted in the "blaming" way. What would the emotionally intelligent version have sounded like?

🤔 Pattern-hunting together

Look at these emotionally intelligent sentences. What pattern connects each group?

Group 1: "I feel anxious." / "I'm overwhelmed." / "I feel disappointed."

Group 2: "I feel left out." / "I feel hurt." / "I feel taken for granted."

Group 3: "I understand your frustration." / "I realise this is difficult." / "I accept that we disagree."

What's the difference between Group 1, Group 2, and Group 3? What is each group doing?
Group 1 — Labelling feelings: Simply naming the emotion you experience.
Group 2 — Taking responsibility: Naming how a situation makes you feel — without blaming someone else.
Group 3 — Empathising: Acknowledging the other person's experience.

🗂️ The EQ Language Toolkit

Here are six functions of emotionally intelligent language:

FunctionExamples
Labelling feelingsI feel / I am + anxious, frustrated, overwhelmed, relieved, uneasy
Taking responsibilityI feel + hurt, left out, undervalued, taken for granted
EmpathisingI understand / I accept / I realise + that this is hard, how you feel, your concern
SuggestingI/you could / might + try, consider, take a step back
Stating needsI need / I'd like / I want to + be heard, have more time, feel included
Being positiveI'd feel better if... / It would help if... / What if we...

Language to avoid: commands (you should...), obligation (you must...), blaming (you're insensitive, you're making me...)

🔄 Transform it

Take each "low EQ" sentence and transform it into a "high EQ" version. Say your answer out loud, then click to compare.

1. "You're so selfish — you never think about anyone else!"
Transform this — use at least one function from the toolkit.
Possible: "I feel hurt when my needs aren't considered. I'd like us to think about each other more." (labels feeling + states need + suggests)
2. "This is ridiculous. You should have told me earlier."
Transform this — try to use a different combination of functions.
Possible: "I feel frustrated because I didn't have enough time to prepare. It would help if I could know earlier next time." (labels feeling + takes responsibility + positive suggestion)
3. "You're making me angry. Just stop talking."
Transform this — aim for at least two different functions.
Possible: "I'm feeling overwhelmed right now. I need a moment to think before we continue." (labels feeling + states need)

🎭 Real-world scenarios

Choose 3 of these 5 scenarios. For each one, respond using emotionally intelligent language. Speak for at least 30 seconds per scenario.

A — The feedback

B — The friend

A close friend has cancelled plans with you for the third time this month. You're starting to feel like the friendship is one-sided. What do you say to them?

C — The disagreement

In a team meeting, a colleague publicly dismisses your suggestion as "unrealistic." Others are watching. How do you handle it?

D — The boundary

Your boss keeps asking you to stay late. You're exhausted and it's affecting your personal life. How do you raise this issue?

E — The misunderstanding

Someone you care about has completely misunderstood something you said and is upset. They won't let you explain. How do you approach this?
