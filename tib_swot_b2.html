The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — The story everyone told

Open almost any business textbook and you will find SWOT analysis: a simple way to look at an organisation's Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities and Threats. It is used by companies, charities, schools, hospitals and even students planning their careers. It may be the most widely used planning tool in the world.

For decades, the internet and many books repeated the same origin story. In the 1960s, it said, a management consultant called Albert Humphrey led a big research project at Stanford Research Institute in California, studying hundreds of companies to find out why their planning failed. From that project, the story went, came SWOT.

The story came mainly from Humphrey’s own later account, published in 2005. It was repeated so often that it became accepted as fact. But when researchers looked for evidence from the 1960s — reports, papers, documents with Humphrey's name on the idea — they found almost nothing.

1. Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats.

Part 2 — The detectives

A few years ago, Richard Puyt, a researcher in the Netherlands, became curious. If SWOT was so important, why was its history so unclear? Together with two colleagues, Finn Lie and Celeste Wilderom, he began to dig. They searched archives, read old company reports and interviewed people who had worked at Stanford Research Institute decades earlier.

In 2023, they published what they had found. The idea could be traced back to the 1950s, to planners at the aircraft company Lockheed who were trying to understand why long-range plans so often failed. That work led to Robert F. Stewart, who in 1962 became head of a group at Stanford Research Institute studying the theory and practice of planning.

Stewart's group developed a way of helping managers plan together. It asked them to look at what was Satisfactory, what were Opportunities, what were Faults and what were Threats. The acronym was not SWOT but SOFT, and the institute published the approach in 1965. According to the researchers, Humphrey had been a member of the team — but not its leader, and not the inventor.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Puyt and his colleagues search for the truth?

2. Where could the idea be traced back to?

3. What was the original acronym, and what did it stand for?

1. They searched archives, read old company reports and interviewed people who had worked at the institute.

2. To planners at the aircraft company Lockheed in the 1950s.

3. SOFT: Satisfactory, Opportunity, Fault, Threat.

Discuss:

Part 3 — From SOFT to SWOT

Within a few years, the words changed. “Satisfactory” became strengths, and “faults” became weaknesses. SOFT became SWOT, which is easier to say and remember. Around the same time, professors at Harvard Business School were teaching a similar way of thinking: compare a company's internal strengths and weaknesses with the external opportunities and threats around it. The idea spread through business schools and consulting firms until almost everyone used it.

Its simplicity is its strength — and also its weakness. Critics say that many SWOT analyses are just four lists with no decisions. People often overlook the most important question: so what? In 1982, a professor named Heinz Weihrich suggested a fix, the TOWS matrix, which asks how to use each strength to grab an opportunity or defend against a threat.

The story of SWOT has a lesson of its own. Even the world's most famous tools can have a forgotten history, and a fact repeated a thousand times is not necessarily true. Sometimes it takes detectives with patience — and an archive — to find out what really happened.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did “satisfactory” and “faults” become?

2. What is the main criticism of many SWOT analyses?

1. Strengths and weaknesses.

2. They are just four lists with no decisions — they skip the “so what?” question.

3. It asks how to use strengths to seize opportunities or defend against threats — it turns the lists into strategy.

How the tool works

SWOT is a checklist in a grid. The top row is internal — things you control. The bottom row is external — things in the world around you. The left column helps your goal; the right column harms it.

Using it, step by step

  1. Write down the goal first. A strength for one goal can be a weakness for another.
  2. Fill the internal boxes honestly: what are we good at, and what do we do badly?
  3. Fill the external boxes: what is changing around us that could help or hurt?
  4. Ask “so what?”: how can each strength grab an opportunity or block a threat? Choose two or three actions.

Try it yourself

A small family restaurant has done a SWOT analysis. Which box does each point belong in? Remember: internal or external? Helpful or harmful?

1. A large office building with 2,000 workers is opening across the street.
2. The chef has won several local cooking awards.
3. The kitchen equipment is old and often breaks down.
4. Loyal customers who have eaten there for twenty years.
5. A cheap fast-food chain plans to open next door.
6. Food prices across the country are rising quickly.

1. Opportunities — external and helpful — lots of new lunch customers.

2. Strengths — internal and helpful.

3. Weaknesses — internal and harmful.

4. Strengths — internal and helpful — something the restaurant has built.

5. Threats — external and harmful.

6. Threats — external and harmful — the restaurant can't control it.

Where the tool breaks

Four lists are not a plan. Without the “so what?” step, SWOT produces pages of notes and no decisions.

No priorities. A tiny weakness and a huge one look the same on the page.

Easy to be too kind to yourself. Teams write long strengths lists and short weaknesses lists.

Talk it through:
  • Why do people find weaknesses harder to write than strengths?
  • What would you add to the restaurant's SWOT? What should it do first?

Grammar — must have, might have, can't have

Detectives — and historians — make guesses about the past from evidence. English uses modal + have + past participle to show how sure we are.

must have + past participle — you're almost certain something happened.
“The streets are wet. It must have rained in the night.”
might / may / could have + past participle — it's possible.
“I can't find my keys. I might have left them at work.”
can't / couldn't have + past participle — you're almost certain something did NOT happen.
“She can't have seen the message — her phone was switched off.”
Watch out: the opposite of must have is can't have — not mustn't have.
Pronunciation: in speech, have is weak: must've, might've, can't've. Never write “must of”.
