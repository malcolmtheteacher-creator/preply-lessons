Questioning Certainty

These four phrases help you question beliefs, evaluate evidence, and express doubt with precision. Click each one to explore it:

"It's widely assumed that... but in reality..." Click to expand
MEANING

Name a common belief, then show it's wrong. A controlled way to challenge popular thinking.

FORM

It's widely assumed that [belief]. But in reality, [the truth / what the evidence shows].

PRONUNCIATION

Stress: "It's WIDELY asSUMED that... but in reALity..." Pause before "but" for contrast.

Example in context

"It's widely assumed that our most vivid memories are our most accurate ones. But in reality, researchers have shown that confidence and accuracy are completely independent."

Now you try:

Use this phrase about something people commonly believe that isn't quite right — about health, work, education, or anything else.

"The evidence suggests that... rather than..." Click to expand
MEANING

Present what research or facts actually show, while correcting a misconception. Sounds authoritative but careful.

FORM

The evidence suggests that [what's actually true], rather than [what people think].

PRONUNCIATION

/ˈev.ɪ.dəns/ — stress on first syllable. "The Evidence sugGESTS that... RAther than..." Slight emphasis on "rather" to mark the contrast.

Example in context

"The evidence suggests that memories are reconstructed from fragments each time we recall them, rather than being played back like a recording."

Now you try:

Use this phrase to correct a common misconception about something you know well — your field of work, your country, or a topic you've studied.

"How reliable is [X] when...?" Click to expand
MEANING

A rhetorical question that casts doubt. You're not really asking — you're suggesting the answer is "not very reliable."

FORM

How reliable is [thing] when [condition that undermines it]?

PRONUNCIATION

Rising intonation on "reliable", falling at the end. "How reLIable is memory when..." Sounds thoughtful and questioning.

Example in context

"How reliable is eyewitness testimony when a single word in the question can change what the witness 'remembers' seeing?"

Now you try:

Use this phrase to question something people usually trust: the news, online reviews, first impressions, gut feelings.

"That calls into question whether..." Click to expand
MEANING

Something you've just heard or seen makes you doubt a related idea. Formal, analytical — sounds like you're building an argument.

FORM

[Fact/finding], which calls into question whether [previously accepted idea].

PRONUNCIATION

"...which CALLS into QUEStion whether..." Stress on "calls" and "question". Often follows a dramatic pause.

Example in context

"25% of Loftus's subjects developed detailed memories of events that never happened. That calls into question whether any childhood memory can be fully trusted."

Now you try:

Think of a fact or statistic that surprised you recently. Use "That calls into question whether..." to say what it makes you doubt.

The Evidence

Read each finding. Then use the target phrase to discuss what it means — don't just summarise, question what it tells us.

🧪 The Lost in the Mall (Elizabeth Loftus, 1995)

Loftus gave participants written accounts of 4 childhood events. Three were real (confirmed by family). The fourth — being lost in a shopping mall — was completely fabricated. 25% of participants developed detailed, emotional memories of the fake event. Some added vivid details: colours, smells, feelings of panic. Their brains had constructed a complete false memory from nothing.

Use "It's widely assumed that... but in reality..." — What do most people assume about their childhood memories? What does Loftus's experiment suggest?

🔄 Reconsolidation: Remembering Changes Memory

Every time you recall a memory, your brain doesn't play it back — it rebuilds it from fragments. And in rebuilding it, the memory changes. You're not remembering the original event — you're remembering your last recall of it. Each time you tell a story, the story shifts. Each retelling rewrites the memory.

Use "The evidence suggests that... rather than..." — How does memory actually work, versus how people think it works?

🚗 The Misinformation Effect (Loftus & Palmer, 1974)

Participants watched a video of a car accident. Half were asked: "How fast were the cars going when they smashed into each other?" The other half: "...when they contacted each other?" Same video. The "smashed" group estimated 41 mph. The "contacted" group: 31 mph. A single word changed what they "saw."

Use "How reliable is [X] when...?" — What does this experiment tell us about eyewitness testimony? About how questions shape answers?

💡 Flashbulb Memories (Talarico & Rubin, 2003)

People reported vivid, confident memories of exactly where they were on 9/11. When checked 3 years later, many accounts were significantly wrong. But here's the key: their confidence remained high. They still felt certain, even as their accuracy dropped. Confidence and accuracy turned out to be completely independent.

Use "That calls into question whether..." — If confident memories can be wrong, what does that call into question about how we judge truth?

Relationships
