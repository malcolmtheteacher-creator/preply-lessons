The Match Girls' Strike

How more than a thousand women and girls walked out of a London factory and won, with the whole city watching.

Today: you'll tell the story of a group of low-paid workers who took on a powerful company, and you'll master the verbs people use to make demands, refuse them, and reach agreements.
Warm up — before the words. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
  • Why might people choose to stop working rather than simply ask for better conditions?
  • What can ordinary workers do when they have little money and little power?
  • Can you think of a time when public opinion changed how a company behaved?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words, four short activities. In each one, think first and say your answer aloud — then reveal it and see how close you were.

Activity 1 · Guess from the situation

1. Their wages were a pittance: after twelve hours on their feet, many went home with barely enough to buy bread for the week.
a pittance (n) — a very small amount of money, far too little to live on comfortably.
2. Deep-sea welding is hazardous work: every year, divers are hurt by explosions, pressure and cold that they cannot always predict.
hazardous (adj) — dangerous; likely to cause injury, illness or harm.
3. The newspaper's exposé of conditions inside the factory shocked its readers; within a week, politicians were being asked difficult questions about it.
an exposé (n) — a report in the media that reveals a hidden scandal or wrongdoing to the public.

Activity 2 · Which word fits? (first letter given)

4. The money taken out of your pay before you receive it — for fines, say, or for the cost of tools. D_________
a deduction (n) — an amount subtracted from wages. Example: deductions for lateness left her with half her pay.
5. To give a worker their job back after they were sacked unfairly. R________
to reinstate (v) — to restore someone to a job or position they lost. Example: the tribunal ordered the company to reinstate her.
6. To damage someone's face or body so badly that their appearance is changed for life. D_________
to disfigure (v) — to spoil the natural appearance of a person or thing, often permanently. Example: the fire left him badly disfigured.

Activity 3 · Complete the phrase

7. Crowds gathered outside the gates to stand together in s_________ with the strikers.
solidarity (n) — unity and mutual support among people with a shared aim. Common phrases: in solidarity with, show solidarity, a show of solidarity.
8. A single shocking photograph can g_________ a whole country into action.
to galvanise (v) — to shock or excite people into taking action. Common phrases: galvanise support, galvanise public opinion, galvanise into action.

Activity 4 · Choose the meaning

9. Their biggest grievance was the system of fines: a worker could lose a day's pay for dropping a single match.
(a) a reason to feel treated unfairly   (b) a type of payment   (c) a legal document
(a) — a grievance (n) is a real or felt cause for complaint about unfair treatment.
10. Neither side wanted to look weak, so each made a concession before the deal was signed.
(a) a final refusal   (b) a public apology   (c) something given up to reach an agreement
(c) — a concession (n) is something you agree to give up in order to reach a deal.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The factory in Bow

In the 1880s, the Bryant & May factory in Bow, in the East End of London, was one of the biggest match makers in Britain. Most of its workers were women and girls from poor families, and some were barely in their teens. They stood at their benches from early morning until evening for a pittance, often only a few shillings a week, and the rules made even that small sum shrink. A worker could face a deduction from her wages for talking, for being late, or for dropping a match. Meanwhile the company's shareholders were paid generous dividends.

The work itself was hazardous. Matches were made with white phosphorus, and the women breathed its fumes all day. They ate their meals at the same benches, in the same air. Some developed "phossy jaw", a painful infection that rotted the bone of the face. It could disfigure a worker for life and, in the worst cases, it killed.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who worked at the factory, and what were their wages and rules like?

2. What was "phossy jaw", and what caused it?

1. Mostly women and girls from poor families; very low pay, and deductions from it for talking, lateness or dropping a match.

2. A painful infection that rotted the jawbone, caused by breathing the fumes of white phosphorus used in making the matches.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think workers put up with conditions like these for so long? What would it have taken to say "no"?

Part 2 — The article and the walkout

In June 1888, a campaigning journalist named Annie Besant spoke to some of the workers and wrote an exposé headed "White Slavery in London". It appeared on 23 June in a small newspaper called The Link, and it described the fines, the pay and the illness in plain, angry detail. The company was furious. Managers pressed workers to sign a statement saying they were treated fairly, and when one woman refused, she was dismissed.

That was the last straw. In early July, around 1,400 women and girls put down their tools and walked out. They had almost no savings and no plan for a long fight. But they marched through the streets, and a strike committee was formed to speak for them. Newspapers took up the story, and money began to arrive from supporters across London. With every day, public solidarity grew.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Besant's article do, and how did the company react?

2. What made the workers walk out, and what helped them keep going?

1. It exposed the pay, fines and illness to the public; the company was furious and pressed workers to sign a statement that they were treated fairly.

2. A woman who refused to sign was dismissed, which was the last straw; they were helped by newspaper coverage, a strike committee and money from supporters.

Discuss:
  • The workers walked out with almost no savings. Was that brave, reckless, or both? What would you have done?

Part 3 — A victory, and a question

The strike lasted about two weeks. Under growing public pressure, the directors agreed to meet a delegation of the women, and the dispute ended in a deal. The company ended the fines and the unfair deductions, agreed to reinstate the woman who had been dismissed, and provided a separate room for meals. Each side had made a concession, but the workers had won the main points. They also founded a union of their own, for women match makers.

The deal did not end every danger. Phossy jaw continued for years, until white phosphorus was replaced and then banned by law in Britain in the early twentieth century. But the strike proved that poorly paid, unskilled workers could win against a powerful company, and it helped galvanise other groups of low-paid workers in the years that followed.

One question is still argued about. For a long time, the story was told as a victory for Besant. Some historians, such as Louise Raw, argue instead that the women organised the walkout themselves, and that Besant joined a fight that was already under way. Who deserves the credit, and who gets it?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the workers win?

2. What is the disagreement about who deserves the credit?

1. An end to fines and unfair deductions, the dismissed woman reinstated, a separate room for meals, and a union of their own.

2. The story was long told as Besant's victory; some historians argue the women organised the strike themselves and she joined later.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think credit for a group's success so often goes to one well-known person? Does it matter who gets remembered?

Grammar — verbs of demand, refusal & agreement

A strike is a conversation in action: someone demands, someone refuses, and eventually someone agrees. English has a neat set of verbs for each step, and each one takes its own pattern. Get the pattern right and you can report any negotiation precisely.

The patterns, side by side:
• demand + noun / demand that + clause — "They demanded an end to the fines." "They demanded that the fines be abolished."
• insist on + -ing / insist that + clause — "They insisted on being paid fairly." "They insisted that she get her job back."
• refuse / agree / threaten / promise + to + base verb — "The directors refused to negotiate." "They finally agreed to talk." "The company threatened to close."
• urge / warn + person + to + base verb — "She urged the women to hold firm." "Friends warned them not to go back."
Watch out: after insist on you need -ing, never to: "insisted on meeting", not "insisted to meet". After refuse and agree you need to + base verb, never -ing.

1. Fill the gap

Write the missing word.

1. The women insisted being paid fairly.

2. The managers refused negotiate with them.

3. The strikers demanded the fines be scrapped.

1. on (insist on + -ing)

2. to (refuse + to + base verb)

3. that (demand that + clause)

2. Fix the form

Write the correct form of the verb in brackets.

1. She urged the women (join) the strike committee.

2. They insisted on (meet) the directors in person.

3. The company threatened (close) the factory.

1. to join (urge + person + to + base verb)

2. meeting (insist on + -ing)

3. to close (threaten + to + base verb)

Task B · When is a strike justified? (4 minutes)
