II. Elizabeth's England: 1558–1603

The Woman Who Married England

When Mary I died in November 1558, her half-sister Elizabeth was in danger. The Protestant princess had been imprisoned in the Tower, suspected of treason. Now, at age 25, she became queen. Her coronation took place on 15 January 1559 in Westminster Abbey.

What Elizabeth understood — and what Marshall dramatizes beautifully — was that her survival depended on one thing: making herself indispensable to her people, married to the nation itself.

I am married to England. As a good wife, I have cared for my husband's welfare, so as a good queen, I will care for the welfare of my country. — Elizabeth I (quoted in various forms by contemporary chroniclers; Marshall's version emphasizes this theme)
The historian's note: The famous version of this quote appears in multiple sources, including Sir John Hayward's Annals of the First Four Years of Queen Elizabeth, though exact wording varies. The sentiment became one of Elizabeth's defining declarations — she would remain unmarried and dedicate herself wholly to her realm. In an age when a woman's authority was constantly questioned, this was a masterstroke of political identity.

A Middle Way in Religion

England in 1558 was torn apart by religious violence. Mary I had burned over 200 Protestants, earning the name "Bloody Mary." Elizabeth's task was to find a settlement that would neither provoke Catholic Europe nor alienate her Protestant subjects.

The Elizabethan Religious Settlement (1559)

The Act of Supremacy: Elizabeth became Supreme Governor of the Church of England (deliberately avoiding the more absolute title "Supreme Head").

The Act of Uniformity: A single prayer book (the Book of Common Prayer) was mandated, with moderate Protestant language that many Catholics could tolerate.

The Middle Way: Elizabeth's genius was to avoid extremes. The church kept bishops (satisfying those who liked hierarchy), but the theology was Protestant. It was, as many said, "Catholic in everything but the Pope."

The historian's note: This "Elizabethan Settlement" would define the Church of England for centuries. It was controversial in its own time — strict Protestants wanted more reform, and Catholics saw it as heresy. But it worked. England had no religious civil wars like those tearing France and the Netherlands apart. The price was eternal vigilance and the persecution of both radical Protestants ("Puritans") and Catholics who rejected the settlement.

Mary Queen of Scots: A Cousin, a Rival, a Prisoner

The greatest threat to Elizabeth was her own cousin, Mary Queen of Scots. Mary was beautiful, educated, a Catholic, and — from the perspective of Catholic monarchs — the rightful queen of England. Her claim was dangerous.

Mary's story is one of heartbreak and tragedy. She had been queen of Scotland from infancy, then queen of France through marriage. When her husband died, she was widowed at 18 in a hostile Catholic court. She fled to Scotland, her native kingdom, but her reign was chaotic. In 1568, defeated in a civil war and fearing execution, Mary did something that would seal her fate: she fled to England and threw herself on Elizabeth's mercy.

Mary was unhappy and unwise. The Queen of England feared and hated her cousin, fearing for her own throne, knowing that every Catholic in Europe would rally to Mary if they could. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXX

For 19 years, Elizabeth kept Mary imprisoned. The Catholic powers (Spain, France, the Pope) plotted to free her or kill Elizabeth and put Mary on the throne. There were multiple plots, multiple escape attempts. Finally, in 1587, after a plot to assassinate Elizabeth was discovered, Mary was tried for treason and condemned to death.

The Execution of Mary Queen of Scots (8 February 1587)

Mary walked to the scaffold with dignity. She wore a black dress with a white veil. When the executioner held up her head, her lips still moved in prayer. She had been queen of three kingdoms (Scotland, France, and by Catholic claim, England) and died a prisoner.

Elizabeth, when told of the execution, raged against her advisors. She had not formally signed the death warrant (though everyone knew she wanted Mary dead). The ambiguity gave her deniability in the eyes of Catholic powers.

III. The Spanish Armada & the Golden Age

Philip II Seeks Revenge: 1588

Mary's execution infuriated King Philip II of Spain. Philip was the most powerful Catholic monarch in Europe. He had once been married to Mary I of England and considered himself Elizabeth's enemy. With the Pope's blessing and the backing of every Catholic power, Philip decided to invade England, overthrow Elizabeth, and restore Catholicism.

In May 1588, a fleet of extraordinary size sailed from Spain. It was not called a war fleet — it was given a name that spoke of absolute confidence:

In May one hundred and twenty-nine great ships sailed out from Spain. Philip the King had called his fleet the Invincible Armada. He felt so sure of success that he had given his fleet so grand a name. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXXI

Drake's Bowls and the Singing of Spain's Beard

Sir Francis Drake was one of the greatest sea captains who ever lived — part explorer, part merchant, part pirate. Before the Armada ever sailed, Drake had made a daring raid on the Spanish port of Cádiz, where Spanish war vessels were gathered, preparing for invasion.

Drake sailed boldly into the Spanish harbor and sank and burned more than thirty of the great Spanish vessels. When asked what he had done, Drake laughed and said "This was just singeing the King of Spain's beard." — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXXI

Now, in July 1588, when scouts reported that the Armada was approaching England, Drake and the other English commanders were playing bowls on the Hoe at Plymouth. The story, as Marshall tells it, has become legend:

The English captains were playing bowls when they were told that the Spaniards were coming. "There is time to finish the game and beat the Spaniards too," said Drake. And calmly they finished their game before hurrying to the ships. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXXI
The historian's note: This famous story may be partly legend. A version was recorded by Drake's nephew John Drake about 20 years after the event. But it captures something true about English confidence in 1588. The Spanish Armada was supposed to be invincible. The English were outnumbered. But they had faster ships, better gunners, and commanders who were not afraid.

Elizabeth at Tilbury

While the naval battle raged off the coasts of Devon and Cornwall, an invasion army of 30,000 Spanish soldiers waited in the Netherlands, ready to land and conquer. Elizabeth, now 55 years old, made one of the most extraordinary decisions of her reign: she rode out to Tilbury, where English troops were gathering to resist invasion, and she addressed them herself.

Elizabeth's Tilbury Speech (30 July 1588)

Standing before her assembled soldiers in armor (or so the stories say), Elizabeth spoke words that would echo across the centuries:

"I know I have the body of a weak and feeble woman; but I have the heart and stomach of a king, and of a king of England too, and think foul scorn that Parma or Spain, or any prince of Europe should dare to invade the borders of my realm."

She promised to live or die with her people. She told them she would be "in the midst of you" in battle.

The historian's note: The full text of Elizabeth's Tilbury speech survives in multiple versions. The most famous quote about her "weak and feeble" body and "heart and stomach of a king" is authentic, recorded in letters written shortly after the event. It was a speech of genius — using the very fact of her being a woman as a paradoxical source of strength. She positioned herself as married to her people, willing to die with them.

The Great Storm and the Broken Armada

The naval battles of late July and early August 1588 were fierce but inconclusive. English ships harried the Spanish fleet, but neither side achieved a decisive victory. Then, at a critical moment, the weather changed.

A terrible storm arose. The great Spanish ships, built for the warm Mediterranean, were not made for the wild North Sea. They were driven northward by the tempest, shattered on the rocks of the Scottish and Irish coasts. About fifty maimed and broken wrecks — all that remained of the Invincible Armada — limped back to Spain. Thousands of Spanish sailors perished. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXXI

The English celebrated the victory as divine providence. Medals were struck with the inscription "God Breathed and They Were Scattered." Whether by skill, luck, or the hand of God, England had survived invasion. Elizabeth's throne was secure.

The Elizabethan Flowering: Shakespeare, Raleigh, Drake

The defeat of the Armada marked the beginning of English confidence. The 1590s and early 1600s saw an extraordinary explosion of English culture. This was the age of:

William Shakespeare (1564–1616) — writing his greatest plays: Hamlet, Macbeth, A Midsummer Night's Dream, The Tempest.

Sir Walter Raleigh (c. 1554–1618) — exploring Virginia in the New World, bringing back tobacco and potatoes.

Sir Francis Drake — the first Englishman to sail around the world (1577–1580). Continuing his voyages and raids on Spanish treasure.

English exploration and commerce — the foundation of what would become the British Empire.

Marshall captures this well. The defeat of the Armada did not just save a kingdom — it liberated it. For the next 15 years of Elizabeth's life, England would know peace, prosperity, and an outburst of creative genius unmatched in the nation's history.

Room 14: The Elizabethan Age

You've been building this palace for 13 lessons. Room 14 holds the greatest triumph of an English monarch: Elizabeth I defeating the Armada and giving England a golden age.

The 5 Images for Room 14

1. Elizabeth in her white face paint and red wig — her coronation at Westminster Abbey, 1558. She stands alone, hand raised. All the power of a nation given to a woman.

2. Mary Queen of Scots in black on the scaffold — her white veil, the executioner waiting, February 1587. The fate of those who threaten the queen.

5. The Globe Theatre, Shakespeare performing — actors in costume on a wooden stage, the audience roaring. The age of cultural genius that Elizabeth's victory made possible.

VI. Your Turn: Discussion

Choose 2–3 of these to reflect on.

Why did Elizabeth never marry?

She said she was "married to England." Was this a choice, a political necessity, or both? What does it tell us about power, women, and the 16th century?

Did Elizabeth have to execute Mary?

Mary was her cousin, a queen in her own right, and a prisoner for 19 years. Could Elizabeth have found another way? What does her decision tell us about rule?

Why was Drake's beard-singeing so effective?

One raid before the Armada sailed delayed it a year and killed 30 Spanish sailors. How can a small, bold action change the course of history?

Was the storm divine providence?

Marshall and the English believed God saved them. But could the Spanish have won if the weather had been better? What's the role of luck in history?

Why does Elizabeth inspire us now?

500 years later, what makes her one of the most famous monarchs? Is it her political skill, her courage, her independence, or something else?

The golden age: Why did culture flourish?

Shakespeare, Raleigh, Drake all emerged or thrived in Elizabeth's reign. What conditions create periods of genius — peace, confidence, patronage, competition?
