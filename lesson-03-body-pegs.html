The singing hand

Northern Italy, about a thousand years ago. In the choir school of a cathedral, a monk named Guido, from the town of Arezzo, has a problem that every choirmaster had. The great church chants had no written notes that anyone could easily read. A boy learned each melody by listening to an older singer sing it again and again, and it could take many years to learn enough to sing in church.

Guido wanted a quicker way. He is remembered for several ideas, but the one that lasted longest was strangely simple. He taught his singers to use the palm of their own hand.

What was the problem Guido wanted to solve?
Singers had to learn each chant by ear, and it took many years to learn enough of them.
Talk about it. How do you learn a new song? How long does it take to be sure of it?

On the left hand, every fingertip and every joint stood for a note, from the lowest to the highest. The teacher pointed at the joints one after another while the boys sang, and the boys learned to think of a note and a place on the hand as the same thing. Later they could do it silently. The hand was a map of sound that they carried everywhere, and no one could lose it or forget it at home.

Even the names of the notes came from a song. Guido used a hymn to Saint John whose lines began with the syllables ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la. Each line began one note higher than the last, so the singers could hear what each name sounded like. Those syllables are the ancestors of do, re, mi.

How did the hand work as a memory device?
Each joint and fingertip stood for a note, so the learner could point to a place on the hand to recall a sound.
Talk about it. Why is your own body a good place to keep things? What are the advantages over a notebook?

The 'Guidonian hand' stayed in music books for centuries, drawn in the margins of manuscripts and printed textbooks. People simply found it useful. It was always with them, it was a fixed order, and it was easy to touch while they thought.

That is exactly what we want from our first memory place. In this room you will not use a hand but your whole body: ten places from head to toe, always in the same order. They are the simplest possible memory board.

Why does the body make a good memory board?
It is always with you, the order never changes, and you can touch or think about each place.
Talk about it. Which of your own body parts would you give the job of remembering your phone number? Why?

Ten pegs, always in the same order

A peg is a fixed place you hang a picture on. If the pegs never change and always come in the same order, they can hold a list for you.

One to ten, head to toe. Use this order every single time.

  1. Say the pegs in order. Head, eyes, nose, mouth, neck, chest, stomach, legs, feet, toes. Do it until it is automatic. It takes a few minutes.
  2. Turn each item into a picture. If you cannot picture it, substitute a sound-alike (see room 2).
  3. Put the picture ON the peg. Make it touch, cover or attack that part of your body. You should feel it there.
  4. Add movement and a sense. It moves, it makes a noise, it has a smell.
  5. To recall, walk down your body. Start at the head, and see what is sitting on each place.

Turning a name into something you can place

Brazil is not a picture. But Brazil sounds like bra. So you place a giant red bra across your chest (peg 6), with straps made of snakes. Russia sounds like rushing: a torrent of water rushes down your head. Hard words always turn into something. If one resists, pick any sound-alike.

What the body can and cannot do

  • It is portable and instant. No building needed, and you can use it on a bus.
  • It holds ten. More would need places that are crowded or hard to tell apart. For longer lists you will use a building (room 4).
  • It can be reused once the list is no longer needed. Clear the pegs by imagining them empty and clean.
