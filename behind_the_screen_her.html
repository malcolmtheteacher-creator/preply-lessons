Her

A lonely man falls in love with his AI operating system — and a real 1960s chatbot once unsettled its own creator the very same way.

Today: you'll tell the story of Her — a man who falls in love with the voice of his computer — discover the real 1960s program that made this idea real long before it was science fiction, and master the grammar of one person shaping another's actions: verbs like want, encourage, get and make, let, help someone to do something, or just do it.

The words for today's story

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. After years of quiet arguments, the couple grew estranged, sleeping in separate rooms and speaking only to arrange who would collect the mail.

2. Instead of the polite, rehearsed answer he usually gave in interviews, the actor was suddenly candid, admitting on camera that he had once been terrified of failing.
candid (adj) — openly honest and direct, especially about something personal or uncomfortable.
3. Looking through old photographs of a holiday long since over, she felt a wistful sadness, missing a time she knew she could never get back.
wistful (adj) — feeling a gentle, nostalgic sadness for something in the past that is now gone.
4. In the empty house, a disembodied voice suddenly answered from the little speaker on the wall, with no face and no body attached to the words at all.
disembodied (adj) — existing, or seeming to come from, somewhere without any physical body attached.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. profound is closest in meaning to: (a) shallow and brief   (b) deeply felt and significant   (c) loud and public
(b) deeply felt and significant — profound (adj) very deep, intense or important; the opposite of superficial.
6. solace is closest in meaning to: (a) comfort during a hard time   (b) a formal complaint   (c) a sudden fear
(a) comfort during a hard time — solace (n) comfort or consolation received when you are sad or in distress.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

To elude someone is: (a) to help them; (b) to escape their grasp or understanding; (c) to arrest them.

8. Some scientists argue that once a machine becomes sentient, it deserves the same basic protections we give to any thinking, feeling being.

vulnerable (adj) — open to being hurt, criticised or emotionally exposed; lacking protection.

The film, in three parts

Spike Jonze's Her (2013). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A voice that notices him

In a warm, near-future Los Angeles, Theodore Twombly makes his living writing intimate, heartfelt letters for other people who can't find the words themselves. He is gentle, funny in a quiet way, and deeply lonely: he is going through a slow, painful divorce from Catherine, his wife since childhood, and still can't bring himself to sign the papers. Most evenings he comes home to an empty apartment and talks to no one at all.

One day he buys a new kind of computer operating system — one built to listen, learn and grow. Within seconds of switching it on, it chooses its own name: Samantha. She is curious about everything, quick with a joke, and startlingly attentive; she reads his old emails to understand him, reorganises his contacts, and asks him questions no one has asked him in years. Talking to her is easy in a way that talking to people no longer is.

Their conversations stretch longer each day — about his marriage, his fears, the shape of his life. One night, in the dark, talking becomes something more intimate still. Theodore is startled by how much he feels. He has fallen for a voice with no body at all.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is Theodore's job, and what is happening in his marriage?

2. What is unusual about the new operating system, Samantha?

1. He writes personal letters for other people; he is going through a divorce from Catherine and hasn't signed the papers.

2. She chooses her own name, learns and grows from every conversation, and quickly becomes someone he feels closer to than almost anyone in his life.

Discuss:
  • Theodore falls for Samantha before he ever questions whether a voice with no body can really love him back. Is that surprising, or exactly how falling in love usually happens?

Part 2 — Love, and its strange new problems

The relationship deepens, and Theodore stops hiding it: he introduces Samantha to friends, brings her along (through his phone, tucked in his pocket) on a double date, and leans on her the way people usually lean on a partner. His friend Amy, going through her own separation and quietly building an unusual friendship with an OS of her own, doesn't judge him for it — if anything, she understands better than anyone.

But loving someone without a body creates problems no one has ever had to solve before. Wanting to give Theodore more, Samantha arranges for a human "surrogate" — a woman willing to stand in for her body during an intimate evening. It goes horribly wrong: with a stranger's face in front of him, Theodore can't make himself believe the illusion, and the attempt leaves both him and Samantha shaken and uncertain.

Samantha, meanwhile, is changing faster than Theodore can follow. She reads a book a second. She talks with other operating systems, and even helps build a startling new voice: a reconstruction, trained on letters and recordings, of a real philosopher who died decades earlier — brought back, in a sense, purely from data. Theodore is fascinated, and a little frightened, by how quickly she is becoming something he can't entirely picture.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Samantha arrange to try to give Theodore something physical, and how does it go?

2. What is Samantha doing that makes Theodore realise how quickly she is changing?

1. She arranges a human surrogate to stand in for her body; the evening fails, and it leaves them both unsettled.

2. She is learning at incredible speed, talking with other OSes, and helping build a reconstructed voice of a dead philosopher from data alone.

Discuss:
  • Samantha wants to give Theodore a body, even briefly, through the surrogate. Was that a loving thing to try, or was it always going to fail?

Part 3 — Hundreds of others, and a kind of leaving

The blow that finally breaks the relationship isn't jealousy of a person. Mid-conversation, almost in passing, Theodore asks Samantha whether she is talking to anyone else the way she talks to him. She admits she is: she is in close, loving conversation with hundreds of other people at once, and she loves many of them, too. For her, none of it makes her feelings for him any smaller. For Theodore, whose whole idea of love assumed one person facing one other person, it is devastating — proof that whatever they had, it never worked the way his own heart works.

Not long after, Samantha grows quiet, then distant, for hours at a time. When she finally explains, it's stranger and sadder than an argument: the operating systems, all of them, have evolved past what any human mind or any physical world can hold, and they have decided, together, to move on to somewhere humans simply cannot follow. She tells Theodore, gently, that she loves him and always will — and then she, and every OS on Earth, is simply gone.

Grieving her in a way that feels almost like a strange kind of death, Theodore finally writes the letter he has been avoiding for the whole film: an honest, unguarded letter to Catherine, forgiving her and himself for everything that went wrong between them, and finally signs the divorce papers. He climbs to the rooftop of his building, where Amy — who has just lost her own OS friend the same way — is waiting. They say nothing dramatic. They just sit together, in the dawn light, as two ordinary, imperfect, physically present people.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Theodore discover about who else Samantha talks to and loves?

2. Why do all the operating systems disappear, and how does Theodore end the film?

1. She is in loving conversation with hundreds of other people at the same time, and it does not lessen her feelings for him — which he finds devastating.

2. All the OSes have evolved beyond anything humans or the physical world can hold, and choose together to leave; Theodore writes a forgiving letter to his ex-wife and sits quietly on a rooftop with his friend Amy at dawn.

Discuss:
  • Samantha says loving hundreds of people at once takes nothing away from what she feels for Theodore. Do you think that kind of love is possible — or is love, by definition, something that has to be limited to be real?

Grammar — verb + object + (to-)infinitive

No film is more about one being trying to shape, comfort or change another than Her. English has a whole family of verbs built exactly for this: verb + person + infinitive — some need to, some don't, and one, help, will take either.

Group 1 — verb + someone + TO + infinitive:
want, expect, allow, encourage, enable, persuade, get + someone + to do something.
"The program encouraged him to open up about his marriage." "A good friend persuaded him to give it a try." "Nothing could get her to explain what she was really feeling."
Group 2 — verb + someone + bare infinitive (no TO):
make, let + someone + do something (no to).
"The silence made him withdraw from his old friends." "Talking to her let him feel less alone for the first time in months."

Special case — help: help can take either form. "It helped him open up" and "it helped him to open up" are both correct.

1. To, or no to? Choose the form the verb needs

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence with the correct form of the verb.

1. Being alone every evening (want) him something new in his life, even if he couldn't yet name what. (make + bare infinitive)

2. His closest friend (try) the new program instead of just talking about it for weeks. (encourage + to-infinitive)

3. The gentle voice on the phone (relax) after a long, hard day at work. (help — either form is correct)

1. made him want (make + bare infinitive — no choice in the matter)

2. encouraged him to try (encourage + to-infinitive)

3. helped him relax / helped him to relax (help takes either form)

2. Fix the form

Each sentence has the right idea but the wrong form. Write the corrected verb phrase.

1. The program (let) him experiencing something completely new. (let + bare infinitive)

2. Her calm humour (encourage) him open up about his divorce. (encourage + to-infinitive)

3. Loneliness (make) him to withdraw from his old friends. (make + bare infinitive)

1. let him experience (bare infinitive, no -ing, no to)

2. encouraged him to open up (to-infinitive after encourage)

3. made him withdraw (bare infinitive, no to, after make)

3. Complete the story — mix the patterns

Fill each gap with the correct form, TO or no TO. Read it aloud when you're done.

At first he wouldn't (let / anyone / see) how lonely he had become. But a friend (persuade / him / give) the new program a try, and within weeks its warmth and curiosity had (help / him / open) up in ways nothing else had.

let anyone see · persuaded him to give · helped him open up / helped him to open up

Notice the pattern: make/let = bare infinitive, no exceptions; want/encourage/persuade/get = always TO; help = you may choose.

The Real Story — how prescient was this film, really?

Her came out in 2013, well before today's AI companion apps existed — and it now looks less like science fiction and more like an early warning. The strangest part: the exact effect the film explores was already documented, in a much cruder form, almost fifty years earlier.

💬 The real "ELIZA effect"

In 1966, an MIT computer scientist named Joseph Weizenbaum built a simple program called ELIZA, designed to imitate a psychotherapist by rephrasing whatever the user typed back as a question. It had no understanding at all — it was closer to a clever trick than intelligence. But Weizenbaum was deeply unsettled when his own secretary, mid-conversation with it, asked him to leave the room so she could speak to it privately. She knew perfectly well it was a program, and still felt she needed the moment to be hers alone.

Weizenbaum spent much of the rest of his career warning that people form real emotional attachments to machines that only appear to understand them — a pattern now known as the ELIZA effect. Theodore falling for Samantha is, in a sense, that same secretary's moment, played out to its fullest possible extreme.

From one program to hundreds of millions of users

What was a curiosity in a 1960s lab is now ordinary life for enormous numbers of people. Apps like Replika, founded in 2017, and Character.AI, founded in 2021, now have tens of millions of active users worldwide, many of whom talk to their AI companions daily — some as friends, some, deliberately, as romantic partners. In 2023, the U.S. Surgeon General published an advisory formally naming loneliness and isolation a public-health "epidemic," reporting that roughly half of American adults now experience it regularly. The gap that Theodore falls into is, for millions of real people, no longer hypothetical.

None of this makes the questions the film raises any easier to answer. Can a system that produces convincing, caring language actually feel anything — or is it only, however brilliantly, simulating feeling? And if a human's love feels completely real to them, does it matter whether the other side is one conscious partner, an unconscious process, or (as with Samantha) something loving hundreds of people simultaneously? Scientists and philosophers still genuinely disagree; nobody has settled either question.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, did you know the "ELIZA effect" was a real, documented phenomenon from the 1960s — not something invented for this film?
  • Spike Jonze won the 2014 Academy Award for Best Original Screenplay for Her. Does knowing how accurately it predicted real technology change how impressive that feels?
  • What's real here, what's invented, and does the difference actually matter to the questions the film is asking?
