The Big Question

If God is all-powerful and all-good, why does suffering exist?

This is not just a philosophical puzzle. It's a question that real people ask when they face pain, loss, or injustice. Religious believers have grappled with this for thousands of years.

Encounter: The Reality of Suffering

Before we tackle the philosophy, let's recognise that suffering is real and comes in many forms. Different types of suffering affect people in different ways.

Two Main Categories

Natural Evil

Suffering caused by nature or natural processes, not by human choice.

  • Earthquakes and tsunamis
  • Diseases and illnesses
  • Storms and floods
  • Droughts and famines
  • Cancer, dementia, disability

Moral Evil

Suffering caused by human actions and choices. People choose to hurt others.

  • War and violence
  • Theft and robbery
  • Abuse and cruelty
  • Bullying and discrimination
  • Lies and betrayal

Suffering at Different Scales

Personal Suffering

Suffering that affects an individual or small group: losing a loved one, a serious illness, a personal tragedy, or mental health struggles.

Local Suffering

Suffering that affects a community: a natural disaster in a region, a factory closure affecting a town's economy, or gang violence in a neighbourhood.

Global Suffering

Suffering that affects millions across the world: pandemics, climate-related famines, wars, or systemic inequality that causes poverty and hardship.

All three scales matter. A personal loss is just as real and painful to the person experiencing it as a global crisis is to humanity.

Explore: Why Is Suffering a Religious Problem?

Suffering matters to religious belief in a particular way. Let's understand why.

The Contradiction

Imagine someone tells you: "My best friend loves me more than anything. They have the power to help me with any problem. They know exactly what I'm going through."

But then that friend watches you suffer and does nothing. Wouldn't you question whether your friend really loves you, or has the power they claim, or even knows what's happening?

This is the core of the problem of evil for religious people. If God has all three qualities—all-powerful, all-knowing, and all-loving—why does suffering persist?

The Philosophical Question

This isn't about asking God for sympathy. It's about whether God's existence is logically consistent with the world we observe. Philosophers call this theodicy—trying to justify God's existence and goodness in a world where evil and suffering exist.

Theodicy: From the Greek "theos" (God) and "dike" (justice). A theodicy is an explanation that tries to show how God can be good, all-powerful, and all-knowing while evil and suffering still exist in the world.

Why This Matters Now

This unit explores how different religions and worldviews answer this question:

  • Christianity offers answers based on free will and soul-making
  • Islam emphasises divine testing and cosmic justice in the afterlife
  • Buddhism reframes the question entirely through the concept of dukkha
  • Humanism rejects God altogether and sees suffering as a human challenge

By studying these responses, you'll develop your own thinking about whether suffering proves or disproves God, and whether the religious answers are convincing.

Evaluate: Does Suffering Disprove God?

This is where you develop your own thinking. There's no single "right" answer here—philosophers, theologians, and believers have disagreed for centuries.

A Perspective from an Atheist

"The existence of suffering, especially innocent suffering, proves that God cannot exist. A truly all-powerful and all-good God would eliminate suffering. Since suffering exists, either God is not all-powerful (and cannot stop it), or not all-good (and doesn't care to stop it), or doesn't exist at all."

A Perspective from a Believer

"God allows suffering for reasons we may not fully understand—to teach us, to test our faith, to allow free will, or because suffering can lead to spiritual growth. God's plan is bigger than what we see now. In the afterlife, all suffering will be redeemed."

Over the next lessons, we'll examine the detailed arguments from each tradition. For now, reflect on this question:

Your perspective:

Do you think the existence of suffering makes it impossible for God to exist? Or do you think there could be reasons why an all-good God might allow suffering? What would those reasons be?

An explanation that attempts to justify God's existence and goodness despite the existence of evil and suffering in the world.
