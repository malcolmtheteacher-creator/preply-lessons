Types of Suffering and the Question of Evil

What Is Suffering?

Suffering is pain—physical, emotional, psychological, or spiritual. It's the experience of something deeply wrong: hunger, grief, disease, fear, loss. Suffering is universal; every human being experiences it. But it raises profound questions, especially for those who believe in God. If God is all-powerful and all-loving, why would He create a world where suffering exists?

Two Types of Evil

Philosophers and theologians use the word "evil" to describe suffering caused in the world. However, not all suffering comes from the same source. Understanding the difference is crucial to thinking about this problem.

Natural Evil

Natural evil is suffering caused by natural events—things beyond human control. These are part of how the physical world works. Earthquakes, hurricanes, droughts, diseases, and genetic disorders are natural evils. They happen because of the laws of nature (gravity, weather patterns, bacteria), not because anyone chooses to cause harm.

Examples: The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami killed 230,000 people. A child is born with cystic fibrosis. A young person develops leukemia.

Moral Evil

Moral evil is suffering caused by human choices and actions. These are acts of cruelty, injustice, and violence that happen because people choose to harm others. Unlike natural evil, moral evil is within human control—it reflects human free will and human decisions to cause suffering.

Examples: War and genocide. Child abuse. Slavery. Terrorism. Betrayal and emotional cruelty. Theft and poverty caused by exploitation.

The Differences Matter

Why distinguish between these? Because they raise different theological problems. Natural evil is harder to explain if God is all-powerful—God could have created a world without earthquakes or diseases. But moral evil is different: if humans have free will to choose right or wrong, then moral evil might be the price of freedom. Many theologians argue God respects human choice, even when that choice leads to harm. However, this doesn't solve the natural evil problem.

The Scale of Suffering

Statistics can feel abstract, but they represent real human pain. Consider these:

  • Poverty: 700 million people live in extreme poverty, struggling to afford basic food and shelter.
  • Disease: Malaria kills 600,000 people annually, mostly children in Africa who had no choice in the matter.
  • War: Over 100 million people have been killed in wars in the past 100 years—many of them civilians who were simply in the wrong place.
  • Natural disaster: Earthquakes in seismically active regions kill thousands regularly. When Hurricane Maria hit Puerto Rico in 2017, it killed nearly 3,000 people, many unable to access basic medical care.
  • Childhood suffering: Millions of children suffer from preventable diseases, malnutrition, and abuse—through no fault of their own.

The sheer scale of suffering—especially the suffering of the innocent and the powerless—is what makes this question so urgent and so difficult.

What God's Attributes Have to Do with Suffering

In Christianity, Judaism, and Islam, God is traditionally described with three key attributes that make the problem of suffering acute:

Omnipotence

God is all-powerful. Nothing limits God's power. If God is omnipotent, God has the power to prevent or stop any suffering at any moment.

Omnibenevolence

God is all-loving and perfectly good. God wants good things for creation and wants to prevent suffering. God would not desire suffering if it could be avoided.

Omniscience

God is all-knowing. God knows everything that will happen. If God is omniscient, God knew suffering would occur before creating the world.

The problem emerges when you put these together: If God is all-powerful, all-loving, and all-knowing, why does suffering exist? An all-powerful God could stop it. An all-loving God would want to stop it. An all-knowing God knew it would happen. Yet suffering persists. This logical puzzle is called the Problem of Evil or theodicy (the attempt to justify God's goodness despite evil).

Initial Religious Responses

Believers have proposed several explanations. These are not all equally convincing, but they represent how religions think about this problem:

1. "God Has a Plan"

Many Christians believe that God allows suffering because it serves a purpose in God's larger plan for creation and redemption. Even tragic suffering may ultimately lead to greater good—spiritual growth, community bonds, or divine purpose. This is comforting but raises hard questions: What greater good justifies a child's painful death from cancer? Why couldn't God achieve that good without such suffering?

2. "Suffering is a Test"

Some Islamic interpretations suggest that suffering is a test of faith. Allah tests believers to strengthen their character and their relationship with God. A life without challenge would not develop moral or spiritual depth. This explanation works better for some suffering (a student struggling with exams) than others (a baby born with a fatal disease).

3. "Suffering Results from Human Free Will"

Many Christian philosophers argue that God gave humans free will—the genuine ability to choose right or wrong. Moral evil exists because humans freely choose harm. God respects our freedom even when we misuse it. This explains some suffering but not natural evil, which has nothing to do with human choice.

4. "Suffering is Karma"

In Buddhism and Hinduism, suffering results from karma—the consequences of actions, often across multiple lifetimes. You suffer because of actions you or your ancestors committed. This is impersonal law, not punishment from a judge. It's morally neutral: cause leads to effect. However, this raises the problem of innocent suffering—a newborn cannot have earned suffering through past actions.

Key Scripture and Sacred Texts

Different traditions grapple with suffering in their core texts:

"The Lord gave and the Lord has taken away; may the name of the Lord be praised." Job 1:21 (Bible, Christianity)

Job says this after losing everything—his children, his wealth, his health. Yet he maintains faith. The Book of Job is the Bible's longest exploration of why the innocent suffer.

"And we know that in all things God works for the good of those who love him, who have been called according to his purpose." Romans 8:28 (Bible, Christianity)

Paul suggests that God works to bring good even out of suffering. But what "good" justifies the suffering itself?

"With trials, God does not burden a soul beyond what it can bear. Along with the trial, God will also provide a way out so that you may be able to endure it." Quran 2:286 (Islam)

This verse emphasizes that Allah (God) does not overburden believers. Every trial comes with capacity to endure and a way through.

"Now this, monks, is the noble truth of suffering: birth is suffering, aging is suffering, illness is suffering, death is suffering; union with what is displeasing is suffering; separation from what is pleasing is suffering; not to attain what one wants is suffering; in brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging are suffering." Samyutta Nikaya 56.11 (Buddhism)

The Buddha's First Noble Truth identifies suffering (dukkha) as a fundamental reality of existence. Unlike other religions that try to justify why suffering exists, Buddhism accepts it as a fact and focuses on how to end suffering through enlightenment.

What About Humanism?

Humanists do not believe in God or the supernatural. For humanists, suffering has no divine purpose or cosmic explanation. It is simply part of the human condition. Suffering results from natural causes (disease, accidents) or human actions (cruelty, neglect). The humanist response is not to seek religious meaning in suffering but to work practically to reduce it: advance medicine, build just societies, educate people, and help those who suffer. From a humanist perspective, we humans are responsible for addressing suffering—not God, because God does not exist in humanist worldviews.

Summary of Key Ideas

As we move forward in this unit, remember these core concepts:

  • Suffering is universal, but its sources differ: some suffering is natural (beyond human control), some is moral (caused by human choices).
  • The existence of suffering creates a theological problem for monotheistic religions, especially given traditional concepts of God as all-powerful and all-loving.
  • Different religious traditions and worldviews propose different explanations, from divine purpose to karma to human free will.
  • These explanations are attempts to reconcile belief in God with the existence of suffering—a process called theodicy.
  • The problem is particularly acute when innocent people (children, for example) suffer through no fault of their own.

How Different Traditions Begin to Explain Suffering

Now let's compare how different worldviews approach the question of suffering. Each has developed different responses based on its core beliefs about God, human nature, and the world's purpose.

Christianity

Core Belief: God is all-powerful, all-knowing, and all-loving. God created a perfect world, but humans chose to disobey (the Fall/Original Sin). Suffering entered the world as a consequence of human rebellion against God.

How They Explain Suffering: Most suffering results from the brokenness of the world after the Fall. God allows suffering to exist but promises redemption through Jesus Christ. Many Christians believe suffering can have spiritual value—it can draw people closer to God, refine faith, and deepen compassion. Christ himself suffered, modeling how believers should endure suffering.

Challenge: If God is all-powerful, why not prevent the Fall? And why do innocent people (children, those who haven't had a chance to "choose") suffer?

Islam

Core Belief: Allah is all-powerful and all-knowing. Everything happens by Allah's will. Muslims believe in qadar (divine decree)—the idea that Allah determines what happens.

How They Explain Suffering: Suffering is a test from Allah to strengthen faith and purify the soul. It is never without purpose in Allah's plan. Those who endure with patience will be rewarded. Believers are taught to accept suffering with submission (islam means submission). Suffering is also seen as an opportunity to practice virtue and compassion.

Challenge: If everything is Allah's will, including cruelty and murder, does this make Allah responsible for evil? And does saying it's "a test" really explain why suffering must exist?

Buddhism

Core Belief: There is no creator God. The universe follows natural laws, including karma (cause and effect). Suffering (dukkha) is a fundamental characteristic of existence.

How They Explain Suffering: Buddhism doesn't try to justify suffering or explain why a good God would allow it—there is no such God. Instead, suffering exists because of tanha (craving and attachment). People suffer because they cling to things that cannot last, desire things they don't have, and resist change. The path to ending suffering is through enlightenment (Nirvana), which breaks the cycle of craving and attachment.

Challenge: Doesn't accepting suffering as inevitable, rather than fighting to reduce it, lead to passivity? Some criticize Buddhism for offering spiritual escape rather than practical solutions to injustice.

Humanism

Core Belief: There is no God or afterlife. Humans are responsible for creating meaning and addressing injustice in this life only. Reason and science are the best tools for understanding the world.

How They Explain Suffering: Suffering has no cosmic purpose or divine meaning. It results from natural causes (disease, accidents, natural disasters) or human causes (cruelty, ignorance, injustice). Humanists focus not on explaining why suffering exists but on reducing it through medicine, technology, education, and just social systems. Human compassion and reason are sufficient to address suffering.

Challenge: If suffering has no meaning or purpose, does that make it easier or harder to endure? Some find comfort in religious meaning-making; humanists must find meaning through human relationships and actions alone.

Evaluating Arguments About Suffering and God

Philosophy advances by carefully examining different arguments and evaluating their strengths and weaknesses. Below are two contrasting arguments about whether suffering proves anything about God's existence. Read both carefully, then answer the evaluation questions.

Argument 1: Suffering Disproves God

The Logical Argument

Premise 1: If God exists, God must be all-powerful (omnipotent)

This is part of the traditional definition of God in Christianity, Islam, and Judaism. An all-powerful being has the ability to prevent anything, including suffering.

Premise 2: If God exists, God must be all-good (omnibenevolent)

A perfectly good God would not want suffering to exist. A good parent doesn't want their child to suffer needlessly. An infinitely good God would want even more to prevent suffering.

Premise 3: Yet suffering clearly exists

We have overwhelming evidence of suffering—disease, disaster, war, abuse, poverty, grief. This is not in question.

Conclusion: Therefore, an all-powerful and all-good God cannot exist

An all-powerful God could prevent suffering. An all-good God would prevent suffering. If both were true of God, suffering wouldn't exist. Since suffering does exist, God as traditionally described cannot exist.

Strengths of This Argument:

  • It's logically clear: the conclusion follows from the premises if the premises are true.
  • It takes suffering seriously—doesn't minimize or dismiss the real pain of real people.
  • It points out a genuine tension between belief in God and the existence of suffering.
  • It has convinced many thoughtful people, including some former believers who became atheists.

Weaknesses and Challenges:

  • It assumes God's purposes are knowable to humans. Perhaps God has reasons for allowing suffering that we cannot understand.
  • It doesn't account for free will: if God gave humans genuine freedom, some suffering (moral evil) is the price of that freedom.
  • It doesn't account for the possibility of soul-making—the idea that suffering can develop character, compassion, and spiritual depth.
  • Some argue that limited human understanding cannot prove anything about an infinite God.

Argument 2: Suffering Doesn't Disprove God

The Free Will Defense

Premise 1: God gave humans free will

God created humans with genuine freedom to choose right or wrong. This freedom is a great good—it allows for love, moral achievement, and authentic relationships. You cannot truly love someone you are forced to love; you cannot be morally good if you have no choice.

Premise 2: Free will inevitably leads to some evil

If humans have genuine freedom, some will misuse it. They will lie, steal, hurt others, wage war, and commit terrible acts. This moral evil is the price of free will.

Premise 3: A world with free will and some evil is better than a world of puppets

God could have created robots programmed to be good—but a world of puppets would lack genuine love, sacrifice, courage, and moral achievement. A world where beings freely choose good is better than a world where goodness is programmed.

Conclusion: Therefore, God can be all-powerful and all-good while allowing moral evil

Moral evil (suffering caused by human choices) doesn't disprove God because it's the necessary cost of free will—and free will is a tremendous good.

Strengths of This Argument:

  • It acknowledges the value and importance of human freedom and genuine choice.
  • It explains moral evil clearly: human beings, not God, are responsible for human cruelty.
  • It preserves belief in God's goodness by showing that allowing evil is compatible with being good (given the value of free will).
  • It resonates with many people's lived experience of real choice and real responsibility.

Weaknesses and Challenges:

  • It doesn't explain natural evil. A child born with a painful genetic disease has not made any choice. Where is the free will defense here? Natural disasters kill people who made no choice to cause them.
  • Even if free will justifies some moral evil, does it justify the extremes? Does God need to allow genocide, child abuse, and torture to preserve free will?
  • If God is omniscient, God knew exactly which free choices people would make. Did God create people knowing they would make terrible choices that would cause immense suffering?
  • Does saying "free will is worth the cost" really comfort someone who has lost a child to disease or disaster? The argument feels abstract to those who suffer.

Key Terminology

Make sure you understand these important terms. They will appear again in future lessons as we deepen our exploration of how religions respond to suffering.

Natural Evil
Suffering caused by natural events and forces (disease, earthquakes, aging) beyond human control.
Moral Evil
Suffering caused by human choices and actions (war, cruelty, abuse).
Omnipotent
All-powerful; having infinite power and authority. A key attribute of God in monotheistic religions.
Omnibenevolent
All-loving and perfectly good. A key attribute of God in monotheistic religions.
Omniscient
All-knowing; having complete knowledge of all things, including the future. A key attribute of God in monotheistic religions.
Theodicy
An attempt to justify God's goodness and power in the face of evil and suffering. From Greek: "theos" (God) and "dike" (justice).
The Problem of Evil
The philosophical challenge of reconciling the existence of suffering with belief in an all-powerful, all-knowing, all-loving God.
Free Will
The genuine ability to choose between different courses of action. Free will is seen by many as necessary for moral responsibility.
Karma
In Hinduism and Buddhism, the moral law of cause and effect. Actions have consequences across lifetimes; suffering results from past actions.
Dukkha
The Pali/Sanskrit word in Buddhism for suffering or dissatisfaction. It's the first of the Four Noble Truths.
Enlightenment (Nirvana)
In Buddhism, the state of being free from suffering, craving, and the cycle of rebirth. Achieved through understanding the nature of reality.
Worldview
A comprehensive perspective on life that includes beliefs about meaning, morality, purpose, and how the world works. Can be religious or non-religious.
