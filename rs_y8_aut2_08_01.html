Why Does Suffering Exist?

YEAR 8 · UNIT 2: THE PROBLEM OF EVIL

Learning Intention

In this lesson, you will explore different types of suffering and discover how major world religions and non-religious worldviews begin to answer one of life's most challenging questions: why does suffering exist if God is all-powerful and all-loving?

Opening Question

Take a moment to think about this question: Why does suffering exist?

What is an example of suffering you've witnessed (in real life, news, or media)? Write a few sentences describing it. Don't worry about having a "right answer"—this is just your honest reflection.

Why This Question Matters

This isn't an abstract philosophical puzzle. Real people experience real suffering—from illness and loss to injustice and violence. For many people, especially those who believe in God, suffering raises urgent questions:

  • If God is all-powerful, why can't God stop suffering?
  • If God is all-loving, why would God allow suffering?
  • Is there any meaning to suffering, or is it just random and cruel?
  • How should we respond to suffering when we see it?

Two Types of Suffering

Before we can think clearly about whether God and suffering can coexist, we need to distinguish between two types:

Natural Evil

Suffering caused by natural events and forces beyond human control:

  • Disease (cancer, COVID-19, genetic conditions)
  • Natural disasters (earthquakes, tsunamis, hurricanes)
  • Aging and decay
  • Accidents due to natural forces

Moral Evil

Suffering caused by human choices and actions:

  • War and violence
  • Abuse, bullying, and cruelty
  • Theft, betrayal, and injustice
  • Negligence and harm caused by human decision

Why This Distinction Matters

When philosophers and theologians talk about "the problem of evil," they're asking how both types of suffering can exist if God is omnipotent (all-powerful) and omnibenevolent (all-loving). The distinction between natural and moral evil is important because different religious and philosophical arguments address them differently. Some arguments explain moral evil through human free will but struggle with natural evil. Others take a different approach entirely.

What Different Traditions Say About Suffering

Important note: These summaries reflect the major ideas in each tradition but don't represent everyone who follows them. Individual believers have many different perspectives.

Christianity

Christianity wrestles deeply with suffering. While there's no single Christian answer, major themes include:

  • Redemptive suffering: Jesus suffered and died, redeeming humanity through His sacrifice. Suffering can be meaningful and can connect us to Christ's sacrifice.
  • Free will: God gave humans free will. Some suffering results from human misuse of that freedom.
  • Soul-making: Suffering can develop character, compassion, faith, and spiritual depth. It isn't always meaningless.
  • Mystery: Some suffering remains a mystery that tests faith. God's purposes aren't always clear to us.
"Consider it pure joy, my brothers and sisters, whenever you face trials of many kinds, because you know that the testing of your faith produces perseverance." James 1:2-3

Islam

Islam teaches that Allah is just and merciful. Key Islamic perspectives on suffering include:

  • Allah's wisdom: Allah allows suffering for reasons beyond human understanding. Allah's wisdom is perfect, even if we cannot comprehend it.
  • Testing and purification: Suffering tests faith and purifies the soul. It can bring people closer to Allah.
  • Free will and divine will: Humans have free will in their choices, but Allah knows all outcomes. This balance is difficult to fully understand.
  • Patience and reward: The Qur'an encourages patience in suffering. Those who endure with faith will be rewarded in paradise.

Buddhism

Buddhism has a fundamentally different approach to suffering than religions focused on God:

  • Dukkha (suffering) is universal: Suffering is part of existence. Not because of a cruel God, but because of the nature of reality itself.
  • The cause is craving (tanha): We suffer because we crave, cling to, and become attached to things that are impermanent. We resist change.
  • No punishment, only cause and effect: Suffering isn't punishment from God; it's the natural result of our actions and attitudes through karma.
  • Liberation is possible: Enlightenment (Nirvana) comes from understanding and releasing attachment. We can be free from suffering.

Humanism

Humanism is a non-religious worldview. Humanists don't believe in God, so they approach suffering differently:

  • No divine cause: Suffering isn't caused by God or divine will because there is no God. It's caused by natural events and human choices.
  • Human responsibility: Humans are responsible for reducing suffering. We do this through medicine, technology, education, and just social systems.
  • Meaning without God: Humanists find meaning in reducing suffering, building community, and improving life for all people—not through divine purpose but through human effort.
  • Focus on this life: Unlike religions focused on an afterlife, humanists focus on reducing suffering in this life, which is the only one we know we have.

The Problem of Evil: Two Arguments

Argument 1: Suffering Proves God Doesn't Exist

The Logical Problem of Evil (Epicurus)

Premise 1: If God exists, God must be all-powerful (omnipotent)

This is part of the traditional definition of God in Christianity, Islam, and Judaism. An all-powerful being has the ability to prevent anything, including suffering.

Premise 2: If God exists, God must be all-good (omnibenevolent)

A perfectly good God would not want suffering to exist. A good parent doesn't want their child to suffer needlessly. An infinitely good God would want even more to prevent suffering.

Premise 3: Yet suffering clearly exists

We have overwhelming evidence of suffering—disease, disaster, war, abuse, poverty, grief. This is not in question.

Conclusion: Therefore, an all-powerful and all-good God cannot exist

An all-powerful God could prevent suffering. An all-good God would prevent suffering. If both were true of God, suffering wouldn't exist. Since suffering does exist, God as traditionally described cannot exist.

Strengths of This Argument:

  • It's logically clear: the conclusion follows from the premises if the premises are true.
  • It takes suffering seriously—doesn't minimize or dismiss the real pain of real people.
  • It points out a genuine tension between belief in God and the existence of suffering.
  • It has convinced many thoughtful people, including some former believers who became atheists.

Weaknesses and Challenges:

  • It assumes God's purposes are knowable to humans. Perhaps God has reasons for allowing suffering that we cannot understand.
  • It doesn't account for free will: if God gave humans genuine freedom, some suffering (moral evil) is the price of that freedom.
  • It doesn't account for the possibility of soul-making—the idea that suffering can develop character, compassion, and spiritual depth.
  • Some argue that limited human understanding cannot prove anything about an infinite God.

Argument 2: Suffering Doesn't Disprove God

The Free Will Defense

Premise 1: God gave humans free will

God created humans with genuine freedom to choose right or wrong. This freedom is a great good—it allows for love, moral achievement, and authentic relationships. You cannot truly love someone you are forced to love; you cannot be morally good if you have no choice.

Premise 2: Free will inevitably leads to some evil

If humans have genuine freedom, some will misuse it. They will lie, steal, hurt others, wage war, and commit terrible acts. This moral evil is the price of free will.

Premise 3: A world with free will and some evil is better than a world of puppets

God could have created robots programmed to be good—but a world of puppets would lack genuine love, sacrifice, courage, and moral achievement. A world where beings freely choose good is better than a world where goodness is programmed.

Conclusion: Therefore, God can be all-powerful and all-good while allowing moral evil

Moral evil (suffering caused by human choices) doesn't disprove God because it's the necessary cost of free will—and free will is a tremendous good.

Strengths of This Argument:

  • It acknowledges the value and importance of human freedom and genuine choice.
  • It explains moral evil clearly: human beings, not God, are responsible for human cruelty.
  • It preserves belief in God's goodness by showing that allowing evil is compatible with being good (given the value of free will).
  • It resonates with many people's lived experience of real choice and real responsibility.

Weaknesses and Challenges:

  • It doesn't explain natural evil. A child born with a painful genetic disease has not made any choice. Where is the free will defense here? Natural disasters kill people who made no choice to cause them.
  • Even if free will justifies some moral evil, does it justify the extremes? Does God need to allow genocide, child abuse, and torture to preserve free will?
  • If God is omniscient, God knew exactly which free choices people would make. Did God create people knowing they would make terrible choices that would cause immense suffering?
  • Does saying "free will is worth the cost" really comfort someone who has lost a child to disease or disaster? The argument feels abstract to those who suffer.

Deeper Evaluation: Comparing Worldviews

Religious Approaches

Christianity, Islam, Buddhism

Religious traditions attempt to find meaning in suffering. They offer frameworks (redemption, divine testing, karma, enlightenment) that reframe suffering as part of a larger spiritual purpose or natural law.

Strength: Provides comfort and meaning for those experiencing suffering.

Challenge: Does explaining suffering make it acceptable? Can a framework really console someone in intense pain?

Philosophical Approaches

Argument 1 & 2: Logic and God

Philosophical arguments try to use logic to prove or disprove God's existence based on suffering. They reason from premises to conclusions about God's nature.

Strength: Honest engagement with a real problem. Doesn't shy away from difficult questions.

Challenge: Can logic solve a problem that may be beyond human understanding?

Humanist Approaches

Non-religious worldview

Humanists don't try to justify God's existence. Instead, they focus on practical solutions: medicine, technology, education, and justice systems to reduce suffering.

Strength: Focuses on action and real solutions rather than abstract debate.

Challenge: Does action alone provide meaning for those who suffer? Can we really eliminate all suffering through human effort?

Emotional Truth vs. Logical Truth

Beyond arguments and beliefs

A person might logically accept that free will justifies some suffering—but emotionally, it doesn't ease the pain of losing a child. Different truths matter in different contexts.

Strength: Acknowledges that humans are more than logic. Meaning involves heart as well as mind.

Challenge: If logic and emotion conflict, how do we live with that tension?

1. What is the difference between natural evil and moral evil?

Natural evil is suffering caused by natural forces and events (earthquakes, disease, drought) that are beyond human control. Moral evil is suffering caused by human choices and actions (war, abuse, cruelty). This distinction matters because it raises different theological questions: natural evil is harder for an all-powerful God to justify, while moral evil might be justified by human free will.

2. Why is the existence of suffering especially problematic if God is omnipotent, omnibenevolent, and omniscient?

If God is omnipotent (all-powerful), God has the ability to prevent all suffering. If God is omnibenevolent (all-loving and good), God would want to prevent suffering. If God is omniscient (all-knowing), God knew suffering would happen before creating the world. Given all three qualities, suffering should not exist—yet it does. This creates the logical problem of evil (theodicy).

3. What does Buddhism teach about the cause of suffering?

Buddhism teaches that suffering (dukkha) is caused by tanha—craving, attachment, and desire. People suffer because they cling to things that are impermanent, desire things they don't have, and resist change. Unlike religions that try to justify why a good God allows suffering, Buddhism accepts suffering as a fundamental aspect of existence and teaches that enlightenment (Nirvana) comes from breaking the cycle of craving and attachment.

However, this defense does not explain natural evil (suffering caused by natural forces), which has nothing to do with human choice.

Key Terminology

Make sure you understand these important terms. They will appear again in future lessons as we deepen our exploration of how religions respond to suffering.

Natural Evil
Suffering caused by natural events and forces (disease, earthquakes, aging) beyond human control.
Moral Evil
Suffering caused by human choices and actions (war, cruelty, abuse).
Omnipotent
All-powerful; having infinite power and authority. A key attribute of God in monotheistic religions.
Omnibenevolent
All-loving and perfectly good. A key attribute of God in monotheistic religions.
Omniscient
All-knowing; having complete knowledge of all things, including the future. A key attribute of God in monotheistic religions.
Theodicy
An attempt to justify God's goodness and power in the face of evil and suffering. From Greek: "theos" (God) and "dike" (justice).
The Problem of Evil
The philosophical challenge of reconciling the existence of suffering with belief in an all-powerful, all-knowing, all-loving God.
Free Will
The genuine ability to choose between different courses of action. Free will is seen by many as necessary for moral responsibility.
Karma
In Hinduism and Buddhism, the moral law of cause and effect. Actions have consequences across lifetimes; suffering results from past actions.
Dukkha
The Pali/Sanskrit word in Buddhism for suffering or dissatisfaction. It's the first of the Four Noble Truths.
Enlightenment (Nirvana)
In Buddhism, the state of being free from suffering, craving, and the cycle of rebirth. Achieved through understanding the nature of reality.
Worldview
A comprehensive perspective on life that includes beliefs about meaning, morality, purpose, and how the world works. Can be religious or non-religious.
