The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — A circle, not a line

In the 1920s, Walter Shewhart was working at Bell Telephone Laboratories in the United States, studying how to make factory products more reliable. The usual way of thinking about production was a straight line: write a specification, produce the product, inspect it at the end.

In a book published in 1939, Shewhart argued for something different: “These three steps must go in a circle instead of in a straight line.” Specification, production and inspection, he explained, were like the steps of science — making a hypothesis, carrying out an experiment and testing the hypothesis.

The book was edited by a 39-year-old statistician called W. Edwards Deming. He was going to carry Shewhart's circle to the other side of the world.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the usual “straight line” way of thinking?

2. What did Shewhart compare the three steps to?

3. Who edited Shewhart's book?

1. Write a specification, produce the product, inspect it at the end.

2. The steps of science: making a hypothesis, carrying out an experiment and testing it.

3. W. Edwards Deming, then aged 39.

Discuss:

Part 2 — The Deming wheel

In 1950, Deming taught an eight-day seminar on quality control for Japanese managers and engineers, organised by the Japanese Union of Scientists and Engineers. He turned the straight line of design, produce, sell into a circle by adding a fourth step: redesign through market research. Then round again, and again.

The Japanese called it the “Deming wheel”. Japanese executives then recast it in their own terms as Plan, Do, Check, Act. Nobody knows exactly who did it. As Ronald Moen, who later researched the history of the cycle, wrote: “No one person claimed authorship.”

Together with seven basic quality tools — among them the Pareto chart and the fishbone diagram — PDCA became the foundation of kaizen, the Japanese approach of continuous, step-by-step improvement. They are still used today.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Deming change the straight line in 1950?

2. Who turned the Deming wheel into PDCA?

3. What did PDCA become part of in Japan?

1. He added a fourth step — redesign through market research — and made it a circle.

2. Japanese executives; no one person claimed authorship.

3. The foundation of kaizen (continuous improvement), together with the seven basic quality tools.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think nobody claimed to have invented PDCA?

Part 3 — Study, not check

You might expect Deming to be pleased. He wasn't. In his four-day seminars in the 1980s he often warned audiences that the word “check” was wrong, because in English it means “to hold back”. What he wanted was not checking but studying — learning from the results.

In 1990, commenting on a manuscript by Moen and two colleagues, he wrote: “be sure to call it PDSA, not the corruption PDCA.” A year later, shown a chart labelled Plan/Do/Check/Act, he replied: “How the PDCA ever came into existence I know not.” His final version, in 1993, was the Shewhart Cycle for Learning and Improvement: Plan, Do, Study, Act.

Deming died at the end of 1993. Today both names are used, often by people who have never heard of the argument. But the difference matters. “Check” asks: did it work? “Study” asks: what did we learn?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Deming dislike the word “check”?

2. What did he want people to call the cycle?

3. What is the difference between checking and studying?

1. Because, he said, in English it means “to hold back”.

2. PDSA — Plan, Do, Study, Act.

3. Checking asks whether it worked; studying asks what you learnt.

Discuss:
  • Does the name of a method matter, or only how people use it?

How the tool works

Four steps in a circle. Each turn of the wheel tests one small change, and what you learn becomes the plan for the next turn.

Using it, step by step

  1. Plan: describe the problem, your idea for a change, and what you expect to happen — your prediction.
  2. Do: try the change on a small scale — one team, one week, one shop.
  3. Check (or Study): compare what happened with what you predicted. What surprised you?
  4. Act: keep the change and make it standard, adjust it, or drop it — then plan the next turn.

Try it yourself

A café wants shorter queues at lunchtime. Which step of the cycle is each action?

1. The manager compares this week's waiting times with last week's.
2. They decide to try a separate counter for takeaway coffee, and predict that queues will be five minutes shorter.
3. For one week, a second barista serves takeaway orders only.
4. The new counter works, so it becomes the normal way of working in all three cafés.
5. Staff notice that the takeaway counter was quick, but the main queue was still long because of food orders.
6. The team agrees exactly what they will measure, and for how long, before anything changes.

1. Check — comparing the results with what was expected.

2. Plan — an idea for a change, plus a prediction.

3. Do — a small-scale trial.

4. Act — making a successful change standard.

5. Check — studying what happened — including the surprise.

6. Plan — deciding how you will judge the test, before you start.

Where the tool breaks

The circle stops at Do. Many teams try something and never find out whether it worked.

Too slow for emergencies. In a crisis there may be no time for a careful trial; a faster loop may fit better.

Small tests, big problems. Some changes can't be tested on a small scale — and some results take years to appear.

Grammar — zero and first conditionals

PDCA is built on predictions: if we change this, that will happen. English uses two simple conditionals — one for things that are always true, one for real possibilities in the future.

Zero conditional: if/when + present simple, present simple — for facts and things that always happen.
“If you heat ice, it melts.”  “When the light is red, the machine stops.”
First conditional: if + present simple, will + base verb — for a real possibility in the future.
“If the trial works, we'll use it in every shop.”
Instead of will, you can use may, might, could or an imperative.
“If the results are good, we might expand.”  “If anything goes wrong, call me.”
when or if? When = you're sure it will happen; if = it may or may not.
“When the test ends, we'll look at the data.”  “If the test fails, we'll try again.”
Watch out: no will in the if part: ~~If it will work, we will keep it~~ → “If it works, we'll keep it.”
