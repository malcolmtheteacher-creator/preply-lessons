The Yellow Wallpaper

Charlotte Perkins Gilman — a mind, a room, and a pattern that will not keep still.

Today: you'll read one of the most unsettling first-person narratives in English and interrogate how its language works — the grammar of doubt, evidence and qualification through which a narrator quietly forfeits, or seizes, our trust.

Now — the words for today's story

Each word is embedded in a context that should let you infer its precise sense and register. Read aloud, hypothesise the meaning, then reveal.

1. Ordered to rest and do nothing, she spent the long summer in a state of enforced convalescence that felt less like healing than like slow erasure.
convalescence (n) — the gradual recovery of health after illness; the period of recuperation.
2. Every meaningful occupation was proscribed: she was permitted neither to work nor to write, on the authority of those who claimed to know her mind better than she did.
to proscribe (v) — to forbid or prohibit, especially by authority. (Do not confuse with prescribe, to recommend or order a remedy.)
3. Her tone by evening turned querulous — a low, fretful complaining that she herself despised but could no longer suppress.
querulous (adj) — habitually complaining in a peevish, fretful way.
4. The room was capacious — airy, high-windowed, almost absurdly large for one convalescent — and yet it closed around her like a fist.
capacious (adj) — roomy; capable of holding a great deal.
5. A grey torpor settled over her days: hours dissolved into one another, and even the effort of feeling seemed too much to summon.
torpor (n) — a state of physical or mental inactivity; lethargy, sluggishness.
6. His diagnosis was reassuringly firm; hers remained equivocal, shifting with the light, refusing to resolve into a single certain shape.
equivocal (adj) — open to more than one interpretation; ambiguous, uncertain.
7. To read, to write, to think for oneself — in that household these were quietly subversive acts, threats to an order that depended on her compliance.
subversive (adj) — seeking or serving to undermine an established authority or order.
8. She came to feel immured — not merely confined but sealed in, walled up alive within the pattern of the room.
to immure (v) — to shut in or imprison, especially by enclosing within walls.
9. The wallpaper's design was frankly grotesque — distorted, unnatural, at once comic and repellent — and the longer she stared, the more it seemed to writhe.
grotesque (adj) — comically or repulsively distorted; unnatural and disturbing.
10. What began as fancy hardened, by degrees, into conviction: she no longer supposed there was a woman in the paper — she knew it.
conviction (n) — a firmly held belief; the quality of being utterly certain.

The story, in three parts

Read each part closely. After it, a close-reading question (inference, not recall), then one point to discuss.

Part 1 — "What is one to do?"

It is very seldom that mere ordinary people like John and myself secure ancestral halls for the summer. A colonial mansion, a hereditary estate, I would say a haunted house, and reach the height of romantic felicity — but that would be asking too much of fate!

John is practical in the extreme. He has no patience with faith, an intense horror of superstition, and he scoffs openly at any talk of things not to be felt and seen and put down in figures. John is a physician, and perhaps that is one reason I do not get well faster. You see, he does not believe I am sick! And what can one do? If a physician of high standing, and one's own husband, assures friends and relatives that there is really nothing the matter with one but temporary nervous depression — a slight hysterical tendency — what is one to do?

So I take phosphates and journeys and air and exercise, and am absolutely forbidden to "work" until I am well again. Personally, I disagree with their ideas. But what is one to do?

Close reading — then reveal:

1. The phrase "what is one to do?" recurs three times. What does its repetition, and the impersonal "one", reveal about the narrator's relation to her own agency?

2. Where in this passage does the narrator make a grave accusation while appearing to make none?

1. The refrain stages her helplessness as rhetorical resignation; the impersonal "one" distances her from her own experience, as though she has been argued out of the first person — out of the right to say plainly "I".

2. "John is a physician, and perhaps that is one reason I do not get well faster." The single word "perhaps" lets a damning charge — that her husband's authority is impeding her recovery — pass as idle speculation.

Discuss:
  • Whose account of her condition is the text asking us to trust — the narrator's, or her husband's? On what does that trust rest, and how early does the story begin to unsettle it?

Part 2 — The paper

The most enduring thing in the room is the paper. I never saw a worse paper in my life. One of those sprawling, flamboyant patterns committing every artistic sin. It is dull enough to confuse the eye in following, pronounced enough to constantly irritate and provoke study; and when you follow the lame, uncertain curves for a little distance they suddenly commit suicide — plunge off at outrageous angles, destroy themselves in unheard-of contradictions.

The colour is repellent, almost revolting: a smouldering, unclean yellow, strangely faded by the slow-turning sunlight. It is a dull yet lurid orange in some places, a sickly sulphur tint in others. No wonder the children hated it! I should hate it myself if I had to live in this room long.

There comes John, and I must put this away — he hates to have me write a word.

Close reading — then reveal:

1. The narrator describes an inanimate pattern with verbs of intention and violence ("commit suicide", "destroy themselves"). What does this projection tell us about her state, before she says a word about herself?

2. The paragraph on the paper is elaborate; the sentence about hiding her writing is abrupt. What does that contrast in length and rhythm enact?

1. She reads agency, contradiction and self-destruction into the wallpaper — displacing onto it feelings she is forbidden to name in herself. The paper becomes a screen for what cannot be said directly.

2. The lavish attention to the paper versus the clipped, furtive line about John enacts both her hunger for something to fix her mind on and the surveillance that cuts her expression short mid-thought.

Discuss:
  • The text we are reading is the very writing she is forbidden to do. What does this paradox — a secret document of a silenced voice — do to our sense of the narrator's reliability and her resistance?

Part 3 — "I've got out at last"

I didn't realise for a long time what the thing was that showed behind — that dim sub-pattern — but now I am quite sure it is a woman. By daylight she is subdued, quiet. It is the pattern that keeps her so still. It keeps me quiet by the hour.

At night, in any kind of light — in twilight, candlelight, lamplight, and worst of all by moonlight — it becomes bars! The outside pattern, I mean; and the woman behind it is as plain as can be. She is all the time trying to climb through. But nobody could climb through that pattern — it strangles so.

"I've got out at last," said I, "in spite of you and Jane! And I've pulled off most of the paper, so you can't put me back!" Now why should that man have fainted? But he did, and right across my path by the wall, so that I had to creep over him every time!

Close reading — then reveal:

1. Track the pronouns across this passage: the woman behind the paper, "me", "I". By the final lines, what has happened to the boundary between the narrator and the figure she has been watching?

2. The last line is delivered without alarm or explanation. How does the narrator's calm registration of John's collapse complete the story's inversion of power?

1. The trapped woman and the narrator merge: "it keeps her so still" slides into "it keeps me quiet", until "I've got out" claims the escape as her own. Observer and observed have become one.

2. Her flat, almost puzzled tone ("Now why should that man have fainted?") reverses the story's hierarchy: the diagnosing husband lies unconscious on the floor while she, no longer asking permission, simply creeps over him — authority literally beneath her.

Discuss:
  • Is the ending a liberation, a defeat, or something the story deliberately refuses to let us settle? Build the strongest case you can for a reading that holds two of these at once.

Grammar — The Language of Unreliability

How much a narrator asks us to believe is encoded in grammar: in modal verbs, evidential and stance adverbs, hedges and rhetorical questions. This story is a masterclass in it — the narrator's every qualification both concedes and resists. Here we make that machinery explicit.

The toolkit of (un)certainty:
• Epistemic modals grade commitment to truth: must (confident inference) > should > may / might / could (open possibility). "It must be a woman" vs "It might be a woman."
• Evidential / stance adverbs flag the source or strength of a claim: evidently, apparently, seemingly, supposedly, surely, presumably, perhaps. "Apparently there is nothing the matter with me" quietly attributes the claim to someone else.
• Copular verbs of perception — seem, appear, look — report an impression rather than assert a fact: "the pattern seems to move."
• Hedges & discourse framing — I suppose, somehow, in a way, if, and the rhetorical question — soften, defer, or disown a proposition even as it is uttered.

1. Calibrate the certainty

Complete each gap with a word matching the degree or source of (un)certainty described in brackets.

1. The sub-pattern be a woman — I cannot yet be sure. (bare possibility)

2. By moonlight the paper to become bars. (reported impression, not asserted fact)

3. , there is nothing the matter with me — or so I am told. (the claim belongs to others)

4. She has watched it for hours; it be a woman now. (confident inference)

1. might / may / could (open possibility)

2. seems / appears (perception verb — impression, not fact)

3. Apparently / Supposedly (evidential — attributes the claim elsewhere)

4. must (strong epistemic inference)

2. Introduce unreliability

Rewrite each flat assertion so the narrator's certainty is quietly undermined — using a modal, a perception verb, an evidential adverb or a hedge. (More than one good version exists.)

1. The woman escapes the pattern at night. →

2. John does not understand my condition. →

3. I am perfectly well. →

1. e.g. "The woman seems to escape the pattern at night." / "I suppose the woman escapes…"

2. e.g. "John does not appear to understand my condition." / "Perhaps John does not understand…"

3. e.g. "I am, apparently, perfectly well." / "I must be perfectly well — everyone assures me so." (Note how the added evidential turns a claim into a reported, doubtful one.)

3. Close analysis

In the sentence "John is a physician, and perhaps that is one reason I do not get well faster," which single word does the heaviest work, and what exactly does it accomplish? Write your analysis, then reveal a model.

"perhaps". It hedges a serious indictment — that her husband's authority is obstructing her recovery — into tentative musing. The evidential enacts her trained self-doubt: the syntax permits a genuine insight to masquerade as idle speculation, so that the narrator can voice a forbidden truth while seeming to disown it. The reader is left to decide whether "perhaps" is timidity or a covert, deniable accusation.

Going deeper — take the reading further

Argue these out loud. There are no settled answers — only interpretations you can defend from the text. Where you can, cite the story's own language as evidence.

Useful language for argument: "The text invites us to…", "What complicates that reading is…", "One might object that…, yet…", "It is no accident that…", "The ambiguity is not a flaw but a design."
1 · The reliability question

How much of what the narrator reports can we trust — and does the story want us to reach a verdict at all? Consider how the first-person form makes us complicit: we have no vantage point outside her account. Is an "unreliable" narrator a failure of testimony, or a more truthful representation of a mind under pressure than any neutral observer could give?

2 · Who gets to name reality

Set the husband's vocabulary ("nothing the matter", "a slight tendency", "temporary nervous depression") against the narrator's lived experience. The story stages a contest over whose language is allowed to define what is real. What is at stake in that contest, and how does the ending settle — or refuse to settle — it?

3 · Ambiguity as design

Gilman never confirms whether the woman in the paper is hallucination, symbol, or the narrator's own reflected self. Why might a writer choose irresolution over explanation? What would the story lose if it told us plainly what "really" happened?

4 · The confined and the created

The narrator is forbidden to write, yet the text exists because she writes it in secret. Read the story as an argument about the relation between constraint and creation: does restriction here silence the imagination, or provoke it into its most radical act? Bring in other works — literary or otherwise — where confinement and creativity are entangled.

To finish — the harder questions

  1. Does the story diagnose a mind, or does it indict the people and language that confined it? Can it do both without contradiction?
  2. The narrator "wins" only by a reading in which victory and catastrophe are indistinguishable. What does the story suggest about the price of asserting a self against an order that denies it?
  3. A century on, why does this text still unsettle readers more than many far more explicit horror stories?
