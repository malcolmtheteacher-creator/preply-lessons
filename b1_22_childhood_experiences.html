B1 - My Childhood Experiences

Conversation Theme: Modals of Past Obligation and Permission

Time: 3:00

Getting Started

Think back to your childhood. What were the rules in your home? What were you allowed to do? What were you not allowed to do?

Lead-In Topics

  • Did you grow up in a city or the countryside?
  • What rules do you remember most clearly?
  • How were children different then compared to now?
  • Was your childhood easier or harder than children today?
  • If you could change something about your childhood, what would it be?

Grammar: Modals of Past Obligation and Permission

We use different modals to talk about obligations, permissions, and habits in the past.

Key Modal Structures

Obligation (had to, was/were permitted to): Something was required or expected.

"We had to wear a uniform in primary school."

Prohibition (was/were not allowed to, was/were not permitted to): Something was forbidden.

"We were not allowed to call teachers by their first names."

No Obligation (didn't have to): Something was not required, but was possible.

"They didn't have to worry about traffic."

Permission/Ability (could, was/were allowed to): Something was permitted or possible.

"I could stay out until 9pm."

Past Habit (would): Something happened regularly in the past.

"I would spend summers with my grandmother."

Example Sentences

1. "In primary school, we had to wear a uniform" Obligatory
2. "We were not allowed to call teachers by first names" Prohibited
3. "I would spend summers with my grandmother" Past Habits
4. "I was not permitted to use the internet more than 90 minutes" Prohibited
5. "They didn't have to worry about traffic" Not Necessary
6. "I could stay out until 9pm" Allowed

Practice Exercises

Exercise 1: Choose the Correct Modal

Click "Reveal Answer" to check each one.

1.When I was young, I couldn't / would go to the beach every weekend in summer.

✓ Answer: would — This expresses a past habit (repeated action).

2.My parents didn't have to / weren't allowed to let us eat candy before dinner.

✓ Answer: weren't allowed to — This expresses prohibition. "Didn't have to" means it wasn't necessary, which changes the meaning.

3.At school, students had to / could wear formal shoes, even in summer.

✓ Answer: had to — This is an obligation or requirement. "Could" would mean permission or ability.

4.My older sister would / couldn't stay out until 10pm, but I had to / wasn't permitted to go home by 8pm.

✓ Answer: could and was not permitted to (or "wasn't allowed to") — The first is permission; the second is prohibition.

5.We didn't have to / weren't allowed to do homework on weekends if we finished it during the week.

✓ Answer: didn't have to — This means it wasn't required. "Weren't allowed to" would mean it was forbidden, which is different.

6.In my childhood home, children had to / could respect their parents and weren't allowed to / didn't have to argue with them.

✓ Answer: had to and weren't allowed to — Both express obligation and prohibition respectively. This was a strict rule.

Interview Activity

Use these questions to have a conversation about childhood experiences and rules. Think about your own answers, and be ready to explain your choices.

Interview Questions

1. Clothes and School Uniforms:
Did you have to wear a uniform? If yes, what was it like? If no, what could you wear? Were you allowed to wear what you wanted, or were there rules?
2. Homework and School Rules:
How much homework did you have to do? When did you have to finish it? Could you stay up late to play, or did you have to go to bed early? Were you allowed to watch TV before finishing your homework?
3. Staying Out Late:
What time did you have to be home? Could you play outside until it was dark? Were you allowed to stay at a friend's house overnight? What were the rules about going out?
4. Chores and Family Responsibilities:
Did you have to help with chores? What did you have to do? Were you allowed to choose which chores you would do, or did your parents decide?
5. Food and Meals:
Did you have to eat everything on your plate? Were you allowed to eat snacks between meals? What foods did you have to eat that you didn't like? Could you choose what you wanted for dessert?
6. Celebrations and Special Days:
What traditions did your family have? Did you have to participate in family celebrations? Were you allowed to stay up late on special occasions? What couldn't you do during holidays?

Reflection: Similarities and Differences

After your interview, think about these questions:

  • What rules do you and your conversation partner have in common?
  • What rules were different?
  • Was one childhood more strict than the other?
  • Which rules made sense to you? Which seemed unfair?

Discussion Topics

Explore these questions to go deeper into the theme of childhood experiences.

Thinking About Your Childhood

City vs. Countryside:
Was your childhood in a city, a small town, or the countryside? How did your location affect the rules you had to follow? What could you do that children in different places couldn't do?
Were Children Well-Behaved?
Thinking about the rules you had, do you think children in your era were better-behaved than today's children? What made the difference — the rules, or something else?
Harder Then or Now?
Do you think childhood was harder for you than it is for children today? What challenges did you have to face? What challenges do children face now that you didn't have?
If You Could Change Things:
If you could redo your childhood, what would you change? What rules would you change? What would you do differently? What parts would you keep the same?
The Purpose of Rules:
Now that you're an adult, do you understand why your parents or guardians had those rules? Do you think they were fair? If you have (or had) children, what rules do you think are important?

Comparing Cultural Perspectives

Around the World:
The rules children have to follow can be very different depending on the country and culture. What do you think are universal rules that all children should follow? What rules are specific to your culture?
Then and Now:
How have the rules for children changed since you were young? Are modern rules stricter or more relaxed? What do you think about these changes?
