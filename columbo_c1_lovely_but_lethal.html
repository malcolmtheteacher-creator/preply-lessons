Lovely But Lethal

A cosmetics queen with a secret formula, a rival, and an itch that gives everything away.

Today: you'll tell the story of a glamorous businesswoman, a secret formula and a very small clue, and you'll master reporting verbs — admit, deny, insist, accuse and more.

The words for today's story

Work it out from the situation

Read each sentence aloud and say what you think the bold word means — then reveal.

Everything about the launch party was designed to dazzle: gold dresses, flashing cameras, models who seemed never to blink. The magazines loved the glamour.
glamour (n) — an exciting, attractive quality linked to beauty, fame, luxury and wealth.
Her office was spotless: not a speck of dust on the glass desk, not a crease in the white carpet, not a paper out of line. It was immaculate.
immaculate (adj) — perfectly clean and tidy.
In the beauty trade, companies steal each other's designers and spy on each other's plans; rivals will do almost anything to win. It is a cutthroat business.
cutthroat (adj) — extremely competitive, with no mercy for rivals.

Which word?

Read the meaning and try to find the word before you reveal. The first letter is given.

Which word means an important discovery that suddenly makes progress possible?  b__________ (n)
a breakthrough (n) — a major discovery or development. Scientists announced a breakthrough in cancer treatment.
Which word means a feeling that something is true, without having proof?  h____ (n)
a hunch (n) — an instinctive feeling. I have a hunch that she is lying.

Complete the phrase

The police searched the whole flat but could not find a t____ of evidence.  (5 letters, begins with t)
a trace (n) — a very small sign or amount of something left behind. a trace of poison / no trace of him.

Choose the meaning

Pick a, b or c, then reveal.

shrewd — “She was a shrewd negotiator and rarely lost a deal.”  shrewd means:
a) rude and impatient   b) generous with money   c) clever at judging situations, especially for gain
(c) — shrewd (adj) means sharp and clever in practical matters, especially business.
contaminated — “The river was contaminated by waste from the factory.”  contaminated means:
a) made impure by something harmful   b) cleaned thoroughly   c) hidden away
(a) — contaminated (adj) means made dirty or dangerous by a harmful substance.
exploit — “Some employers exploit their workers, paying them very little for long hours.”  exploit means:
a) explore carefully   b) use someone or something unfairly for your own gain   c) protect from danger
(b) — to exploit (v) means to take unfair advantage of someone or something. (It can also mean to use a resource fully.)
stake — “The investor demanded a stake in the company's future profits.”  stake here means:
a) a share or financial interest   b) a wooden post   c) a legal warning
(a) — a stake (n) in this sense is a share or financial interest in a business. (A stake can also be a pointed wooden post.)
Use them out loud:
  • Describe a cutthroat business or sport you know about.
  • Have you ever had a hunch that turned out to be right? You don't have to share personal details — an invented example is fine.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, try the quick check from memory, then talk about the question.

Part 1 — A cosmetics empire

Viveca Scott has built a cosmetics empire on her glamour and her talent for selling hope. Her latest product, a wrinkle remover, could be the most successful she has ever launched.

There is one problem: the secret formula belongs to someone else. It was created by a chemist who was once her lover, and who now wants a share of the profits.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is Viveca's new product?

2. Why is the chemist a problem for her?

1. A wrinkle remover.

2. The secret formula is his, and he wants a share of the profits.

Discuss:
  • Is selling “hope” in a bottle an honest business? Why do people buy it?

Part 2 — A charming suspect

Viveca does not intend to share anything. When the chemist is found dead, she is elegant, charming and extremely helpful to the police.

Columbo arrives in her gleaming world of mirrors and beautiful models. He feels out of place, but he has a sharp eye for whatever does not belong.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Viveca behave after the chemist's death?

2. Why does Columbo feel out of place?

1. She is charming, elegant and helpful.

2. Because he is surrounded by mirrors, models and glamour — a world very different from his own.

Discuss:
  • Why can charm work as a disguise? Can you think of a real or fictional example?

Part 3 — The itch

Columbo notices that both he and Viveca are scratching. He wonders why, and traces the itch to poison ivy oil on a broken microscope slide.

The oil proves that she handled that slide at the time of the murder. A beautiful, confident woman is trapped by a tiny, invisible trace of a plant.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What do Columbo and Viveca have in common?

2. What does the poison ivy oil prove?

1. They are both itching.

2. It proves she handled the broken slide when the murder happened.

Discuss:
  • Which convinces people more: a confession or a physical clue? Why?

Grammar — reporting verbs

When you tell a story, a rumour or a news item, you report what people said — and the verb you choose changes the meaning. Said is neutral; admitted, denied, insisted and accused each add attitude. But each reporting verb takes its own pattern.

The patterns:
• verb + -ing — admit, deny, suggest, recommend: “She admitted having been in the laboratory.” “He denied taking the formula.”
• verb + that-clause — insist, claim, explain, suggest: “She insisted that she had been at a party all evening.”
• verb + to-infinitive — refuse, agree, promise, threaten: “She refused to share the profits.”
• verb + object + to-infinitive — warn, advise, persuade, invite: “He advised her to be careful.”
• verb + object + of / for + -ing — accuse … of, blame … for, apologise for: “He accused her of stealing his work.”
Watch out: we say suggest doing or suggest that, but not “suggest to do”. And denied taking, not “denied to take”.

1. Report the speech

Complete the reported sentence with the correct form (a verb form, a preposition, or both).

1. “I didn't take the formula,” said the assistant. → The assistant denied the formula.

2. “I won't share the profits,” said Viveca. → Viveca refused the profits.

3. “You stole my work!” he told her. → He accused her his work.

1. taking (deny + -ing)

2. to share (refuse + to-infinitive)

3. of stealing (accuse someone of + -ing)

2. Fix the pattern

Write the verb in brackets in the correct form after the reporting verb.

1. The detective warned her (not / touch) anything on the desk.

2. She admitted (lie) about where she had been.

3. He apologised (keep) her waiting so long.

1. not to touch (warn someone + to-infinitive)

2. lying (admit + -ing)

3. for keeping (apologise for + -ing)

3. Choose the right verb

Complete each sentence with denied, accused or insisted — whichever fits the meaning in brackets.

1. Viveca having any reason to hurt him. (she said it was not true)

2. His friends her of stealing his idea. (they blamed her)

3. She on being treated with respect. (she refused to accept anything less)

1. denied (deny + -ing)

2. accused (accuse … of)

Line 1. “It was not me. I was nowhere near the laboratory.”
For example: She denied having been near the laboratory. / She claimed that she had been nowhere near it.

For example: He threatened to tell everyone the formula was his. / He demanded that she share the profits.

For example: He advised her to be more careful. / He warned her not to touch the slide.

Useful language: reporting verbs — admit, deny, insist, claim, accuse … of, refuse, warn, advise. To tell the story: “She's charming on the surface, but…”, “What gives her away is…”, “The turning point comes when…”. For opinions: “It seems to me that…”, “I'd say…”.
