Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Deep inside the power station, the reactor split atoms hour after hour, and the enormous heat it threw off was used to boil water, spin turbines, and light up half a region.
a reactor (n) — the part of a nuclear power station where atoms are split to produce energy.
2. The engineers' worst fear was a meltdown: the core growing so hot that the fuel itself melted, burned through its container, and could no longer be stopped.
a meltdown (n) — a severe accident in which a reactor's core overheats and melts, releasing radiation.
3. To seal the wrecked core away, engineers raced to raise a vast concrete sarcophagus over the reactor, entombing the radioactive ruins beneath thousands of tonnes of steel and cement.
a sarcophagus (n) — a massive protective casing (originally a stone coffin) built to contain something dangerous or entomb it.
4. Days after the eruption, fine grey fallout was still drifting down on towns hundreds of miles away, settling on roofs, fields and washing lines like a dirty snow.
fallout (n) — radioactive (or other harmful) particles that fall from the air after an explosion or accident.
5. Inspectors closed the beach because of contamination: the water and the sand had been poisoned by waste, and were no longer safe to touch.
contamination (n) — the state of being made dirty, poisoned or dangerous by an unwanted substance.
6. What began as a small fault grew, hour by hour, into a full catastrophe — homes lost, lives lost, a whole region changed forever.
a catastrophe (n) — a sudden, enormous disaster causing great damage or suffering.
7. Rather than admit the leak, the authorities moved to suppress it — destroying the reports, silencing the staff, and telling the public that nothing at all had gone wrong.
to suppress (v) — to deliberately prevent information from being seen, published or spoken about.
8. Doctors warned that even a few minutes of exposure to the leak could harm the body — and the men who had stood closest to it for hours fell ill within days.
exposure (n) — the state of being unprotected against something harmful, such as radiation, cold or danger.
9. The land around the plant became an exclusion zone: fences went up, soldiers turned cars back at the gates, and ordinary people were forbidden to enter.
an exclusion zone (n) — a sealed-off area that people are not allowed to enter, usually because it is dangerous.
10. They were called the liquidators — the soldiers, miners and workers sent in afterwards to clean up the mess, bury the waste and make the ruined site safe.
a liquidator (n) — one of the workers sent in to clean up and contain the Chernobyl disaster (from the Soviet phrase for "dealing with the consequences").

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A test in the dark

Just outside the small Soviet town of Pripyat, in what is now northern Ukraine, stood the Chernobyl nuclear power station — four huge reactors that lit homes and powered factories across the region. Pripyat itself was a model town: new, clean and young, built for the plant's workers and their families, with an average age under thirty. On the night of 25 April 1986, the engineers at Reactor No. 4 prepared for a safety test. The idea sounded sensible enough. If the power ever failed, would the reactor's own spinning turbines keep the vital cooling pumps running for the few seconds before the backup generators kicked in?

To run the test they had to take the reactor down to very low power — and there the trouble began. The RBMK reactor, the Soviet design used at Chernobyl, behaved dangerously when run slow, and the men at the controls had not been told just how unstable it could become. Worse, to keep the test going they switched off safety systems and pulled out almost all the control rods that normally calm the reaction. Shortly after one in the morning on 26 April, the reactor's power surged out of control in seconds. An operator slammed the emergency shutdown — but on this flawed design, that final button made things worse, not better.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the safety test actually trying to find out?

2. Name two things the engineers did that made the reactor dangerously unstable.

1. Whether the reactor's still-spinning turbines could keep the cooling pumps running for the brief gap before the backup generators started, if the power failed.

2. They ran it at very low power (where the RBMK design was unstable), switched off safety systems, and pulled out almost all the control rods.

Discuss:
  • The operators were never warned how unstable the design could be. When something goes wrong, where should the blame fall — on the people at the controls, or on the people who built and hid the flaw?

Part 2 — The town that had to leave

Two enormous explosions tore the top off Reactor No. 4. The thousand-tonne lid was thrown aside, the roof was blown open, and the burning core was left exposed to the night sky, pouring radiation straight into the air. Firefighters arrived within minutes, knowing there was a blaze but not that they were standing in an invisible storm of radiation. Many received fatal doses that night. Of the workers and firefighters taken to hospital, 28 would die within months of acute radiation sickness.

And yet, in Pripyat, life carried on at first. Children went to school. People hung out washing in air that was already poisoned. For a day and a half the authorities said almost nothing. Only on the afternoon of 27 April — some 36 hours after the explosion — did the buses arrive. Around 47,000 residents were told to pack a small bag for "a few days." Within hours the town got evacuated, and its people were driven away. They never came back. Pripyat stands empty to this day: a ghost town of silent flats, a rusting fairground, and a swimming pool with no water.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why were the first firefighters in such terrible danger?

2. How long after the explosion was Pripyat evacuated, and what were residents told?

1. The blast left the burning core exposed, so they were working in intense, invisible radiation without knowing it; many received fatal doses, and 28 workers and firefighters died within months.

2. About 36 hours later, on the afternoon of 27 April; the roughly 47,000 residents were told to pack a small bag for just "a few days" — but they never returned.

Discuss:
  • Officials waited a day and a half before moving anyone. What might make a government delay a warning even when lives are at stake?

Part 3 — A secret too big to keep

The Soviet government said nothing to the wider world. Then, on the morning of 28 April, alarms began sounding at the Forsmark nuclear plant in Sweden, more than a thousand kilometres away. Staff there checked their own reactor for a leak and found none — the radiation was blowing in on the wind, from the east. When Sweden demanded answers, Moscow could no longer stay silent, and that evening it finally admitted, in a few brief sentences, that there had been an accident at Chernobyl. Even then the truth was held back. The scale of the disaster was said to have been hidden for days, and in nearby Kyiv the May Day parades went ahead as planned, with thousands marching beneath an invisible cloud.

The clean-up became one of the largest operations of its kind in history. Hundreds of thousands of workers — soldiers, miners and volunteers, the so-called liquidators — were sent in to bury contaminated soil, wash down streets, and build a vast concrete "sarcophagus" over the ruined reactor in just a few months. A 30-kilometre exclusion zone was sealed off around the plant, and it remains in place today. It is now believed that many workers got exposed to dangerous doses of radiation, and the long-term health toll is still argued over. Above all, the disaster shattered public trust: a secretive system had hidden a catastrophe from its own people, and many historians see Chernobyl as one of the cracks that helped bring down the Soviet Union itself.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the outside world first learn that something had happened?

2. What were the liquidators sent in to do?

1. Radiation alarms went off at the Forsmark plant in Sweden on 28 April; the staff found no leak of their own, traced it to the wind from the east, and pressure from Sweden forced Moscow to admit the accident.

2. To clean up and contain the disaster — burying contaminated soil, washing down streets, and building the concrete sarcophagus over the ruined reactor.

Discuss:
  • The accident stayed hidden until a country a thousand kilometres away picked it up on its instruments. In today's connected world, could a disaster of this size still be covered up — even for a day?

Grammar — The get-passive & Passive Reporting

This story is full of things that happened to people and places — a town that was emptied, workers who were harmed — and things that were only ever claimed or reported, never quite confirmed. English has two neat tools for exactly that, and Chernobyl shows off both.

1. The get-passive — for things that happen TO people and places.
Instead of be + past participle, everyday spoken English often uses get + past participle. It feels more informal and dramatic, and it stresses that something happened to the subject — often suddenly or unluckily.
• "Within hours the town got evacuated." (= was evacuated)
• "Dozens of workers got exposed to dangerous doses." (= were exposed)
• "The roof got blown off the reactor." (= was blown off)
Form: get / got / gets / is getting + past participle. Use it in conversation and storytelling; in a formal report you'd still write was/were + participle.
2. Passive reporting — for what is said, claimed or believed.
When you want to report a claim without naming who said it (and often without fully committing to it), English uses impersonal passive patterns:
• It + passive + that… — "It was claimed that the plant was safe." / "It is now believed that dozens of workers were exposed."
• Subject + passive + to-infinitive — "The disaster was said to have been hidden for days." / "Officials are reported to have delayed the warning."
Use to + infinitive for something happening at the same time ("is said to be safe") and to have + past participle for something earlier ("is said to have been hidden"). Common reporting verbs: said, reported, believed, thought, claimed, known, alleged.

1. Rewrite with the get-passive

Make these more informal by replacing was/were + participle with got + participle.

1. The town was evacuated overnight. → The town overnight.

2. The reactor's roof was blown off. → The reactor's roof .

3. Many firefighters were exposed to radiation. → Many firefighters to radiation.

4. The whole area was sealed off. → The whole area .

1. got evacuated

2. got blown off

3. got exposed

4. got sealed off

All informal "happened to it" passives — fine in speech, replaced by was/were in a formal report.

2. Report it — without naming who said it

Turn each claim into impersonal passive reporting. Use the structure in brackets.

1. People believe that the design was flawed. → It that the design was flawed. (It + passive + that)

2. They reported that the government had delayed the warning. → The government delayed the warning. (was reported to have)

3. People say that the truth was hidden for days. → The truth hidden for days. (subject + passive reporting verb + to-infinitive)

1. is believed (It is believed that the design was flawed.)

2. was reported to have (an earlier action → to have + participle)

3. is said to have been (earlier, passive event → to have been + participle)

3. Put them together — narrate a paragraph

Fill each gap with a get-passive or a passive-reporting structure, whichever fits. Read it aloud once you've finished.

When the core exploded, the roof (blow) clean off, and within a day the town of Pripyat (evacuate). For days the Soviet state stayed quiet; it (claim) that everything was under control. The scale of the accident (say) to have been hidden, and it is now (believe) that hundreds of workers (expose) to dangerous radiation.

got blown · got evacuated · was claimed (it was claimed that…) · was said (was said to have been hidden) · believed (it is now believed that…) · got exposed

Notice the split: get-passives for what happened to the place and the people; reporting passives for what was only claimed or believed.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The operators were never warned how dangerous their reactor was. When disaster strikes, how do we fairly divide the blame between the people at the controls and the system that put them there?
  2. The clean-up cost hundreds of thousands of liquidators their health. Were they heroes, victims, or both — and how should a country remember people like that?
  3. Many see Chernobyl as one of the cracks that helped bring down the Soviet Union. Can a single disaster really topple a whole system — or does it only reveal what was already breaking?
