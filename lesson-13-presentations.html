The auction

Rome, about a hundred years before the birth of Christ. Quintus Hortensius Hortalus, a famous lawyer, was known for a memory that his friends found hard to believe. He could listen to a long argument in court, and then stand up and answer every point in turn, without a note.

According to the writer Seneca the Elder, who tells the story a century later, his friends once made a bet. Hortensius sat through a whole day at an auction, listening to the sales. In the evening he stood up and recited, in order, everything that had been sold, who bought it and for how much. The auctioneer's clerk checked the records. Hortensius did not make a single mistake.

What did Hortensius do after the auction?
He recited everything that had been sold, with the buyers and prices, in order, and the records agreed.
Talk about it. Is it more impressive to remember a day of speeches or a day of prices? Which would you find harder?

To Hortensius and the men he competed with, this was not a party trick. A Roman orator had to speak for hours in public with no screen, no cards, and no microphone. The ancient teachers of public speaking, whose books we still have, divided their art into five parts, and memory was one of them.

The five were: finding what to say, putting it in order, choosing the words, keeping it in mind, and delivering it with voice and gesture. A speaker who forgot his place lost the case. So memory was taught as carefully as grammar.

Why did Roman orators need memory so much?
They spoke for hours with no notes, screens or microphones, and losing the thread could lose the case.
Talk about it. What is the longest you have spoken in public without notes? How did you feel?

Today we give talks with slides, and most of us still read from them, which is why so many talks are dull. A speaker who reads loses the audience. A speaker who looks up, who has the talk in their head and the audience in their eyes, is a different person.

This is exactly what the palace method makes possible. You do not memorise a script word for word. You memorise five or six pictures in five or six places, and the words come as you walk. The Romans did it that way, and you can too.

What do you memorise for a talk, if not the script?
A few strong pictures, one for each main point, placed in a route you can walk.
Talk about it. Think of a talk you have to give soon. What is its main point in one sentence?

A talk in five or six rooms

The ancient teachers named five parts of rhetoric. The fourth one, memory, is the part you are learning here, and the other four tell you what to put in your palace.

InventionFinding what to sayA lightbulb

  1. Write the talk as five or six points. An opening hook, three main points and a closing. One line each.
  2. Turn each point into one vivid picture. Not the words: the idea. A sad moon for 'sleep problems'.
  3. Choose a palace with the same number of places. A short route you know well: door, hall, kitchen, stairs, bedroom.
  4. Place the pictures along the route. In the order of the talk. The walk is the structure.
  5. Rehearse by walking. Say the talk aloud as you move through the palace, ten minutes at a time.
  6. Add a calming ritual for the moment before you speak. A slow breath, a hand on the table, and a picture of the first place.
A talk on why sleep matters
Five points: hook, three reasons, call to action.
Front door: hookA giant yawning moon is sitting on the doorstep, with a pillow for a face: 'Who slept badly last night?'
Hall: memoryA filing cabinet in the hall, with sleeping drawers snoring as each memory is put in.
Kitchen: moodA grumpy toast is leaping from the toaster, shouting at the butter.
Stairs: healthA bandaged heart is climbing the stairs, panting.
Bedroom: call to actionA bed with a clock saying 10 p.m. and a big green tick.
The picture: As you walk in, a yawning moon on the doorstep starts the talk, and the rest follows as you go from room to room.

Four tips from the old teachers

  • Remember ideas, not sentences. The words will come, and fresh words sound alive.
  • Keep a spare place for the moment your mind goes blank: a picture that says 'start the next point'.
  • Rehearse out loud. Practise the sound of speaking, not only the thought.
  • Practise on a walk. Moving helps your mind link the words to places.
