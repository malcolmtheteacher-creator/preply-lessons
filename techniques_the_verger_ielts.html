The Verger

A short story by W. Somerset Maugham, rebuilt as a Band 7 IELTS workout — Reading, Grammar, Writing Task 2 and Speaking

Reading passage

Read the passage once for general meaning. The paragraphs are labelled A–G so you can find information quickly in the Reading Tasks tab. This is an adapted version of Maugham's 1929 story. A Band 7 reader should aim to read it and answer all the questions in about 20 minutes — the time you would have per passage in the real Academic test.

AFor sixteen years, Albert Edward Foreman had been the verger of St Peter's, a fine old church in a wealthy part of London. A verger is a church official who looks after the building and helps to run the services. Albert carried out his duties with great dignity. He was tall and well dressed, and in his long black gown he looked so respectable that strangers sometimes mistook him for a senior member of the clergy. He took enormous pride in his work and had served three vicars faithfully.

BOne afternoon the new vicar, a young and energetic man, asked Albert to stay behind. With two church officials beside him, the vicar explained, in an embarrassed way, that he had recently made a surprising discovery: Albert could neither read nor write. The previous vicar had known this and had not minded, but the new vicar found it unacceptable. He gave Albert three months to learn to read and write. If he failed, he would lose his position.

CAlbert listened calmly. He had never learned as a boy, he explained, and he felt he was too old to begin now. He believed, quite sincerely, that he had done his job perfectly well for many years without these skills. Politely but firmly, he refused. The vicar, disappointed, told him that in that case he would have to leave. Albert accepted the decision without argument and agreed to resign as soon as a replacement could be found.

DWalking home that evening, Albert felt low and uncertain about his future. He had saved a little money, but not enough to live on for long. He wanted a cigarette to calm his nerves, but when he searched the long street he was walking down, he could not find a single shop that sold tobacco. It struck him as strange that such a busy street had no tobacconist. "I can't be the only person walking here who wants a smoke," he thought. "A small shop selling tobacco and sweets might do very well in a place like this."

EThe idea would not leave him. The very next day he found a small shop to rent on that street, and within a day he had taken it. He soon resigned from the church and opened his business. It did well from the start. After a year or two he opened a second shop in another street that also had no tobacconist, and then a third. Over ten years he built up a chain of ten successful shops and became a wealthy man, collecting the takings himself each week and depositing them at the bank.

FOne morning the manager of his bank asked to see him. He pointed out that Albert had a very large sum of money sitting in his account — more than thirty thousand pounds — and suggested that he invest it in safe government securities to earn a better return. Albert said he would prefer to leave the decisions to the bank, and that he would simply sign whatever papers were necessary. The manager assured him this would be easy: all he had to do was read the documents and sign them.

GAt this, Albert admitted, with a quiet smile, that he could not read or write at all, and never had been able to. The manager was astonished. "Good heavens," he cried, "just imagine what you might have become if you could read and write!" Albert thought for a moment and replied that he knew exactly what he would have been: he would still be the verger of St Peter's church.

Before the questions — Band 7 reading strategy:
  • Skim first (2 minutes): read the whole passage once for gist before looking at any question. Strong candidates do not read question-by-question.
  • Read the heading options before you scan — for Matching Headings, decide the main idea of each paragraph in your own words first, then find the heading that fits.
  • Watch for paraphrase and distractors. The answers will almost never repeat the words of the question; the test rewards recognising meaning, not matching words.

Band 7 reading habits these tasks build:
  • Matching Headings: summarise each paragraph's main idea in your own words before reading the options, then match for meaning. Cross out distractors as you go.

Grammar for Band 7

Band 7 Writing and Speaking both require "a variety of complex structures" used with "frequent error-free sentences". This story hands you the perfect models. We will drill the conditionals the ending turns on, then the complex and cohesive sentences that lift a Task 2 essay.

1. The third & mixed conditional — straight from the last line

The story's final exchange is a conditional: "What would you have become if you could read?" — "I would still be the verger." Use the third conditional (If + past perfect, would have + past participle) for an imagined, unreal past, and the mixed conditional (If + past perfect, would + base verb) when a past cause has a present result.

1. If Albert (learn) to read as a boy, he (never / leave) the church. (third)

2. If the new vicar (not / insist) on literacy, Albert (not / open) his first shop. (third)

3. If Albert had been able to read, he (be) a poor verger today, not a wealthy man. (mixed — present result)

4. He (not / be) so rich now if that street (have) a tobacconist. (mixed)

1. had learned / would never have left

2. had not insisted / would not have opened

3. would be (mixed: past condition, present result)

4. would not be / had had

2. Complex noun phrases & relative clauses — written range

Band 7 writing avoids strings of short sentences. Combine each pair into one complex sentence using a relative clause (who / which / whose / where) — and decide if it needs commas (non-defining) or not (defining).

1. Albert was a verger. He could neither read nor write.
→

2. He noticed a long street. The street had no tobacconist.
→

3. The bank manager was astonished. He had assumed Albert could read.
→

1. Albert was a verger who could neither read nor write. (defining — no comma)

2. He noticed a long street that / which had no tobacconist. (defining)

3. The bank manager, who had assumed Albert could read, was astonished. (non-defining — commas)

3. Cohesion & sentence transformation — the Band 7 sentence

Coherence & Cohesion is a full quarter of your Task 2 mark. Rewrite each simple idea using the structure in brackets, so your essays read with flexible, accurate linking — not a list of "Firstly… Secondly… Finally".

1. He lost his job. He became rich. (Use "Despite + -ing")
→

2. He could not read. He still ran a successful business. (Use "Although")
→

3. The street had no tobacconist. He decided to open one. (Use "Since")
→

1. Despite losing his job, he became rich. / Despite having lost his job, he went on to become wealthy.

2. Although he could not read, he ran a successful business.

3. Since the street had no tobacconist, he decided to open one.

Notice how each version subordinates one idea to another — that is the grammatical "range" examiners reward at Band 7.

Carry these into the Writing and Speaking tabs:
  • At least one accurate conditional in your essay and in Speaking Part 3 signals range instantly.
  • Mix sentence lengths: one short sentence for emphasis, surrounded by longer complex ones.
  • Use subordinators (although, since, despite, whereas) rather than only and / but / so.

Paraphrasing — the core Band 7 skill

In Reading you recognise paraphrase; in Writing and Speaking you produce it. Band 7 means a "good range of vocabulary used flexibly", so practise saying the same idea in a higher register.

Writing Task 2 — opinion essay (Band 7 target)

The story raises a real IELTS question about education and success. Plan and write a full essay of at least 250 words in about 40 minutes. The notes below show what a Band 7 response needs that a Band 6 one lacks.

Write about the following topic:

Some people believe that formal education and qualifications are essential for success in life. Others think that personal qualities, such as determination and the ability to spot opportunities, matter more.

Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Step 1 — Understand the task

This is a "discuss both views and give your opinion" essay. To reach Band 7 on Task Response you must do all three things well: present each view fairly and develop it with reasons and examples, and make your own position clear and consistent from introduction to conclusion. A one-sided answer, or an opinion that only appears at the end, caps you at Band 6.

Step 2 — A clear four-paragraph structure

ParagraphWhat goes in it
IntroductionParaphrase the topic, outline both views, and state your own position in one clear sentence.
Body 1The case for formal education (job prospects, professional careers, social mobility). One central idea, fully developed, with a specific example.
Body 2The case for personal qualities (determination, entrepreneurial flair, spotting opportunities — like Albert). One central idea, fully developed, with an example.
ConclusionRestate your position and synthesise both sides. Add no new ideas.

Step 3 — Higher-band linking language

FunctionBand 7 phrases
Introducing a viewIt is widely held that… / Proponents of this view argue that…
BalancingWhile there is some truth in this, … / This argument, however, overlooks…
Giving examplesA case in point is… / This is well illustrated by…
Stating your opinionIn my view, … / I would argue that… / My own position is that…
ConcludingOn balance, … / Weighing both arguments, I am convinced that…
What separates Band 7 from Band 6 (the four criteria):
  • Task Response (7): both views developed and a clear, consistent position throughout — not just mentioned in the conclusion.
  • Coherence & Cohesion (7): logical progression, paragraphing, and cohesive devices used flexibly — not mechanical "Firstly/Secondly".
  • Lexical Resource (7): a good range of less common vocabulary (use items from the Vocabulary tab) with only occasional slips.
  • Grammatical Range & Accuracy (7): a variety of complex structures and frequent error-free sentences. Include at least one conditional and some subordination from the Grammar tab.

Step 4 — Model introduction (Band 7)

It is widely assumed that formal education and qualifications are the decisive factor in a successful life, yet others maintain that personal characteristics such as determination and an eye for opportunity matter far more. While I acknowledge the clear advantages of education, I would argue that, without the personal drive to use it, qualifications alone rarely guarantee success.

Notice how this introduction paraphrases the prompt rather than copying it, signals both views, and states a clear, consistent opinion in advance. Now write the remaining three paragraphs.

Self-check before you finish:
  • Did I write 250+ words, cover both views, and develop each with a reason and an example?
  • Is my opinion clear in the introduction and conclusion, and the same in both?
  • Did I use less common vocabulary, a conditional, and at least two different subordinators?
  • Could I cut any sentence that just repeats an idea? (Band 7 is concise, not padded.)

Speaking — Parts 1, 2 and 3 (Band 7 target)

The story's themes — work, education, success, money — are extremely common in IELTS Speaking. Work through all three parts. Band 7 means speaking at length, using a range of structures and some less common vocabulary, with only occasional hesitation. Use the timer for Part 2.

Time remaining: 2:00
Part 1 · Familiar questions (give extended answers)

Answer in two or three sentences each — never one word. Add a reason or example to show range.

  • Do you work or are you a student?
  • What skills are most valued in your field?
  • How important are qualifications where you come from?
  • Would you ever consider starting your own business?
  • How do you usually respond to unexpected setbacks?
Part 2 · Long turn (cue card — speak for 2 minutes)

Describe a person you know who became successful without traditional qualifications.

You should say:

  • who this person is
  • what they do
  • how they achieved their success
  • and explain why you find them impressive.

Take one minute to plan (use the 1-minute timer), then speak for the full two minutes without long pauses. Aim to use past tenses, at least one conditional ("If she hadn't taken that risk…"), and a couple of items from the Vocabulary tab.

Part 3 · Two-way discussion (abstract)

These questions are more abstract. Develop each answer: state a view, justify it, then add an example or a contrasting idea.

  • Are formal qualifications becoming more or less important in today's job market? Why?
  • Why do you think some people succeed in business despite limited education?
  • To what extent should schools prioritise practical skills over academic knowledge?
  • Is society sometimes too quick to judge people by their qualifications?
  • How might definitions of "success" differ across generations or cultures?

Band 7 speaking boosters

  1. Extend and justify. Make a point, give a reason, then add an example or a "but". Short answers cap your Fluency score.
  2. Show grammatical range. Mix tenses and slip in a conditional in Part 3: "If I hadn't gone to university, I might well have…".
  3. Use less common vocabulary naturally — "entrepreneurial flair", "a blessing in disguise", "resilience" — but don't force it.
  4. Manage hesitation with natural fillers — "That's an interesting question…", "I suppose it depends on…" — rather than falling silent.
