IELTS SKILLS · B2 (Band 5.5–6.5) · 60 min

The Verger

A short story by W. Somerset Maugham, rebuilt as an IELTS workout — Reading, Writing Task 2 and Speaking

Reading passage

Read the passage once for general meaning. The paragraphs are labelled A–G so you can find information quickly in the Reading Tasks tab. This is an adapted version of Maugham's 1929 story, graded for B2 readers. Aim to read it in about 4 minutes before you start the questions.

AFor sixteen years, Albert Edward Foreman had been the verger of St Peter's, a fine old church in a wealthy part of London. A verger is a church official who looks after the building and helps to run the services. Albert carried out his duties with great dignity. He was tall and well dressed, and in his long black gown he looked so respectable that strangers sometimes mistook him for a senior member of the clergy. He took enormous pride in his work and had served three vicars faithfully.

BOne afternoon the new vicar, a young and energetic man, asked Albert to stay behind. With two church officials beside him, the vicar explained, in an embarrassed way, that he had recently made a surprising discovery: Albert could neither read nor write. The previous vicar had known this and had not minded, but the new vicar found it unacceptable. He gave Albert three months to learn to read and write. If he failed, he would lose his position.

CAlbert listened calmly. He had never learned as a boy, he explained, and he felt he was too old to begin now. He believed, quite sincerely, that he had done his job perfectly well for many years without these skills. Politely but firmly, he refused. The vicar, disappointed, told him that in that case he would have to leave. Albert accepted the decision without argument and agreed to resign as soon as a replacement could be found.

DWalking home that evening, Albert felt low and uncertain about his future. He had saved a little money, but not enough to live on for long. He wanted a cigarette to calm his nerves, but when he searched the long street he was walking down, he could not find a single shop that sold tobacco. It struck him as strange that such a busy street had no tobacconist. "I can't be the only person walking here who wants a smoke," he thought. "A small shop selling tobacco and sweets might do very well in a place like this."

EThe idea would not leave him. The very next day he found a small shop to rent on that street, and within a day he had taken it. He soon resigned from the church and opened his business. It did well from the start. After a year or two he opened a second shop in another street that also had no tobacconist, and then a third. Over ten years he built up a chain of ten successful shops and became a wealthy man, collecting the takings himself each week and depositing them at the bank.

FOne morning the manager of his bank asked to see him. He pointed out that Albert had a very large sum of money sitting in his account — more than thirty thousand pounds — and suggested that he invest it in safe government securities to earn a better return. Albert said he would prefer to leave the decisions to the bank, and that he would simply sign whatever papers were necessary. The manager assured him this would be easy: all he had to do was read the documents and sign them.

GAt this, Albert admitted, with a quiet smile, that he could not read or write at all, and never had been able to. The manager was astonished. "Good heavens," he cried, "just imagine what you might have become if you could read and write!" Albert thought for a moment and replied that he knew exactly what he would have been: he would still be the verger of St Peter's church.

Before the questions — quick orientation (this is a reading skill too):
  • Skim: in one sentence, what is the whole passage about?
  • Scan: which paragraph would you go to for the amount of money Albert had? For the reason he was asked to leave?
  • Predict: what kind of question is the last line (paragraph G) likely to be tested with — fact, or the writer's main point?

Reading tasks

Four IELTS question types, in order of difficulty. Do them with the passage open in the first tab. Try to finish all four in about 15 minutes — roughly the time you would have per passage in the real test.

Task 1 — True / False / Not Given

Do the statements agree with the information in the passage? Choose TRUE if the statement agrees, FALSE if it contradicts, and NOT GIVEN if there is no information about it. Remember: "Not Given" means the passage simply does not say — do not use your own knowledge.
1. Albert had worked at St Peter's for more than fifteen years.
2. The previous vicar had also wanted Albert to learn to read.
3. Albert argued angrily with the new vicar about the decision.
4. Albert's first shop made a profit very quickly.
5. Albert's wife encouraged him to open the first shop.

IELTS reading tips you just practised:
  • True vs Not Given is the hardest distinction. "Not Given" = the text neither confirms nor denies it. Q5 is Not Given because the wife is never mentioned.
  • For "which paragraph" tasks, scan for the idea, not matching words. The answers rarely repeat the exact wording of the question.
  • For sentence completion, the answer is always a word taken directly from the text — and watch the word limit.

literacy

Paraphrasing — the core IELTS skill

In both Reading and Writing, you score by saying the same idea in different words. Practise turning the passage's language into your own.

From the passageA higher-band paraphrase
He lost his position.He was dismissed from his post / He was made redundant.
The business did well.The venture flourished / proved highly successful.
He could not read or write.He was illiterate / He lacked basic literacy skills.
A setback became an opportunity.An apparent misfortune turned into a turning point.

Writing Task 2 — opinion essay

The story raises a real IELTS question about education and success. Plan and write a full essay of at least 250 words. In the real test you would have about 40 minutes.

Write about the following topic:

Some people believe that formal education and qualifications are essential for success in life. Others think that personal qualities, such as determination and the ability to spot opportunities, matter more.

Discuss both views and give your own opinion.

Step 1 — Understand the question

This is a "discuss both views and give your opinion" essay. You must do three things: explain view 1 fairly, explain view 2 fairly, and make your own position clear (and consistent). A common B2 mistake is to discuss only one side — the examiner is checking Task Response, so you must cover both.

Step 2 — A simple, safe structure

ParagraphWhat goes in it
IntroductionParaphrase the topic + state that you will discuss both views + give your opinion in one sentence.
Body 1The case for formal education and qualifications (better job prospects, professional careers, security). One main idea, explained, with an example.
Body 2The case for personal qualities (determination, spotting opportunities, self-made success — like the verger). One main idea, explained, with an example.
ConclusionRestate your opinion and summarise. Do not add new ideas.

Step 3 — Useful language

FunctionPhrases
Introducing view 1On the one hand, it is often argued that… / Supporters of… believe that…
Introducing view 2On the other hand, others contend that… / However, it can equally be argued that…
Giving examplesFor instance, … / A clear example of this is…
Giving your opinionIn my view, … / I would argue that… / Personally, I am inclined to believe…
ConcludingIn conclusion, … / To sum up, while both views have merit, …
What the examiner is marking (the four criteria):
  • Task Response — Did you answer the whole question (both views + your opinion) with developed ideas?
  • Coherence & Cohesion — Clear paragraphs, logical order, linking words used naturally (not over-used).
  • Lexical Resource — A range of accurate vocabulary; some topic words and collocations from the Vocabulary tab.
  • Grammatical Range & Accuracy — A mix of simple and complex sentences, mostly accurate. Try at least one conditional ("If he had been able to read, he would have…").

Step 4 — Model introduction (B2 level)

It is sometimes claimed that formal education and qualifications are the key to a successful life, while others insist that personal characteristics such as determination matter far more. This essay will consider both positions before explaining why I believe the two are not really in competition, but work best together.

Notice how the introduction paraphrases the question (it does not copy it), signals both views, and states a clear opinion. Now write the three remaining paragraphs yourself.

Self-check before you finish:
  • Did I write at least 250 words and cover both views?
  • Is my opinion clear in the introduction and the conclusion, and the same in both?
  • Did I use at least one complex sentence and one piece of topic vocabulary?

Speaking — Parts 1, 2 and 3

The story's themes — work, education, success, money — are extremely common in the IELTS Speaking test. Work through all three parts. Use the timer for Part 2: one minute to prepare, then speak for the full two minutes.

Time remaining: 2:00
Part 1 · Everyday questions (4–5 short answers)

Answer naturally, in two or three sentences each. Don't give one-word answers, but don't over-talk.

  • Do you work or are you a student?
  • What skills are most useful in your job or studies?
  • Is it important to have qualifications where you live? Why?
  • Would you like to start your own business one day?
  • How do you usually deal with unexpected problems?
Part 2 · Long turn (cue card — speak for 2 minutes)

Describe a person you know who became successful without a traditional education or qualifications.

You should say:

  • who this person is
  • what they do
  • how they became successful
  • and explain why you admire them.

Take one minute to make notes first (use the 1-minute timer), then speak for two full minutes without stopping. Try to use some past tenses and at least one phrase from the Vocabulary tab.

Part 3 · Discussion (deeper, two-way)

These questions are more abstract. Give your opinion, explain it, and add an example or a contrast.

  • Are formal qualifications becoming more or less important these days? Why?
  • Why do some people succeed in business without much education?
  • Should schools teach practical skills as well as academic subjects?
  • Do you think people are sometimes judged unfairly by their qualifications?
  • How might the idea of "success" differ between generations or cultures?

Speaking band-boosters

  1. Extend every answer. Make your point, give a reason, then add an example or a "but". Examiners reward developed answers.
  2. Use linking phrases to sound fluent: "That's a good question…", "It depends on…", "Take my own case, for example…".
  3. Show a range of grammar. Mix tenses and try one conditional in Part 3: "If I hadn't gone to university, I might have…".
  4. Don't fear hesitation — use natural fillers ("Let me think…", "I suppose…") rather than going silent.
