BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Thirteen Days

Thirteen days in October 1962, when a handful of exhausted men in one room decided whether the world would still be there on Monday. The tape recorder was running.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Thirteen Days — two weeks in which a photograph, a naval line drawn in the sea and one private conversation kept the world out of a nuclear war — discover how much of it is true and what the film quietly leaves out, and master the whole family of words English uses instead of if: unless, provided that, as long as, on condition that, otherwise, in case and supposing.
Before we begin — talk it through: When a decision is enormous and there is very little time, is it better to trust the experts or to trust your own judgement? Have you ever had to make a serious decision without enough information? And can you think of a moment in history when the world was, in your view, genuinely lucky?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. Neither driver would turn the wheel; each raced straight at the other, betting that the other would lose his nerve first. It was pure brinkmanship, and it very nearly killed them both.
brinkmanship (n) — the tactic of pushing a dangerous situation right to the edge of disaster in order to force the other side to give way first.
2. Before the rescue teams went in, a small aircraft made a reconnaissance flight over the valley, photographing every road and collapsed bridge so that the crews would know exactly what they were walking into.
reconnaissance (n) — the act of observing an area, usually from a distance or from the air, to gather information before acting. (Often shortened to recon.)
3. The letter was an ultimatum: pay everything you owe by Friday midday, or we change the locks and you are out on the street. There was no third option offered.
an ultimatum (n) — a final demand with a deadline, stating what will happen if it is refused. (Plural: ultimatums or ultimata.)
4. He said he had come to return a book, but that was only a pretext; what he actually wanted was to see whose coat was hanging in the hall.
a pretext (n) — a false reason given publicly to hide the real one; an excuse used as cover for what you truly intend.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. conciliatory is closest in meaning to: (a) threatening   (b) peace-making   (c) uninterested
(b) peace-making — conciliatory (adj) describes words or actions intended to calm anger and win the other side round. The verb is to conciliate.
6. inadvertent is closest in meaning to: (a) unintended   (b) deliberate   (c) repeated
(a) unintended — inadvertent (adj) means done by accident, without meaning to. The adverb inadvertently is very common: he inadvertently deleted the file.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. What began as a disagreement between two neighbours escalated week by week until the whole street had taken sides and the police were involved.
To escalate is: (a) to end suddenly; (b) to grow steadily more intense and more serious; (c) to explain in greater detail.
(b) — to escalate (v) — to become, or to make something become, progressively more serious or more violent. The noun is escalation.
8. She walked into the middle of the shouting, made one dry joke, and defused the entire situation in about ten seconds.
To defuse something is: (a) to make a tense or dangerous situation calm and safe; (b) to make it far worse; (c) to record it carefully.
(a) — to defuse (v) — literally, to remove the fuse from a bomb; figuratively, to take the danger and tension out of a situation.

D. Odd one out, and a collocation

9. Three of these describe someone quick to reach for military force. One does not. Which is the odd one out?
hawkish  ·  belligerent  ·  warlike  ·  cautious
cautious is the odd one out. hawkish (adj) — favouring aggressive or military solutions in a conflict. Its opposite in politics is dovish; the people themselves are called hawks and doves.
10. Which word completes the phrase naturally?
The airline keeps a contingency ______ ready for every kind of emergency, from a volcano to a strike: (a) plan   (b) apology   (c) holiday
(a) plan — a contingency (n) is a possible future event that you cannot be sure about but must prepare for. A contingency plan is what you will do if it happens.

The film, in three parts

Roger Donaldson's Thirteen Days (2000), with Bruce Greenwood as President Kennedy, Steven Culp as his brother Robert, and Kevin Costner as White House aide Kenny O'Donnell. Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it. History has already spoiled the ending anyway.)

Part 1 — Fourteen photographs

October 1962. A U-2 spy plane returns from a reconnaissance flight over Cuba, and the photographs it brings back change everything: the Soviet Union is secretly building launch sites for nuclear missiles ninety miles from the coast of Florida. Once they are ready, most of the eastern United States is within a few minutes' flight. Nobody in Washington knew. President Kennedy is shown the pictures over breakfast.

He assembles a small group of advisers — soon known as the ExComm — and shuts them in a room to find an answer before the missiles become operational. The Joint Chiefs of Staff arrive with a clear recommendation: bomb the sites, then invade the island. The generals are certain, forceful and impatient. Kennedy is not. He can see the next move on the board: if American bombs fall on Soviet troops, the Soviets will take West Berlin, and then nobody stops anything.

Days pass in cigarette smoke and argument. Every option is bad. Do nothing and the missiles go live. Strike and you may start the war you are trying to prevent. Slowly a third idea surfaces — a ring of warships around Cuba, turning back any vessel carrying military cargo. It is not an attack. It is not surrender. It is a way of buying time.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What do the U-2 photographs show, and why does that matter so much?

2. What do the Joint Chiefs want to do, and why does Kennedy hesitate?

1. Soviet nuclear missile sites under construction in Cuba, about ninety miles from Florida — close enough to strike most American cities with almost no warning.

2. They want air strikes followed by an invasion. Kennedy fears that killing Soviet troops in Cuba would push the Soviets to seize West Berlin, and that the whole thing would escalate into nuclear war.

Discuss:
  • The generals in the room have spent their lives preparing for exactly this. Kennedy overrules them anyway. When should a leader trust the specialists — and when is their expertise precisely the problem?

Part 2 — A line drawn in the sea

Kennedy goes on television and tells the country what is happening. He announces a naval "quarantine" of Cuba — carefully not the word blockade, because a blockade is legally an act of war. Soviet ships are already steaming towards the line. Nobody knows whether they will stop, and everybody understands that a shot fired at sea will not stay at sea.

At the United Nations, the American ambassador Adlai Stevenson confronts his Soviet counterpart in front of the world's cameras, demands a straight answer about the missiles, and says he is prepared to wait "until hell freezes over". Then he has the photographs wheeled in on easels. It is a small victory in a week with very few.

Meanwhile the pressure never stops. Low-flying aircraft are sent over Cuba for closer pictures and come back with holes in them. In one of the film's most-repeated scenes, O'Donnell telephones a pilot before he flies and tells him that if he reports being fired on, the rules will oblige the United States to retaliate — so he must not report it. The ships slow, and some turn back. The relief lasts almost no time at all: the work on the missile sites is going faster, not slower.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is the word "quarantine" used instead of "blockade"?

2. What happens to the aircraft flying low over Cuba, and what does O'Donnell tell the pilots?

1. Because a blockade counts in international law as an act of war; "quarantine" allows the same action without formally declaring one.

2. They are shot at and come back damaged. In the film O'Donnell tells a pilot not to report being fired on, because an official report would trigger automatic retaliation.

Discuss:
  • To keep the peace, someone has to conceal the truth from the chain of command. Is that a brave act or a dangerous one? Where is the line between wise discretion and a lie that nobody voted for?

Part 3 — The Saturday everything nearly broke

Two messages arrive from Moscow. The first is long, personal and almost pleading: remove the missiles in exchange for a promise never to invade Cuba. The second, arriving the next day and sounding as if it were written by a committee, is colder and demands more — the withdrawal of American missiles from Turkey. Which letter is the real one? Nobody in the room knows.

Then, on the same Saturday, a U-2 is shot down over Cuba and its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson, is killed. Standing orders say the Americans must now strike back at the missile site that fired. Kennedy refuses. He will not let a single, possibly unauthorised, act of shooting decide the fate of the world; a man is dead and the President will not answer with the invasion everyone around him is now demanding.

Instead the brothers gamble. They answer the friendlier first letter as if the second had never arrived, and Robert Kennedy is sent to meet the Soviet ambassador privately. Publicly: the missiles leave Cuba, and America promises not to invade. Privately, and on the strict understanding that it can never be spoken of, the American missiles in Turkey will go too. On the Sunday morning, Moscow radio announces that the missile sites will be dismantled. The men in the room have been awake for thirteen days, and they have not lost.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is confusing about the two messages from Moscow, and how do the Kennedys deal with it?

2. What happens to Major Anderson, and why is Kennedy's response so significant?

1. The first is warm and offers a simple trade; the second is harsher and demands the removal of American missiles from Turkey. They choose to reply to the first and ignore the second.

2. His U-2 is shot down over Cuba and he is killed. Standing orders required retaliation; Kennedy refuses to strike back, and so refuses to let one incident start the war.

Discuss:
  • A pilot is killed and the President does nothing about it — deliberately. Was that a betrayal of the man who died, or the only decision that honoured him?

Grammar — unless, provided that, as long as

Thirteen days of conditions, terms, warnings and precautions. English has a whole family of words for this, and each one does something if cannot do: name the single exception, set the terms of a bargain, threaten a consequence, or take a precaution against something that may never happen.

The exception — unless:
unless means except if. It names the one thing that would change the outcome: "The blockade stays in place unless the missile sites are dismantled."
Careful — unless is not always the same as if… not. It works in real conditions about the future, but not when you are imagining an unreal or hypothetical situation: "If the spy plane hadn't photographed the sites, nobody would have known" — you cannot say unless here. Nor with a would-reaction: "The generals would have been furious if he had not acted." Use unless for the real, live exception, not for the imagined past.
The terms of a bargain — provided (that) / providing (that) / as long as / on condition that:
All four mean only if this one condition is met, and all four are stronger and more deliberate than plain if: "The ships may pass provided that they carry no weapons." "We will not invade the island as long as the missiles are gone." "He agreed on condition that the arrangement was never made public."
• as long as is the most conversational; provided that and providing that are neutral-to-formal; on condition that is the most formal and belongs to agreements and terms.
• as long as also has a time meaning ("stay as long as you like") — the context tells you which.
The threat — otherwise; the precaution — in case; the hypothesis — supposing:
• otherwise = if not, then…. It is not a conjunction: it looks back at the sentence before. "The sites must be dismantled; otherwise we act." You cannot say otherwise the sites are dismantled.
• in case = a precaution, not a condition. You act first, because something might happen later: "Keep the aircraft fuelled in case the talks collapse" — you fuel them now, whether or not the talks ever collapse. Compare "Keep the aircraft fuelled if the talks collapse" — that means only fuel them afterwards. Different actions entirely.
• supposing (that) / suppose = imagine that…. It opens a hypothesis, very often as a question: "Supposing he refuses — what then?"
Punctuation and form:
• Conditional clause first → comma: "Provided that the ships turn back, nobody is boarded."
• Conditional clause second → normally no comma: "Nobody is boarded provided that the ships turn back."
• otherwise follows a semicolon or a full stop, and usually takes a comma after it: "Dismantle the sites; otherwise, we act."
• As with if, use a present tense for future meaning after all of these: unless he agrees (not unless he will agree).
• that is optional in provided (that), providing (that) and supposing (that).

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

Unusually for a historical drama, the room in Thirteen Days is largely right. What is missing is everything that happened outside it — including the two or three moments when the crisis very nearly ended in the other direction, and nobody in that room ever knew.

🎙️ The film is built on real recordings

Kennedy had a secret taping system in the Cabinet Room, and the ExComm debates of October 1962 were recorded. The film draws on The Kennedy Tapes (1997), edited by the historians Ernest R. May and Philip D. Zelikow — not, despite the shared title, on Robert Kennedy's 1969 memoir. That is why the arguments sound so unglamorous and so real: much of what those men say, they actually said. The basic shape is also accurate. The U-2 photographs were taken on 14 October 1962; Kennedy announced the naval "quarantine" — deliberately not "blockade", since a blockade is legally an act of war — on 22 October; Khrushchev announced the withdrawal on 28 October.

The man in the film who was barely there

Kenneth P. O'Donnell was real. He was Robert Kennedy's Harvard room-mate and football team-mate, and he served as special assistant and appointments secretary to the President — a genuinely close friend who ran the President's diary. He was not the third Kennedy brother, and he was not the moral centre of the missile crisis.

The people who were in that room said so bluntly. Robert McNamara, Kennedy's defence secretary, said that O'Donnell had no role whatsoever in the missile crisis. Arthur Schlesinger Jr. said the same. Ted Sorensen, whose actual job much of the film's O'Donnell material resembles, objected too. Ernest May — whose book the film is based on — explained the choice frankly: O'Donnell was inserted as a kind of inside "everyman", so that the audience would have someone to watch the crisis with. Scenes such as O'Donnell stiffening Adlai Stevenson's spine before the United Nations, or personally instructing a pilot to lie to his superiors, are inventions.

It is worth being precise about why this matters. The film is honest about the decisions and dishonest about who made them — and the effect is to move the credit from cabinet officers, diplomats and the President himself onto a likeable White House staffer. It is a very modern kind of distortion.

What the film cannot show: the submarine

On 27 October 1962 — the Saturday the film builds to — American destroyers were forcing a Soviet submarine, B-59, to the surface near the quarantine line by dropping practice depth charges as signals. The crew had been submerged for a long time in appalling heat, the air was foul, and they had been out of radio contact with Moscow. They did not know whether the war had already started. And unknown to the Americans, B-59 carried a nuclear-armed torpedo.

According to the accounts of surviving crew, the captain, Valentin Savitsky, ordered that torpedo prepared. On most such submarines two officers had to agree; on B-59 a third signature was needed, because the brigade's chief of staff, Vasili Arkhipov, happened to be aboard. Arkhipov refused, argued that the explosions were signals rather than an attack, and talked the captain down. The order was never passed to the torpedo officer. In 2017 the first Future of Life Award was given in his name, and collected in London by his daughter and grandson.

Be careful here, because the honest version is better than the legend. The evidence rests largely on oral testimony from crew members, first aired publicly at a conference in 2002; Arkhipov himself, speaking in 1997, said almost nothing about his own part, and the National Security Archive notes that surviving submariners' accounts differ. What is beyond dispute is that a nuclear weapon was aboard, that the crew were blind and exhausted, and that the decision sat with three frightened men in a hot steel tube — while the President of the United States, on screen and in life, had no idea the submarine existed.

Turkey, Anderson, and the warheads nobody counted

The film does show the secret half of the bargain, and it was real: American Jupiter missiles — fifteen in Turkey, thirty in Italy — were withdrawn in the months that followed, and Robert Kennedy's private assurance to Ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin on 27 October was the price of the settlement. What the film cannot convey is how long that stayed buried. Only a handful of American officials knew; the trade was denied for decades and was not properly acknowledged until Dobrynin's account emerged in 1989. For a generation, the crisis was taught as a story of pure American nerve. It was partly a story of a quiet swap.

The pilot's death is real too. Major Rudolf Anderson Jr. was shot down over Banes, Cuba, on the morning of 27 October by a Soviet SA-2 surface-to-air missile — the only combat death of the entire crisis. He became the first person ever awarded the Air Force Cross. And here the truth is even more alarming than the film: the shoot-down was not ordered by Moscow. Two Soviet deputy commanders in Cuba, unable to reach their own commander or the capital in the minutes available, decided to take the responsibility themselves. The single act that came closest to triggering an American invasion was a local decision made by men under time pressure.

One last thing, which nobody on either side of the screen knew in 1962: there were already close to a hundred short-range tactical nuclear weapons on Cuba, undetected by American reconnaissance. Had the invasion the generals wanted gone ahead, the landing force would have met nuclear weapons on the beaches. Robert McNamara only learned this at a conference in Havana in 1992, thirty years later, and reportedly had to steady himself against a table.

Take these into the discussion:
  • The film is accurate about the decisions and inaccurate about who made them. Which kind of error matters more in a film that says "this really happened"?
  • Kennedy is remembered for holding his nerve. But an unauthorised shoot-down, an invisible submarine and a hundred uncounted warheads suggest a large amount of luck. How much of that fortnight was judgement, and how much was chance?
  • Arkhipov's story is famous, and the evidence for it is oral testimony that does not entirely agree with itself. Should we tell a story like that at all — and if so, how?
  • If you were making this film, would you have invented a character to watch the crisis through? What would you have gained, and what would you have owed the audience?

Task B · The room (4 minutes)

Every military expert in that room wanted to strike first, and they had reasons. Kennedy overruled them and chose to wait. Build an argument for one side and then, out loud, argue the other: when a threat is real and the clock is running, is it wiser to act early while you still control the timing, or to accept greater risk in order to leave the other side a way out? Bring in the fact that a nuclear-armed submarine and a hundred hidden warheads were sitting there unseen — does that strengthen your case or destroy it?

Task C · What history remembers (4 minutes)

A film gave one man the credit and history gave another none at all. Talk about how we decide who gets remembered for preventing a disaster — and whether the people who quietly stop bad things ever get their due. Make your case.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  1. The crisis was survived partly by good judgement and partly by luck. Which of the two frightens you more, and what would you want changed today?
