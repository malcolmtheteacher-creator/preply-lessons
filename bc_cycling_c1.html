The Bicycle: Freedom on Two Wheels

C1 Level · Speaking & Discussion Focus

Where do you stand?

For something so simple — two wheels, a frame, a chain — the bicycle stirs surprisingly strong feelings. Some people see freedom; others see a nuisance in the traffic. Let us start with your own experience.

Warm up — talk it through:

When did you last ride a bicycle? What do you remember about how it felt?

Is a bicycle mainly a toy, a sport, a means of transport, or something more? Has that changed across your life?

Would you describe the place you live as friendly to cyclists, or hostile to them? What makes you say so?

A claim to react to

“The bicycle is the most efficient machine ever invented for turning human effort into distance.” Do you find that surprising? Convincing?

A note on this
It is a widely cited claim: a person on a bicycle travels far further on a given amount of energy than a person walking, and more efficiently than almost any animal or machine over the same distance. The point of the tab is not the number — it is what such efficiency has meant for ordinary people.

More Than a Machine

Read the passage, then work through the questions. The aim is not to memorise facts but to have something to argue about.

When the “safety bicycle” arrived in the 1880s — two equal wheels, a chain drive, and soon after, air-filled tyres — it did something the earlier, dangerous high-wheelers never could: it put mobility within reach of almost anyone. For the first time, an ordinary person could travel several towns away, alone, cheaply, and without asking anyone's permission.

That last point mattered enormously. In the 1890s the bicycle became caught up in the movement for women's rights. It offered independent movement and, not coincidentally, more practical clothing. The American campaigner Susan B. Anthony went so far as to say cycling had done “more to emancipate women” than anything else — an overstatement, perhaps, but a revealing one.

A century later the bicycle had a stranger reputation. In elite sport it became the emblem of the doping era: the Tour de France, that most gruelling of races, was engulfed in scandal, culminating in Lance Armstrong being stripped of seven titles in 2012. The same machine could stand for both honest freedom and industrial-scale cheating.

Today the argument has moved to the city. Some places — the Netherlands is the famous example — rebuilt their streets around the bicycle after public protest in the 1970s, and now barely think about it. Others remain shaped around the car, with cyclists left at the mercy of the traffic. And into this old debate rolls a new character: the electric bike, which lets almost anyone climb a hill without breaking sweat — and which raises a quietly awkward question. If the effort is optional, is it still cycling?

1. Why did the safety bicycle matter so much more than the machines before it?

Show Answer
It was safe and affordable enough for ordinary people, so it put independent, permission-free travel within almost everyone's reach — not just the wealthy or the athletic.

2. What is the “awkward question” the electric bike raises?

Show Answer
If a motor removes much of the physical effort, it is unclear whether the activity still counts as “cycling” in the traditional sense — a question about identity and effort, not just technology.

Now argue it out:

The passage says the same machine can stand for “honest freedom and industrial-scale cheating.” Can you think of another object or technology with a similarly split reputation?

Was Susan B. Anthony exaggerating, or do you think a simple machine really can change how a whole group of people live?

Should a city inconvenience drivers in order to make room for cyclists? Who should decide?

Saying It Well

At C1, a good opinion is rarely a flat “I agree” or “I disagree.” You concede a point, then push back; you put the emphasis exactly where you want it. Here are three tools for doing that.

1. Concede, then counter

Acknowledge the other side first — it makes your own point land harder.

  • Admittedly, cycling in heavy rain is miserable, but that is a handful of days a year.
  • Granted, e-bikes cost more up front; nonetheless, they pay for themselves within a year.
  • It is true that some cyclists ignore the rules, yet the same could be said of drivers.

Your turn: complete the concession

“Admittedly, learning to ride in city traffic can be frightening at first, ______________________.”

Show a model
“… but it soon becomes second nature, and the confidence carries over into the rest of your life.” (Any counter that begins with but / yet / nonetheless / all the same works.)

2. Cleft sentences — point the spotlight

Start with What… or The thing that… to spotlight the idea that matters most.

  • What really strikes me about cycling is how quickly it becomes a habit.
  • The thing that puts people off is not the effort — it is the traffic.
  • What we would lose in a car-free city is harder to name than what we would gain.

Rewrite for emphasis

Plain: “I love the silence of cycling early in the morning.” → Emphasise the silence.

Show Answer
“What I love about cycling early in the morning is the silence.”

3. Inversion — a formal, emphatic opening

Begin with a negative or limiting phrase and invert the verb. It sounds confident and a little literary.

  • Not only is it cheap, but it is also the fastest way across a crowded city.
  • Rarely do we question how much space we hand over to cars.
  • Only when a city removes the traffic do people discover they wanted the streets back.

Invert it

Plain: “We rarely think about how little exercise a normal day contains.”

Show Answer
“Rarely do we think about how little exercise a normal day contains.” (Note the word order: Rarely do we think, not Rarely we think.)

Over to You

Three speaking tasks. Try to weave in the key words and at least one of the three language tools from the last tab — a concession, a cleft sentence, or an inversion.

Time remaining: 10:00

Task 1 — Take a side (then switch)

Motion: “City centres should be closed to private cars.”

Speak for two minutes in favour. Then, without stopping to prepare, speak for two minutes against your own argument. Concede at least one point to the other side each time.

Task 2 — A journey

Describe a bicycle journey — real or imagined — from the moment you set off to the moment you arrive. Make me feel it: the effort, the freedom, the traffic, the weather. Use at least four of the key words.

Task 3 — A thought experiment

Imagine that, from tomorrow, everyone in your city cycled everywhere. What would change for the better? And — be honest — what would we quietly lose?
