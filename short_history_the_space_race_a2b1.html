The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — The first satellite

After the Second World War, two big countries did not trust each other: the United States (the USA) and the Soviet Union (the USSR). They did not fight directly. But they wanted to show the world who was stronger and better. People called this time the Cold War.

Both countries built big rockets. A rocket can carry a bomb — but it can also carry a machine into space. In 1957, the USSR was first. They put a small metal ball into space. Its name was Sputnik. It went around the Earth and made a "beep, beep" sound on the radio. It was the first satellite in the world.

People in the USA were very surprised and worried. They thought they were going to win any science contest easily. But now the USSR was in front! One month later, the USSR sent a dog named Laika into space. She was the first animal to go around the Earth. Sadly, the machine could not bring her home, and she did not come back.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Sputnik, and which country made it?

2. Why were people in the USA worried?

1. Sputnik was the first satellite. It was a small metal ball that went around the Earth. The USSR (the Soviet Union) made it.

2. They thought they were going to win easily, but the USSR was first. They were afraid the USSR was stronger.

Talk about it:
  • A small ball in space made a big country very worried. Why can a small thing make people feel afraid?

Part 2 — The first person in space

The USSR was first again. On 12 April 1961, a young pilot named Yuri Gagarin went into a small space capsule on top of a rocket. He said one word: "Let's go!" Then he flew up into space. He went around the Earth one time. His flight was only 108 minutes. He was the first person in space.

Gagarin came home a hero. Everyone knew his name. The USSR was very happy and proud. They showed the whole world: "Look what we can do!" The USA was not happy. Their first astronaut, Alan Shepard, flew three weeks later, but his flight was shorter.

The USA wanted a big new plan. In 1961, the President, John F. Kennedy, made a promise. He said: "We are going to send a man to the Moon, and bring him home, before 1970." It was a very big plan. At that time, the USA had only fifteen minutes of space flight! But now they had a clear goal.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was Yuri Gagarin, and what did he do?

2. What big promise did President Kennedy make?

1. Yuri Gagarin was a pilot from the USSR. In 1961 he was the first person in space. He went around the Earth one time.

2. Kennedy said the USA was going to send a man to the Moon and bring him home before 1970.

Talk about it:
  • Kennedy made a very big promise about the Moon. Is it good for a leader to promise something very difficult? Why?

Part 3 — To the Moon

The USA started a very big project. About 400,000 people worked on it. It was not easy, and it was not always safe. In 1967, there was a fire in a space capsule on the ground, and three astronauts died. But the work went on.

In 1968, three American astronauts flew around the Moon and came home. Then, in July 1969, it happened: two men landed on the Moon. Hundreds of millions of people watched on TV. A man walked on the Moon for the first time. The USA kept Kennedy's promise. The USSR's big Moon rocket did not work well, so they stopped trying to land a person on the Moon.

The Space Race was a contest about power, not only about science. But it gave the world many good things. Today we have satellites for our phones, for TV and for the weather. We also got the first photos of the whole Earth from space. The race cost a lot of money, and some people died. But it changed our world — and people still ask: should we go to space to win, or to explore together?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened in July 1969?

2. Name one good thing the Space Race gave the world.

1. Two American men landed on the Moon. A man walked on the Moon for the first time. The USA kept Kennedy's promise.

2. Any one: satellites for phones, TV or the weather; the first photos of the whole Earth; new science and technology.

Talk about it:
  • The Space Race cost a lot of money. Was it a good idea to spend so much to reach the Moon? Why, or why not?

Grammar: past simple / be going to

We use two easy things to tell this story: the past simple for what happened, and be going to for a plan someone had.

Past simple = a finished action in the past. Regular verbs add -ed: "The rocket landed." Many verbs are irregular: go → went, be → was/were, make → made, fly → flew. "Gagarin went into space in 1961."
be going to + verb = a plan or a thing we are sure about: "We are going to send a man to the Moon." In the past, we say was/were going to: "The USA thought they were going to win."
Feel the difference:
• past simple = it happened. "Sputnik flew around the Earth."
• be going to = it was the plan. "Kennedy said the USA was going to land on the Moon."

1. Past simple — write the verb

Put the verb in brackets into the past simple.

1. In 1957, the USSR (put) the first satellite into space.

2. Yuri Gagarin (go) into space in 1961.

3. In 1969, two men (land) on the Moon.

1. put (irregular: put → put)

2. went (irregular: go → went)

3. landed (regular: land → landed)

2. be going to — plans

Complete each sentence with going to and the verb in brackets.

1. Kennedy said: "We are (send) a man to the Moon."

2. The USA thought they were (win) the race easily.

3. Next year, the astronauts are (fly) around the Moon.

1. going to send (going to + base verb)

2. going to win (were going to + base verb)

3. going to fly (going to + base verb)

3. Tell a short part of the story

Fill each gap with the past simple, or with going to (in the correct form). Read it aloud when you finish.

In 1957, the USSR (send) the first satellite into space. The USA thought they were (win) easily, but they were wrong. Then, in 1961, Gagarin (become) the first person in space. Kennedy said the USA was (go) to the Moon.

sent (past simple) · going to win (a plan) · became (past simple) · going to go (a plan)

Use the past simple for what happened, and going to for a plan.
