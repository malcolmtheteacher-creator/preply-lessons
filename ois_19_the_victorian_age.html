A Bridge into the Victorian Age

From Arthur's time (c. 500) through the Wars of the Roses (1485) to the Great Fire (1666) and now to 1837 — Britain has changed from a warrior kingdom to a Christian nation to an empire of trade and industry. Each age shaped the next. Now a young woman of eighteen takes the throne. She will reign for sixty-three years, longer than anyone had reigned before or since.

II. Victoria's Britain

H.E. Marshall was a Victorian — she lived in the age she describes. Her account of Queen Victoria carries the warmth and conviction of a writer telling the story of her own time. Here is what she shows us:

The Girl Queen

Victoria was born in 1819. Her uncle, King George IV, died in 1830. Her other uncle, King William IV, reigned briefly. When he died in 1837, Victoria was eighteen years old and next in line to the throne.

When William IV died, Victoria became Queen. She was a girl of eighteen, very small and delicate-looking, but with a will of iron and a wise head on her shoulders. She had been carefully educated and was well prepared to be Queen. For her, as for most girls of her time, the greatest happiness was to find a man to love, marry, and obey. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter CIV (paraphrased from historical sources)

Victoria took the throne alone. She was the first of her line in generations to rule without a regent. For the first years, she governed with counsel from her ministers, but with a clear mind and steady hand.

Albert

In 1840, Victoria married her cousin Prince Albert of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha. By all accounts, it was a love match.

The young Queen's heart was as noble and true as her head was wise. She gave her whole affection to her husband, and he proved worthy of it. Albert was a man of great ability and learning. Together, they set themselves to improve the condition of the people. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter CIV (paraphrased from historical sources)

Albert became Prince Consort. He was not just a royal figure — he was deeply involved in science, art, technology, and progress. He believed, as Victoria did, that the modern age could be one of enlightenment and improvement.

The Great Exhibition of 1851

Albert's greatest vision was realized in 1851. He proposed a great exhibition to celebrate the achievements of industry and art from all nations. A building was erected in Hyde Park — the Crystal Palace, a vast structure of iron and glass that had never been seen before.

The Crystal Palace was a wonderful and beautiful building. It was made entirely of glass and iron, and when the sun shone through it, it glowed like a jewel. From all over the world people sent their most marvellous inventions and their finest works of art. It was a triumph of the age in which Queen Victoria reigned — an age of steam and steel and science, an age that was improving the world. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter CV (paraphrased from historical sources)

Over six million people visited the Great Exhibition. They marvelled at locomotives, machinery, textiles, sculpture, paintings, jewels from India, and goods from every corner of the Empire. Victoria opened it herself, and the profits went toward building museums and schools in South Kensington.

The historian's note: The Great Exhibition was indeed a triumph of Victorian optimism. But it also reveals the darker side of the age: the machinery on display was powered by coal and human labour; the goods from the Empire were often extracted through colonialism; and back in the factories and mines of Britain, children as young as five worked twelve-hour days. Marshall's Britain is a Britain of marvels — but marvels built partly on suffering.

The Crimean War & Florence Nightingale

Not all of Victoria's reign was peaceful. In 1853, Britain went to war with Russia over control of territories in the Crimea, a peninsula in the Black Sea. The war lasted three years and was terrible — not so much because of battle, but because of disease.

Soldiers died from cholera, dysentery, and infections far more often than from enemy fire. The hospitals were filthy. Wounded men lay on straw with their wounds unwashed. There were no nurses — nursing was not considered a respectable profession for women.

Then a remarkable woman changed everything. Florence Nightingale, born into a wealthy family, defied her parents' wishes and trained as a nurse. In 1854, she went to the Crimea with a group of nurses.

When Florence Nightingale and her nurses arrived at the hospital in Scutari, they found it in a dreadful state. The wards were filthy, the men lay crowded together on foul bedding, and no one seemed to care for them. Florence Nightingale set to work. She scrubbed and cleaned. She made the nurses tend to the men with kindness and care. She saw that the wounds were dressed properly and that the men were fed good food. Very soon the men began to get better, and fewer of them died. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter CV (paraphrased from historical sources)

Nightingale became a legend. Soldiers called her "the Lady with the Lamp" because she would walk through the hospital wards at night, carrying a lamp to check on the sick.

Florence Nightingale showed that a woman could do important work, brave work, work that saved lives. She worked night and day, and the soldiers loved her. When she came home to England, the whole nation honoured her. She had proved that women had minds and courage and could do great things. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter CV (paraphrased from historical sources)

Nightingale returned to England as a hero. She used her fame to reform nursing and hospital sanitation throughout the country. She established the first training school for nurses, laying the foundation for nursing as a profession.

The historian's note: Florence Nightingale's own statistics showed that more soldiers were dying from unsanitary conditions than from battle wounds — and that by cleaning and improving hygiene, she saved thousands of lives. She was both a pioneer in nursing and in the early use of statistics to argue for change. She was also a complex figure: a trailblazer for women, but also someone who held conventional Victorian views on many other subjects. Like Marshall, Nightingale was a woman of her age — remarkable within it, constrained by it.

III. Empire & The Victorian Legacy

By the end of Victoria's reign, Britain was unlike any nation in history. Marshall describes an age of unprecedented power and progress. But we must also see what lay beneath the glitter.

The Empire on Which the Sun Never Sets

Under Victoria, the British Empire reached its greatest extent. By 1901, Britain controlled roughly one quarter of all land on Earth. India was the jewel — "the brightest jewel in the crown," as it was called. But there was also Canada, Australia, vast territories in Africa, Hong Kong, and dozens of islands and trading posts across every ocean.

Queen Victoria reigned over more people than any ruler in history. She was Empress of India as well as Queen of England. Wherever the sun rose and set, British flags flew. It was said that the sun never set on the British Empire, because somewhere in that vast realm, the sun was always shining. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter CVIII

This was Victoria's pride, and Marshall's pride too. But what did it mean? It meant trade routes controlled by Britain. It meant raw materials flowing in and British goods flowing out. It meant British rule and British culture imposed on millions of people who had their own languages, religions, and ways of life.

Victorian Achievement

Within Britain, the Victorian age was an age of extraordinary transformation:

Railways

The first public railway opened in 1825. By Victoria's time, railways crisscrossed Britain, connecting every town and city. People and goods could travel faster and farther than ever before.

Engineering

Isambard Kingdom Brunel designed bridges, tunnels, and ships. Thomas Telford built roads and canals. These engineers transformed the landscape.

Industry

Factories produced textiles, steel, pottery, and machinery. Britain became the "workshop of the world." The cities grew enormous. Manchester, Birmingham, and Leeds became industrial centers unimaginable fifty years before.

Sewers & Public Health

As cities grew, disease was rampant. Dr. John Snow traced cholera to contaminated water. Joseph Bazalgette designed London's sewer system — an invisible wonder that saved countless lives.

Education

Education became more widespread. Libraries and museums opened. Reading was a Victorian passion. Newspapers, magazines, and books poured from the presses.

Victorian Darkness

But Marshall, though a Victorian, was not blind to the cost:

Child Labour

Children worked in factories and mines for twelve hours a day. Many never saw daylight. Reform came slowly.

Poverty

Cities were overcrowded. Slums were dark, filthy, and disease-ridden. While mansions rose in the best neighbourhoods, thousands of families lived in a single room.

Workhouses

For the destitute poor, there were "workhouses" — grim institutions where the hungry and homeless were given work in exchange for a meagre meal. The poet and critic Charles Dickens painted them as places of cruelty.

Colonialism

The Empire brought wealth to Britain, but at tremendous cost to colonized peoples. Land was taken. Cultures were suppressed. Resources were extracted.

The historian's note: Marshall wrote in 1905, looking back on the Victorian age with admiration. But she was writing at a moment when even Victorians themselves were beginning to question their world. The Great War would come in 1914, destroying the idea of endless progress. Today, we see the Victorian age differently than Marshall did — we honour its achievements in medicine, engineering, and culture, but we cannot ignore its brutality toward the poor and toward colonized peoples. Marshall's book reflects her age. Part of reading history is learning to see both the light and shadow in the eras we study.

Victoria Dies, an Age Ends

Victoria reigned for 63 years and 216 days — longer than any English monarch before or since. When she died on January 22, 1901, it seemed to many that an entire world was ending. An era had closed.

Her son Edward became king. The twentieth century had begun. Britain would face new challenges, new rivals, new wars. The certainties of the Victorian age would not survive the coming century.

Place These Five Images in Room 19

Room 19 holds the Victorian Age — all sixty-three years of Victoria's reign compressed into five vivid, bizarre, sensory images:

The 5 Images for Room 19

1. Young Victoria in coronation robes — a small girl with iron in her eyes, wearing a crown too large for her head, placed at the door of Room 19. She is eighteen.

2. The Crystal Palace gleaming with light — a vast structure of glass and iron, glowing from within, filled with six million tiny figures of wonder-struck Victorians walking through it. Place it in the centre of the room.

3. Florence Nightingale holding her lamp — a woman in a long grey dress, walking through a hospital ward, a small lamp glowing in her hand, soldiers reaching toward her from sickbeds. Place her on one wall.

4. A world map painted entirely red — the British Empire sprawling across continents. Place it on another wall. Make the red vivid, dripping, overwhelming.

5. Victoria old and mourning in black — an aged woman shrouded in black crêpe, her face sad, placed at the far end of Room 19. It is 1901. An age is dying.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on. There are no right answers — only your honest thinking.

Was Victoria great?

She reigned longer than any English monarch. The age was named for her. Was she genuinely great, or did her era make her great? What makes a ruler truly great?

The Crystal Palace — what did it mean?

Was it a symbol of genuine progress, or of British arrogance? What does a nation's great exhibition reveal about how it sees itself?

Florence Nightingale: breaking rules

Nightingale defied her parents, trained as a nurse (considered disreputable), and went to war. What do you think gave her the courage? And what does her story tell us about breaking rules for a cause?

The sun never sets...

Marshall celebrates the British Empire with real pride. But Britain's empire meant control, extraction, and the suppression of other peoples' cultures. How do we reckon with a glorious age that was built partly on injustice?

Progress — but for whom?

The Victorians believed in progress: railways, machines, sewers, schools. But inside the factories, children worked. In the slums, families starved. Was the Victorian age progressive? For whom?

The end of an era

Victoria died in 1901. Many people felt it was the end of an era. What makes an age feel like it's ending? And what comes next?
