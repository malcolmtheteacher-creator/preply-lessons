2. Encounter

Philosophers, sociologists, and theologians have different answers to "why religion exists." Here are three of the most influential.

The Three Explanations

👥

1. Sociological: Emile Durkheim (1858–1917)

The Theory: Religion exists because humans are social creatures. We need community. Religion creates and holds together social groups through shared rituals, shared moral codes, and a sense of belonging.

Key Idea: When a community gathers for a ritual — a church service, a mosque prayer, a Buddhist meditation group — something happens. Individuals stop being alone. They become part of a collective identity. The ritual makes that community real and strengthens bonds. The sacred object or moment isn't important because it's literally divine; it's important because it represents the group to itself.

Why it matters: Religion doesn't exist primarily because God is real (Durkheim was a sociologist, not a theologian). It exists because humans evolved to live in groups, and groups need glue. That glue is ritual, symbol, and shared meaning.

Durkheim studied Aboriginal Australian religions and concluded that the totem (a sacred symbol) was really just the tribe recognising itself as sacred.

🧠

2. Psychological: Sigmund Freud (1856–1939)

The Theory: Religion exists because humans are psychologically vulnerable. We experience fear, loneliness, mortality. Religion is wish-fulfilment — it helps us cope with anxiety by offering supernatural comfort.

Key Idea: Humans face three core anxieties: fear of death (we know we will die), need for protection (we feel helpless in a dangerous world), and need for meaning (why am I here?). Religion answers all three. It promises an afterlife, a caring God who protects us, and a cosmic purpose. These aren't true answers — they're comforting illusions. We invented God the way a child invents an imaginary friend, to ease our psychological pain.

Why it matters: Religion is a symptom of human weakness, Freud argued. But it's an understandable symptom. Understanding why we need these illusions tells us something true about the human psyche.

Freud famously called religion "the universal neurosis of mankind" — a coping mechanism we all share.

✨

3. Theological: What Religions Say About Themselves

The Theory: Religion exists because God is real and humans are made to seek the divine. It's not about society needing glue, or humans needing psychological comfort. It's about reality: a transcendent, sacred reality that humans are drawn toward.

Key Idea: A Christian theologian would say: God created humans in God's image. We have an innate hunger for the divine because God placed it in us. Prayer, worship, and faith aren't illusions or social glue — they're authentic encounter with something real. A Muslim would add: God has given humans an instinct toward God (the fitra). A Buddhist would say: humans naturally seek understanding and freedom from suffering. Across traditions, this view holds that the sacred is not invented; it's discovered.

Why it matters: This explanation takes seriously what believers actually say. It doesn't reduce religion to sociology or psychology. It treats the religious claim as legitimate: that the transcendent exists, and humans respond to it.

This is what believers would emphasise in response to Durkheim and Freud: "Yes, religion creates community and brings comfort. But that's not why it exists. Those are side-effects of encountering something real."

3. Explore

Here's the challenge: which explanation fits best? Five real-world scenarios. For each one, decide: is this best explained by Durkheim (Social), Freud (Psychological), or Theology (Divine)? Some might fit more than one — that's the point.

Scenario 1: The Community Response

Scenario

A village is devastated by flooding. Days later, people gather in the local church to pray together.

What draws them together? Is it:

  • Durkheim? The ritual itself rebuilds their sense of community and shared identity.
  • Freud? They seek comfort and reassurance in the face of trauma and helplessness.
  • Theology? They genuinely believe God listens and can help them.

Possible answer: All three could apply. Durkheim focuses on the social healing (being together). Freud focuses on the psychological comfort. Theology says the comfort comes from believing God hears them. In real life, all three are happening at once.

This is why these explanations are powerful but also limited — they capture part of the truth, not the whole picture.

Scenario 2: A Child's Question

Scenario

A five-year-old asks their parent: "What happens when Grandma dies?" The parent answers: "She goes to heaven, where she's happy and at peace."

Which explanation fits best?

  • Durkheim? This sustains social bonds (we're still connected to those who've died).
  • Freud? This is classic psychological comfort — facing the terror of death with a consoling story.
  • Theology? The parent believes it's true, and is sharing a genuine belief.

Best fit: Freud (though theology claims it too).** Freud would see this as a perfect example of how religion eases the existential anxiety of death. The child is terrified. The parent offers a soothing answer. Whether the answer is "true" or not, it serves a psychological function: it soothes fear.

A believer would say: the comfort is real because the belief is true. A Freudian would say: the comfort is real whether or not the belief is true.

Scenario 3: Deep Peace

Scenario

A person sits in silent meditation. They report afterwards: "For 20 minutes, I felt completely at peace. All my worries dissolved. I felt connected to something vast." They've never taken a drug; nothing external changed.

What explains this?

  • Durkheim? The sense of belonging to a meditation community caused the peace.
  • Freud? The mind created a soothing experience to escape anxiety.
  • Theology? They encountered something transcendent — a genuine spiritual reality.

All three could claim this. Durkheim might emphasise the ritual and community. Freud might say the mind generates this peace as self-soothing. A theologian (Buddhist or otherwise) would say the person glimpsed a genuine reality. This is where the explanations truly diverge — on what the experience means.

The experience itself is real. The disagreement is whether it's pointing to something external (theology) or internal (psychology).

Scenario 4: The Family Ritual

Scenario

A family gathers for the Passover Seder every year. The children groan beforehand, but afterwards, everyone feels connected and proud of their heritage. The ritual creates a sense of "this is who we are."

Which explanation fits?

  • Durkheim? Exactly. The ritual creates and maintains community identity.
  • Freud? Less relevant here — there's no obvious anxiety being soothed.
  • Theology? The family believes God commanded this remembrance, giving it sacred meaning.

Best fit: Durkheim.** This is a classic case of ritual creating social cohesion. The family exists as a community because it performs this ritual together. Durkheim's theory shines here.

But notice: Durkheim doesn't say whether the theological claim is true or false. Theology is compatible with his observation — the ritual both creates community and honours God.

Scenario 5: The Crisis of Meaning

Scenario

A person has a serious illness. They turn to prayer and faith for the first time. They say: "Before, I never understood why anything mattered. But now I know God has a plan for me. That gives me strength to face this."

Which explanation fits?

  • Durkheim? Maybe — community and ritual might follow, but that's not the primary need here.
  • Freud? Absolutely. The anxiety of meaninglessness and death drives the search for comfort and significance.
  • Theology? The person has discovered a genuine truth about reality and their place in it.

Best fit: Freud.** This exemplifies Freud's insight: humans need meaning, especially in the face of suffering and mortality. The person found it through religion.

Freud would say: this isn't evidence that God exists, but evidence that our minds need to believe something does. Theology would say: this is evidence of genuine encounter with the sacred.

• • •

What You Might Notice

Each scenario could fit more than one explanation. That's not a bug; it's the point. Durkheim, Freud, and theologians aren't necessarily contradicting each other. A single religious act might:
• Create social cohesion (Durkheim) and
• Provide psychological comfort (Freud) and
• Point toward something transcendent (Theology)

The explanations answer different questions. They're not mutually exclusive.

Time's up! Here's what the three explanations were:

1. Durkheim (Sociological): Religion is a social technology. It creates community identity through shared rituals and symbols. The "sacred" is really the group recognising itself. Example: A church congregation becomes a real community through gathered worship.

2. Freud (Psychological): Religion is wish-fulfilment. Humans face death, helplessness, and meaninglessness. Religion soothes these anxieties with comforting stories — a protective God, an afterlife, cosmic significance. It's not true, but it feels necessary. Example: A dying person finds comfort in believing in heaven.

3. Theology (Divine): Religion exists because the transcendent is real. God (or the sacred, the ultimate reality) actually exists, and humans are made to seek it. The community and comfort aren't illusions; they're side-effects of encountering something genuine. Example: A person experiences deep peace in meditation because they've touched something real.
