Seven Deaths and a Hundred Million Dollars

In 1982 someone poisoned Tylenol capsules on shop shelves. The company was not to blame — and it pulled every bottle in America anyway.

Easier version: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll work through the case that became the world's standard example of crisis management — and you'll practise the time language leaders need when every hour matters: the moment we knew, not until the tests are back, no sooner had we announced…
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • Think of a problem at work that was not your fault but was still your responsibility. What did you do first — and what did you wish you had done first?
  • In a crisis, who decides how fast an organisation moves: the lawyers, the communications team, or the person at the top?
  • Is it possible to do the right thing and the profitable thing at the same time in a crisis — or does it only look that way afterwards?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Within days the company had stopped production, contacted every distributor, and removed thirty-one million bottles from shops across the country. It was the largest recall of a consumer product the country had seen.
a recall (n) / to recall (v) — an organised withdrawal of a product from sale because it may be unsafe. Collocations: a voluntary recall, a product recall, to issue / announce a recall.
2. Officials worried that national headlines would inspire other people to try the same crime. Within weeks, police were investigating dozens of copycat cases involving other products.
a copycat (n, often used as an adjective) — a person who imitates a crime or an idea they have seen reported. A copycat attack, copycat products. Informal but widely used in the press and in risk reports.
3. After 1982, a sealed plastic ring around the neck and a foil layer under the cap became normal on medicine bottles. If either was broken, the customer would know at once. The industry moved to tamper-evident packaging.
tamper-evident (adj) — designed so that any interference is visible. To tamper with something = to interfere with it secretly and harmfully. Note: packaging cannot be tamper-proof, only tamper-evident or tamper-resistant.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: liability · credo · market share. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means the percentage of all sales in a category that one company or brand takes?
market share (n) — “Tylenol held more than a third of the market.” Verbs: to gain, lose, win back, erode market share.
2. Which term means legal responsibility for harm or loss, which may lead to paying compensation?
liability (n) — “The lawyers were worried about liability.” Adjective: liable (for). In accounts, liabilities are also simply what a company owes.
3. Which term means a short written statement of the beliefs and priorities that guide an organisation?
a credo (n) — from Latin I believe. Close to mission statement or statement of values, but older and more personal in tone. Register: formal, and often used a little ironically when a company does not live by its own.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Analysts said the name itself was worth far more than the factories. Losing the public's trust would destroy the product's brand equity. Brand equity is…
  (a) the share of the brand owned by its founders   (b) the money spent on advertising a brand   (c) the extra value a product has because people know and trust its name
(c) — brand equity (n): the commercial value that comes from the name rather than the product itself. It is why customers pay more for a known brand of paracetamol than for an identical generic one.
2. The cost of the withdrawal was reported in that year's results as a one-off charge against profits. A charge here is…
  (a) an amount deducted from earnings to account for a cost or loss   (b) a formal accusation made by the police   (c) a fee paid to shops for shelf space
(a) — a charge (n, accounting): to take a charge, a one-off / exceptional charge, a restructuring charge. Careful: the police meaning also appears in this case, so context decides.

D · Collocation & word building

1. The company's written values listed doctors, nurses, patients, employees, communities — and only last, the owners. Everyone with an interest in the outcome is a ___holder. Complete the word, and name the everyday noun inside it.
a stakeholder — anyone affected by, or with an interest in, an organisation's decisions. The noun inside it: a stake (an interest, or money at risk). Contrast shareholder, who owns part of the company. Stakeholder capitalism vs shareholder value is still a live debate.
2. Two months after the withdrawal, the product came back with new packaging, discount coupons and a campaign aimed at doctors. The company ___launched it. Add the prefix, then say what other business verbs take the same one.
relaunched — to relaunch (v) / a relaunch (n): to introduce a product again, usually after a problem or a redesign. The same prefix: rebrand, restructure, reposition, reinstate, rebuild trust.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — Three days in Chicago

At the end of September 1982, over roughly three days, seven people in the Chicago area died suddenly. The link between them was found quickly: each had taken Extra-Strength Tylenol capsules shortly before collapsing. Tests showed that the capsules had been filled with cyanide.

Tylenol was made by McNeil, a subsidiary of Johnson & Johnson, and it was one of the most valuable products the group owned — the leading painkiller in the United States, with more than a third of the market. Investigators soon established that the poison had not entered the bottles in a factory. Someone had taken packets from shop shelves, opened the capsules, added cyanide, and put the bottles back to be bought. The capsules of that era could be pulled apart and closed again by hand, and the bottles had no seals that showed they had been opened.

So in the strict sense, the company was not at fault. It was the victim of a crime nobody had thought to plan for. But it was the company's name on the bottle, and for several days nobody could say how many bottles had been poisoned, or where. The first calls to the company came not from the police but from journalists. The question for the chairman, James Burke, and his team was not whose fault this was. It was what to do before anyone knew the answer.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the company not legally or technically responsible for the poisoning?

2. What made the situation so difficult in the first few days, even though the cause was clear?

1. The poison was added after the product left the factory: someone took bottles from shops, contaminated the capsules and returned them to the shelves. Manufacturing was not the cause.

2. Nobody knew how many bottles had been contaminated or where they were, the company's name was on every packet, and the company learned of the crisis through the press rather than from its own systems.

Discuss:
  • When you are not to blame but your name is on the problem, whose view of responsibility matters more — the lawyer's or the customer's? Think of an example from your own working life.

Part 2 — Pull everything

The company stopped all Tylenol advertising at once, set up telephone lines for worried customers and doctors, and spoke to the press openly and often. Then it went much further than anyone required. It withdrew Tylenol capsules from every shop in the country — around thirty-one million bottles, at a cost reported at roughly a hundred million dollars. It offered to exchange capsules already in people's homes for solid tablets, free.

This was not the obvious choice. The poisonings had happened in one city. Federal officials reportedly worried that a national withdrawal would tell future criminals that this kind of attack worked. Inside and outside the company, there were people who argued that a national recall would destroy a product that could otherwise be saved. Tylenol's share of the market fell from over a third to well under a tenth within weeks, and at least one well-known advertising figure predicted publicly that the name would never sell again.

The company came back in November 1982 — around six weeks after the deaths — with a relaunch built around new packaging: a glued box, a plastic seal around the neck of the bottle, and a foil seal under the cap. It sent a large sales force to talk to doctors and pharmacists, and it distributed discount coupons across the country. Within about a year, Tylenol had won back most of its lost market share. The murders themselves were never solved. One man was convicted of trying to extort money from the company over the case, but no one was ever charged with the killings. Tampering with consumer products became a federal crime, and tamper-evident packaging became normal across the industry.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did some people, including officials, doubt that a national recall was the right decision?

2. What three things did the relaunch combine, and how quickly did the brand recover?

1. The deaths were in one area, so a national recall could look excessive; it might teach future criminals that product tampering was an effective attack; and it risked destroying the product rather than saving it.

2. Tamper-evident triple-sealed packaging, a large campaign aimed at doctors and pharmacists, and discount coupons for the public. Within about a year the brand had regained most of the market share it lost.

Discuss:
  • The company went further than the law or the authorities required. When in your organisation has doing more than required been the right call — and when would it have been a waste of money?

Part 3 — The legend and the later record

The case became a legend almost immediately. Business schools taught it as the model of crisis management: act fast, put the public first, tell the truth, accept the cost. Burke himself pointed to the company's credo — a one-page statement written in the 1940s that listed its responsibilities to doctors, nurses, patients and parents first, to employees and communities next, and to shareholders last. The document, he said, had made the decision easier, because it told people what the company had already promised.

The later record is more complicated. In 1986 another person died from a tampered Tylenol capsule, and the company stopped selling capsules for its non-prescription products altogether. Around 2009 and 2010, quality problems at McNeil factories led to repeated recalls of children's medicines and to criticism from regulators and Congress — including over a case in which contractors had been sent to buy up a faulty product from shops quietly instead of announcing a recall. The company that had written the textbook was accused of forgetting it.

In 2023 Johnson & Johnson separated its consumer-health brands, Tylenol among them, into a new company called Kenvue. In 2025 the brand was pulled into a new kind of trust crisis when the US government publicly linked the use of the drug in pregnancy to autism; the company and major medical organisations said the evidence did not show that the drug causes autism. Later that year, Kimberly-Clark agreed to buy Kenvue. So the question the case leaves open is sharper than the legend: was 1982 a triumph of values — or a rare crisis in which the company was clearly the victim, doing right was affordable, and nobody had to choose?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What role did the company's written credo play in the way the 1982 decision was later explained?

2. Name two later events that make the legend more complicated.

1. It had put patients, doctors and parents first and shareholders last, so the chairman argued the decision was already implied by what the company had promised; it gave people permission to act quickly and expensively.

2. Any two of: a further tampering death in 1986 and the end of non-prescription capsules; manufacturing quality failures and children's medicine recalls around 2009–2010, including a quiet buy-back of faulty product instead of an open recall; the 2025 public dispute about use in pregnancy and the brand's later sale.

Discuss:
  • Your organisation probably has written values. If a crisis started tomorrow, would they make any real decision easier — or would everyone look at the lawyers?

Grammar — time clauses under pressure

A crisis is told in hours, not years. Board updates, press statements and incident reviews are built on precise time language: what happened first, what you are waiting for, and what will happen the moment a condition is met. At C1 the difference between when, once, until and by the time is not style — it is a commitment.

1 · Future meaning, present form. After when, once, as soon as, the moment, until, before, after, by the time, English uses a present tense for the future — never will.
• “We'll brief the staff as soon as the lab results arrive.” (NOT will arrive)
• Use the present perfect when completion matters: “Once we have checked every site, we'll publish the list.”
• The moment / the minute / the instant = immediately when: “The moment the fault was confirmed, the line was shut down.”
2 · Order and deadlines.
• By the time + past simple → past perfect in the main clause: “By the time the story broke, the supplier had already been replaced.”
• Until = the state continues up to a point: “The plant stays closed until the inspectors sign it off.”
• Not until … that (cleft) puts the delay in the spotlight: “It was not until the second week that the scale became clear.”
3 · Inversion for a sequence that hit hard. Formal, written, or deliberate spoken emphasis:
• No sooner had + subject + past participle … than: “No sooner had the new system gone live than the complaints began.”
• Hardly / Scarcely had … when: “Hardly had the auditors arrived when the chief financial officer resigned.”
• Only after / Only when / Not until + clause, then auxiliary + subject: “Only when the data were published did the regulator act.”

Use it sparingly. One inversion in a statement sounds controlled. Three sound like a speech.

1. Choose the verb form after the time word

Put the verb in brackets into the correct form. Think about whether the meaning is future, past, or finished before another event.

1. We will not restart deliveries until the laboratory that the batch is clean. (confirm)

2. By the time the regulator published its report, the company its supplier. (already / change)

3. The moment the helpline tomorrow morning, calls will go straight to trained pharmacists. (open)

4. Once every shop its stock, we will announce the relaunch date. (return — completion matters)

1. confirms   2. had already changed   3. opens   4. has returned

Sentences 1, 3 and 4 all refer to the future — and none of them uses will after the time word.

2. Rewrite with inversion

Complete the second sentence so that it means the same as the first. Write only the missing words.

1. We only understood the size of the problem after the third report. → Only after the third report the size of the problem.

2. The share price didn't recover until the new packaging reached the shops. → Not until the new packaging reached the shops recover.

3. The press conference had barely started when the first question about liability came. → Hardly when the first question about liability came.

4. We had just announced the withdrawal and then a second case was reported. → No sooner had we announced the withdrawal a second case was reported.

1. did we understand   2. did the share price   3. had the press conference started   4. than

Notice the auxiliary: past simple → did; past perfect → had. And no sooner pairs with than, hardly with when.

3. The statement — complete it, then read it aloud

Complete this model statement from a company in a product-safety crisis. The meaning you need is in brackets. Then read it aloud at the pace you would use in front of cameras.

“Our first responsibility is to the people who use our products. (at the exact second that) we learned of the reports, we stopped all advertising and withdrew the product nationwide. We will not return it to shelves (up to the point when) we can guarantee that every package is secure. (before that point in the future) the new packaging reaches pharmacies, every customer will have been offered a free exchange. Only when the investigation is complete (auxiliary + subject) comment on its findings. And (from the point when — one word) the first new shipments arrive, we will publish the test results in full.”

The moment (or As soon as) · until · By the time · will we · once

Look at what each time phrase promises. Until is a condition the public will hold you to; by the time is a deadline. Choose them the way a lawyer would.

Going deeper

A note on the figures: the numbers of bottles, costs and market-share percentages here are rounded and come from public reporting at the time; the later history of the brand is still developing.

What the case actually tests. The legend says: values first, and the market will reward you. The first reading takes that seriously. A written credo that had put patients above shareholders for forty years gave a large organisation permission to act in hours rather than weeks, and to spend money that no lawyer would have signed off. Without it, the reasonable local recall — the one the authorities preferred — is the likely outcome, and the brand may well have died slowly of doubt.

The second reading is less comfortable. The company was clearly a victim; the public knew it; the product was hugely profitable; and a hundred million dollars was small against the value of the name. Doing the right thing and protecting the brand pointed in exactly the same direction. That is the easiest kind of crisis to handle well. The real test of values is the crisis where the company is at fault, the fix is expensive, and silence might work — and on that test, the quiet buy-back of faulty medicine a generation later is at least as informative as 1982.

A third point is about legends themselves. A case that becomes the textbook can make an organisation feel it has already proved its character, so later failures are harder to see from inside. And the conditions have changed: in 1982 the company had days to decide what to say. Today a crisis is public within minutes, the facts are contested from the first hour, and — as 2025 showed — the attack on trust may come not from a criminal but from a government, with the science on the company's side and public opinion divided.
Talk it through:
  • Separate the two explanations — values and calculation. If you could only keep one, which better predicts how a company will behave in its next crisis?
  • Name a crisis your industry could face where the organisation would be clearly at fault. Would its written values help — and who, specifically, would invoke them?
  • Authorities preferred a narrower response; the company chose a wider one. When should a company go beyond what regulators ask, and when is that overreaction?
  • In 1982 the company had days. If the same thing happened this afternoon, what would you need to have decided in the first sixty minutes?

Speak

All of it out loud, against the clock. In a crisis a leader speaks to the board, the public, the partners and the workforce on the same day — and all four versions will be compared.

Useful language: the moment we learned… · we will not … until… · by the time … we had already… · only when … will we… · our first responsibility is to… · we are not yet in a position to say… · and to close: what I can commit to today is…

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

You are the chairman, four days into the crisis. A journalist says: “The poisonings were in one city. You're pulling a hundred million dollars of product from the whole country. Aren't you just teaching the next criminal that this works — and buying yourself good headlines while you do it?”

Answer properly: take the concern seriously, explain the sequence of what you knew and when, state what will happen before the product returns, and end with a commitment rather than a hedge. Then do it again in ninety seconds — the compression is the exercise.

Task C · Four rooms, one crisis (4 minutes)

Take one product or service problem — real or invented — and deliver the first announcement four ways, one minute each. Notice how the time language changes: what you promise, and by when.

  • To the board: the full picture, including what you do not yet know and the likely cost.
  • To customers and the public: what to do right now, in plain words, without speculation.
  • To retailers or partners: exactly what they must do, and when they will hear from you next.
  • To your own staff: honest, calm, and clear about who may speak to the press.

Prefer not to use your own organisation? Take one of these instead:

  • A food producer finds broken glass in a single jar from one factory.
  • An airline grounds an aircraft type after an incident at another carrier abroad.
  • A software company discovers that a supplier's error exposed customer data.
  • A toy maker receives one report of a choking incident in another country.
