Where Did the Free Time Go?

Keynes promised his grandchildren a fifteen-hour week. The productivity arrived; the leisure was intercepted. A history of the working week — and of the arguments over who stole the difference.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Being seen at your desk mattered more than what the desk produced: staying late as theatre, sick at work as loyalty. Management researchers call it presenteeism.
presenteeism (n) — attendance performed as commitment, productive or not; the mirror-twin of absenteeism, and by some estimates the costlier of the two. The four-day-week movement's first target.
2. First the emails colonised the evening, then the weekend, then the beach. Work did not ask; it simply encroached.
to encroach (on/upon) (v) — to advance gradually into territory not yours: encroach on leisure / rights / borders. Noun: encroachment. The word implies stealth — nobody votes for it.
3. Each pay rise bought a bigger life whose upkeep consumed the rise; satisfaction returned to baseline, and the running continued. Psychologists call the machine the hedonic treadmill.
the (hedonic) treadmill (n) — adaptation that converts gains into new baselines. To be stuck on the treadmill; to step off it. One of the leading suspects in the case of the missing leisure.

The story, in three parts

Part 1 — The invention of the evening

Free time, as a mass possession, is an artefact — younger than the railway and considerably harder to build. Industrialisation did not merely lengthen work; it changed its ownership: where agrarian labour had followed the sun and the season, punctuated by feast days in startling number, the factory bought time itself, and took as much of it as law and exhaustion allowed. Fourteen-hour days were unremarkable; children worked them. Against this, in 1817, Robert Owen distilled a demand into arithmetic so clean it fit on a banner — eight hours labour, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest — and a century of strikes, prosecutions, funerals and legislation slowly made the slogan ordinary. It is worth saying precisely: the weekend is not a tradition. It is a settlement, extracted clause by clause from people who fought it.

What complicated the morality tale was capital discovering its own case for rest. Henry Ford — no sentimentalist — doubled wages in 1914 and instituted the five-day week in 1926, reasoning aloud that exhausted men built defective cars and that workers without leisure or income could hardly become customers for the products of their own labour. Consumption, in other words, required time in which to consume. Kellogg's, from 1930, went further still: six-hour shifts, adopted partly to spread Depression-era employment, which then refused to die on schedule — sections of the workforce, having reorganised their lives around gardens, sports and children, clung to the six-hour day until 1985 against decades of managerial pressure. One Michigan town quietly ran a fifty-year controlled experiment in the leisure society, and liked it.

Into this trajectory, in 1930, John Maynard Keynes dropped the era's most elegant extrapolation. In Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren he reasoned that compound growth and mechanisation would, within a century, solve "the economic problem" outright; the residual work — perhaps fifteen hours a week, three-hour shifts — would exist chiefly to satisfy the old Adam in us who needs occupation. Humanity's real crisis, he suggested, would be existential: how to live wisely and agreeably once the striving stopped. He was writing into the teeth of the Depression, which makes the confidence either magnificent or delusional — the story's business is to establish which.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. In what sense is the weekend "a settlement, not a tradition"?

2. What was capital's own case for rest — and what did the Kellogg's episode demonstrate?

1. It was extracted, clause by clause, through a century of industrial conflict and legislation — Owen's 8-8-8 arithmetic turned into law against sustained resistance. Nothing about it is natural or inevitable.

2. Ford's: tired workers build worse products, and consumption requires paid, rested consumers. Kellogg's six-hour shifts, meant as Depression job-sharing, survived among workers until 1985 — a fifty-year demonstration that people, given the leisure society, keep it.

Discuss:
  • Ford's argument makes leisure instrumental — rest so that you produce and consume. Does the case for free time need a nobler foundation, or is the instrumental one safer precisely because it's selfish?

Part 2 — The interception

On the arithmetic, Keynes has been vindicated embarrassingly: productivity in the rich world has multiplied roughly fivefold since his essay, ample to fund his fifteen hours several times over. And for decades the world tracked his curve — average hours fell steadily for a hundred years. Then, around the 1970s and 80s, the descent simply stalled, most sharply in the Anglophone economies, while output per hour climbed on without us. The dividend kept being declared; it stopped being paid in time. The interesting question — the one whole literatures now quarrel over — is who intercepted it.

The suspects cooperate rather than compete. Distribution: the gains flowed disproportionately upward, and for households below the top, stagnant real wages made the second full salary a structural necessity rather than an emancipation — two earners now purchasing what one once had. Adaptation: the hedonic treadmill converted each gain into a new baseline, with the house, the schools and the standards inflating in step with the means to afford them. Status: in a neat inversion of Veblen, the elite stopped advertising idleness and began advertising scarcity of time — overwork as the badge of mattering, the crowded calendar as this century's carriage and footmen. And then technology dismantled the very boundary the factory age had accidentally gifted: Ford's gate closed behind the worker each evening because work was a place; the smartphone abolished the place, and with it the closing. The research on "always-on" availability is unambiguous — the mere expectation of reachability, before any message arrives, keeps the stress physiology idling like an engine at a red light that never changes.

And one finding deserves italics in every household that blames itself: time-diary studies show today's parents devote more hours to active childcare than their 1960s counterparts did — while both partners work. Modern intensive parenting expanded precisely as paid work refused to contract; the double burden is not a failure of scheduling but the collision of two expanding institutions on a fixed twenty-four hours. Exhaustion, in this light, is not a character flaw. It is an incidence question — the study of where a burden actually lands — and it has landed, with historic precision, on the kitchen calendar.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. In what sense was Keynes right, and where exactly did the curve break?

2. Name the four cooperating suspects — and the finding that "deserves italics".

1. Productivity multiplied ~fivefold, funding his fifteen hours many times over; hours fell for a century, then stalled around the 1970s–80s (worst in Anglophone economies) while output climbed on — the dividend declared but unpaid in time.

2. Unequal distribution (making dual full-time earning structural), hedonic adaptation, status inversion (conspicuous busyness), and technology's abolition of the boundary. The italicised finding: parents today do more active childcare than 1960s parents, while both work — the exhaustion is incidence, not incompetence.

Part 3 — Reopening the question

The counter-movement began with a businessman's irritation rather than a manifesto. In 2018 Andrew Barnes, reading mid-flight that office workers achieve barely three productive hours a day, offered his New Zealand firm a wager dressed as a gift: a hundred per cent of pay for eighty per cent of the hours, provided a hundred per cent of the output survived. It did — the trial went permanent, and the framing proved as contagious as the result, because it recast the shorter week not as generosity but as an exchange: focus purchased with Fridays, meetings culled, distraction treated as a cost centre. Iceland scaled the experiment to a society — public-sector trials from 2015 to 2019 whose published verdict, "an overwhelming success," preceded negotiations that extended shorter-hours rights to the great majority of the national workforce. Britain, in 2022, supplied the largest corporate sample: sixty-one firms under academic observation, revenue essentially flat, attrition and sick days down, and — the statistic that travelled — more than nine in ten electing to continue when the trial expired. Germany's pilots followed; so did others; the metalworkers' union had already, in 2018, struck the first major deal granting workers a right to a 28-hour week for family phases.

Intellectual honesty requires the counter-column, stated at full strength. The trials self-select for believers and for compressible, meeting-heavy office work; nobody has demonstrated the compression of a hospital ward, a classroom or a delivery round, and "output maintained" is a claim that grows softer the closer one inspects its measurement. France's 35-hour law remains the cautionary counter-example — a boundary imposed by statute that markets and managers spent two decades quietly tunnelling under. It is entirely possible that the four-day week becomes another perk of the already-privileged, deepening the very leisure inequality it advertises against. The debate, in short, is real — which is precisely what distinguishes this decade from the four silent ones before it.

Because that is the historical novelty worth ending on: for the first time since roughly the moon landings, the length of the working week is again a live political and commercial question — trialled, unionised, legislated, negotiated in job interviews by a generation that regards the always-on settlement as neither natural nor binding. Whether the pause ends or the ratchet holds, the story's one secure lesson stands: every hour of discretionary time your household possesses was invented, demanded and won by people assured it was impossible — and the assurance is currently being tested again, in public, with your Tuesday as the stake.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What made the New Zealand framing "contagious", and what did Iceland and the UK add?

2. State the counter-column at full strength.

1. It was an exchange, not a gift — full pay for full output in fewer hours, with focus engineered. Iceland scaled it to a society (rights for most of the workforce); the UK supplied the largest corporate evidence (61 firms, flat revenue, >90% continuing); Germany's union added a negotiated right to 28 hours.

2. Self-selecting, office-heavy samples; no demonstrated compression for wards, classrooms or delivery rounds; soft output measurement; France's 35-hour law as the tunnelled-under precedent; and the risk that the shorter week becomes a privilege that deepens leisure inequality.

Grammar — the unreal present: it's time, would rather, as if, suppose

English keeps a set of expressions that force the verb one step into the past to talk about the present — the grammar of impatience, preference and pretence. They are exactly the structures this topic is argued in.

The impatience form — it's (high/about) time + past:
• "It's high time we reclaimed the evening." (= we should have already; mild reproach built in)
• Compare: "It's time to go" (neutral, infinitive) vs "It's time we went" (overdue).

The preference forms — would rather / would sooner:
• Same subject → bare infinitive: "I'd rather work four days."
• Different subject → past: "I'd rather my employer measured output, not hours." · "I'd sooner we didn't answer emails at night."
The pretence and thought-experiment forms:
• as if / as though + past (unreal): "He checks his phone as if the office owned his evenings." (it doesn't — officially) · with real likelihood, present is fine: "It looks as if the trial is working."
• suppose / supposing / what if + past: "Suppose we all left at four — what would actually break?"
• if only + past for the present wish: "If only the week had a fifth evening."

Register note: the past here signals unreality, not time — and were beats was in careful speech: "He behaves as though he were indispensable."

Going deeper

A note on the record: figures are rounded; Keynes wrote his own hedges into the essay; trial findings are the researchers' published headlines with their critics' objections retained; and the Kellogg's episode is drawn from the standard histories of the six-hour day.

What the story actually tests. First, the difference between affordability and incidence. Keynes solved the wrong problem brilliantly: he proved the leisure society affordable and assumed affordability would decide. But time, unlike money, is distributed by bargaining power, status competition and default settings — and each of those channels routed the dividend elsewhere. The exportable analytical habit: whenever a promised benefit fails to arrive, stop asking whether it exists and start asking through whose hands it passes.

Second, boundaries as public goods. One family's phone-free evening is willpower; a society's is infrastructure — legal rights to disconnect, negotiated hour-caps, cultural closing times. The history is blunt on this: individual resolve lost to the factory bell and loses to the notification; every durable gain in free time was collective and codified. Which reframes the self-help literature of "balance" as, at best, first aid — and at worst, the privatisation of a structural problem into personal guilt.

Third, the status engine, hardest of the suspects because it lives in us. So long as busyness signals importance, any liberated hour gets reinvested in visible striving — the treadmill runs on admiration, not necessity. The genuinely subversive act the story points to is Kellogg's Michigan: a community that let gardens, teams and children carry status instead. Whether a modern economy can re-run that experiment at scale is precisely what the current decade of trials is, underneath the productivity metrics, actually testing.

Talk it through:
  • "Ask through whose hands the benefit passes." Apply the incidence habit to another vanished promise — cheaper energy, paperless offices, labour-saving homes.
  • Boundaries as public goods: which of your own limits would survive only if everyone kept them — and what follows for how they should be set?
  • Design the status swap: what would have to carry prestige in your profession for a person leaving at four to be envied rather than suspected?
  • Keynes's real question was philosophical: what is abundance for? Answer it in one sentence you would defend — then check your calendar for evidence that you believe it.
