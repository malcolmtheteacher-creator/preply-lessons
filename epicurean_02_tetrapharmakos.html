The Four-Part Cure

The Tetrapharmakos (τετραφάρμακος) — literally "four-drug remedy" — was a summary of Epicurean philosophy that could be memorised and applied like medicine. It appears in a papyrus from the Epicurean library at Herculaneum.

💊
Why "Medicine"?
Remember: Epicurus saw philosophy as therapy. These four statements are meant to be taken like pills — swallowed, digested, and applied whenever fear or anxiety arises. They treat the four main sources of human misery.
1

Don't fear god

ἄφοβον ὁ θεός
"God is not to be feared"
The gods exist but don't intervene in human affairs. No divine punishment awaits.
2

Don't worry about death

ἀνύποπτον ὁ θάνατος
"Death is nothing to us"
When we exist, death doesn't. When death exists, we don't. There's nothing to fear.
3

The good is easy to attain

εὐκτητὸν τἀγαθόν
"What is good is easy to get"
True pleasure (tranquility) requires only simple things: friends, food, shelter, philosophy.
4

The bad is easy to endure

εὔκαρτον τὸ δεινόν
"What is terrible is easy to endure"
Severe pain is brief; chronic pain is bearable. The mind can always find refuge.
Your turn: Look at these four remedies. What pattern do you notice? What kinds of fears or anxieties is Epicurus targeting?

The Pattern:

Each remedy targets a fundamental human anxiety:

  • Fear of divine punishment (religious anxiety)
  • Fear of death (existential anxiety)
  • Fear of not getting what we want (desire/lack anxiety)
  • Fear of suffering (pain anxiety)

Epicurus believed that if you can dissolve these four fears, you achieve ataraxia — complete tranquility. The Tetrapharmakos is a complete kit for mental peace.

Remedy 1: Don't Fear God

First Remedy
"God is not to be feared"

What Epicurus Believed About the Gods

Epicurus was not an atheist. He believed gods existed — but his conception of them was radically different from traditional Greek religion:

  • The gods exist — as perfect, immortal, blessed beings
  • They are perfectly happy — experiencing eternal ataraxia
  • They don't intervene — managing human affairs would disturb their peace
  • They don't punish or reward — they have no interest in human behaviour
  • They dwell "between worlds" — in the spaces between universes

The blessed and immortal nature knows no trouble itself, nor causes trouble to any other, so that it is never constrained by anger or favour. For all such things exist only in the weak.

— Principal Doctrine 1

Your turn: Why might Epicurus have believed this about the gods? And why would this belief be therapeutic — how does it "cure" religious anxiety?

Why this view?

  • If gods are perfect, they must have perfect peace (ataraxia)
  • Getting involved in human affairs would disturb that peace
  • Anger, jealousy, punishment — these are disturbances, weaknesses
  • Perfect beings wouldn't have them

Why it's therapeutic:

  • No fear of divine punishment in this life
  • No fear of punishment after death
  • No anxiety about pleasing capricious gods
  • No superstitious rituals needed to avoid divine wrath
  • The universe is not hostile — just indifferent

Note: This doesn't mean abandoning religious practice. Epicurus himself participated in festivals. But the motivation changes: from fear to appreciation.

⚠️
Why This Was Dangerous
In the ancient world, denying divine providence was socially and legally dangerous. Later, Christian writers would use Epicurus's theology as evidence that he was godless and immoral. This contributed to centuries of hostility toward his philosophy.

Remedy 2: Don't Worry About Death

Second Remedy
"Death is nothing to us"

The Argument

This is perhaps the most famous Epicurean teaching. The argument goes:

Accustom yourself to believing that death is nothing to us, for good and evil imply the capacity for sensation, and death is the privation of all sentience... Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not.

— Letter to Menoeceus

The Logic, Step by Step

  1. Premise 1: Good and bad require sensation (experience)
  2. Premise 2: Death is the complete end of sensation
  3. Conclusion 1: Therefore, death cannot be bad for the one who dies
  4. Premise 3: While we exist, death does not exist for us
  5. Premise 4: When death exists, we no longer exist
  6. Conclusion 2: Therefore, we never "experience" death — it's never present to us
Your turn: Does this argument convince you? Try to identify at least one strong point and one objection someone might raise.

Strengths of the argument:

  • Logically valid — if the premises are true, the conclusion follows
  • Eliminates fear of being dead (no hell, no nothing)
  • Points out we don't grieve for the time before we were born
  • Focuses attention on life, not afterlife

Common objections:

  • "I fear dying, not death" — Epicurus would say: then fear pain, not death. They're different.
  • "I'll miss future experiences" — The Symmetry Argument: you also "missed" infinite past experiences. Why mourn the future but not the past?
  • "Death is bad because it deprives me of goods" — This "deprivation" objection is the strongest philosophical counter. We'll examine it in Lesson 14.
  • "My loved ones will suffer" — True, but that's their suffering, not yours. Help them while you live.

Remedy 3: The Good Is Easy to Attain

Third Remedy
"What is good is easy to get"

What Is "The Good"?

For Epicurus, the highest good is pleasure — but not the pleasure of excess. The highest pleasure is:

  • Ataraxia — tranquility, freedom from mental disturbance
  • Aponia — freedom from physical pain

These are katastematic (static) pleasures — states of stable contentment — rather than kinetic (active) pleasures like eating or drinking.

The wealth required by nature is limited and is easy to procure; but the wealth required by vain ideals extends to infinity.

— Principal Doctrine 15

Why It's Easy

Epicurus taught that natural and necessary desires are easy to satisfy:

  • Hunger? → Simple food satisfies it
  • Thirst? → Water is everywhere
  • Cold? → Basic shelter and clothing
  • Loneliness? → Friendship (which costs nothing)
  • Confusion? → Philosophy (free to learn)

It's only vain desires — for luxury, fame, power — that are endless and impossible to satisfy.

Your turn: Is this naive or profound? Consider: In what ways is Epicurus right that the good is easy to attain? In what ways might he be wrong or oversimplifying?

Where he seems right:

  • Hedonic treadmill research shows more wealth doesn't increase happiness past a point
  • Many people with simple lives report high life satisfaction
  • Gratitude practices (appreciating what you have) improve wellbeing
  • Consumer culture does create artificial, unsatisfiable desires

Where he might oversimplify:

  • Poverty is real — not everyone can easily get food, shelter, security
  • Chronic illness makes "freedom from pain" not so easy
  • Social structures (racism, sexism, class) create barriers
  • Epicurus was writing for those with at least basic needs met

Possible defense: Epicurus might say he's describing what's necessary for happiness, not what's sufficient. Injustice should be fought — but the point is that wealth beyond sufficiency doesn't increase happiness.

Remedy 4: The Bad Is Easy to Endure

Fourth Remedy
"What is terrible is easy to endure"

Epicurus's Argument About Pain

This might seem like the hardest remedy to accept. Epicurus's reasoning:

Continuous bodily pain does not last long; instead, extreme pain lasts only a very short time, and even that degree of pain which slightly exceeds bodily pleasure does not persist for many days at once. Long illnesses allow of an excess of bodily pleasure over pain.

— Principal Doctrine 4

The Logic

  • Severe pain is brief: The body can't sustain extreme agony indefinitely — it either passes, or kills you
  • Chronic pain is manageable: If pain persists, it must be at a level the body can bear
  • The mind has resources: Even in pain, memory and anticipation of pleasures remain
  • Death is the limit: At worst, death ends all pain (and death is nothing to fear)
📜
Epicurus Practised What He Preached
In his final letter, written while dying in agony (likely from kidney stones), Epicurus wrote: "On this happy day, which is the last of my life, I write to you. The pains in my bladder and bowels continue... But all these are counterbalanced by the joy in my soul at the memory of our conversations together."
Your turn: This remedy is often considered the weakest. What's your honest reaction? Where does the argument succeed or fail?

Where the argument has force:

  • Anticipatory fear of pain is often worse than the pain itself
  • Humans do adapt to chronic conditions (hedonic adaptation)
  • Mental attitude genuinely affects pain experience (placebo, mindfulness)
  • Having a framework for thinking about pain may itself be therapeutic

Weaknesses:

  • Some chronic pain is genuinely not "easy to endure" (nerve damage, severe illness)
  • Mental suffering (depression, grief) doesn't follow the same logic
  • "Death is the limit" is cold comfort to those not ready to die
  • Saying pain is "easy" may seem dismissive to sufferers

A charitable reading: Epicurus isn't saying pain is pleasant or trivial. He's saying the fear of future pain causes more suffering than necessary. Most pain is either brief or bearable; most fears of pain are exaggerated.

📚 Review from Lesson 1

From Lesson 1: Who Was Epicurus?

Without looking back, answer these questions:

1. Where was Epicurus born, and what happened to his family when he was about 19?

Answer: Born on the island of Samos (341 BCE). His family were Athenian colonists who were expelled from Samos when he was about 19 — they became refugees and settled in Colophon (modern Turkey).

2. Why did Epicurus call philosophy "health of the soul"? What metaphor was he using?

Answer: He saw philosophy as therapy or medicine. The soul is "sick" with fear, anxiety, false beliefs, and endless desires. Philosophy is the cure — "empty is the argument that doesn't relieve any human suffering."
