The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — A meeting in Boston

In 1948, at a convention of the American Psychological Association in Boston, a small group of college examiners met informally. Their problem was practical. Every year their universities set long comprehensive exams, and every year writing them took an enormous amount of work.

One of them was Benjamin Bloom, associate director of the Board of Examinations at the University of Chicago. His idea was simple: if test questions could be classified by the kind of thinking they required, universities could share their banks of questions instead of each writing them from scratch.

The group met about twice a year. In 1956 it published Taxonomy of Educational Objectives, Handbook I: Cognitive Domain. It sorted thinking into six levels, from the simplest to the most complex: Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis and Evaluation.

3. What were the six original levels?

1. Writing their long annual exams took an enormous amount of work.

2. Their banks of test questions.

3. Knowledge, Comprehension, Application, Analysis, Synthesis, Evaluation.

Part 2 — An unfamiliar word

At first, hardly anyone noticed. “Taxonomy” was an unfamiliar word, and the book was written for examiners rather than for classroom teachers. But over the following decades it spread, and it was eventually translated into 22 languages.

When researchers used the framework to analyse real courses and tests, the results were uncomfortable. The questions were weighted heavily towards the bottom level: recognising and recalling information. The framework made it easy to see what was missing — the levels where students apply, analyse and judge.

In 2001 a team led by Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl, who had worked on the original handbook, published a revision. The levels became verbs — Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create — and the top two changed places: Synthesis, renamed Create, moved above Evaluate. The revision also relaxed the idea of a strict ladder: the levels were allowed to overlap.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the book get little attention at first?

2. What did analyses of real tests show?

3. Name two changes in the 2001 revision.

1. “Taxonomy” was an unfamiliar word, and the book was written for examiners.

2. Most questions only asked students to recognise or recall information.

3. Any two: the levels became verbs; Synthesis became Create and moved to the top, above Evaluate; the strict hierarchy was relaxed.

Discuss:

Part 3 — The trouble with the bottom

Bloom's taxonomy, usually drawn as a pyramid, is now part of teacher training in many countries, and teachers use its verbs — explain, compare, judge, design — to write lesson aims. But it can have a side effect. If remembering is the “lowest” level, it can start to look unimportant.

In 2016 the biologist Susan Lawler, of La Trobe University in Australia, described what had happened in her own field. Teaching students to identify plants and animals had been dropped from courses as too simplistic — the bottom of the pyramid. “The problem is that identifying a plant or insect is not simple at all,” she wrote. To name an insect correctly, you need to know what a mandible, a maxilla and a rostrum are. Meanwhile, people from industry were complaining that graduates couldn't identify common plants and animals.

Her argument was that the higher levels depend on the lower ones: you cannot analyse, evaluate or create in a subject you know nothing about. The pyramid is a way of seeing different kinds of thinking — not a reason to stop remembering.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What do teachers use Bloom's verbs for?

2. Why had identification been dropped from Lawler's courses?

3. Why, according to Lawler, is identification not simple?

1. To write lesson aims.

2. Because it was seen as too simplistic — the bottom level of the pyramid.

3. Because it needs a lot of detailed knowledge — for example, what a mandible, a maxilla or a rostrum is.

How the tool works

Six levels of thinking, from the simplest at the bottom to the most complex at the top (the 2001 version). Each level has its own verbs — which makes it easy to check what a question really asks.

Using it, step by step

  1. Read the question or task and find the verb: list, explain, compare, judge, design…
  2. Ask what students must actually do in their heads — the verb can mislead (“explain” is only Remember if the explanation was learnt by heart).
  3. Place each task on the pyramid. Then look at the whole test or lesson: are all the tasks on one level?
  4. Add tasks at the missing levels — but keep the bottom: higher thinking needs knowledge to work on.

Try it yourself

A teacher has written six questions about a short story. Which level of the 2001 taxonomy does each one mainly ask for?

1. Write a new ending for the story, told by the dog.
2. What is the name of the main character's sister?
3. Explain in your own words why the father leaves home.
4. Was the mother right to keep the secret? Give your reasons.
5. Which details in the first paragraph prepare us for the ending?
6. Use the story's advice to plan what you would say to a friend in the same situation.

1. Create — producing something new.

2. Remember — recalling a fact from the text.

3. Understand — explaining the meaning shows understanding.

4. Evaluate — making a judgement and supporting it.

5. Analyse — breaking the text into parts and seeing how they connect.

6. Apply — using an idea in a new, real situation.

Where the tool breaks

Verbs aren't levels. “Explain” can be pure memory if the explanation was learnt by heart.

It can make knowledge look cheap. The bottom of the pyramid is the foundation, not the least important part.

Thinking isn't a ladder. Real tasks mix levels; even the 2001 revision relaxed the strict order.

  • Think of a test or textbook page you know well. Which levels does it use most?

Grammar — subject and object questions

Bloom's bottom level is full of questions like Who wrote it? and What did they write? They look similar, but one uses did and the other doesn't. It depends on whether the question word is the subject or the object.

Object questions ask about the object of the verb. Use do/does/did and question word order.
“Bloom met the examiners.” → “Who did Bloom meet?” (answer: the examiners)
Subject questions ask about the subject. No do/does/did — keep statement word order.
“Bloom met the examiners.” → “Who met the examiners?” (answer: Bloom)
This works with who, what, which, whose, how many.
“What happened in 1956?”  “How many people came?”  “Which level comes first?”
Other auxiliaries stay in their normal place in subject questions.
“Who has read the report?”  “What can go wrong?”
Watch out: ~~Who did write the book?~~ → “Who wrote the book?”   ~~What did happen?~~ → “What happened?”
