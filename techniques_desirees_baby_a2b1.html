Désirée's Baby

Louisiana, long ago. A young wife, a new baby — and a proud man's terrible mistake.

Key words — learn them, then make your guess

First, let's learn eight words. Read each word and its sentence, guess the meaning, then click Reveal to check. Say each word out loud.

1. When you find something, you discover it — you see it and take it. (Past: found.) You can find money, keys… or even a lost child.
to find (found) (verb) — to discover something or someone.
2. When two people marry, they become husband and wife. (Past: married.)
to marry (married) (verb) — to become husband and wife.
3. A proud person thinks: "I am important. My family is important." Sometimes proud people cannot say sorry.
proud (adjective) — feeling that you (or your family) are important; too proud people cannot say sorry.
4. Your skin is the outside of your body. Skin can be light or dark.
skin (noun) — the outside of your body.
5. A slave was a person who was not free. Other people "owned" them and forced them to work. This was real in America long ago.
a slave (noun) — a person who was not free and was forced to work. Slavery is this terrible system.
6. A secret is something you know but do not tell other people.
a secret (noun) — something you know but do not tell.
7. When you send someone away, you tell them: "Go. You cannot stay here."
to send away (sent away) (verb) — to tell someone to go and not come back.
8. When you burn something, you destroy it with fire. (Past: burned.)
to burn (burned) (verb) — to destroy something with fire.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Before you read: this story happens about 170 years ago in Louisiana, in the south of the USA. At that time, black people there were slaves: they were not free, and the colour of a person's skin decided their whole life. Kate Chopin wrote this story to show how cruel and wrong that world was.

Part 1 — The girl at the gate

Long ago, in Louisiana, a man called Monsieur Valmondé found a little girl. She was asleep by the big stone gate of his farm. Nobody knew her family. Nobody knew her name. Madame Valmondé took the girl and loved her like a daughter. They called her Désirée.

Désirée grew up beautiful and kind. One day, a young man rode past the same stone gate. His name was Armand Aubigny. He saw Désirée and fell in love in one minute. Armand was rich, and he was proud: his family name was one of the oldest names in Louisiana. Monsieur Valmondé said, "Be careful. We do not know Désirée's family." Armand did not listen. "I will give her my name," he said.

So Armand and Désirée married, and she went to live in his big house, L'Abri. It was a dark, sad place. The people who worked there were slaves — they were not free. And Armand was hard and cold with them.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did Monsieur Valmondé find Désirée?

2. Why was Monsieur Valmondé worried about the marriage?

1. Asleep by the big stone gate of his farm, when she was a little girl. Nobody knew her family or her name.

2. Because nobody knew Désirée's family — and Armand's family was very old and very proud.

Talk about it:
  • Armand says, "I will give her my name." Why is his name so important to him? Is a name really so important?

Part 2 — The baby

Désirée and Armand had a baby boy. At first Armand was happy and kind — kinder than ever before. Désirée was happy too.

But when the baby was about three months old, something changed. Visitors came to the house and looked at the baby in a strange way. Armand stopped smiling. He did not look at his wife or his son. One hot afternoon, Désirée looked at her baby. Then she looked at the little servant boy who stood near him. She looked from one face to the other, again and again. Her own face went white.

She called Armand. "Look at our child," she said. "What does it mean? Tell me." Armand answered coldly: "It means that the child is not white. It means that you are not white."

"It is not true!" Désirée cried. "Look at my hand! My skin is whiter than yours, Armand!" But Armand turned and walked away.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. When did things start to change, and how?

2. What does Armand say about the child — and about Désirée?

1. When the baby was about three months old. Visitors looked at the baby in a strange way, and Armand became cold and stopped smiling.

2. He says the child is not white — and that Désirée is not white.

Talk about it:
  • In this story, people think skin colour tells them everything about a person. Why is that idea wrong?

Part 3 — The letter

Désirée wrote to Madame Valmondé: "They say I am not white. Please tell them it is not true." Her mother's answer was short: "My Désirée, come home to your mother who loves you. Come with your child."

Désirée showed the letter to Armand. "Shall I go?" she asked. "Yes, go," he said. "Do you want me to go?" "Yes. I want you to go."

Désirée took her baby in her arms. She did not take the road to her mother's house. She walked across the empty fields to the bayou — the deep, slow water. She did not come back.

Some weeks later, Armand made a big fire behind the house. He burned the baby's little bed. He burned Désirée's dresses and her letters. Then, in a drawer, he found one more letter — an old letter from his own mother to his father. He read: "I thank God that our dear Armand will never know that his mother belongs to the race that is cursed with slavery."

Armand's mother was black. The secret was his.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Madame Valmondé write to Désirée?

2. What did Armand find in the drawer, and what did it say?

1. "Come home to your mother who loves you. Come with your child." She offered Désirée a home.

2. An old letter from his own mother. It said that Armand's mother was black — the secret was his, not Désirée's.

Talk about it:
  • The secret was Armand's, not Désirée's. How does the last letter change how you see everything in the story?

Grammar: look / look like

This whole story is about faces — how people look, and who they look like. In English, there are two patterns, and they are different.

look + adjective — how a person or thing seems: "Désirée looked sad." "The baby looks healthy." "You look tired."
Careful: no "like" before an adjective!
look like + noun — the same appearance as a person or thing: "The baby looks like his father." "L'Abri looked like a sad, dark place."
Question: "What does he look like?" — "He's tall, with brown hair."

1. Finish the sentences

Write look, looks or looked — with or without like.

1. The big house at L'Abri dark and sad.

2. "Look at his little hands!" said Désirée. "He his father!"

3. Madame Valmondé held the baby for a long time. Her face worried.

4. In her thin white dress, Désirée a stone statue.

1. looked (+ adjective: dark and sad)

2. looks like (+ noun: his father)

3. looked (+ adjective: worried)

4. looked like (+ noun: a stone statue)

2. Questions and answers

Finish the two mini-dialogues. Write look, looks, look like or looks like.

1. "What does the baby ?" — "He's beautiful! He his mother — the same eyes."

2. "How does Armand today?" — "Not good. He angry and cold."

1. What does the baby look like? — He looks like his mother.

2. How does Armand look today? — He looks angry and cold.

3. Tell a short part of the story

Write looks, looked, looks like or looked like. Then read the little story out loud.

Madame Valmondé came to visit. The house dark and sad, like always. The baby was growing fast, and he strong and healthy. But when Désirée stood him next to the little servant boy, the two children brothers. Désirée went white. "Look at my hand!" she cried. "My skin whiter than yours, Armand!" At the end of the story, alone by the fire, the proud Armand a man who has lost everything.

looked · looked · looked like · looks · looks like

look + adjective tells us HOW; look like + noun tells us WHO or WHAT.

Going deeper — talk about the big ideas

Now take the story's ideas into the real world. Talk about each one out loud. There are no right answers — say what you think, and say why.

1 · Faces and families

People often say things like "You look like your grandmother!" Why do families love to talk about faces? Is it always a friendly game? Think of examples from families in films or TV.

2 · Judging by looks

In the story, people decide everything about a person from a face. Today, do people still judge others by how they look — clothes, age, height? Where does this happen (shops, work, school)? Is it fair?

3 · A name or a heart?

Armand's old family name is like a treasure for him — more important than the people in his house. Are family names important in your country? What is more important: a good name or a good heart? Why?

4 · Old stories today

This story is from 1893, but people still read it all over the world. Why? What can an old story teach us that the news cannot?
