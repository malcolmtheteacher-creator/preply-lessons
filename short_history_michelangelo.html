Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. A cardinal in Rome decided to commission a statue for his own chapel: he chose the artist, agreed the price in writing, and paid for the marble himself.
to commission (v) — to formally order and pay for a work of art or building to be made. Also a noun: a commission — the job itself.
2. No artist of the age could work without a rich patron — a banker, a duke or a pope who paid the bills, chose the subject and expected loyalty in return.
a patron (n) — a wealthy or powerful person who supports an artist financially and gives them work.
3. He would choose his stone in the quarries, then chisel away at it for months, striking the block with a hammer and a sharpened iron tool until a figure appeared.
to chisel (v) — to cut and shape stone or wood with a sharp hand tool struck by a hammer. Also a noun: a chisel — the tool itself.
4. A fresco must be painted quickly, straight onto wet plaster, because once the wall dries the colour can no longer sink in and the day's work is lost.
a fresco (n) — a wall or ceiling painting made on fresh, wet plaster, so that the colour becomes part of the surface.
5. To reach a ceiling twenty metres above the floor, a wooden platform of poles and planks — the scaffolding — had to be built first and fixed into the walls.
scaffolding (n, uncountable) — a temporary structure of poles and boards put up so that workers can reach high parts of a building.
6. He was a deeply devout man who prayed daily, read the Bible closely, and came to see his last great building as an offering to God rather than a job.
devout (adj) — sincerely and seriously religious; committed to one's faith in practice, not just in name.
7. The two men circled each other for years, each refusing to praise the other's work; the rivalry between them pushed both to attempt things they might otherwise have avoided.
a rivalry (n) — a long competition between two people or groups who each want to outdo the other. Also: a rival (n) — the competitor.
8. The work was slow and painstaking: every hand, every curl of hair and every fold of cloth was finished with enormous care over many months.
painstaking (adj) — done with great care, patience and attention to small detail, however long it takes.
9. On the morning of the ceremony the cloth was pulled away to unveil the statue, and the crowd saw it for the first time.
to unveil (v) — to remove a covering and show something new to the public for the first time.
10. Centuries of candle smoke had darkened the paintings, so a long restoration was carried out to clean them — and the colours that emerged shocked everybody.
restoration (n) — the careful work of cleaning and repairing an old artwork or building to bring it closer to its original state.

The story, in three parts

Part 1 — The boy in the Medici garden

Michelangelo Buonarroti was born in 1475 in a small town near Arezzo, where his father was serving a short term as a local official, and he was brought up in Florence. As an infant he was placed with a wet-nurse in a village of stonecutters outside the city, and he is said to have joked in later life that he had taken in hammers and chisels along with his nurse's milk. The remark is almost certainly too neat to be true — it reaches us through the two contemporaries who wrote about him, both of them building a legend — but it points at something real. He wanted, from very early, to work stone. His father was against it. The Buonarroti considered themselves minor gentry, and to be a craftsman who cut marble for money was, to them, a step down. The boy was beaten for it, and went on drawing anyway.

At thirteen he was apprenticed to Domenico Ghirlandaio, the busiest fresco painter in Florence, whose workshop was covering church walls with crowded, elegant scenes. It was there that he learned how a fresco is actually made: the plaster, the speed, the impossibility of correcting a mistake. He stayed only a short time. Within a year or two he had been noticed and taken into the orbit of Lorenzo de' Medici, the banker-statesman who ruled Florence in all but name and had gathered a collection of antique sculpture in a garden near San Marco. He was given access to it, and to the older sculptor who supervised the young men there. For roughly three years he lived in the Medici household as something between a student and an adopted son. He ate at the family table, listened to poets and philosophers argue over dinner, and copied Greek and Roman marbles until he understood how the ancients had made stone look like breathing flesh.

That garden shaped everything. It gave him a patron of the first rank, a classical education he could never have bought, and a lifelong conviction that sculpture was the highest of the arts and that he himself was a sculptor — not a painter, whatever anyone else might want. It also gave him his broken nose: a fellow pupil, provoked once too often by Michelangelo's sharp tongue, struck him in the face, and the flattened nose is visible in every portrait of him afterwards. Then the world he had been let into collapsed. Lorenzo died in 1492; two years later the Medici were driven out of Florence, and the city fell under the influence of the fierce preacher Savonarola, whose sermons against luxury and worldly art stayed with Michelangelo for the rest of his life. The young sculptor left for Bologna, and then for Rome, carrying with him a habit that never left him: he described himself, in letter after letter, simply as Michelangelo, sculptor.

1. He was apprenticed to the fresco painter Ghirlandaio and learned wall painting; then he was taken into Lorenzo de' Medici's household and studied antique sculpture in the Medici garden, among poets and scholars.

2. It is attributed to Michelangelo himself but reaches us only through early biographers who were building a legend around him — it is a good story, not reliable evidence.

Part 2 — The block, the tomb and the ceiling

In Rome, still in his early twenties, he was commissioned by a French cardinal to carve a Pietà for a chapel in St Peter's: the dead Christ across his mother's knees, cut from a single block of Carrara marble and polished until the stone looked soft. It made him famous overnight, and it is the only work he ever signed. According to his early biographers, he overheard visitors attributing it to a rival from Milan, returned at night with a lamp and cut his name across the band over the Virgin's breast — then, ashamed of the vanity, never signed anything again. Back in Florence, he was offered a challenge nobody else wanted: an enormous block of marble quarried decades earlier, roughly worked by another sculptor, spoiled, and left in a cathedral yard so long that Florentines simply called it the Giant. Out of that awkward, damaged stone he chiselled the David, and when it was unveiled in 1504 the city set it not in the cathedral but outside the seat of government, a naked shepherd boy turned into a civic symbol of a small republic that had survived its giants.

Then came Pope Julius II, a patron as forceful as Michelangelo himself. In 1505 the artist was summoned to Rome and given a commission of extraordinary ambition: a free-standing tomb for the pope, crowded with dozens of marble figures. It should have been the work of his life. Instead it became what he is reported to have called the tragedy of the tomb. Julius lost interest, the money stopped, the design was cut down again and again, and the contract was renegotiated with the pope's heirs for the next forty years. What finally survives is a much smaller monument in a Roman church, dominated by a seated Moses, horned in the medieval fashion, muscles gathered as if he were about to stand. It is one of the greatest sculptures ever made, and Michelangelo regarded the whole project as a humiliation — decades of painstaking work and negotiation for a fraction of what had been promised.

Worse, from his point of view, Julius diverted him to paint. Between roughly 1508 and 1512 he was set to cover the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel, a commission he did not want, in a medium he insisted was not his. He designed his own scaffolding, bridged across the chapel high above the floor, and worked from it standing, head tilted back and arm above him — not lying down, despite the picture most of us carry. He wrote a comic, self-mocking poem about what it was doing to his body: the beard pointing at heaven, the chin driven into the chest, the paint dripping onto his face. Above him grew the Creation, the Flood, the drunkenness of Noah, and around them the prophets and sibyls, hundreds of figures in all, painted in fresco with a boldness of colour and a physical force nobody had attempted on such a scale.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was unusual about the block of marble the David was carved from?

2. Correct this common belief: "Michelangelo painted the Sistine ceiling lying on his back."

1. It was a huge piece of Carrara marble quarried decades before and already roughly cut and spoiled by an earlier sculptor, then abandoned; Florentines called it the Giant. Michelangelo carved his David out of that damaged block, and it was unveiled in 1504 as a symbol of the republic.

2. He didn't. He worked standing on scaffolding of his own design, with his head bent back and his arm raised above him — exhausting, but not lying down.

Discuss:
  • The ceiling was forced on an unwilling artist by a patron who wanted something else. Does great work more often come from freedom, or from pressure and constraint?

Part 3 — The dome, and the making of a genius

He came back to that chapel more than twenty years later, an older and darker man, to paint the Last Judgement across the altar wall. The Reformation had split Christendom; Rome itself had been sacked; and the new fresco showed it — a churning wall of naked bodies rising and falling around a merciless Christ, with the artist's own features widely thought to be worked into the flayed skin held by St Bartholomew. The nudity caused a scandal almost at once, and after the Council of Trent draperies were painted over parts of it by another artist, mocked ever afterwards as the breeches-maker. In old age Michelangelo turned to architecture. From 1546, past seventy, he was made architect of St Peter's, and it is his design that governs the great dome. He refused payment, treating the work as a duty owed to God rather than a commission, and although he did not live to see the dome completed, it was built substantially to his plan. He was devout, austere and careless of comfort; he quarrelled with popes, patrons and assistants; and he wrote hundreds of poems, barely known in his lifetime.

The rivalries mattered too. He and Leonardo da Vinci, a generation apart and temperamentally opposed, were once set to paint battle scenes on facing walls of the same hall in Florence; neither was finished, and the two men seem to have disliked each other thoroughly. Later the young Raphael, charming where Michelangelo was difficult, absorbed what he saw on the Sistine scaffolding and became a rival at the papal court. Michelangelo outlived them both by decades. He died in Rome in February 1564, a few weeks short of his eighty-ninth birthday, and his body was taken back to Florence, where it was buried in the church of Santa Croce. Within a few years his life had been written up twice, most influentially by Giorgio Vasari, who called him divine and used the word terribilità — an awe close to fear — for the effect of his work. That is the moment when something shifts. The artist stops being a skilled craftsman with a contract and becomes a solitary genius, unrivalled, answerable to nobody, touched by something not quite human.

We are still living inside that idea, and it is worth asking what it costs. Michelangelo was a working man who signed contracts, argued about payment, ran a team of assistants and had his marble hauled by teams of oxen; the myth quietly deletes all of that. The arguments continue in other forms. Between the early 1980s and the 1990s a long restoration stripped centuries of soot and glue from the ceiling, and the colours underneath were so bright that some art historians protested that a final layer of shadow, added by the artist himself, had been washed away with the dirt; others answered that only later grime and repair had gone. The dispute has never been settled. Meanwhile millions of visitors a year pass through the chapel, their breath and dust a slow threat managed by air-conditioning and crowd control. The ceiling is looked at more than any painting on earth, and seen, perhaps, less carefully than almost any.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Michelangelo do in his last years, and on what terms?

2. What is the argument about the cleaning of the Sistine ceiling?

1. From 1546, in his seventies, he was made architect of St Peter's and designed its dome. He refused payment, regarding the work as a religious duty; the dome was completed after his death, largely to his design.

2. The restoration of the 1980s and 1990s removed centuries of soot. Some scholars argued that final shadowing applied by Michelangelo himself was removed along with the dirt; others held that only later grime and repairs were taken off. The question is genuinely disputed.

Discuss:
  • Calling someone a genius makes them fascinating — and makes their working life invisible. Is the myth of the lone genius worth keeping, or does it do more harm than good?

Grammar — the passive with by-agents and attribution

Art history runs on two questions: what was made, and who made it. English answers both with the passive. When we know the maker but want the artwork in focus, we name them with by: the David was carved by Michelangelo, the ceiling was commissioned by Julius II. When we are less sure, English offers a family of careful hedges — is attributed to, is thought to have been, is said to have — that let you report a claim without swallowing it.

How it is formed:
1. Passive + by-agent: object + the right tense of be + past participle + by + the doer.
• "The Pietà was carved by Michelangelo in his twenties."
• "The tomb had been commissioned by Pope Julius II."
2. Attribution structures:
• is/are attributed to + person: "The poem is attributed to Michelangelo."
• is/are thought (believed, said, known) to + infinitive: "He is said to be difficult." (now) / "He is said to have been difficult." (then)
• It is thought/said that + clause: "It is said that he signed the Pietà at night."
When we choose each one:
• Use the plain passive when the doer is unknown or irrelevant: "The block had been abandoned for decades."
• Add by when the doer is new, surprising or the real point: "The draperies were added by another painter." Don't add by just to fill space.
• Use attribution when the evidence is thin, contested or second-hand: "The remark is thought to have been invented by his biographers." It is the honest, academic register — and how historians talk on the podcast.
• Watch the time: is said to be = still true now; is said to have been = true in the past.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. He called himself a sculptor and resented being made to paint — yet the ceiling is what the world knows him for. Is an artist the best judge of their own work?
