Encounter: Christian Baptism and Confirmation

Introduction: Why Water? Why Oil?

Baptism and confirmation are two of the most important sacraments in Christianity. Both involve water, oil (chrism), and the community. But Christians disagree fundamentally about who should be baptised and when. Some baptise infants; others baptise only those old enough to declare their own faith. This lesson explores both approaches and asks: What does baptism mean? What does it achieve spiritually? How does confirmation mark a transition to adult faith?

Part One: Baptism

What is Baptism? The Basics

Baptism is a Christian sacrament involving water and words of commitment or promise. The person is baptised "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19). Water washes away sin, marks entry into the Church, and symbolises new life and resurrection. However, Christian tradition is divided on who should be baptised and when.

Infant Baptism: Catholic, Anglican, and Orthodox Traditions

The Practice

In infant baptism, a baby (usually a few weeks old) is brought to church by parents. The child is too young to understand or decide for themselves, so parents and godparents make baptismal promises on the child's behalf. Water is poured or sprinkled on the baby's head (not full immersion). The sign of the cross is made. Oil (chrism) is used to anoint the head. A white garment is given, symbolising purity. A candle is lit from the Paschal candle (the great candle of resurrection). The baby is named publicly.

The Theology

The theological reasoning: babies are born with original sin (the spiritual damage inherited from Adam and Eve's disobedience in Genesis). Baptism cleanses this original sin and welcomes the child into God's grace. The parents promise to raise the child in the Christian faith. God's grace is given unconditionally; the child doesn't need to earn it or understand it. Later, at confirmation (teenage years), the person will choose to affirm the faith that was chosen for them as infants.

Key Symbolism in Infant Baptism: Water = cleansing from original sin and washing into new life. The sign of the cross = marking as Christian. White garment = purity and innocence. Oil/chrism = anointing as priest, prophet, and king in Christ. Paschal candle = light of Christ and resurrection. Godparents = community witnesses and spiritual guides.

Believer's Baptism: Baptist and Pentecostal Traditions

The Practice

In believer's baptism, the person is old enough (usually teenage or adult) to make their own conscious decision to follow Jesus and declare their faith publicly. Full immersion in water is the standard—the person is completely submerged (dunked under), then brought up again. This usually happens in a river, lake, or baptismal pool. There is no infant baptism; each person chooses baptism as their own declaration of faith. The promise comes from the believer themselves, not from parents.

The Theology

The theological reasoning: baptism is a public declaration of personal faith. It must be a conscious choice. You cannot inherit faith from your parents; each person must choose it for themselves. Going under the water symbolises dying to the old life of sin. Rising from the water symbolises resurrection to new life in Christ. This is based on Jesus's own baptism by John the Baptist (Matthew 3:13-17), where Jesus—as an adult—publicly committed to his mission. Baptism is believers' entry into covenant with God through their own choice.

Key Symbolism in Believer's Baptism: Immersion = death to old life and resurrection to new life (Romans 6:3-4). The person going under = dying with Christ to sin. Rising from water = rising with Christ to new life. Public declaration = commitment witnessed by the community. Personal choice = faith is not inherited but chosen by the individual.

Scripture and Theological Significance

Key Scripture—Matthew 28:19-20: "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you; and lo, I am with you always, to the close of the age." (Jesus's Great Commission)

Key Scripture—Acts 2:38: "Peter said to them, 'Repent, and be baptised every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins; and you shall receive the gift of the Holy Spirit.'" (Baptism as response to repentance and faith)

Key Scripture—Romans 6:3-4: "Do you not know that all of us who have been baptised into Christ Jesus were baptised into his death? We were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, so that as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life." (Symbolism of death and resurrection)

What Baptism Accomplishes

Across traditions, baptism is believed to accomplish these things:

  • Cleansing from sin: Original sin (Catholic view) or personal sins (Baptist view) are washed away by water.
  • Entry into the Church: The person becomes a member of the Body of Christ, the Christian community.
  • Reception of the Holy Spirit: God's Holy Spirit comes upon the baptised person, empowering them for Christian life.
  • New birth: The person is "born again" (John 3:3-7)—dying to the old self and rising to new spiritual life.
  • Covenant with God: A sacred bond is established between God and the believer (or between God and the parents/godparents in infant baptism).
  • Public declaration: The community witnesses and affirms the person's new status as Christian.

Denominational Approaches to Baptism

Roman Catholicism

Infant baptism is the norm. Water is poured or sprinkled. Chrism oil is used. Original sin is cleansed. The child is washed into God's grace. Later, confirmation (around age 12-13) allows the person to affirm their faith personally.

Anglicanism (Church of England)

Similar to Catholicism—infant baptism by sprinkling. However, Anglicanism accepts that some churches practise believer's baptism. Confirmation in teenage years is important for public affirmation of faith and reception of the Holy Spirit through the bishop's laying on of hands.

Orthodox Christianity

Infant baptism by triple immersion. The child is fully submerged three times (Father, Son, Holy Spirit). Chrism anointing follows immediately. Confirmation (Chrismation) happens at the same time, not years later. The child is fully initiated into the church immediately.

Baptist Churches

Believer's baptism only. Full immersion in water. The person must be old enough to make their own conscious profession of faith. No infant baptism. Baptism follows conversion and commitment to Jesus as personal Lord and Saviour.

Pentecostal Churches

Believer's baptism by full immersion. Strong emphasis on baptism in the Holy Spirit (often marked by speaking in tongues). Baptism is a dramatic declaration of faith and entry into charismatic Christian experience.

Methodist and Presbyterian

Many practise infant baptism, though some allow believer's baptism. Water is often sprinkled or poured rather than immersion. Confirmation in teenage years is valued, though not always understood as a sacrament.

Part Two: Christian Confirmation

What is Confirmation?

Confirmation is a sacrament in which a teenager or young adult publicly affirms the Christian faith that (in many cases) was chosen for them by their parents when they were baptised as infants. In confirmation, the person makes their own conscious choice: "Yes, I choose to be Christian." They are anointed with chrism oil and strengthened by the Holy Spirit. In Catholic and Anglican churches, the bishop lays hands on the candidate. In Orthodox churches, confirmation (Chrismation) happens at baptism itself, not years later.

Catholic and Anglican Confirmation: The Teenage Transition

When and Where?

Confirmation typically occurs when a person is 12-14 years old (Catholic) or 13-16 years old (Anglican). It is a significant church ceremony, usually performed by a bishop. The candidate is prepared through a period of religious education and study. They choose a confirmation name, often the name of a saint they admire or feel connected to spiritually.

The Ceremony

The bishop anoints the candidate with chrism oil on the forehead while laying hands on them. The words are: "Be sealed with the gift of the Holy Spirit." The candidate may recite a personal statement of faith. Godparents or sponsors are present, witnessing the commitment. The community celebrates. It is both a solemn religious moment and a rite of passage into teenage years and adult Christian responsibility.

The Theology of Confirmation: In infant baptism, the parents chose Christianity for the child. The child received God's grace without understanding. Now, as a teenager capable of reason and choice, confirmation allows the person to say: "I choose this faith for myself. I affirm what my parents began. I commit to living as a Christian." Confirmation is the transition from childhood faith (inherited, received passively) to adult faith (chosen, embraced actively).

The Gifts of the Holy Spirit in Confirmation

When a person is confirmed, the church teaches that they receive or are strengthened in the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit. These are mentioned in Isaiah 11:2-3 and represent spiritual strengths for Christian living:

  1. Wisdom: The ability to see God's purpose and direction in life.
  2. Understanding: Deep insight into Christian teaching and faith.
  3. Counsel: Sound judgment and ability to advise others wisely.
  4. Fortitude (Courage): Strength to live out faith even when it's difficult or unpopular.
  1. Knowledge: Awareness of God's presence and action in the world.
  2. Piety (Reverence): Respect and love for God; desire to worship and pray.
  3. Fear of the Lord: Not terror, but awe and reverence; sense of God's majesty and holiness.

Confirmation and Van Gennep's Model

Confirmation is a perfect example of van Gennep's rite of passage model:

Separation: The young person is "separated" from ordinary childhood life through preparation classes and spiritual education. They step out of their regular routine to focus on faith.

Liminality: During the confirmation class period (months or years), the person is in a threshold state—not quite a child, not yet an adult Christian. They are learning what adult faith means. They are becoming.

Incorporation: The confirmation ceremony is the moment of reintegration. The bishop anoints them. The community witnesses. They emerge with a new status: a confirmed Christian, ready to take adult responsibility for their faith, to receive communion more fully, and to participate actively in parish life.

Orthodox Approach to Confirmation (Chrismation)

In Orthodox Christianity, the understanding differs. An infant is baptised (by triple immersion), and immediately after, chrismation happens. The oil (chrism) is applied to the forehead, eyes, ears, nostrils, lips, chest, hands, and feet. There is no separate teenage confirmation because the child is fully initiated into the church from baptism. However, as they grow, they take on fuller participation in church life—receiving communion regularly and eventually taking adult roles. The transition is less marked by ceremony and more by gradual deepening of practice.

Why Confirmation Matters: The Bigger Picture

Confirmation represents a crucial moment in religious development. It marks the shift from religion as something the family does for you (childhood) to religion as something you choose for yourself (adulthood). A teenager standing before a bishop, choosing a saint's name, being anointed with sacred oil—this is saying: "I am no longer a Christian simply because my parents are. I am Christian because I choose to be." This is a profound personal claim on faith. It's the difference between inherited religion and chosen religion. And it happens at exactly the right moment—in the teenage years when young people are developing their own identities and values, asking questions about who they are and what they believe.

Comparing Christian Baptism to Other Rites of Passage

How do Christian baptism and confirmation compare to the Hindu and Islamic rites you studied earlier?

Upanayana (Hindu Sacred Thread)

Similar: Both mark entry into a new spiritual phase and increased religious responsibility. Both involve learning sacred knowledge. Both are celebrated by the community. Both involve preparation and ritual objects (thread vs. water/oil).

Different: Upanayana is for young men only; Christian baptism includes all genders. Upanayana is about beginning religious education; baptism is about cleansing from sin and entering the church. Upanayana is one-time; Christian confirmation is separate from baptism.

Aqiqah (Islamic Birth Ceremony)

Similar: Both mark entry into a religious community. Both occur early in life (Muslim childhood, Christian infancy). Both involve a meal/celebration and community witnesses. Both are carried out by parents on behalf of a child.

Different: Aqiqah is a naming and sacrifice; Christian infant baptism is spiritual cleansing. Aqiqah is one event; Christian tradition has both baptism and confirmation. Aqiqah is specifically a Muslim coming-into-community; Christian baptism is seen as salvation.

Jewish Bar/Bat Mitzvah

Similar: Both confirmation and Bar/Bat Mitzvah occur in teenage years. Both mark transition from childhood to adult religious status. Both involve public ceremony and community recognition. Both require preparation and demonstrate knowledge/commitment.

Different: Bar/Bat Mitzvah focuses on Torah reading skill; confirmation focuses on spiritual choice. Bar/Bat Mitzvah is a Jewish community recognition; confirmation is a Christian sacrament. Bar/Bat Mitzvah happens once and marks legal adulthood; confirmation affirms faith chosen in infancy.

Evaluate: The Meaning of Infant Baptism and Believer's Baptism

The Debate: Is Infant Baptism Meaningful?

Different Christian traditions have fundamentally different views on baptism. One question stands at the centre: Is baptising an infant—who cannot understand or choose—a meaningful religious action? Or is baptism only meaningful when a person is old enough to understand and choose for themselves? Both traditions defend their approach seriously.

Consider both perspectives carefully:

Argument 1: Infant Baptism is Deeply Meaningful and Theologically Sound

The Catholic and Anglican view holds that infant baptism is not just meaningful—it's essential. Infants are born with original sin inherited from Adam and Eve. They need cleansing and God's grace immediately, not when they're old enough to understand. Grace is a gift from God; it's not something you have to earn or understand to receive. God loves all people, including babies, and offers salvation unconditionally. The parents and godparents make promises on the child's behalf, but God's action is real. Later, at confirmation, the child affirms what was done for them. This respects both God's initiative (grace is freely given) and human choice (confirmation allows personal affirmation). The community—the church—raises the child in faith. Baptism incorporates the child into a loving community that will nurture their growth in faith.

Argument 2: Believer's Baptism Alone Respects Personal Choice and Faith

The Baptist and Pentecostal view argues that infant baptism, however well-intentioned, is theologically problematic. Faith is a personal choice—you cannot inherit it. Baptism in the New Testament always follows faith and repentance (Acts 2:38). It is a response to the Gospel. A baby cannot make this response. Baptising an infant treats baptism as a cultural tradition rather than a genuine spiritual commitment. It suggests you can be Christian by accident of birth, not by personal choice. Moreover, if someone is baptised as an infant, they may grow up thinking they're automatically Christian without ever making a real personal commitment to Jesus. Believer's baptism ensures the person is old enough to understand what they're doing, to consciously declare "I choose to follow Jesus," and to make a real, personal covenant with God. This respects human free will and ensures genuine commitment, not inherited ritual.

Your Evaluation

Both traditions make serious theological arguments. Consider:

  • Is grace something God gives freely without our understanding (supporting infant baptism), or is it something we must consciously accept (supporting believer's baptism)?
  • Can parents make spiritual promises on behalf of their children, or must each person make their own choices?
  • Is baptism more about what God does (grace, cleansing) or about what we do (commitment, choice)?
  • What does Jesus's own baptism as an adult suggest about the right age for baptism?
  • How important is personal, conscious choice in religious commitment?

Write a reasoned conclusion addressing which view you find more theologically sound, and why. Acknowledge the strengths of both positions before explaining your reasoning.

Key Terms Summary

Baptism
A Christian sacrament involving water, cleansing from sin, and entry into the Church. Administered in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
Infant Baptism
Baptism of a baby by water poured or sprinkled, with parents and godparents making promises. Practised by Catholics, Anglicans, and Orthodox. Believed to cleanse original sin.
Believer's Baptism
Baptism of a person old enough to make their own conscious profession of faith in Jesus. Full immersion in water is standard. Practised by Baptists and Pentecostals.
Immersion
Full submersion of a person under water during baptism. Symbolises death to old life and resurrection to new life in Christ (Romans 6:3-4).
Chrism / Chrism Oil
Sacred oil used to anoint a person during baptism and confirmation. Symbolises anointing as priest, prophet, and king in Christ, and the gift of the Holy Spirit.
Original Sin
In Catholic theology, the spiritual damage inherited by all humans from Adam and Eve's disobedience. Infant baptism is believed to cleanse original sin.
Confirmation
A Christian sacrament (Catholic/Anglican) in which a teenager publicly affirms the faith chosen for them at infant baptism. Involves anointing with chrism oil and laying on of hands by a bishop.
Confirmation Name
A name, often of a saint, chosen by a person being confirmed. It symbolises their personal commitment to Christian faith and their connection to a spiritual guide or model.
Godparent
An adult who witnesses and makes promises on behalf of a child during infant baptism. Traditionally a spiritual guide and mentor throughout the child's Christian upbringing.
Paschal Candle
A large candle representing Christ's resurrection, lit during Easter celebrations. During infant baptism, a baptismal candle is lit from it, symbolising the child's entrance into Christ's light.
Holy Spirit
In Christian theology, the third person of the Trinity. Received or strengthened in baptism and confirmation, empowering believers for Christian life and witness.
Sacrament
In Christian tradition, a sacred ritual act believed to convey or symbolise God's grace. Baptism and confirmation are two of the seven sacraments in Catholic/Orthodox Christianity.
Repentance
Turning away from sin and turning towards God. In believer's baptism, repentance and faith precede baptism as a personal decision.
The Body of Christ
A metaphor for the Christian Church as the body of which Jesus is the head. Baptism is understood as incorporation into this spiritual body.
Church (Ecclesia)
The community of Christian believers. Baptism marks entry into the Church as a member of God's people. Can mean a local congregation or the universal Christian community.
Central Insights from This Lesson: Baptism is central to Christian faith—a washing away of sin and entry into God's community. But Christians differ profoundly on who should be baptised and when. Infant baptism reflects a view of God's grace as unconditional and freely given; confirmation allows later personal choice. Believer's baptism emphasises personal choice and conscious commitment from the start. Confirmation as a teenage rite of passage perfectly illustrates van Gennep's model: it's the moment when childhood faith (received) becomes adult faith (chosen). Understanding these differences reveals deep theological questions about grace, free will, childhood, and what it means to become Christian.
