Encounter: Christian Baptism and Confirmation

What is Baptism?

Baptism is the most important Christian initiation rite. The person is baptised "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" using water. It marks entry into the Church and symbolises new life. But Christians disagree about a big question: should babies be baptised, or only people old enough to choose for themselves?

Infant Baptism vs. Believer's Baptism

Infant Baptism

Practised by Catholics, Anglicans, and Orthodox Christians. A baby is brought to church by parents. Water is poured or sprinkled on the baby's head. Parents and godparents make promises on the child's behalf. The baby is anointed with chrism (sacred oil), given a white garment for purity, and a candle is lit from the Paschal candle (representing Christ's resurrection).

Why? Catholics believe babies are born with original sin inherited from Adam and Eve. Baptism cleanses this sin and welcomes the child into God's grace. The child doesn't need to understand — God's love is given freely.

Believer's Baptism

Practised by Baptists and Pentecostals. Only people old enough to understand and choose are baptised. The person is fully immersed (submerged) in water — going under represents dying to the old life of sin, and rising up represents resurrection to new life in Christ.

Why? Faith must be a personal choice. You can't inherit it from your parents. Jesus himself was baptised as an adult by John the Baptist (Matthew 3:13-17). Baptism should be a conscious public declaration: "I choose to follow Jesus."

How Different Denominations Practise Baptism

Catholic

Infant baptism by pouring water. Chrism oil used. Original sin cleansed. Confirmation later (age 12-13) to affirm faith personally.

Anglican

Infant baptism by sprinkling. Similar to Catholic practice. Confirmation in teenage years through the bishop's laying on of hands.

Orthodox

Infant baptism by triple immersion (three times for Father, Son, Holy Spirit). Chrismation (confirmation) happens immediately — the child is fully initiated at once.

Baptist

Believer's baptism only. Full immersion. The person must be old enough to make their own profession of faith. No infant baptism.

Pentecostal

Believer's baptism by full immersion. Strong emphasis on receiving the Holy Spirit. Baptism is a dramatic public declaration of faith.

Confirmation: Choosing Your Own Faith

What Happens?

In Catholic and Anglican churches, confirmation happens in the teenage years (ages 12-16). A bishop anoints the person with chrism oil and lays hands on them, saying "Be sealed with the gift of the Holy Spirit." The person may choose a confirmation name — often a saint they admire. The community witnesses their commitment.

Why Does It Matter?

At infant baptism, parents chose Christianity for their child. At confirmation, the teenager says: "I choose this faith for myself." It's the shift from inherited religion (what your family does) to chosen religion (what you personally believe). This is a powerful moment of personal identity and commitment.

Key idea: Confirmation is a sacrament — a sacred ritual believed to convey God's grace. The person receives or is strengthened in the gifts of the Holy Spirit, preparing them for adult Christian life.

Comparing Christian Rites to Other Traditions

Upanayana (Hindu Sacred Thread)

Similar Both mark entry into a new spiritual phase with increased religious responsibility. Both are celebrated by the community with special ritual objects.

Different Upanayana is for young men only; baptism includes everyone. Upanayana begins religious education; baptism cleanses from sin.

Aqiqah (Islamic Birth Rite)

Similar Both mark a baby's entry into a religious community. Both are carried out by parents on behalf of a child too young to choose.

Different Aqiqah involves sacrifice and naming; infant baptism involves water and spiritual cleansing. Christianity adds confirmation later; Islam has no equivalent second ceremony.

Bar/Bat Mitzvah (Judaism)

Similar Both confirmation and Bar/Bat Mitzvah happen in the teenage years. Both mark the shift from childhood to adult religious responsibility.

Different Bar/Bat Mitzvah centres on Torah reading and demonstrating knowledge; confirmation centres on a personal declaration of faith and anointing with oil.

Nikah (Islamic Marriage)

Similar Both are witnessed by the community and involve promises or declarations. Both mark a change in status.

Different Baptism marks entry into faith; Nikah marks entry into married life. Baptism can happen to babies; Nikah requires informed adult consent.

Evaluate: Is Infant Baptism Meaningful?

The Debate

Is baptising a baby — who cannot understand or choose — a meaningful religious action? Or is baptism only meaningful when a person consciously decides for themselves?

Perspective 1: Infant Baptism is Essential

Catholics and Anglicans argue that babies need God's grace immediately — they shouldn't have to wait until they're old enough to understand. Grace is a free gift from God, not something you earn. Parents and godparents promise to raise the child in faith, and later at confirmation the person chooses for themselves. This respects both God's unconditional love and human free will.

Perspective 2: Only Believer's Baptism is Real Baptism

Baptists and Pentecostals argue that faith must be a personal choice. In the New Testament, baptism always follows repentance and belief (Acts 2:38). A baby cannot repent or believe. Baptising an infant treats Christianity as something you inherit by accident of birth, not something you genuinely choose. Believer's baptism ensures real, conscious commitment.

Perspective 3: Both Approaches Have Value

Some argue both traditions capture something important. Infant baptism shows that God's love comes first — before we can do anything to deserve it. Believer's baptism shows that faith must eventually become personal. Perhaps the combination of infant baptism followed by confirmation actually does both: God's grace given freely, then the person's own choice added later.

Question 1: What is the main difference between infant baptism and believer's baptism?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: In infant baptism (Catholic, Anglican, Orthodox), a baby is baptised with water poured or sprinkled, and parents make promises on their behalf. In believer's baptism (Baptist, Pentecostal), only people old enough to choose are baptised, by full immersion in water.

Question 2: What do the water, chrism oil, and white garment symbolise in infant baptism?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Water = cleansing from original sin and new life. Chrism oil = anointing with the Holy Spirit. White garment = purity of the newly baptised child. Together they show the child is washed clean, blessed by God, and welcomed into Christ's light.

Question 3: What does full immersion symbolise in believer's baptism?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Going under the water symbolises dying to the old life of sin. Rising from the water symbolises resurrection to new life in Christ. This is based on Romans 6:3-4 and reflects Jesus's teaching about being "born again."

Question 4: What is confirmation, and why is it important as a rite of passage?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Confirmation is a sacrament in the teenage years where a person publicly affirms the faith their parents chose for them at infant baptism. A bishop anoints them with chrism oil. It marks the shift from childhood faith (received from parents) to adult faith (chosen personally).

Key Terms

Baptism
A Christian sacrament using water to mark entry into the Church and cleansing from sin. Done "in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit."
Infant Baptism
Baptism of a baby, with parents and godparents making promises. Practised by Catholics, Anglicans, and Orthodox Christians. Believed to cleanse original sin.
Believer's Baptism
Baptism of a person old enough to make their own declaration of faith. Full immersion in water. Practised by Baptists and Pentecostals.
Immersion
Being fully submerged under water during baptism. Symbolises death to old life and resurrection to new life in Christ.
Chrism
Sacred oil used to anoint a person during baptism and confirmation. Represents the gift of the Holy Spirit.
Original Sin
In Catholic theology, the spiritual damage all humans inherit from Adam and Eve. Infant baptism is believed to cleanse it.
Confirmation
A sacrament where a teenager publicly affirms the faith chosen for them at infant baptism. Involves anointing with chrism and the bishop's laying on of hands.
Godparent
An adult who witnesses infant baptism and makes promises on the child's behalf. A spiritual guide throughout the child's upbringing.
Sacrament
A sacred ritual believed to convey God's grace. Baptism and confirmation are two of the seven sacraments in Catholic and Orthodox Christianity.
Holy Spirit
The third person of the Trinity in Christian belief. Received or strengthened in baptism and confirmation, empowering believers for Christian life.
Central insight: Christians agree that baptism is essential — but disagree profoundly about who should be baptised and when. Infant baptism reflects God's grace given freely; believer's baptism reflects personal choice and commitment. Confirmation bridges the gap: the teenager affirms what their parents began. Understanding these differences reveals deep questions about grace, free will, and what it means to become Christian.
