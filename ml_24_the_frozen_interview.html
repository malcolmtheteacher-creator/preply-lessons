The Frozen Interview

Forty minutes you've prepared a fortnight for — and eight minutes in, the man deciding your future stops moving, mouth open, mid-question. Now what?

The Scene 🎬

You've done everything right. Clean shirt, tidy background, notes just out of shot, the tricky answers rehearsed until they sound unrehearsed. It's four o'clock on a Friday, the job is genuinely a good one, and Frank — who runs the team you'd be joining — has just started asking you the question you were hoping for.

And then he stops. Not pauses — stops. His face is a still photograph of a man halfway through a word, one eye shut, hand raised somewhere near his ear. The little bar in the corner has gone from three green blocks to one grey one. You have no idea whether he can hear you, whether you're frozen too, or whether he's been talking cheerfully to a photograph of you for the last twenty seconds.

Nothing you rehearsed covers this. But the interview hasn't stopped — it's just moved. What Frank is now finding out about you isn't how you handle the Renner project question. It's how you handle a mess you didn't cause, in front of someone you want to impress, with no face to read.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. What's the worst technical failure you've had in a call that mattered? What did you actually say in the moment — and what do you wish you'd said?
  2. When a call freezes, some people go silent and wait, some start apologising immediately, and some pretend they can hear perfectly well and hope. Which are you? Which of the three annoys you most in other people?

Who's who

You
The Candidate 💼

Prepared for every question except this one. You want the job, you want to sound unbothered, and you badly don't want to spend your forty minutes saying "sorry, can you hear me?" into a grey square.

The other voice
Frank, the Interviewer 🧊

He's interviewing you for the job, and his face has just frozen mid-question. He's not unkind — he's just busy, slightly harassed, working from a spare room, and about as good at this as anyone else is.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The connection breaks more than once, and each time somebody could be blamed. Who does the candidate actually blame — and what does that do to Frank?

2. They end up not doing a video interview at all. Who suggested the change, and what did they add to stop it landing as a demand?

3. After the two of them talk over each other, the candidate doesn't simply launch into an answer. What do they do first, and why does it help Frank?

1. Nobody, and then themselves. The first break is described as something that happened rather than something Frank did, and when Frank starts blaming his own spare room, the candidate takes it off him and puts it on their own end instead. Frank stops apologising and stops fiddling, which is the point — one of you worrying about it is quite enough.
2. The candidate did, but they offered it as a question rather than an announcement, and said they were happy either way, which leaves Frank free to say no. He's the one with the job to give, so the decision stays his even though the idea was theirs.
3. They say where the conversation had got to and name the question Frank had asked, so Frank doesn't have to remember it himself. After a mess, whoever puts the thread back in the other person's hand is doing them a favour — and it makes the candidate look calm rather than lost.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the moment the line lets you down and you still have to hold the conversation together. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. All of these live or die on how light you keep them. The words "can you still hear me?" said tightly, quickly, with a rising edge, tell the other person that this is a disaster and they should feel bad; the same words said slowly, almost cheerfully, tell them it's just weather and you're not troubled. Drop your voice, slow down, and sound faintly amused rather than faintly desperate — that's the whole trick.

🧊 Saying the obvious thing out loud

Phrase 1

His face has stopped, mid-word. Somebody has to name it, and it may as well be you. Which opener names the problem without making it his fault and without sounding cross?

A. "You're frozen."
B. "Sorry — I think I've lost you for a second there."
C. "Your video's gone again."
B — "Sorry — I think I've lost you for a second there."
A and C both put the fault on him, which means he now has a small problem to solve while you watch. B does the opposite: you have lost him, so the trouble is yours, not his. "I think" hedges it in case you're wrong, and "for a second" makes it tiny and temporary rather than the start of something. The "sorry" isn't an apology for anything — it's simply what we put in front of an interruption in British English so that it stops being one.
Phrase 2

The screen is still. You don't yet know whether he's frozen only on your side, or whether you've both dropped and he's staring at a photograph of you. Which line finds that out fastest while sounding relaxed?

A. "Hello? Hello? Are you there?"
B. "I think your internet's gone."
C. "Can you still hear me, or is it just me?"
C — "Can you still hear me, or is it just me?"
A is just noise and gives him nothing to answer, and B guesses at a cause you can't possibly know yet. C asks the one question that actually sorts it out, because his answer tells you which side the trouble is on. And "or is it just me?" is the useful half: it offers to be the one with the problem, so nobody has to defend themselves before you've even started.
Phrase 3

He's back, but the last thing you heard was his voice chopped into flat metallic pieces — the adjective English uses here comes from the machines that are built to look and talk like people. Complete it: "You went a bit ___ — would you mind saying that again?"

"You went a bit robotic — would you mind saying that again?"
"Robotic" describes a voice that has been broken up and flattened by a poor connection until it sounds machine-made rather than human. It's worth having, because it explains precisely what you heard, which is far more useful to him than "you were breaking up" — he now knows it's the sound, not the picture. "Would you mind" then turns the repeat into a small favour you're asking rather than an instruction, and "a bit" keeps the whole thing minor.

🔧 Taking the problem off them

Phrase 4

Video uses far more of a connection than sound does, so dropping yours is the quickest thing to try. Offer to do it — in one short sentence, presented as an experiment rather than a sacrifice, and without making a production of it. Say it out loud, then type it.

"I'll turn my camera off and see if that helps."
Notice two things. "I'll" means you're doing it now, not asking whether you may — you've taken the decision away from him, which saves everyone half a minute of "no, don't worry". And "see if that helps" makes it a test rather than a promise, so if it changes nothing you haven't failed at anything. The offer is also quietly generous: you're the one giving something up.
Phrase 5

He's started blaming his own house, his own wifi, his own spare room — and he's now more worried about the connection than about you. You want to take that off him in one line, without turning it into a long confession about your own broadband. Which version does it?

A. "I'm so sorry, my internet is genuinely awful, it's been like this for weeks, I keep meaning to ring them."
B. "I think it's my end, not yours."
C. "It could be either of us really, hard to say."
B — "I think it's my end, not yours."
Your "end" is simply your side of the connection — your flat, your wifi, your machine — and it's the ordinary word for it. B works because it's four seconds long and it finishes the argument: he can stop apologising and get back to the interview. A takes the blame but then keeps talking, so the connection is still the subject; C leaves it unresolved, and he'll go on worrying at it. Taking the blame is only kind if you take it quickly.
Phrase 6

You want ten seconds to go and stand somewhere better, and you want him to wait without feeling that he's been abandoned. Put the pieces in order:

two secondsnearer the router.Bear with mewhile I move
"Bear with me two seconds while I move nearer the router."
"Bear with me" is a fixed request meaning "stay with me, be patient" — it's the polite way of asking someone to wait for something you're doing rather than something you're deciding. The rest is the important part: you tell him how long ("two seconds", which is never two seconds and everybody knows it) and why. A silence you've explained isn't a silence; it's just a pause with a reason.

🔁 Untangling it and getting back on

Phrase 7

With the delay, you've both started speaking at the same moment, and now you're both stopping and starting. Untangle it in one line, and give him the floor. Put the pieces in order:

you go —Sorry,over you.I'm talking
"Sorry, you go — I'm talking over you."
To talk over someone is to speak while they're still speaking. What makes the line work is that you name yourself as the one doing it, even though it's plainly the delay's fault and not yours — that's what stops the polite stand-off before it starts. "You go" then hands him the floor in two words, so somebody has decided and you can both move on.
Phrase 8

The line's fine again and you have to restart — but restarting from nothing wastes the eight minutes you've already spent. Pick the conversation up where it fell over, and hand him back his own question. Put the pieces in order:

You were askingWhere hadRenner project.we got to?about the
"Where had we got to? You were asking about the Renner project."
The question on its own would just move the work onto him, which isn't much of a gift. The second sentence is what does the job: you answer your own question by naming the thing he'd asked about, so he doesn't have to hunt back through his notes. It also tells him, without any boasting, that you were listening properly right up to the moment the screen died.

💼 Changing the plan, and landing it

Phrase 9

You've both had enough of the freezing, and the phone would simply work. But it's his interview and his decision — you're the one asking for the job. Which version puts the idea on the table and genuinely leaves the choice with him?

A. "Can we just do it on the phone? This is hopeless."
B. "I'll ring you on the phone instead, this isn't working."
C. "Shall we just do it on the phone instead? I'm happy either way."
C — "Shall we just do it on the phone instead? I'm happy either way."
"Shall we" makes it a suggestion for the two of you rather than a decision you've made about him, and "just" makes it a small change rather than a rescue operation. The second sentence is what keeps you out of trouble: "I'm happy either way" means you have no stake in the answer, so he can say no without disappointing you. A complains, and B — which is the same idea — simply tells him what's happening, which isn't yours to do here.
Phrase 10

It's over, it went fine in the end, and you want to close it without a grovelling apology — one word for the quality he showed by not getting annoyed while you shuffled around after your router. Complete it: "Sorry about all that — thanks for being ___ with it."

"Sorry about all that — thanks for being patient with it."
Two moves in one short line. "Sorry about all that" clears the whole mess in five words rather than going back over it — "all that" is doing the work, because it means everything and therefore nothing in particular. Then you thank him for something specific he did, which quietly says he behaved well, and people rather like being told that. The last thing he hears is a compliment instead of an apology, and that's the note you want to leave on.

🔑 Make them yours

These aren't interview phrases — they're the language of every call you'll have this year, which is why they're worth owning properly. Pin three of them to your own life so you'll actually reach for them under pressure. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I think it's my end, not yours." — think of a call where the other person spent ages apologising for a bad line. What could you have said to stop them?

"Bear with me two seconds while I move nearer the router." — a moment you regularly go quiet on a call. What are you actually doing, and how would you explain it?

"Where had we got to? You were asking about…" — a real conversation of yours that gets interrupted often. What would you name to pick it back up?

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your interview. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer — a frozen screen doesn't give you thirty seconds to craft each sentence.

The Twist

😬 "Let's park it and try again next week"

One more round — you're the candidate again, no script, no safety net. Twelve minutes in, before you've answered a single real question, Frank says it: "Look, this clearly isn't working. Let's park it and pick it up next week when your line's behaved itself." It sounds like kindness. It isn't, quite — his diary is a month deep, "next week" has a way of becoming never, and you'd be the candidate he remembers as the one the call went wrong with. But push too hard and you're the difficult one, on a Friday afternoon, at four o'clock. Start the timer. Decide, out loud, in the moment: do you take the reschedule gracefully and protect the relationship, or do you keep it alive right now — the phone, ten minutes, the two questions that matter? Either choice can be the right one. Make it, commit to it, and sell it in under thirty seconds. One rule: you don't get to say both.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I named each problem the moment it happened, instead of hoping it would go away.
  • I never left Frank holding the blame — even when it plainly wasn't mine.
  • Every time I went quiet, he knew how long for and why.
  • I picked the conversation back up where it broke, rather than starting again from nothing.
  • I sounded unbothered — like someone dealing with weather, not a disaster.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Nobody trained us for this. A generation ago, the worst a conversation could do to you was a crossed line or a bad connection you rang back on, and everything important was settled in a room with a table in it.

Now a fair amount of your life — the job, the diagnosis, the flat, the goodbye — arrives through a small square that might stop working halfway through. And so we've all quietly become part-time engineers of our own conversations, which is a job none of us applied for and most of us do badly.

The odd part is that the repair language matters more than the answers do. Anyone can be impressive when the line is clean. What people actually remember is who stayed easy to talk to when it wasn't — who took the blame, kept it small, and handed the thread back.

Two bigger questions

  1. How much of what an interview measures is really the person, and how much is their broadband, their spare room, and whether the neighbours are streaming something? Is a video interview a fair test of anyone — and if not, why does almost everyone still use one?
  2. We've got a whole new set of manners for a technology that's barely twenty years old — who to blame, when to apologise, whether your camera is on. Where did those rules come from, given nobody wrote them down? And which one do you think we'll look back on as obviously ridiculous?
