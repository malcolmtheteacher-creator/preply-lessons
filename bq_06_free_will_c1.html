If your brain seems to decide before you know it, who is really in charge, and can anyone be blamed?

The ideas, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The clockwork universe

In 1814 the French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace described an imaginary being, later called Laplace's demon. If it knew the position and speed of every particle in the universe at one moment, and all the laws of nature, it could calculate everything that would ever happen. The future would be as certain as the past.

If the demon is possible in principle, then so is a conclusion that many people find uncomfortable. Your decisions are caused by your brain, which is caused by your genes, your upbringing and your experiences, which are caused by earlier events, in a chain that runs back before you were born. At no point does a free act seem to enter.

Modern physics complicates the picture, because at the smallest scales events seem to involve chance. But chance is not freedom either. A choice that comes from random events in the brain is no more yours than one that is fixed in advance. Both pictures seem to leave the person out.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What could Laplace's demon do?

2. Why does randomness not rescue free will?

1. Calculate everything that will ever happen, if it knew the position and speed of every particle and all the laws of nature.

2. Because a choice produced by chance is no more under your control than a predetermined one.

Discuss:

Part 2 — The experiment

In the early 1980s the American neuroscientist Benjamin Libet asked volunteers to watch a fast-moving clock and flex a wrist whenever they felt like it. They were to note the clock position at the moment they became aware of the urge. Sensors recorded the brain's electrical activity.

The result seemed to be shocking. A build-up of activity in the brain, called the readiness potential, began about half a second before the movement, and well before people said they had decided. The brain, it seemed, had started the action before the conscious mind knew about it.

Many popular articles concluded that free will had been disproved. Scientists and philosophers have been much more cautious. Later work suggested that the readiness potential may reflect background noise in the brain rather than a decision. And flexing a wrist at a random moment is not much like choosing a career or deciding to tell the truth. It may be that the experiment says very little about the choices we care about.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Libet's volunteers do, and what did he measure?

2. Why have many people been cautious about the conclusion?

1. They flexed a wrist when they felt like it and noted the clock when they became aware of the urge, while brain activity was recorded.

2. The readiness potential may only be background noise, and a trivial movement is not like an important decision.

Discuss:
  • If your brain begins an action before you are aware of deciding, does that prove you are not the one deciding?

Part 3 — Three ways out

There are three main responses. Hard determinists say that free will is an illusion and that we should accept it. Libertarians, in the philosophical sense, say that we really do originate some of our actions, and that determinism must therefore be false. And compatibilists, among them David Hume and, more recently, Daniel Dennett, argue that both can be true.

Compatibilists change the question. Freedom, they say, does not mean escaping all causes. It means acting on your own reasons and desires, without being forced or tricked. A person who walks away from an argument because they have thought it over is free in a sense that matters, even if the thinking has causes.

The debate has practical stakes. If no one could have done otherwise, can we blame or praise anyone? Courts still treat most people as responsible, but they also take account of brain injury, mental illness and coercion. The question is whether this is a sensible practice, or a compromise that has not yet been explained.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What are the three main responses to the problem?

2. How do compatibilists define freedom?

1. Hard determinism (free will is an illusion), libertarianism (we truly originate some actions) and compatibilism (both can be true).

2. As acting on your own reasons and desires without being forced or deceived, even though those reasons have causes.

Discuss:
  • Is acting on your own reasons enough to be free, or do those reasons need to come from somewhere you control?

Grammar — hedging and speculating

Big claims invite big objections. Skilled speakers qualify what they say, so that it is accurate rather than weak. English has many ways to soften a claim and to speculate, and they sound more precise, not less.

Four ways to hedge:
• Modal verbs — "It may well be an illusion." "Chance might play a role."
• Verbs that soften — "The results would appear to show that the brain acts first." "People tend to overestimate their control."
• Adverbs and adjectives — "Arguably, the experiment is flawed." "It is conceivable that the feeling is a story the brain tells." "Largely shaped by upbringing."
• Phrases — "It is likely that…" "To some extent…" "is thought to…"
Feel the difference:
• appear to / seem to hedge what the evidence shows.
• may well / might hedge a possibility.
• tend to / largely / to some extent hedge how general the claim is.
Good hedging is selective: do not hedge everything, or you will say nothing.

Free or caused?

Five situations. Say whether the person acted freely and what theory you are using, then reveal.

1. A person signs a contract with a weapon pointed at them.
Coercion. Even compatibilists say this is not free: the person is being forced, so their reasons are not their own.
2. A person decides to become vegetarian after months of thinking, though their upbringing shaped what they care about.
Compatibilism. They acted on their own reasons after reflection. Their values have causes, but the choice is still theirs.
3. A man cannot stop gambling although he knows it is ruining his family.
Reduced control. Most people see this as a case in the middle: he acts on desires he does not endorse. Responsibility is partial rather than absent.
4. A scan shows that a violent offender has damage to the part of the brain that controls impulses.
Responsibility and explanation. Courts may take this into account. The harder question is why this case should differ from one with no visible damage.
5. You feel certain that, in the same situation, you could have chosen differently.
The feeling. The feeling is real but does not settle the matter. A determinist says it comes from not knowing all the causes; a libertarian says it is the evidence.
