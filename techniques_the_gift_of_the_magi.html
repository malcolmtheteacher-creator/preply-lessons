Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The gold pocket-watch was a family heirloom, handed down from grandfather to father to son, each one winding it every morning of his life.
an heirloom (n) — a valued object passed down through a family over generations.
2. Their flat was shabby — the carpet worn thin, the paint peeling, the furniture tired — but it was scrubbed spotlessly clean.
shabby (adj) — worn out and in poor condition through long use or lack of money.
3. Above every other possession, he prized his father's watch, valuing it more than anything else he owned.
to prize (v) — to value something extremely highly. A prized possession is the thing you treasure most.
4. For a moment her courage faltered; she paused on the step, half-turned back, then forced herself on.
to falter (v) — to lose strength or steadiness for a moment; to hesitate or waver.
5. She had gazed at the combs in the shop window for months with something close to reverence, a deep, almost worshipful admiration.
reverence (n) — deep respect or worshipful admiration for someone or something.
6. Their savings were meagre — a few coins in a jar — nowhere near enough for the presents they dreamed of giving.
meagre (adj) — very small in amount; barely enough.
7. He didn't argue over the price — he was in no mood to haggle — and simply paid what the shopkeeper asked.
to haggle (v) — to argue or bargain over the price of something.
8. The chain was plain and unassuming, its worth in its quality rather than in any show or sparkle.
unassuming (adj) — modest; not trying to attract attention or seem important.
9. The hairdresser lifted the long cascade of brown hair, which fell and rippled like water down past her knees.
a cascade (n) — something falling in large flowing quantities, like a small waterfall.
10. There is a bitter irony in it: each gift, so lovingly chosen, was made useless by the very gift given in return.
irony (n) — an outcome that is the opposite of what was expected or intended, often with a pointed twist.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — One dollar and eighty-seven cents

One dollar and eighty-seven cents. That was all. And sixty cents of it was in pennies — pennies saved one and two at a time by bullying the grocer and the butcher until one's cheeks burned. Three times Della counted it. One dollar and eighty-seven cents. And the next day would be Christmas.

There was clearly nothing to do but flop down on the shabby little couch and cry. So Della did. She and Jim — James Dillingham Young — lived in a furnished flat that cost eight dollars a week. It was not exactly poverty, but it came very close, and Jim's wages had lately shrunk.

Yet in all that meagre living, the young couple owned two treasures in which they took a fierce and equal pride. One was Jim's gold watch, an heirloom that had belonged to his father and his grandfather before him. The other was Della's hair — long, rippling, shining like a cascade of brown water, falling below her knee and making almost a garment of itself.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How much money does Della have, and why is that a problem?

2. What are the couple's two great treasures?

1. One dollar and eighty-seven cents, painfully saved in pennies — nowhere near enough for the Christmas present she wants to buy Jim, and Christmas is tomorrow.

2. Jim's gold watch, inherited from his father and grandfather, and Della's long, beautiful hair.

Discuss:
  • The couple are poor, yet fiercely proud of two possessions. What makes an object truly precious — its price, or what it comes to mean?

Part 2 — The sacrifice

Suddenly Della whirled from the window and stood before the mirror. Her eyes were shining, but her face had lost its colour within twenty seconds. Rapidly she pulled down her hair and let it fall to its full length. For a moment her courage faltered, and a tear or two splashed on the worn red carpet. Then on went her old brown jacket and her old brown hat, and with a whirl of skirts she fluttered out of the door and down to the street.

Where she stopped, the sign read: "Madame Sofronie. Hair Goods of All Kinds." "Will you buy my hair?" asked Della. "Twenty dollars," said Madame, lifting the mass with a practised hand. "Give it to me quick," said Della.

The next two hours tripped by on rosy wings. She was in no mood to haggle; she ransacked the shops for Jim's present. She found it at last — a platinum chain for his watch, plain and unassuming, its value in its substance and not in its sparkle. It was so like Jim. Twenty-one dollars they took from her, and she hurried home with the eighty-seven cents that were left, picturing Jim proudly checking the time a hundred times a day.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Della do to raise the money, and how much does she get?

2. What does she buy for Jim, and why does it suit him so well?

1. She sells her long hair to Madame Sofronie for twenty dollars.

2. A plain platinum watch-chain; its worth is in its quality, not its show — quiet and honest, just like Jim.

Discuss:
  • Once Della decides, the hair is gone within a paragraph. What does the sheer speed of her decision tell us about her?

Part 3 — The turn

At home, Della repaired the damage as best she could. Within forty minutes her head was covered with tiny, close-lying curls that made her look wonderfully like a truant schoolboy. "If Jim doesn't kill me," she whispered, "he'll say I look like a chorus girl. But what could I do — oh! what could I do with a dollar and eighty-seven cents?"

The door opened and Jim stepped in. His eyes fixed upon Della, and there was an expression in them she could not read, and it terrified her. It was not anger, nor surprise, nor disapproval — none of the feelings she had prepared for. He simply stared. At last he drew a package from his overcoat and threw it on the table. "Don't make any mistake about me, Dell," he said. "There's nothing — no haircut — that could make me love my girl any less. But unwrap that package, and you'll see why you had me going for a while."

White fingers tore at the string. Inside lay The Combs — the set, side and back, that Della had worshipped for months in a Broadway shop window. Beautiful combs, pure tortoise-shell, exactly the shade to wear in the beautiful vanished hair. She hugged them to her, and at last managed to look up with a smile: "My hair grows so fast, Jim!" Then she held out the platinum chain on her open palm. "Isn't it grand? Give me your watch — I want to see how it looks on it." Instead, Jim tumbled down on the couch, put his hands behind his head, and smiled. "Dell," said he, "let's put our Christmas presents away and keep them a while. They're too nice to use just at present. I sold the watch to get the money to buy your combs."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is inside Jim's package, and why is it so painful?

2. What has Jim done to be able to afford it?

1. The tortoise-shell combs Della had longed for — beautiful, and now useless, because she has just sold the hair they were meant for.

2. He sold his treasured gold watch — the family heirloom — to pay for them.

Discuss:
  • Each gift is now useless, yet the story treats the ending as beautiful rather than tragic. Why? What has each of them actually proved?

Grammar — Cleft Sentences

A cleft sentence splits one idea into two clauses in order to shine a spotlight on the part that matters most. This whole story turns on emphasis — what each person valued, what each gave up, who sacrificed for whom — so it's the perfect place to master them.

The two you need:
• It-cleft: It + was/is + [the focus] + that/who + … → "It was her hair that Della sold." (spotlight on her hair). "It was Jim who sold the watch."
• Wh-cleft: What + [clause] + was/is + [the focus] → "What Della sold was her hair." "What Jim prized most was his father's watch."
Why writers reach for them: to correct, contrast or dramatise. "It wasn't the gifts that mattered — it was the love behind them." The cleft holds the important idea back and lands it with weight. Notice that the verb be matches the tense of the story (was/were for the past).

1. Rewrite with an it-cleft, putting the spotlight on the words in CAPITALS

1. Della sold HER HAIR. →

2. JIM bought the combs. →

3. They sold their treasures FOR EACH OTHER. →

1. It was her hair that Della sold.

2. It was Jim who bought the combs.

3. It was for each other that they sold their treasures.

2. Turn each sentence into a wh-cleft beginning with "What"

1. Della wanted a gift worthy of Jim. →

2. Jim admired her courage most of all. →

3. The story shows the true value of a gift. →

1. What Della wanted was a gift worthy of Jim.

2. What Jim admired most (of all) was her courage.

3. What the story shows is the true value of a gift.

Going deeper — take the idea further

You have the story and the words. Now push the ideas outward — argue, disagree, change your mind. Do all of this out loud. There are no right answers here, only good reasons.

Useful language for building an argument: "What's striking is…", "You could argue that…", "It's not X that matters, it's Y…", "On one level… but on another…", "That may be true, but…".
1 · Foolish, or the wisest of all?

O. Henry ends by calling this poor, impractical young couple the magi — the wisest gift-givers who ever lived. Yet each of them ruined the other's gift. Was their sacrifice wise or reckless? Build a case for each side, then say which you actually believe — and why.

2 · "It's the thought that counts"

We say it constantly. Is it a genuine truth — or a comfortable excuse for giving too little? When is the thought enough, and when does effort or money actually matter? Take a position and defend it.

3 · Gifts in a shopping age

Della and Jim had almost nothing, so every gift cost them something real. In a world of one-click buying and wish-lists, has gift-giving lost its meaning? If you could change one thing about the way people give gifts today, what would it be — and would anyone thank you for it?

4 · The useless treasure

Both presents end up practically useless — a chain with no watch, combs with no hair — yet the story calls them precious. Can something with no practical use still be one of the best gifts ever given? Think of examples from history, films, or the world around you.

Task B · Take one side (4 minutes)

Pick one position and argue it as persuasively as you can: "A gift's worth lies in what it cost the giver" or "A gift's worth lies only in what the receiver can use." Then switch: for one minute, make the best possible case for the side you disagree with.

Task C · The perfect gift (4 minutes)

Design the perfect gift for someone who "already has everything" — on a tiny budget. What would it be, and why would it land? Then describe the most thoughtful gift you've ever come across — one you gave, received, or simply heard about, in life, a book or a film. What made it thoughtful rather than expensive?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Is self-sacrifice always admirable, or can giving too much be its own kind of mistake?
  2. Della and Jim speak their love through objects rather than words. What can a gift say that words cannot?
  3. O. Henry hides Jim's secret until the very last line. How does holding it back change the way the whole story lands?
