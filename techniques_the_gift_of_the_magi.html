The Gift of the Magi

O. Henry (1905) — two poor people, one dollar and eighty-seven cents, and the wisest gift of all

Easier version available: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Read in three parts

O. Henry builds his whole story towards a single turn in the last few lines — and then, in a short paragraph, tells you what that turn means. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, and don't skip ahead: the ending only lands if you don't see it coming.

Part 1 — One dollar and eighty-seven cents

One dollar and eighty-seven cents. That was all. And sixty cents of it was in pennies. Pennies saved one and two at a time by bulldozing the grocer and the vegetable man and the butcher until one's cheeks burned. Three times Della counted it. One dollar and eighty-seven cents. And the next day would be Christmas.

There was clearly nothing to do but flop down on the shabby little couch and howl. So Della did it. Della and James Dillingham Young were very much in love. But their income had shrunk, and they were poor. Tomorrow was Christmas Day, and she had only $1.87 with which to buy Jim a present. She had been saving every penny she could for months, with this result.

Now, there were two possessions of the James Dillingham Youngs in which they both took a mighty pride. One was Jim's gold watch that had been his father's and his grandfather's. The other was Della's hair. Had the Queen of Sheba lived across the way, Della would have let her hair hang out the window to dry just to depreciate Her Majesty's jewels — so beautiful it was, falling about her, rippling and shining like a cascade of brown waters, almost to her knees.

Pause and discuss:
  • O. Henry spends his first lines on money — pennies, counting, "the next day would be Christmas." Why open a love story with arithmetic?
  • He tells us their two treasures — Jim's watch and Della's hair — and stops there. Why plant exactly those two objects so early and so clearly?
  • Della "howls," then acts. What does that tell you about the kind of love the story is about?

Part 2 — The sacrifice

Suddenly she whirled from the window and stood before the glass. Her eyes were shining, but her face had lost its colour within twenty seconds. Rapidly she pulled down her hair and let it fall to its full length. On went her old brown jacket; on went her old brown hat. With a whirl of skirts she fluttered out the door and down the stairs to the street, where a sign read: Mme Sofronie. Hair Goods of All Kinds.

"Will you buy my hair?" asked Della.

"I buy hair," said Madame. "Take yer hat off and let's have a sight at the looks of it."

Down rippled the brown cascade. "Twenty dollars," said Madame, lifting the mass with a practised hand. "Give it to me quick," said Della.

The next two hours tripped by on rosy wings. She was ransacking the stores for Jim's present. She found it at last. It surely had been made for Jim and no one else. It was a platinum fob chain, simple and chaste in design — as all good things should be. It was even worthy of the watch. As soon as she saw it she knew that it must be Jim's. It was like him: quietness and value. Twenty-one dollars they took from her for it, and she hurried home with the eighty-seven cents. With that chain on his watch, Jim might be properly anxious about the time in any company.

Pause and discuss:
  • Della sells her one treasure without hesitation. Is that impulsive, or is it the clearest thinking in the story?
  • She buys a chain for Jim's watch. Keep that firmly in mind. Why does the perfect gift she chooses matter so much to what happens next?
  • She now has short hair and eighty-seven cents. She is nervous about Jim's reaction — but not about the money. What does she value, and what doesn't she?

Part 3 — The turn

Jim was never late. Della heard his step on the stair, and she turned white for just a moment. Jim stopped inside the door, as immovable as a setter at the scent of quail. His eyes were fixed upon Della, and there was an expression in them that she could not read, and it terrified her. It was not anger, nor surprise, nor disapproval, nor horror, nor any of the sentiments that she had been prepared for. He simply stared at her fixedly with that peculiar expression on his face.

"Jim, darling," she cried, "don't look at me that way. I had my hair cut off and sold because I couldn't have lived through Christmas without giving you a present. It'll grow out again — you won't mind, will you? My hair grows awfully fast."

Jim looked about the room curiously. "You've cut off your hair?" he asked, laboriously, as if he had not arrived at that fact even after the hardest mental labour.

Out of his trance Jim seemed quickly to wake. He drew a package from his overcoat pocket and threw it upon the table. "Don't make any mistake about me, Dell," he said. "I don't think there's anything that could make me like my girl any less. But if you'll unwrap that package you may see why you had me going a while at first."

For there lay The Combs — the set of combs that Della had worshipped in a shop window on Broadway. Beautiful combs, pure tortoise-shell, with jewelled rims — just the shade to wear in the beautiful vanished hair. And now, they were hers, but the tresses that should have adorned the coveted adornments were gone.

But she hugged them to her bosom, and at length she was able to look up with dim eyes and a smile and say: "My hair grows so fast, Jim!" And then she leaped up and cried, "Oh, oh!" Jim had not yet seen his beautiful present. She held it out to him eagerly upon her open palm. "Isn't it a dandy, Jim? I hunted all over town to find it. You'll have to look at the time a hundred times a day now. Give me your watch. I want to see how it looks on it."

Instead of obeying, Jim tumbled down on the couch and put his hands under the back of his head and smiled. "Dell," said he, "let's put our Christmas presents away and keep 'em a while. They're too nice to use just at present. I sold the watch to get the money to buy your combs. And now suppose you put the chops on."

The magi, as you know, were wise men — wonderfully wise men — who brought gifts to the Babe in the manger. They invented the art of giving Christmas presents. Here I have told you the uneventful chronicle of two foolish children who most unwisely sacrificed for each other the greatest treasures of their house. But in a last word to the wise of these days, let it be said that of all who give gifts, these two were the wisest. Everywhere they are wisest. They are the magi.

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • Now hold both facts together: she sold her hair to buy a chain for his watch; he sold his watch to buy combs for her hair. Neither gift can now be used. What has each of them actually given?
  • The narrator calls them "foolish children" and, in the same breath, "the wisest." How can both be true — and which word does he really mean?
  • The gifts are useless; the love is proven. Is a gift that can't be used a failure, or the whole point?
  • Your turn: Have you ever given, or received, a gift where the thought mattered far more than the thing? What made it land?

The grammar this story runs on: cleft sentences

The whole story turns on what each person gave and who gave up what. When we want to spotlight one part of a sentence like that — to put it under a lamp — English lets us "split" the sentence in two. These are cleft sentences, and they are exactly how a storyteller builds emphasis and a final reveal.

1. It-clefts — "It was … that/who …"

Take a plain sentence and lift one part to the front after It was/is: "Della sold her hair" → "It was her hair that Della sold." Use who for people, that for things. Rewrite each, emphasising the part in bold.

1. Jim sold his watch. → It was Jim sold. (emphasise the object)

2. Della cut off her hair. → It was cut off her hair. (emphasise the person)

3. They proved their love through sacrifice. → It was they proved their love. (emphasise the means)

1. It was his watch that Jim sold.

2. It was Della who cut off her hair. (who for a person)

3. It was through sacrifice that they proved their love.

2. Wh-clefts — "What … is/was …"

Front the sentence with What to spotlight the idea and save the punch for the end: "She wanted a gift for Jim" → "What she wanted was a gift for Jim." Complete each.

1. Della needed money for a present. → Della needed was money for a present. (start with "What")

2. Their poverty could not touch their love. → their poverty could not touch their love. (What … was …)

1. What Della needed was money for a present.

2. What their poverty could not touch was their love.

3. Build the reveal — spotlight the twist

Cleft sentences are how you deliver a reveal with weight. Turn each plain fact into a cleft that lands the irony. (More than one answer can work — check yours against the models.)

What O. Henry does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves that turn a two-page anecdote into the most retold Christmas story in English.

1. Situational irony — the twin twist

Each gift is destroyed by the other's sacrifice: a chain for a watch that's gone, combs for hair that's cut. O. Henry engineers a perfect double irony, so that the more perfectly each chooses, the more useless the gift becomes. The irony doesn't mock them — it exalts them.

2. Foreshadowing — the two treasures

The ending is fair because it is planted. In Part 1 O. Henry isolates exactly two objects — the watch and the hair — and tells us they are the couple's proudest possessions. When both are sacrificed, we feel the click of something prepared, not sprung. He seeds the reveal in plain sight.

3. The withheld reveal — one gift at a time

Notice the order: we learn Della's sacrifice, then Jim's shocked face, then — only at the very end — the combs, and only after that, the sold watch. O. Henry delays and stages each disclosure so the full symmetry hits all at once in the final lines.

4. The narrator steps forward — the reframe

In the last paragraph the storyteller drops the scene and speaks to us directly, calling the pair "foolish children" and then "the wisest… the magi." He reframes foolishness as wisdom and elevates a tiny domestic story into a parable about the nature of giving.

Speaking tasks

Do all four. Each builds on the last. Use the cleft sentences and the effect verbs from the previous tabs. The timer is for your benefit — fill the time rather than escape it.

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · Retell the story (3 minutes)

Tell the story back to me in your own words, about eight to ten sentences. Use at least five words from the Vocabulary tab, and land the ending with a cleft sentence ("It was her hair that…", "What neither of them expected was…"). Beats to help you: a poor young couple, one day before Christmas · Della has only $1.87 · her one treasure is her long hair; his is his father's watch · she sells her hair to buy a chain for the watch · Jim comes home stunned — he has sold the watch to buy combs for her hair · both gifts are now useless, but the love is proven.

Task B · A gift where the thought was everything (4 minutes)

Describe a real gift — one you gave or received — where the thought mattered far more than the object. Take it in three steps: (1) what the gift was and who it was between; (2) what the giver gave up or noticed to choose it; (3) why it landed the way it did. Use at least one cleft sentence to spotlight the heart of it ("What made it special was…").

Task C · Is it foolish or wise? (3 minutes)

Della and Jim each destroyed their own treasure for a gift the other can no longer use. Argue one side to me — foolish or wise — and make me believe it. Then argue the other side just as hard. Use "It was … that…" to pin down exactly what they did right or wrong.

Task D · Write the missing scene (4 minutes)

We never hear Madame Sofronie's thoughts as she cuts off Della's hair, or Jim's as he hands over his grandfather's watch at the shop. Choose one, and narrate that moment aloud — what they notice, what they weigh, whether they hesitate. Use cleft sentences to control what your listener focuses on.

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The story says these "foolish" givers are actually "the wisest." What is O. Henry's definition of a wise gift — and do you share it?
  2. Would the story be better, or worse, if just one of them had sacrificed? What does needing both sacrifices give the ending?
  3. In an age of wish-lists and gift cards, has the "useless but heartfelt" gift become impossible — or more precious than ever?
  4. If Della and Jim could do Christmas again, knowing what you know, what should they have done — and would it have been a better story?
