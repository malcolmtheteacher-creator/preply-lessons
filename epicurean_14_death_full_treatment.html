Death is Nothing to Us

The most radical doctrine: a full treatment of the argument

Before We Begin...

🧠 What Do You Already Know?

What's your reaction to this statement: "Death is nothing to us"? Does it comfort you? Disturb you? Convince you? Write your honest first response.

Today: We're doing a full, careful treatment of Epicureanism's most controversial claim: that death should not be feared because when death comes, you won't be there to experience it. We'll examine the argument, its strongest objections, and what Lucretius said.

Why This Doctrine?

Fear of death is one of the main causes of mental disturbance (ataraxia). It keeps people anxious, makes them pursue dangerous activities (like seeking power or wealth to appear immortal), and ruins the present in worry about the future.

If Epicurus can show that fearing death is irrational, he removes one of the main obstacles to ataraxia. This is the therapeutic purpose: philosophy as medicine for the soul.

The Core Logical Argument

Death is Nothing to Us (Principal Doctrine 2)

Premise 1: All good and bad consists in sensation

What makes something good or bad for you? Experience. Pleasure is sensed as good; pain is sensed as bad. Without sensation, there is no good or bad.

Premise 2: Death is the end of sensation

Death means the end of bodily processes and consciousness. You cease to exist. No sensation is possible once dead.

Conclusion: Death cannot be bad for the one who dies

Since death is the end of sensation, and all badness consists in sensation, death itself is not bad for you. You won't be there to experience badness.

Implication: Fearing death is irrational

We should only fear what is bad for us. But death is not bad for us. Therefore, fearing death is logically unfounded.

Death is nothing to us. When we exist, death is not; and when death exists, then we do not. Thus it never is that we and death exist at the same time.

— Epicurus, Principal Doctrine 2

Your turn: Is the argument logically sound? Can you find any logical flaws? Any assumptions that might be questioned?

The argument is logically valid, but its assumptions can be questioned:

  • Assumption 1: All badness requires sensation. We might object: Can't something be bad for you without you experiencing it? For instance, being betrayed while unaware?
  • Assumption 2: What you don't experience doesn't harm you. But we might ask: What about lost opportunities? If death ends your life earlier than it might have been, doesn't that deprive you of future goods?
  • Validity vs. soundness: The argument is logically valid (conclusion follows from premises). But if the premises are false or questionable, the argument isn't sound (the conclusion isn't necessarily true).

Distinguishing Fear from Rational Concern

Epicurus isn't saying you should be indifferent to death. He's saying you should distinguish between rational concern and irrational fear.

  • Rational concern: "I'd prefer to live a long, healthy life. I'll take reasonable precautions against early death."
  • Irrational fear: "I'm terrified of death happening. I lie awake worrying about it. It ruins my present peace."

Epicureanism permits the first, condemns the second. The difference is emotional reactivity vs. rational planning.

The Symmetry Argument: A Powerful Reformulation

The Ancient Complaint

People worry: "But I'll miss my life! I won't experience the future I was supposed to have. That's bad!"

Epicurus has a clever response, often called the "Symmetry Argument." It's perhaps his most powerful tool against fear of death.

The Symmetry Argument

Setup: Your entire life has two "infinite" periods

Before you were born: infinite time passed when you didn't exist. You experienced nothing during that infinite time. This didn't bother you or harm you.

The symmetry

After you die: infinite time will pass when you don't exist. You'll experience nothing during that infinite time.

The question

If infinite non-existence before your birth didn't harm you, why should infinite non-existence after your death harm you? The two situations are symmetrical.

The conclusion

You should have exactly as much concern about the time after your death as you do about the time before your birth. Since the latter causes no anxiety, neither should the former.

Your turn: Does the symmetry argument persuade you? Is there a relevant difference between pre-birth non-existence and post-death non-existence?

This argument is powerful because most people accept it intuitively.

You probably don't spend time worrying about the year 1000 BCE when you didn't exist. You don't feel you missed something. The symmetry argument says: the year 3000 CE (when you'll be dead for centuries) should be identical — you should feel nothing about missing it.

But some philosophers object: The situations might not be perfectly symmetrical. Pre-death, you had a choice: you could have been born or not. But once born, death involves ending something you value. The asymmetry isn't temporal but about beginnings vs. endings.

Still, the argument is psychologically powerful. Many people find it genuinely comforting: "You're right. I don't care about not existing before I was born. So why fear not existing after?"

So death, the most terrifying of all evils, is nothing to us. When we are alive, death is not present. And when death is present, then we are not.

— Epicurus

Objections and Epicurean Responses

Objection 1: The Deprivation Problem

The objection: "But I'm deprived of future goods! I won't get to see my children grow up, finish my projects, experience more pleasure. That's bad for me, even if I don't experience the badness."

Your turn: If you died before experiencing future goods, would that be bad for you even though you wouldn't experience the badness? Is "deprivation" a real harm?

Epicurean response:

There are two ways to interpret "bad for you":

  • Feeling-based: Something is bad if it causes you suffering. By this standard, deprivation isn't bad — you don't suffer when deprived of something you never know you're missing.
  • Comparative: Something is bad if it makes your life worse than it would have been. By this standard, early death is bad — your life is shorter than it could have been.

Epicurus commits to the feeling-based view: "All good and bad consists in sensation." If you don't feel deprived, you aren't harmed. This is controversial. Many people think deprivation is real harm even without felt suffering.

Practical application: But Epicureanism's practical advice is sound: Live so fully and contentedly now that you aren't consumed by regret about what you might miss. If you've achieved ataraxia and aponia, early death doesn't take much.

Objection 2: The Process of Dying

The objection: "Okay, death itself might not be bad. But dying can be. What about painful, lingering death? That's bad, and Epicureanism doesn't address it."

Your turn: Is this a good objection? How would Epicureanism respond?

Epicurean response: Agreed, but separate issues.

"Death is nothing to us" doesn't address dying. Dying, if painful, is indeed bad — you're experiencing pain. But Epicureanism addresses this differently:

  • Seek medical care to minimize pain
  • If dying becomes too painful, consider hastening death (some ancient sources suggest Epicurus accepted this)
  • Most important: don't let fear of dying make you suffer now. Spend your healthy years in peace, not anxiety about potential future suffering.

In fact, one of Epicurus's letters (to Menoeceus) ends: "In facing death, remember your life. Reflect on good times with friends. That will comfort you." The focus is on living well now, not on fearing death.

Objection 3: But We Do Fear Death Irrationally

The objection: "Your argument is logical, but people still fear death irrationally. Even if I'm convinced intellectually, my emotional fear remains. What good is philosophy if it doesn't help?"

Your turn: Is this a fair criticism? Can Epicureanism respond?

Epicurean response: Philosophy as gradual therapy.

Epicurus believed that understanding the truth about death, repeated and meditated upon, gradually changes emotional response. It's not instant, but like medicine: you take it repeatedly until the disease is cured.

Modern psychology supports this: cognitive reframing (understanding something intellectually) does eventually change emotional response, but it requires practice and repetition.

The Epicurean recommendation: Regularly contemplate the arguments. Discuss them with friends. Remind yourself: "I have no sensation once dead. Fear is irrational. I will practice accepting this until my emotions align with my reason."

Lucretius and the Personification of Nature

Who Was Lucretius?

Lucretius (ca. 94-55 BCE) was a Roman poet who wrote De Rerum Natura ("On the Nature of Things"), a 7,400-line epic poem explaining Epicureanism. For centuries, his was the primary surviving source of Epicurean thought.

In Book 3, Lucretius presents a powerful rhetorical device: he imagines Nature speaking directly to someone afraid of death.

Why do you weep, O mortal? Why fear death? If your past life brings regret, and all pleasures flow away and cannot unite for you, why not abandon life like one who has had his fill at a banquet? If instead you squander what you have and suffer because of it, why demand to live longer, when you might lose it all?

— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura (Book 3, paraphrased)

The Power of the Rhetorical Device

Lucretius's personification of Nature is psychologically brilliant. Here are the key points Nature makes:

  • Your life is long enough if lived well. If you've achieved ataraxia, what more do you need?
  • More life doesn't guarantee more pleasure. You can live another century and still be restless and unsatisfied.
  • Death is fair. Everything that is born must die. You don't deserve special exception.
  • You can't negotiate with nature. Death is inevitable, so accept it with grace.
Your turn: Does Lucretius's approach (Nature's Speech) feel different from Epicurus's logical arguments? Is it more persuasive? Why?

Yes, it's very different in tone and method.

Epicurus uses strict logic to show death is "nothing to us." Lucretius appeals to emotions, imagination, and resignation. He's not proving death is harmless; he's helping you accept death as a natural, universal fact.

Why this matters: Logic alone often doesn't change deep emotional responses. Lucretius's poetic, imaginative approach complements Epicurus's arguments. Together, they address both reason and feeling.

The message: "You can't negotiate with nature. This isn't tragic — it's how the universe works. Accept it, and find peace."
