Performance Review Mastery

C2 Level · Management Communication

Why Performance Reviews Fail

Most performance reviews accomplish nothing. People know weeks before whether they're getting a raise. Managers fill out forms in a hurry. Employees sit in a meeting to hear feedback they should have received months ago. Nothing changes. The engagement scores stay flat.

The Problems with Annual Reviews

Recency bias: You remember the last three months more vividly than the whole year. So the review feels like it's based on recent performance, not annual performance.

Rating inflation: Managers rate everyone as "meets expectations" or "exceeds expectations" because giving low ratings is uncomfortable. This makes differentiation impossible.

Vagueness: Reviews use phrases like "could improve communication" without being specific about what that means or how to improve.

No connection to development: The form asks for development plans, but there's no follow-up. No coaching. No resources. Just a checkbox.

Comparison trap: When reviews are tied to pay, people compare themselves to peers. This creates resentment and politics.

The Case for Continuous Feedback

Organisations like Adobe, Microsoft, and others have moved away from annual reviews toward continuous feedback. Here's why:

  • People improve when they get feedback close to the event, not months later.
  • Managers who check in regularly have better relationships with their people.
  • Employees know where they stand, which reduces anxiety and improves trust.
  • High performers get recognised sooner, which keeps them engaged.
  • Problems are addressed early, not festered.

The challenge: Continuous feedback requires managers to have frequent conversations. It's harder than a single annual meeting. But it builds a culture where feedback is normal, not scary.

Language for Discussing Different Performance Levels

For underperformance: Focus on the gap between current and expected. "Your work has been solid, but in Q3 we expected three major deliverables from you. You completed one. What's getting in the way?" This is specific and opens dialogue rather than accusing.

For high performance: Go beyond "great job." "You delivered the project under budget and the client specifically praised your communication. You showed initiative in identifying the security risk. That's the kind of thinking we need at the next level. How are you feeling about your growth here?"

For plateaued performance: "You're reliable and consistent, which is valuable. I'm sensing you might be looking for a new challenge. What would energise you?" This shows you've noticed they're stable but not growing, and opens space for career conversation.

Language for Career Development Conversations

Discussing high potential: "Based on what I've seen this year and feedback from others, I see real potential in you for a team lead role in the next 18 months. That would require you to strengthen your delegation skills and your ability to handle conflict. Would that be of interest to you?"

Setting realistic expectations: "I want to be honest: moving into senior leadership requires skills you're still developing. It's possible, but it would take time and focused work. Are you committed to that journey?"

Identifying gaps: "To move forward, I see three areas: You're strong technically, but your presentations need more polish. You need to build stronger relationships with the customer. And you'll need experience in P&L management. Here's how I want to help you with each."

The Promotion Conversation

You're conducting a performance review with Jordan, a high-performing individual contributor. They've dropped hints that they want to be promoted to team lead. You think they have great potential, but they're not ready for that role yet. They need more experience with conflict management and strategic thinking. How do you have this conversation honestly without demoralizing them?

Your opening statement

You want to start with their strengths before addressing the readiness question. What do you say?

You're someone the team relies on. I can see why you're thinking about the next step in your career. I want to talk about that, because I think you have real potential."

They say: "So I'm ready for team lead?"

Direct and hopeful. You need to be candid without crushing them. What do you say?

Example: "I see your potential for that, and I want to be honest about where you are now. You're excellent technically. What I want to see develop are two things. First, how you handle conflict. In the product review last month, when Ahmed disagreed with your approach, you shut down the conversation rather than engaging with his perspective. Team leads need to see diverse viewpoints as valuable. Second, you think tactically—short-term project delivery. Team leads need to think strategically—how does this project fit our vision? These are learnable, and I want to help you develop them."

They respond: "Okay, but how long? A year? Two years? I'm getting concerned I'm not growing."

This is fear talking. They're worried about being stuck. You need to be specific and give them a path forward. What's your response?

Example: "I don't want you to feel stuck. Here's what I'm thinking: If you nail these two areas, I'd expect you to be ready in 12-18 months. Here's the concrete plan. I'm putting you on a cross-functional project with Marketing so you learn strategic thinking. You'll own the customer relationship side—which means handling diverse perspectives, not just technical ones. I'm also asking you to present quarterly to the exec team, which will build your bigger-picture thinking. And I want to do a monthly check-in so you get feedback on your progress. How does that feel?"

Close the conversation

You want to leave them motivated, not demotivated. How do you end this?

Example: "I'm genuinely excited about your potential. You're going to be a great leader. This next 12-18 months is your opportunity to build the skills that will make you a stronger one. I'm committed to helping you. And honestly, if you work on this, I think you'll be ready sooner than you think. Let's touch base in two months and see how the new project is going."

Bias in Performance Reviews

Research shows that performance reviews are plagued by bias. We rate people like us higher. We rate women's confidence as "aggressive" and men's as "leadership." We rate minorities' work more harshly. We remember the bad more than the good.

Common Biases to Watch For

Affinity bias: You like people who are similar to you, so you rate them higher. This is mostly unconscious.

Recency bias: You weight recent performance more heavily than the whole year.

Halo effect: Someone had one great project, so you think everything they do is great.

Horn effect: One mistake defines them. "They failed at that, so they're not capable."

Gender bias: Women's assertiveness is seen as aggressive; men's is seen as leadership. Women's mistakes are more remembered.

Racial bias: Studies show identical resumes get rated differently depending on the name.

How to Counteract Bias

  • Use specific examples: Don't say "She's a great communicator." Say "In the client presentation, she synthesised three competing views and found common ground."
  • Calibrate across managers: What does "exceeds expectations" mean? Is it the same for Sarah as for Ahmed? Compare notes.
  • Get 360 feedback: Don't rely on your perspective alone. Peers and direct reports see different things.
  • Document throughout the year: Keep notes on performance as it happens. Don't rely on memory.
  • Slow down your judgments: When you rate someone, ask: "What evidence am I basing this on? Could I be missing something?"

Self-assessment accuracy: People often overestimate their performance. This isn't malice—it's human. Our memory is selective. We remember our successes and the obstacles we faced. We don't remember our mistakes the same way. A good review process accounts for this. It's why multiple perspectives matter.

Growth Mindset in Performance Conversations

Carol Dweck's research shows that how you frame feedback matters enormously. If you say, "You're not good at this," the person internalises it as fixed—"I'm not a public speaker." If you say, "Your presentations could be stronger. Here's what I see and how we can improve it," you're saying it's a skill that can be developed.

Leaders with a growth mindset believe people can develop. So they give feedback as coaching, not criticism. They see mistakes as learning opportunities. This approach builds resilience and courage in people. They're more willing to take risks and try new things.
