How Lottery Money Is Distributed

In the UK, when someone buys a National Lottery ticket:

  • 50% goes to prizes (money that players can win)
  • 28% funds good causes — charities, sports, arts, heritage, and community projects across the UK
  • 12% is government duty (tax)
  • 5% goes to retailers
  • 5% covers operating costs

This lesson focuses on that 28% — how lottery money funds real projects in communities.

Key Collocations: Funding Vocabulary

Prize Fund
The pool of money available as prizes to winners.
Good Causes
Charities, community projects, and organisations serving the public interest.
Lottery Funding
Money distributed by lottery operators to support projects and organisations.
Distribution Bodies
Organisations that decide how to allocate (distribute) lottery money to projects.
Running Costs
Money needed for day-to-day expenses to keep an organisation operating.
Identified Need
A problem or gap in services that has been researched and documented.
Sustainable
Able to continue long-term without running out of money or resources.
Proportionate Contribution
A fair share of funding that matches the scale of the organisation or project.
Project Outcomes
The measurable results or changes that happen because of a funded project.
Budget Plan
A detailed breakdown of how money will be spent on a specific project.
Successful Applicants
Organisations whose grant applications have been approved and who receive funding.
Allocating Funding
Deciding how much money to distribute to different projects or organisations.

Evaluation Summary

As you read, consider four criteria:

How to Evaluate a Grant Application

Need: Is there clear evidence of a problem or gap that the project addresses?
Sustainability: Can the organisation keep the project running long-term without more grants?
Budget: Is the money requested reasonable and proportionate to the project?
Impact: Will this funding create meaningful change in the community?

The Grant Meeting

You are a member of a grants panel. Your task: decide how to allocate £30,000 across these four applications.

Your Options

  • Give all £30,000 to one organisation
  • Split funding across two, three, or all four organisations
  • Reject any application (give it £0)

Decision Checklist

Have I reviewed all four applications?
What are the strengths and weaknesses of each?
Which addresses the most urgent need?
Which is most sustainable long-term?
How can I spend the full £30,000 (or explain why I can't)?
Can I justify my choices using evidence from the applications?

Justification Framework

When presenting your decision, use the functional language from Tab 2. Structure your explanation:

1. Opening: "I think we should allocate the funding as follows:..."
2. Strongest choice: "The strongest application is... because..."
3. Trade-offs: "I have reservations about... however..."
4. Evidence: "The evidence suggests... given the identified need, I would prioritise..."
5. Conclusion: "On balance, this allocation addresses the most urgent needs while ensuring sustainability."

Discussion Questions

Choose 2–3 to discuss with your tutor after presenting your allocation:

Should governments run lotteries?

Is it a fair way to fund good causes, or a tax on hope? What are the arguments for and against?

Apart from winning, why do people gamble?

What psychological or social factors drive gambling behaviour, even when the odds are against players?

How is gambling addictive?

What makes some people unable to stop? How does this differ from other addictions?

Should some forms of gambling be banned?

Is it the government's role to restrict gambling? How would regulation work? What would be unintended consequences?

Large grant or multiple small grants?

Is it better to concentrate funding on one excellent project, or spread it to help more organisations? What are the trade-offs?

Betting habits

How common are betting habits in your country? Are they increasing? What age groups gamble most?
