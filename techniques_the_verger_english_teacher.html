TECHNIQUES IN ACTION · FOR EXPERIENCED ENGLISH TEACHERS · 50 min

The Verger

W. Somerset Maugham (1929) — a story for any teacher who has spent years inside an institution and wondered, quietly, whether the thing it values most is actually the thing they do best

Before we read

Your school, your classroom

Before we open the story, take 3–4 minutes and tell me about the school or institution where you currently teach.

  • How long have you been there?
  • What do your students notice about you in the first ten minutes of meeting you?
  • What are you respected for — not on paper, but in practice?
  • What is one thing the institution officially expects of you that you quietly do differently — and the school has either not noticed, or has chosen not to mind?

Why we're doing this: the story opens with a man whose identity is fused with his post. To read him with full sympathy, we should be able to see our own version of that fusion first.

Read in three parts

Maugham gives us the whole story in about three thousand words. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The ending arrives in a single sentence — do not skip ahead.

Part 1 — The new vicar

There had been a christening that afternoon at St Peter's, Neville Square, and Albert Edward Foreman still wore his verger's gown. For sixteen years he had been verger of this church, and he discharged his duties with quiet dignity. He was a tall, spare man with dignified features, and his face was clean-shaven. In his long black gown he might have been mistaken for a bishop, or at the very least an archdeacon. He took a pride in his work.

But that afternoon the new vicar had asked him to remain behind. The vicar was a red-faced energetic man in the early forties, and Albert Edward still resented the change. He had served three previous vicars and had buried each one with the dignity due to his cloth. This new one was different.

The vicar entered the vestry with two churchwardens and closed the door.

"Foreman, we have something rather painful to say to you. You have been here a great many years and I think his lordship and the wardens will agree with me that you have fulfilled the duties of your office to the satisfaction of everybody concerned."

Albert Edward's heart began to beat a little quicker. There was clearly something wrong.

"But a most extraordinary thing came to my knowledge the other day. I discovered to my astonishment that you could neither read nor write."

The verger's face betrayed no sign of embarrassment.

"The last vicar knew that, sir," he replied. "He said it didn't make no difference. He always said there was a great deal too much education in the world for 'is taste."

The vicar and the wardens exchanged a glance.

"It is the most amazing thing I ever heard. Do you mean to say that you have been verger of this church for sixteen years and never learned to read or write?"

"I went into service when I was twelve, sir. The cook tried to teach me once, but I didn't seem to 'ave the knack for it. And then what with one thing and another I never seemed to 'ave the time."

The vicar drew himself up.

"I cannot allow it. I must ask you to make up your mind to learn. I will give you three months. If at the end of that time you cannot read and write I am sorry, but I shall have to let you go."

Albert Edward had never liked the new vicar. He had said from the beginning that they had made a mistake when they appointed him. He was not the kind of man one wanted at St Peter's, Neville Square.

"I'm very sorry, sir, I'm afraid it's no good. I'm too old a dog to learn new tricks. I've lived a good many years without knowin' 'ow to read and write, and without wishin' to praise myself, self-praise is no recommendation, I don't think I've done my duty in this position any the worse for it. If I could learn now I don't 'ardly think I would."

"In that case, Foreman, I'm afraid you must go."

"Yes, sir, I quite understand. I shall be 'appy to 'and in my resignation as soon as you 'ave found somebody to take my place."

Pause and discuss:
  • The vicar dismisses Albert despite sixteen years of perfect service — because of a single missing qualification on paper. Have you ever seen a similar moment in a school: a colleague judged on a credential rather than on what they actually do well in front of students?
  • Albert doesn't argue. He doesn't beg. He doesn't even seem angry — only quietly clear. Is this dignity, pride, exhaustion, obstinacy, or all four?
  • Grammar spotlight: Albert says "I'm too old a dog to learn new tricks." The structure underneath is: too + adjective + a/an + noun + (infinitive). Build three of your own using this pattern — one about a student, one about a colleague, one about yourself.
    Models: "He's too proud a man to ask for help." / "It's too small a town to keep a secret in." / "She's too good a teacher to be wasted on admin."
  • Open question: As an English teacher, you've spent years inside schools and language institutions. Have you ever had your own "sixteen years" moment — a stretch of time when you suddenly realised the institution wanted you to be a different kind of teacher than the one you'd quietly become?

Part 2 — The walk home, and the idea

When Albert Edward had locked the church door behind him he walked slowly down the steps with a heavy heart. He did not know what he was going to do. It was true that he had saved a small sum of money, but to live on it he would have to be very careful. He sighed deeply.

Albert Edward was not a smoker. He did not drink beer. But he liked a cigarette with his evening cup of tea, and now he felt the need of one badly. It occurred to him that he had not bought any that morning, and as he had no tobacco in the house he thought he would go out and get some. As he passed shop after shop in search of a tobacconist, he noticed with surprise that there was not one on the whole length of this long street.

"That's strange," said Albert Edward, to put an end to the matter, "I can't be the only man as walks along this street and wants a fag. I shouldn't wonder but what a fellow might do very well with a little shop here. Tobacco and sweets, you know."

He gave a sudden start. The thought struck him like a thunderbolt. He turned, walked home, and had his tea.

"You're very silent this afternoon, Albert," his wife remarked.

"I'm thinkin'," he said.

He considered the matter from every point of view, and next day he went down the street and by good luck found a little shop to let that looked as if it would exactly suit him. Twenty-four hours later he had taken it. A month after that he resigned his post as verger. He set up in business as a tobacconist and news-agent.

The undertaking proved a success. In a year or so he thought he might venture on another shop, and he looked about for a long street that had no tobacconist in it. He started a second shop, then a third. In ten years he had acquired no fewer than ten shops, and he was making money hand over fist. He went round to all of them himself every Monday morning, collected the week's takings, and took them to the bank.

Pause and discuss:
  • Albert sees a gap nobody else has noticed — a whole long street with no tobacconist. What kind of seeing is that? Is it the kind a teacher develops, slowly, over years of standing in front of different rooms?
  • The insight comes after the firing — when he is walking, alone, with no plan. Why does insight so often arrive in that state, rather than at a desk or in a planning meeting?
  • Grammar spotlight: Maugham writes "It occurred to him that he had not bought any that morning." Notice the impersonal subject — it — and the pattern: It + occurred to + (someone) + that…. This is gold for sounding natural in English. Try three sentences of your own.
    Models: "It occurred to me that I hadn't checked my email." / "It occurred to her that the answer was obvious." / "It only occurred to me later that he hadn't actually said yes."
  • Open question: "I'm thinkin'," Albert says to his wife — and that's all. He keeps the whole idea inside himself until he has acted on it. As a teacher, are you a thinker-out-loud or a thinker-in-private — and which kind do you trust more when something matters?

Part 3 — The bank manager

One morning when he was at the bank, the cashier told him that the manager would like to see him. Albert Edward was shown into the office. The manager shook hands with him.

"Mr Foreman, I wanted to have a talk to you about the money you've got on deposit with us. D'you know exactly how much it is? Just over thirty thousand pounds. It's a large sum to have on deposit, and I should have thought you'd do better to invest it."

"I wouldn't want to take no risk, sir. I know it's safe in the bank."

"You needn't have the least anxiety. We'll make you out a list of absolutely gilt-edged securities. They'll bring you in a better rate of interest than we can possibly afford to give you."

A troubled look settled on Mr Foreman's distinguished face.

"I've never 'ad anythin' to do with stocks and shares and I'd 'ave to leave it all in your 'ands," he said.

The manager smiled. "We'll do everything. All you'll have to do next time you come in is just to sign the transfers."

"I could do that all right," said Albert uncertainly. "But 'ow should I know what I was signin'?"

"I suppose you can read," said the manager a trifle sharply.

Mr Foreman gave him a disarming smile.

"Well, sir, that's just it. I can't. I know it sounds funny-like, but there it is. I can't read or write, only me name, an' I only learnt to do that when I went into business."

The manager was so surprised that he jumped up from his chair.

"That's the most extraordinary thing I ever heard."

"You see, it's like this, sir. I never 'ad the opportunity until it was too late, and then somehow I wouldn't. I got obstinate-like."

The manager stared at him as though he were a prehistoric monster.

"And do you mean to say that you've built up this important business and amassed a fortune of thirty thousand pounds without being able to read or write? Good God, man, what would you be now if you had been able to?"

"I can tell you that, sir," said Mr Foreman, a little smile on his still aristocratic features. "I'd be verger of St Peter's, Neville Square."

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • Read the last line again, slowly. What did it just do to the whole story? Did it change what kind of story you thought you were reading?
  • The bank manager assumes one specific qualification — literacy — is the foundation of business success. In English teaching, what is the equivalent assumption? What credential do people automatically assume is the foundation of being a good English teacher — and is it actually true?
  • Grammar spotlight: The manager's question — "What would you be now if you had been able to read?" — is a third conditional inside a wh-question. The pattern is: What/Where/Who + would + (subject) + be/do + now + if + (subject) + had + past participle…? Try three of your own about your own life.
    Models: "What would you be doing now if you had become a doctor instead?" / "Where would you be living now if you had moved abroad at twenty?" / "Who would you be friends with now if you hadn't started teaching?"
  • Open question: Maugham doesn't show us the manager's face after Albert's answer. We don't see his reaction. Why not — and is there a kind of silence in your own classroom that does the same work? A pause after a student says something true that the room wasn't expecting?

Vocabulary — click each card to reveal

Ten words from the story that earn their keep in a staff meeting, a parents' evening, a letter of reference, or a quiet conversation with yourself about what to do next.

discharge (one's duties)
verb (formal)
To carry out or perform a duty, responsibility or obligation.
"She discharged her duties as Head of Department for eleven years."
resent
verb
To feel bitter or angry about something — especially something you think is unfair.
"She resented the implication that her grammar wasn't quite right because she wasn't a native speaker."
resignation
noun
A formal statement that you are leaving a position; also: the quiet acceptance of a difficult situation.
"He handed in his resignation the same morning." / "She read the letter with a kind of weary resignation."
venture (on something)
verb
To dare to do something risky, often a new direction.
"After fifteen years at the school, she ventured on opening her own little tutoring practice."
pivot
verb / noun
To turn from one direction to a closely related but different one — in a career, a business, or an argument.
"Many teachers eventually pivot into materials writing, examining, or one-to-one online work."
recognised (qualification)
adjective (past participle)
Officially accepted by a country, employer, or professional body.
"Her CELTA is recognised across the EU but some schools in the UAE still ask for a degree from a native-speaking country."
transferable (skills)
adjective
Able to be moved from one role, industry or country to another.
"Lesson planning, classroom management, and the patience to listen — these are highly transferable skills."
acquire
verb
To get or obtain — assets, languages, skills, qualifications.
"In ten years he acquired ten shops." / "She has acquired an enviable command of English idiom from years of close reading."
obstinate
adjective
Stubbornly refusing to change one's mind or course. Sometimes a virtue.
"He was obstinate about not learning to read — and that obstinacy made him rich."

adjective
Very unusual or remarkable — far beyond what is normal.
"Your students' progress this term has been extraordinary." (A useful word to receive — and to offer.)

Register contrast — how Maugham gives each character a voice

The whole story is built on three voices colliding. Notice how Maugham uses spelling, contractions and word choice to mark each one. This is exactly the thing you teach your own students every week — that being able to choose the right register for the room is half of sounding educated in English.

Your equivalents you use every week: formal-classroom · informal-classroom · parents'-evening · staffroom · social-with-colleagues · your own home language. Six registers, one person — and you switch between them, often within the same hour. Maugham's lesson is that the people with power often do not realise which register the room actually needs.

Open question: Of those six registers, which one do you find slips most easily into the wrong room? (Most teachers have one.)

The grammar this story teaches itself

Three structures Maugham puts on a plate: the third conditional (the ending), reported speech (the firing and the bank scenes), and past perfect (the sixteen years before the story begins). All three are things you teach your own students — and worth quietly drilling for your own production at the same time.

1. Third conditional — the form behind the punchline

The ending hangs on a third conditional. The manager says: "What would you be now if you had been able to read?"

Pattern: If + past perfect, would have + past participle. (Counterfactual past — talking about something that did not happen.)

1. If Albert (learn) to read at twelve, he (stay) a verger.

2. If the new vicar (not be) so concerned about literacy, Albert (never open) a shop.

3. If Albert (find) a tobacconist on that street, he (not have) the idea.

4. The manager thinks: "If only you (know) how to read!"

5. If I (be) the vicar, I (keep) Albert on.

1. had learned / would have stayed

2. had not been / would never have opened

3. had found / would not have had

4. had known (wish / regret — "if only" + past perfect)

5. had been / would have kept

Apply it — your own counterfactuals

Practise three answers aloud, each using a clean third conditional:

  • "If you had taught in the UK instead of Romania, where would you be now?"
  • "If your school had hired a different head five years ago, what would have been different?"
  • "If you had specialised in young learners instead of teenagers, would your week feel different now?"

The point is not to lament — it is to demonstrate that you can think clearly about paths not taken without being trapped by them.

2. Reported speech — the firing scene without quotation marks

Convert Albert's direct speech to reported speech. Watch the tense shift back one step, and the pronoun and time-reference changes. You teach this every term — this is the version where you do it on yourself.

Direct: The vicar said, "I will give you three months."
Reported: The vicar said he Albert three months.

Direct: Albert replied, "I cannot learn now."
Reported: Albert replied that he learn then.

Direct: The manager said, "I have never heard anything so extraordinary."
Reported: The manager said he anything so extraordinary.

Direct: Albert said, "I went into service when I was twelve."
Reported: Albert said he into service when he twelve.

Direct: The manager asked, "What would you be now if you had been able to read?"
Reported: The manager asked what Albert be then if he able to read.

1. would give (will → would)

2. could not (cannot → could not)

3. had never heard (present perfect → past perfect)

4. had gone … was

5. would … had been (third conditional stays unchanged in reported speech — already maximally back-shifted)

Apply it — parents'-evening summaries and staff-meeting reports

Reported speech is the working grammar of any teacher's professional writing. After a parents' evening you might write: "Mrs Popescu mentioned that her son had been finding the homework difficult. The head pointed out that the year group as a whole had slipped on the last test. The Year 9 coordinator suggested that we revisit the seating plan." Try one yourself:

Direct: The parent said: "My daughter is not coping with the reading load and she will need more support at home."
Reported: The parent said that her daughter with the reading load and more support at home.

Answer: was not coping / would need (present continuous → past continuous; will → would)

3. Past perfect — the years before the story begins

The story opens with "For sixteen years he had been verger of this church." That past perfect signals: this is the situation that existed before our story begins. Useful when you're telling your own teaching life to a new colleague, an interviewer, or a class of students who don't know you yet.

1. By the time the new vicar arrived, Albert (serve) three previous vicars.

2. Before he opened his first shop, he (save) a small sum of money.

3. When the bank manager called him in, Albert (build) his business up to ten shops.

4. The cook (try) to teach him to read once, but he never picked it up.

1. had served

2. had saved

3. had built

4. had tried

What Maugham does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are the reason this story still works almost a hundred years later — and the reason it would still work as the centre of a classroom discussion you taught tomorrow.

1. Dramatic irony — we know what the vicar discovers

From paragraph one, Maugham lets us see Albert's dignity, his sixteen years of service, the bishop-like presence. Then the vicar discovers he's illiterate. The discovery destabilises the vicar's assumptions — but not ours. We were already in on it.

2. Compression — ten years in one paragraph

Maugham gives us the firing in a thousand words and the next ten years of business growth in about one hundred. He compresses and distils time around the events that matter — the door opening, not the long walk through it.

3. Register contrast — three voices in one room

The vicar speaks in high-Anglican formal English. The bank manager speaks in measured professional English. Albert drops his 'h's and speaks in working-class London. Maugham juxtaposes these registers to do the characterisation for him — we don't need to be told who has the power; we hear it. This is the same trick you spot every week when a student over- or under-pitches their register.

4. The closing line — the reframe

The last sentence — "I'd be verger of St Peter's, Neville Square" — reframes the entire story. Up to that point we have read a rags-to-riches tale. After it, we read a story about how a missing credential can be a door, how setbacks contain shapes, how dignity travels with the person rather than the post. The whole text rotates around one sentence.

Three speaking tasks

Do all three. The lesson has been leading here — everything in the previous tabs was preparation for what you produce in this one. The timer is for your benefit; it forces you to fill the time rather than escape it.

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · Your school, then the walk home (4 minutes)

Two parts, two minutes each.

Part 1 (2 min): Describe the school or classroom where you currently teach: what you bring into the room, what your students see in the first ten minutes, how your day starts and ends. Use past perfect at least twice ("By the time the bell rang, I had already…" / "For the first three years I had been teaching purely from the textbook…") and concrete detail. Describe one ordinary morning rather than summarising in general.

Part 2 (2 min): Now describe one real walk home from school when something a student or colleague said stayed with you. Report what they said using reported speech. Be honest — don't add optimism that wasn't there.

Task B · The career counterfactual (3 minutes)

A friend asks you: "If you hadn't become a teacher, what would you be doing now?"

Answer honestly for three minutes — not as regret, not as bragging, just a clear look at a path you didn't take. Use at least two clean third conditionals. End with one sentence that turns the question into something positive about the path you did choose.

Aim to sound clear and settled about your choice, not regretful.

Task C · The gap you see in language teaching (4 minutes)

Albert noticed a gap no one else had spotted — a busy street with no tobacconist. In your world — English teaching in your country, online English, materials, the way teachers are trained, the kind of student nobody is currently serving — what is a real gap that nobody is filling?

Argue for two minutes that the gap is real. Then for two more, describe what a service or resource that filled it would look like: who would use it, and where it would sit. Be concrete. Use cleft sentences ("What no one is doing is…" / "The kind of teacher no one is training is…").

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. Albert refuses, at sixty, to learn the one skill the institution wants him to have. Has there been a moment in your own teaching career when a school or a system wanted you to learn or use something you genuinely refused to? What did your refusal protect — and what did it cost?
  2. The bank manager believes literacy is the foundation of business success. A lot of people in the language-teaching world believe being a native speaker is the foundation of good English teaching. What's the parallel — and what do you believe is actually the foundation of being a good English teacher?
  3. Maugham compresses Albert's ten years of expansion into one paragraph. If you compress your own next ten years of teaching into one paragraph, what does the paragraph need to say?
  4. Would you ever teach this story to one of your own classes? What level would they need to be — and what would you cut, change, or add to make it land for them?
