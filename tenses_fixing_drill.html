Fixing Your Tenses

Get the tense right every time · Practice & Correction · Everyday English

Now vs Usually

Two present tenses, two jobs. Mix them up and every sentence sounds slightly off. This fixes it.
Present simple — habits, routines, facts, timetables: I work from home. Water boils at 100 degrees.
Present continuous — happening now, or temporarily around now: I am working from home this week. It is raining.
Watch out: some verbs describe a state, not an action, and do not take the continuous: know, like, want, need, understand, believe, mean, own. Say I understand, never “I am understanding.”

Fill the gap with the right present tense

1. Be quiet for a moment — I ___ (try) to concentrate.

2. My brother ___ (work) in a hospital; he loves his job.

3. Sorry, I ___ (not understand) the question.

4. Listen! The people upstairs ___ (make) a lot of noise again.

5. Fix the verb: She is knowing the answer. → She ___ the answer.

Say it out loud (60 seconds)

Say three things you usually do on a normal day (present simple). Then three things you are doing this week that are different from usual (present continuous).

Finished vs Connected

This is the pair that trips up almost everyone: past simple vs present perfect. Get this one solid and your English jumps a level.
Past simple — a finished time: yesterday, last week, in 2019, two hours ago. → I saw her yesterday.
Present perfect — the time is not finished, or the past still matters now: ever, never, just, already, yet, since, for. → I have just seen her. I have lived here for years.
The golden rule: never put a finished-time word with the present perfect. Not “I have seen him yesterday” — say I saw him yesterday.

Telling a Story

To talk about the past clearly, three tenses work together. Here is how to keep them in their lanes.
Past continuous — the background, already in progress: I was cooking…
Past simple — the event that happens, often interrupting: …when the phone rang.
Past perfect — an earlier past, before another past moment: When I arrived, the train had already left.

Fill the gap

1. While I ___ (cook) dinner, the phone rang.

2. They ___ (watch) TV when the lights went out.

3. By the time we got to the cinema, the film ___ (already / start).

4. She was tired because she ___ (not sleep) well the night before.

5. Fix it: When I arrived, the meeting already started (before me). → …the meeting ___ already ___.

Tell the story (90 seconds)

Tell me about a time something went wrong — you missed a bus, burnt the dinner, got lost. Set the scene with was/were + -ing, then say what happened with the past simple.

Talking Ahead

English has several ways to talk about the future, and the choice carries meaning. Here is the quick map.
will — a decision made now, a prediction, an offer: I'm tired — I 'll go to bed. I think it 'll rain.
going to — a plan already in your head, or evidence you can see: We 're going to paint the kitchen. Look at those clouds — it 's going to rain.
present continuous — a fixed arrangement with a time or person: I 'm meeting Anna at seven.
The classic trap: after when, if, as soon as, before, after, use a present tense for the future — never “will.” I'll call you when I arrive (not “when I will arrive”).

Fill the gap

1. It's cold in here. — Oh, I ___ (close) the window.

2. We ___ (meet) friends for dinner tonight — it's all arranged.

3. Look at that ladder — it ___ (fall)!

4. I'll text you as soon as the train ___ (arrive).

5. Fix it: I will call you when I will get home. → I'll call you when I ___ home.

Say it out loud

Tell me: one fixed arrangement you have this week (present continuous), one plan you've made for this year (going to), and one prediction about tomorrow (will).

Every sentence below has a tense mistake — the kind learners make every day. Type the corrected verb only. No topic, just tenses.

1. Yesterday I go to the market. → Yesterday I ___ to the market.

2. I didn't went to work on Monday. → I ___ to work on Monday.

3. I am here since Monday. → I ___ here since Monday.

4. He is wanting a coffee. → He ___ a coffee.

5. She have finished already. → She ___ finished already.

6. By the time she called, I already left. → …I ___ already ___.

7. We are living here since 2015. → We ___ here since 2015.

8. I will tell you as soon as I will know. → …as soon as I ___.

Before you finish

  • I never put a finished-time word (yesterday, in 2019, ago) with the present perfect.
  • I use since / for with the present perfect, not the present simple.
  • I keep state verbs (know, like, want, understand) out of the continuous.
  • After when / if / as soon as, I use a present tense, not “will.”
  • I use had + past participle for the earlier of two past actions.
