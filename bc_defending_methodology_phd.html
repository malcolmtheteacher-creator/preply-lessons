Defending Methodological Choices

PhD Academic English · Justification · Anticipating Objections

Why methodology is the most attacked chapter

Examiners cannot easily attack your data — it is what it is. They cannot easily attack your literature review — there are too many sources. But your methodology is a sequence of choices you made: this design, this sample, this method, this framework. Every choice excluded other choices. Every exclusion is a possible line of attack.

The mature methodology chapter does not pretend the choices were obvious. It names them as choices, justifies each one, and anticipates the objections a sceptical reader would raise.

The defensive rule

Every methodological choice in your thesis must answer two questions in writing: Why this? and Why not the obvious alternative? If your text answers only the first, the viva will demand the second.

The two-part justification structure

For each significant methodological choice — design, sampling, method, analysis — your text should:

  1. State the choice and the rationale. Not "I chose interviews" but "Semi-structured interviews were chosen because they allow X while avoiding Y."
  2. Acknowledge the alternative not taken. "A survey instrument might have allowed Z, but would have foreclosed the iterative refinement of categories that this study requires."

This is not defensiveness — it is the standard form of mature methodological writing. A chapter that does not do this reads as if the writer didn't know other options existed.

The six standard attacks every PhD methodology faces

You will not be asked these in exactly these words, but every viva probes some version of them. Anticipate them in writing.

ATTACK 1

"Why qualitative / quantitative?"

The attack: your method choice is at odds with the question. A quantitative question demanded a survey; a qualitative question demanded interviews. Either way, you chose wrong.

Defence in writing: Frame the choice as flowing from the research question, not from preference or convenience. "Because this study seeks to understand how participants make sense of X — rather than to measure how widespread X is — a qualitative interpretive approach is required."
ATTACK 2

"Why this sample?"

The attack: your sample is too small, too narrow, unrepresentative, or selected in a way that biases the findings.

Defence in writing: Distinguish the kind of generalisation you are claiming. "The sample is not intended to be statistically representative; it is theoretically purposive, selected to maximise variation along the dimensions identified in the literature." Then defend the size with a transparency move: "Saturation was reached at participant 14; four further interviews confirmed but did not extend the categories."
ATTACK 3

"Why this site / context?"

The attack: your case study or context is idiosyncratic and findings won't transfer.

Defence in writing: Position the case as either typical or extreme on principled grounds. "This case is not chosen because it is representative of all X, but because it constitutes a critical case in the sense of Yin (2018): if the phenomenon does not occur here, it is unlikely to occur elsewhere."
ATTACK 4

"Why this analytic framework rather than another?"

The attack: a different framework would have produced different findings, and you've cherry-picked the one that suits.

Defence in writing: Make the framework's fit explicit. "Thematic analysis, rather than discourse analysis, is appropriate here because the analytic interest is in the substantive content of participants' accounts, not in the discursive operations through which those accounts are constructed."
ATTACK 5

"How do you know you didn't bias the data?"

The attack: your involvement in the field, your prior assumptions, your interview style — all could have shaped what participants said.

Defence in writing: Don't deny it — own it. Reflexivity statements work when they are specific. "My prior involvement in X gave me access to participants who would not have spoken to an outsider, but it also raised the risk that my framing of questions assumed shared categories. The interview protocol was designed to surface these assumptions through..."
ATTACK 6

"How would someone replicate this?"

The attack: your account of what you did is too sketchy. A reader could not redo your analysis from your description.

Defence in writing: Replicability in qualitative research is not exact reproduction but traceability. Provide audit-trail moves: appendix with the interview schedule, an example coding excerpt, a description of how categories were refined across iterations. Then say so explicitly: "While exact replication is not the standard for interpretive work, the analytic process is documented in sufficient detail (Appendix B) for the reader to trace each step from raw data to claim."

The specific language of methodological defence

There is a shared vocabulary of justification that examiners listen for. Using it doesn't mean reciting clichés — it means signalling that you know the terrain.

RATIONALE

How to introduce a choice

This study employs X because the research question requires...

X was selected on the grounds that...

The choice of X reflects a deliberate methodological commitment to...

X is appropriate here given the focus on...

CONTRAST

How to acknowledge the alternative not taken

While Y might also have been used, Y would have foreclosed / obscured / privileged...

An alternative approach — Y — was considered but rejected because...

Although Y is the more conventional choice in this field, the present study departs from it because...

QUALIFIED CLAIM

How to claim only what your method can warrant

The findings should be read as indicative rather than definitive.

This study does not claim to establish X; its more modest aim is to show...

The present analysis is suggestive rather than conclusive, opening rather than closing the question of...

REFLEXIVITY

How to position yourself in the research

It must be acknowledged that the researcher's own position as X has shaped...

I came to this research not as a neutral observer but as...

This positionality is treated here as a methodological resource as much as a limitation.

TRANSPARENCY

How to make your process traceable

The analytic process proceeded in three stages, detailed below.

An audit trail of the iterative refinement of the coding framework is provided in Appendix B.

Decisions made at each stage are documented in the research diary excerpts (Appendix C).

How to write about limitations without undermining yourself

Inexperienced PhD writers either hide limitations (which examiners notice) or list them so extensively that the contribution looks fatally compromised. Mature methodological writing names limitations specifically and says what they constrain — without retreating from what the study can claim.

The limitation rule

Every limitation should be named, contained, and connected to a future-research direction. The pattern is: "This study's X means that Y cannot be claimed; future work using Z could address this."

Three limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the sample is drawn from a single institutional context; while this allowed the depth of engagement that the analysis required, it means that claims about variation across institutional types remain to be tested. A multi-site comparative study would address this. Second, the data were collected over a relatively short period; questions about how the dynamics described here unfold over longer timescales fall outside the scope of this study and would benefit from a longitudinal design. Third, the analytic framework adopted here, while productive for the questions posed, is not the only one available; an analysis using [alternative framework] would likely surface dimensions the present study has held in the background. None of these limitations, however, undermines the central claim of the study — that...

Why this paragraph works

  • Three concrete limitations — not fifteen, not none
  • Each one named precisely — "single institutional context," not a vague gesture
  • Each one paired with a future-research move — turns weakness into research agenda
  • Final sentence reasserts the central claim — limitations contained, not allowed to overflow

What to avoid

  • The bullet-point grovel: a list of fifteen limitations, undifferentiated. Reads as panic.
  • The bland disclaimer: "Like all research, this study has limitations." Says nothing.
  • The hidden limitation: a problem you don't acknowledge that an examiner spots immediately. Worst of the three.

Apply to your own methodology

Bring up your draft methodology chapter, or the methodological section of an article you're preparing.

The defensive audit

Rewrite one defence

Take the methodological choice that currently has the thinnest defence in your text. Rewrite the surrounding paragraph using the two-part justification structure (rationale + acknowledgment of alternative).

Honesty check:

Of your five most important methodological choices, which one was actually driven by convenience — access, time, what you already knew how to do — rather than by the research question? That's the one most likely to be probed in the viva, and the one whose defence in writing you most need to strengthen.
