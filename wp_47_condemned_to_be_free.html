Condemned to Be Free

In which philosophy finally confronts the terrifying question: if there is no essence before existence, then who are you?

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: Existentialism. Heidegger, Sartre, Camus. They ask the question that defines modern life: existence precedes essence. You are not born with a nature. You must create yourself through choice. But this freedom is not liberating—it is terrifying. You are condemned to be free. The absurd surrounds you. And yet, you must live, must choose, must create meaning in a universe that offers none.

I. Before We Begin

Who are you? Are you born with a nature? Or must you create yourself?

♦ ♦ ♦

A knife is made for cutting. That is its essence. But a human? A human has no predetermined essence. You are thrown into the world without instruction. You must choose. And that freedom is absolute—and absolutely terrifying.

II. The Story — Freedom and Nothingness

Martin Heidegger (1889-1976)

Heidegger asked a question that had been forgotten: What is Being? He developed the concept of Dasein—being-in-the-world. We are not minds in bodies. We are creatures always already situated in a world of meaning and relationship. Being-toward-death is fundamental. We know we will die, and this knowledge shapes how we exist.

Jean-Paul Sartre (1905-1980)

Sartre made the existentialist claim explicit: existence precedes essence.

We are our choices. — Sartre

You are not born with a fixed nature. You exist first. Then, through your choices, you create your essence. You are radically free. And this freedom is absolute responsibility. "Hell is other people," Sartre said—because in the presence of another consciousness, I am always being judged, evaluated, turned into an object. And I do the same to others.

Albert Camus (1913-1960)

Camus described the absurd. The world has no inherent meaning. We have a deep need for meaning. These clash. That clash—that absurdity—is the human condition.

But here is Camus's answer: we must imagine Sisyphus happy. He is condemned to roll a boulder up a hill for eternity. It always rolls back down. No meaning. No progress. Yet he can choose to be happy in the doing. He can create meaning through his revolt against meaninglessness.

The Three Core Ideas

1. Existence Precedes Essence: You have no fixed nature. You define yourself through choice.

2. Radical Freedom and Responsibility: You are free. Absolutely. But this means you are responsible for what you make of yourself.

3. The Absurd: The world has no meaning. Yet you must live. You must create meaning without ultimate justification.

III. The Ideas

Idea 1: Existence Precedes Essence

You are not born with a nature. You must create yourself.

A paper cutter is made for cutting paper. That is its essence. But you? You are first thrown into existence. Then you make choices. Those choices become your essence. You are radically free—and radically responsible.

Idea 2: Radical Freedom and Bad Faith

You are free. But you often deny your freedom—this is bad faith.

The waiter who says "I am a waiter" as if that were his essence—he is in bad faith. He is hiding from his freedom. The truth is: he chooses, moment by moment, to be a waiter. He could choose otherwise. Recognizing this is authenticity.

Idea 3: The Absurd and Revolt

Meaning is not given. You must create it even though the universe offers none.

This is not nihilism. It is the opposite. It is the recognition that we must create meaning, knowing that ultimate meaning does not exist. This act of creation against the absurd is freedom in its truest form.
