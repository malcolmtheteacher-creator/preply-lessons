The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The experiment

In 1951, a scientist called Solomon Asch did a famous experiment. A student came into a room with seven or eight other people. The student did not know that the others were actors.

The group looked at a card with one line. Then they looked at a second card with three lines: A, B and C. One of the lines was the same length as the first line. The answer was very easy. Everybody had to say which line was the same, one by one.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who were the other people in the room?

2. How easy was the question?

1. Actors; the student did not know.

2. Very easy.

Discuss:
  • Imagine you are the student. How do you feel?

Part 2 — The wrong answers

On some cards, the actors all gave the same wrong answer. The real student was nearly the last person to speak. Now the student had a problem: his eyes said 'B', but everyone else said 'C'.

What did the students do? About three out of four students gave a wrong answer at least once. Only about one in four always gave the right answer. In a test with no group, students nearly never made a mistake.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What problem did the student have?

2. How many students never followed the group?

1. His eyes said one thing, and the group said another.

2. About one in four.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think so many people gave the wrong answer?

Part 3 — Why did they do it?

After the test, the students told Asch why. Some said, 'I thought the group was right and I was wrong.' Others said, 'I knew they were wrong, but I didn't want to look silly.'

Asch also found something important: it helped a lot if one other person gave the right answer. With just one friend, most students felt brave. This means that one honest voice can change a group.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Give one reason students followed the group.

2. What helped students to say the right answer?

1. They thought the group was right, or they were afraid of looking silly.

2. One other person giving the right answer.

Discuss:
  • Have you ever been the 'one voice' in a group? What happened?

Grammar — should, have to, don't have to

Use should for advice, have to for rules, and don't have to when something is not necessary.

The patterns:
• should + verb — advice or opinion: You should say what you think.
• shouldn't + verb — bad idea: You shouldn't follow the crowd.
• have to + verb — it is necessary (rule): The students had to answer one by one.
• don't have to + verb — it is not necessary: You don't have to agree with your friends.
Be careful:
• mustn't = it is forbidden: You mustn't cheat.
• don't have to = it is OK, but not necessary: You don't have to come.
These are different!

Type the missing word or words.

Join or say no?

Five situations. Say what you should do and why. Then reveal an idea.

1. Everyone at work is staying late, but your work is finished. Should you stay too?
Idea. You don't have to stay. But some people stay because they don't want to look lazy.
2. Your friends are laughing at a joke about a classmate. You think it is unkind. What should you do?
Idea. One honest voice can change a group. You could say, 'That's not funny.'
3. Everyone in your class has the same new trainers. Do you have to buy them?
Idea. No, you don't have to. But the pressure can feel strong, especially for young people.
4. A restaurant is full of people. The one next door is empty. Which one should you choose?
Idea. People often follow the crowd. Full means 'good' to many people. But it is not always true!
5. Is it ever good to follow the crowd?
Idea. Yes. In an emergency, following people to the exit can save your life.
