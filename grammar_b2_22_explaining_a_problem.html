Warm-up

Out loud, before anything else — don't worry about mistakes, just talk.

SPEAK

Think of something in your home that's broken, or stopped working properly, at some point in the last year. Say one sentence about it — what was wrong with it?

Listen in at the counter

Customer: Hi — I brought my laptop in last week. It's been overheating and shutting down without warning.

Assistant: Let's see... yes, here it is. It's being checked over today, actually.

Customer: Oh, good. Do you know roughly when it'll be ready?

Assistant: I can't promise a time, but I'll call you the second it's done.

Look at what's happening in this short exchange. Three completely different jobs, done by three different structures. Can you spot all three? Say them before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "it's been overheating" — the problem's history, still going or only just stopped.
2. "it's being checked over" — what's happening to it right now, without naming who's doing it.
3. "Do you know roughly when it'll be ready?" — a real question, wrapped up to sound polite.

Master these three and you can explain almost anything that's gone wrong — and actually find out what happens next.
Today: telling the story of a problem (it's been doing this...), explaining what's happening to it (it's being looked at / it got knocked / it needs...), and asking politely what happens next (Could you tell me... / Do you know if...) — so you can explain something broken and get a real answer, not just "give it here".

How Long Has This Been Happening?

The tense for telling the story of a problem — when it started, and whether it's still going on.

Spot the pattern

  • "My phone's been switching itself off all week."
  • "The washing machine's been making a strange clunking noise since Saturday."
  • "It's been getting louder every time I use it."

What's the shared machinery, and when would you reach for it? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
have / has + been + -ing. Use it for a problem that stretches from a starting point up to now — still happening, or only just stopped. It usually comes with for, since, lately, or all week.
have / has + been + verb-ing  (+ for / since / lately / all week)
Notice the shift between two tenses: "it's been overheating for days" tells the story so far (still going). "This morning it finally died" is the single moment it happened (simple past). Explaining a problem properly usually needs both.

Quick check

1. Your headphones have had a crackling sound in the left ear for about ten days, and it's not stopping. Use the continuous form of crackle:

"My headphones in the left ear for about ten days."

2. The boiler has cut out every evening since the cold snap started, and it's still doing it. Use the continuous form of cut out:

"The boiler every evening since the cold snap started."

3. The dishwasher's door catch has jammed shut on and off for the last fortnight, and it's not getting any better. Use the continuous form of jam:

"The dishwasher's door catch for the last fortnight."

What's Been Done About It?

Once you've explained the problem, this is how you explain what's happened to it — without saying who did what.

Spot the pattern

  • "It's being looked at right now — should be ready by five."
  • "It got knocked off the shelf and the screen cracked."
  • "Apparently it needs oiling — that's what's causing the squeak."

Three different flavours of the same idea. What do they share, and what's different? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
All three put the thing first, because who did it doesn't matter.
is/are being + past participle — happening right now, in progress.
got + past participle — the spoken favourite for a past event, often an accident.
needs + -ing — looks active (it's an -ing form!) but means passive: "it needs oiling" = "it needs to be oiled".
is / are being + past participle  (happening now)
got + past participle  (spoken, a past event)
needs + verb-ing  =  needs to be + past participle
The trap: "it needs oiling", never "it needs oil it" or "it needs to oil". The thing doesn't do the action — the action happens TO it.

Quick check

1. Right now, someone's actually repairing your printer downstairs — it's in pieces on the desk. Use the continuous passive of repair:

"It downstairs at the moment — try not to worry."

2. Your jacket zip jammed halfway up yesterday, right when you were rushing out the door. Tell a friend what happened, using get + stick:

"The zip halfway up yesterday, of all days."

3. Your bike's back light won't turn on any more, and the shop says the battery inside is corroded beyond saving. Explain it to a friend, using need + change:

"Apparently the battery — it's beyond saving."

Could You Tell Me...?

Turning a direct question into a polite, indirect one — useful the moment you need something from someone.

Spot the pattern

  • "Could you tell me when it'll be ready?"
  • "Do you know if it's still under guarantee?"
  • "I was wondering whether you could take a look at it today."

Each one wraps a real question inside a softer frame. What happens to the word order inside the frame? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Inside the frame, the word order goes back to normal statement order — subject before verb — and do / does / did disappear completely. For a yes/no question, if or whether steps in where a question word would normally go.
Could you tell me + question word + subject + verb...?
Do you know + if / whether + subject + verb...?
I was wondering + whether/if + subject + could / would...
The trap: "Could you tell me when will it be ready?" is wrong — the direct question order has leaked into the frame. It should be "Could you tell me when it will be ready?" Same for do-support: not "Do you know does it come with a warranty?" but "Do you know if it comes with a warranty?"

Quick check

1. The shop hasn't said anything about timing, and you want to know when the phone will be ready. Ask politely, starting with "Could you tell me":

"Could you tell me ?"

2. You want to know if this kind of screen damage is covered by the warranty, but you don't want to sound too direct. Start with "Do you know":

"Do you know ?"

3. Ask, as gently as possible, whether the shop could look at your bike gears today. Start with "I was wondering":

"I was wondering ?"
