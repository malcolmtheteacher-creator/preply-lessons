What the test asks of you, what the examiners score, and three tasks that show you where you stand today.

The test at a glance

PartTimeWhat you do
ListeningAbout 30 minutesFour recordings, 40 questions. You hear each recording once.
Reading60 minutesThree long texts, 40 questions. No extra time to copy answers.
Writing60 minutesTask 1: describe a graph, chart, map or process (at least 150 words, about 20 minutes). Task 2: an essay (at least 250 words, about 40 minutes). Task 2 counts for twice as much as Task 1.
Speaking11 to 14 minutesA live conversation with an examiner: Part 1 (familiar topics), Part 2 (a two-minute talk), Part 3 (a deeper discussion).

Each part gets a band from 0 to 9. Your overall band is the average of the four, rounded to the nearest half band. This route is for the Academic test, where Reading and Writing use academic texts and tasks; Listening and Speaking are the same in both versions. Check with your test centre whether you will sit the test on paper or on a computer.

What the examiners score

Listening and Reading are marked by counting correct answers. Writing and Speaking are marked by an examiner on four criteria each. If you know them, you know what to practise.

Writing — four criteria, each worth a quarter

  • Task Achievement / Task Response — did you answer the question that was asked, fully, with a clear position and developed ideas?
  • Coherence and Cohesion — is it organised into clear paragraphs, with ideas that connect?
  • Lexical Resource — a wide range of accurate vocabulary, used naturally, spelled correctly.
  • Grammatical Range and Accuracy — a mix of simple and complex sentences, mostly accurate.

Speaking — four criteria, each worth a quarter

  • Fluency and Coherence — speaking at length without long pauses, and organising what you say.
  • Lexical Resource — a range of vocabulary and the ability to paraphrase.
  • Grammatical Range and Accuracy — a mix of structures, with few errors that block meaning.
  • Pronunciation — clear sounds, stress and intonation that a listener can follow easily.

Where should you focus?

Choose your honest estimate for each part (half bands are fine to round). If you are not sure, choose the lower band.

Keep an error log

A correct answer does not prove you have learned something; a repeated mistake does prove you have not. From today, every time you get something wrong, write one line:

The mistakeWhy it happenedThe fix
The graph show a riseForgot the –s with a singular subjectThe graph shows a rise
Wrote 190 words in Task 2Spent 15 minutes planningPlan for 5 minutes; write for 30
Missed a True/False/Not Given answerUsed my own opinion, not the textFind the exact sentence first

Read your log before every practice session. By the time you reach the mock tests, it will be the most useful page you own.

A good week

  • One writing step (a Task 2 essay type or a Task 1 lesson)
  • One speaking step, with a recording you listen to
  • One reading or listening step, timed
  • Ten minutes of vocabulary or spelling — the side lessons at the end of the route
  • Five minutes with your error log
