The story, in three parts

This is a sad and serious history, and we will treat it with care. Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — A secret weapon and a last warning

By the summer of 1945, the Second World War was nearly over in Europe. But in the Pacific, the war went on. The United States was fighting Japan, and Japan would not surrender. American leaders were afraid that the fighting would go on for a long time, and that very many soldiers would die on both sides.

For years, in secret, the United States had built a new and terrible weapon: the atomic bomb. It was much more powerful than any bomb before. In July 1945, they tested it in the desert, and it worked.

On 26 July 1945, the Allies sent Japan a last warning. They said: surrender now, or face complete destruction. Japan's government did not accept the warning. So the order was given to use the new bomb.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What new weapon did the United States build in secret?

2. What was the last warning to Japan, and how did Japan answer it?

1. A new and very powerful weapon called the atomic bomb. They tested it in the desert in July 1945, and it worked.

2. The warning said: surrender now, or face complete destruction. Japan's government did not accept it.

Talk about it:
  • Why do you think the leaders wanted to end the war quickly? What were they afraid of?

Part 2 — Hiroshima, 8:15 in the morning

On the morning of 6 August 1945, one American plane flew high over the city of Hiroshima. Below, it was a normal day. People were going to work. Children were outside. It was a busy city of about 350,000 people.

At 8:15, the plane dropped one atomic bomb on the city. In a single moment, a huge explosion destroyed the centre of Hiroshima. Tens of thousands of people died at once. Many more died in the hours and days after. Most of them were civilians — ordinary people, not soldiers.

It was something new and terrible in the world: one bomb, from one plane, had destroyed a whole city.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened in Hiroshima at 8:15 in the morning?

2. Who were most of the people who died, and why is this important?

1. An American plane dropped one atomic bomb. A huge explosion destroyed the centre of the city and killed tens of thousands of people.

2. Most were civilians — ordinary people, not soldiers. This is one big reason people still argue about whether the bombs were right.

Talk about it:
  • It was a normal morning until 8:15. Why do you think people remember that exact time?

Part 3 — Nagasaki, and the end of the war

Three days later, on 9 August 1945, a second plane dropped a second atomic bomb. This time the target was the city of Nagasaki. Again, the bomb destroyed much of the city, and tens of thousands of people died.

Now the world had seen two cities destroyed in three days. On 15 August 1945, Japan said it would surrender. The Second World War, the deadliest war in history, was finally over.

But for the people of the two cities, the suffering did not stop. The bombs left behind an invisible danger called radiation. People who lived through the explosion became sick weeks, months, and even years later. The survivors had to build their cities again from nothing.

Today, Hiroshima and Nagasaki are busy, living cities again. Each year, they hold a ceremony for peace, and they hope that such weapons will never be used again. Almost eighty years later, people still ask one hard question: was it right to use the bombs? Some people say yes — the bombs ended the war quickly and saved lives in the end. Other people say no — Japan was already close to losing, and so many civilians should not have been killed. There is no easy answer. The world has carried the question ever since.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened in Nagasaki, and what did Japan do after it?

2. Why did the suffering continue after the war ended?

1. On 9 August 1945, a second bomb destroyed much of Nagasaki. On 15 August, Japan said it would surrender, and the war ended.

2. The bombs left radiation, an invisible danger. People became sick for years afterwards, and the cities had to be rebuilt from nothing.

Talk about it:
  • Every year, the cities hold a ceremony for peace. Why do you think it is important to remember a terrible event like this?

Grammar: should have + past participle

This story ends with a hard question: was it right or wrong? To talk about a past action and say we think it was right or wrong, English uses should have + the past participle. We use it for an opinion or a regret about the past.

How it works:
should have + past participle = the right thing to do, but it did not happen. "They should have given Japan more time." (They did not give more time — we think that was wrong.)
should not have + past participle = it did happen, but we think it was wrong. "Some people think the second bomb should not have been used." (It was used — but they think that was a mistake.)
From the story — examples:
• "Some people think the second bomb should not have been used."
• "Other people think the bombs should have been used to end the war quickly."
• "Many people say that so many civilians should not have died."
In short answers we can say: "I think they should have." / "I think they shouldn't have."

1. Right or wrong? Make the sentence

Use should have or should not have + the past participle. Write the full form.
