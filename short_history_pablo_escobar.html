Pablo Escobar

The petty thief who built the world's richest cartel — and left a country to count the cost.

Today: you'll tell the story of a man who rose from small-time crime to become one of the richest and most feared criminals in history — and you'll master used to and would, the structures English uses to describe the way things repeatedly were and repeatedly happened in the past.
Warm up — before we open the story. Talk each of these through out loud; there are no wrong answers, only ideas to bring with you.
  • When you hear the words "drug lord" or "cartel", what images or stories come to mind — from the news, from films or series?
  • Some criminals are remembered almost as folk heroes in their home region, yet as monsters elsewhere. How can the same person be seen so differently?
  • Can enormous wealth ever make a society forgive the way it was earned? Why are people sometimes tempted to?

Now — the words for today's story

Here are the ten key words you'll meet. They come in four short tasks, each a different kind of puzzle. Do the thinking first, say your answer out loud, and only then reveal to check.

Task 1 · Work it out from the situation

Read each situation aloud. From the situation alone, decide what the bold word means — then reveal.

1. By his thirties his fortune was so vast that his organisation reportedly spent thousands of dollars a month simply on rubber bands to hold the notes together. He had become a billionaire.
a billionaire (n) — a person whose money and property are worth at least a thousand million (a billion).
2. Nothing moved in the open. It went hidden inside cargo, flown at night, passed hand to hand through people paid to keep quiet, across borders where no one was meant to notice. This was smuggling on an industrial scale.
smuggling (n) — secretly and illegally moving goods into or out of a country.
3. Officials who took his money looked the other way; those who refused often did not last long in the job — or in life. Over the years it reached judges, police and politicians alike, until few institutions were clean. This was deep corruption.
corruption (n) — dishonest behaviour by people in power, especially taking bribes or abusing their position.

Task 2 · Read the meaning, recall the word

Each definition describes one word; the first letter is given. Say the word aloud, then reveal to check it and an example.

4. A large, well-organised criminal group that controls an illegal trade across a whole region or country. (starts with c —)
a cartel (n) — a large, organised group that controls an illegal (or restricted) trade. Example: "The cartel controlled most of the country's cocaine."
5. A person paid to kill someone, usually on the orders of a criminal group. (starts with h —)
a hitman (n) — a person paid to murder someone. Example: "He sent hitmen after anyone who crossed him."
6. Money given secretly and dishonestly to make someone in authority do what you want. (starts with b —)
a bribe (n) — money given secretly to persuade someone to act dishonestly. As a verb, to bribe. Example: "The bribe bought the officer's silence."

Task 3 · Synonym or antonym?

Decide before you reveal.

7. ruthless — is this closer in meaning to merciless, or to gentle?
ruthless (adj) — closer to merciless: showing no pity, willing to hurt others to get what you want. Its opposite would be compassionate.
8. notorious — does this mean simply admired, or famous for a bad reason?
notorious (adj) — famous for a bad reason; well known for something shameful or criminal. Not the same as simply "famous".

Task 4 · Choose the meaning in context (a / b / c)

Read the sentence, choose your answer, then reveal the letter and why.

9. "For years the cartel waged a bloody campaign against the state, setting off car bombs in the cities." Here, campaign means:
(a) a happy celebration   (b) a long, organised series of actions aimed at a goal   (c) a short holiday
(b) a campaign (n) — a planned series of actions carried out over time to achieve a particular aim (here, a violent one against the government).
10. "When the police finally moved in, he tried to flee across the rooftops." Here, flee means:
(a) to run away to escape danger   (b) to surrender calmly   (c) to fall asleep
(a) to flee (v) — to run away quickly, especially to escape danger or capture. (Past form: fled.)

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — From the streets of Medellín

Pablo Emilio Escobar Gaviria was born in 1949, near Medellín, in Colombia. His family was not the poorest in the country, but money was tight, and Escobar grew up hungry for far more than his surroundings could offer. Even as a teenager he was drawn to quick, illegal money. He used to run small schemes on the streets — reselling stolen goods, smuggling untaxed cigarettes, and, according to the stories that grew up around him, stealing gravestones to sand down and sell again.

By his twenties he had moved into more serious crime, including car theft and, some accounts say, kidnapping. But the opportunity that would make him one of the richest men alive was only just appearing. In the 1970s, demand for cocaine in the United States was rising fast, and Colombia sat in the perfect position to supply it. Escobar saw the scale of the profit before most of his rivals did, and he moved to control not just a share of the trade but the routes, the labs and the men who ran them.

Out of this grew the Medellín cartel — a network of traffickers who would come to move a large part of the cocaine reaching America. In the early days Escobar would fly small planes of product north himself; later, the operation grew so vast that he was said to lose enormous sums simply to damp and rats eating the stored cash. He was becoming, quite literally, one of the wealthiest criminals the world had ever seen.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What kinds of petty crime was Escobar involved in as a young man?

2. What change in the 1970s allowed him to become enormously rich?

1. Small street schemes — reselling stolen goods, smuggling untaxed cigarettes, and later car theft and other serious crime.

2. Rising demand for cocaine in the United States; Colombia was well placed to supply it, and Escobar moved to control the trade, building the Medellín cartel.

Discuss:
  • Escobar spotted an opportunity that others missed. When a huge illegal market opens up, what makes one person seize it while others hold back?

Part 2 — "Plata o plomo"

At the height of his power, Escobar was astonishingly rich — by some estimates a billionaire many times over. He built a private estate, Hacienda Nápoles, complete with its own zoo, and he cultivated an image as a friend of the poor. He would pay for housing, football pitches and floodlights in Medellín's poorest districts, and to some people there he was a benefactor. He even won a seat in Colombia's Congress for a time, hoping to turn his fortune into respectability and political power.

But that image sat on top of extraordinary violence. Escobar's method for dealing with judges, police, politicians and journalists became infamous under one phrase: "plata o plomo" — "silver or lead", meaning take the bribe or take the bullet. Those who could be bought were bought; those who could not be intimidated or bribed were, again and again, killed. When the Colombian government moved towards allowing traffickers to be extradited to the United States, Escobar turned his fortune into a war against the state.

The years that followed were among the darkest in Colombia's modern history. Car bombs, the assassination of officials, and attacks on public figures killed and wounded very large numbers of people — police officers, politicians, journalists and ordinary bystanders among them. This was not the quiet crime of the shadows; it was open, sustained warfare aimed at forcing the state to give in. Behind the wealth and the carefully built image of a man of the people lay a campaign of terror that scarred an entire country.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Escobar try to build a positive public image?

2. What did the phrase "plata o plomo" mean, and what did Escobar do when the state moved towards extradition?

1. He funded housing, football pitches and services in poor areas of Medellín, presenting himself as a benefactor, and even won a seat in Congress for a time.

2. "Silver or lead" — take a bribe or a bullet. When the state moved towards extradition to the US, he turned his fortune into a violent war against it, including bombings and assassinations.

Discuss:
  • Escobar was both a giver of gifts to the poor and the source of terrible violence. Why might some people have defended him even as the death toll rose?

Part 3 — The fall

By the early 1990s, the pressure on Escobar was closing in from every side. To avoid extradition, he struck an extraordinary deal with the Colombian government: he would surrender and serve time in a prison he himself had helped design and build. Known as "La Catedral", it was less a prison than a fortified compound, and from inside it Escobar continued to run his empire. When the authorities finally moved to transfer him to a real jail in 1992, he simply walked out and disappeared.

Now he was a fugitive, hunted by a special Colombian police unit with intelligence help from abroad and hounded by rival criminals who had turned against him. One by one, the people around him were captured or killed, and his network of protection fell apart. The man who used to move freely through Medellín as its untouchable king was reduced to hiding in ordinary houses, moving constantly, his options shrinking week by week.

On 2 December 1993, the day after his forty-fourth birthday, he was traced to a house in Medellín. In the gunfire that followed as security forces closed in, Pablo Escobar was killed on the rooftops he had been trying to flee across. His death ended one man's reign, but not the trade he had helped build, nor the wounds he left behind. Colombia spent years afterwards counting the cost — the tens of thousands dead across the era of cartel violence, and a society working to recover from what those years had done to it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was unusual about the prison Escobar agreed to enter, and how did his imprisonment end?

2. How did Escobar's life end, and what does the story say about the wider cost?

1. He helped design and build his own prison, "La Catedral", and kept running his empire from inside it; when the state tried to move him to a real jail in 1992, he escaped.

2. As a hunted fugitive, he was tracked to a house in Medellín and killed during a police operation on 2 December 1993. The wider cost was tens of thousands dead and a country left to recover.

Discuss:
  • Escobar's death did not end the drug trade. Why is it often so hard to stop a criminal enterprise even after its most famous leader is gone?

Grammar — used to / would (past habits & repeated past states)

Escobar's story is full of things that repeatedly happened in the past — the way he behaved, the schemes he ran, the way things once were. English has two neat tools for this: used to and would. Master them and you can paint a whole vanished world in a few sentences.

The two structures:
• used to + base verb — a repeated past habit or a past state that is no longer true: "He used to run small schemes on the streets." "Medellín used to be one of the most dangerous cities on earth."
• would + base verb — a repeated past habit or typical action, common in storytelling: "In the early days he would fly the planes north himself." "Every week he would pay for housing in the poorest districts."
The key difference:
• For repeated actions (habits), both work: "He used to fly the planes" = "He would fly the planes."
• For states (be, have, know, live, like — things that don't "happen"), use used to only. We say "He used to be rich," NOT "He would be rich."
• Negatives and questions use did: "He didn't use to be famous." "Did he use to live there?" (no d on use after did).

1. Can you use would here — or must it be used to?

Write the correct form. If the verb is a state, only used to works; if it's a repeated action, either is fine — write used to / would.

1. As a teenager, Escobar resell stolen goods on the streets. (repeated action)

2. Hacienda Nápoles have its own private zoo. (a past state — "have")

3. In the early days, he fly the small planes north himself. (repeated action)

1. used to / would (a repeated action — both work)

2. used to (a state — "have"; "would have" is not used here)

3. used to / would (a repeated action — both work)

2. Fix the form

Each sentence has an error in the used to / would structure. Write the corrected words.

1. He (use) to run small schemes as a boy. (affirmative — spelling)

2. Medellín (would) be one of the world's most dangerous cities. (a state — wrong structure)

3. Escobar didn't (used) to be famous at first. (negative — spelling)

1. used (affirmative: "used to run")

2. used to (a state, so not "would"): "used to be"

3. use (after "didn't", no d: "didn't use to be")

3. Tell it in the past — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with used to or would (choose used to where the verb is a state). Read it aloud once you're done.
