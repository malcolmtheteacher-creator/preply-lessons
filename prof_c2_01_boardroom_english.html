How Boards Operate

Understanding the structure, roles, and conventions of corporate boards at the highest level.

Board Structure and Hierarchy

Modern boards typically comprise a Chair, CEO (often separate from Chair), and a mix of executive and non-executive directors. The Chair ensures effective governance; the CEO leads operations. Non-executives provide independent scrutiny and specialist knowledge.

The Meeting Cycle

  • Full Board Meetings: Typically quarterly, covering strategy, performance, risk, and compliance.
  • Board Committees: Audit, Remuneration, Nomination, Risk — meet between full board meetings.
  • AGM: Annual shareholder meeting to approve results, dividends, and director elections.
  • Emergency Sessions: Called when urgent decisions are needed (e.g., profit warning, acquisition offer).

Decision-Making Authority

Boards delegate operational authority to management but retain oversight of strategy, major acquisitions, capital allocation, and risk. Decisions require majority approval; dissent is recorded in minutes. Directors have a duty to speak up and challenge—silence can constitute complicity.

The Role of the Company Secretary

The Company Secretary ensures legal compliance, maintains board minutes, manages agendas, and acts as the board's advisor on governance matters. They are often the interface between the board and management.

Speaking with Authority in the Boardroom

At C2 level, boardroom language demands precision, diplomacy, and the ability to challenge without creating conflict.

Proposing Motions and Decisions

Formal language:

"I propose that we approve the acquisition of Horizon Ltd on the terms outlined by management. I move that the board vote on this motion."

Challenging Decisions Diplomatically

Expressing concern without aggression:

"I appreciate the strategic logic, but I'd like to raise a concern about execution risk. Have we sufficiently stress-tested our assumptions about market receptivity?"

Requesting clarity before voting:

"Before we move to vote, I'd welcome clarity on the timeline for board approval. Given the complexity of this transaction, might we benefit from a second meeting to review the due diligence findings?"

Chairing the Discussion

Summarising consensus:

"So, to summarise: we are aligned on the strategic direction, but we're asking management to revisit the integration timeline and present revised assumptions at our next session. Is that a fair reflection of the board's position?"

Handling dissent:

"I note that we have two perspectives on this matter. Let's ensure both are recorded in the minutes. The majority supports proceeding; the dissenting view is noted. Shall we move to a formal vote?"

Difficult Conversations

When addressing underperformance or conflict of interest:

"I must flag a potential conflict of interest here. Given my stake in the competing bidder, I shall recuse myself from this discussion and the vote."

Simulation: Emergency Board Meeting

Your company has just issued a profit warning. The share price has fallen 18% in after-hours trading. The Chair calls an emergency board meeting for tomorrow morning.

The Scenario

You are a Non-Executive Director. Management has underestimated cost inflation in supply chains and missed Q3 revenue targets by 12%. They are proposing to:

  1. Cut executive bonuses (but keep base salaries)
  2. Reduce headcount by 8%
  3. Delay planned R&D investment
  4. Suspend dividend payments

You are concerned about: The severity of the headcount reduction, the impact on innovation, and whether management's cost-control plan is adequate for a prolonged downturn.

Your Task (10 minutes)

Prepare and then deliver your opening statement as a Non-Executive Director. Address:

  • Your support for management's response (where genuine)
  • Your specific concerns and questions
  • An alternative proposal or modification to the plan
  • What additional information you need before voting
After your statement, consider: How would you respond if the CEO became defensive? How would you align your concerns with fiduciary duty? What precedent does this decision set for future downturns?
