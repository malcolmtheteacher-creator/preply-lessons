What makes a war 'just' according to Aquinas?

Answer: Just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, last resort, proportionality, discrimination, probability of success.


Are nuclear weapons justified in Christian ethics?

Answer: Most Christian denominations consider WMDs immoral due to indiscriminate harm and disproportionate destruction.


How do Muslims define jihad differently from 'holy war'?

Answer: Jihad is internal spiritual struggle; lesser jihad (military defence) has strict rules. 'Holy war' is Western misinterpretation.

Just War Theory in Detail

Thomas Aquinas' Seven Criteria (13th century)

  1. Just Cause: War must defend against aggression or protect innocent lives, not for power or greed.
  2. Legitimate Authority: Only a state/government can declare war, not individuals or non-state actors.
  3. Right Intention: Motivation must align with the just cause; no hidden motives like economic gain.
  4. Last Resort: All peaceful alternatives (negotiation, mediation, sanctions) must be exhausted.
  5. Probability of Success: Going to war knowing you'll lose unjustly wastes lives.
  6. Proportionality: Benefits of victory must outweigh the harm and cost of war.
  7. Discrimination: Military force must target combatants, not civilians. Civilian casualties must be minimized.

Application to Modern Conflicts

Example 1: Self-Defence (Just War justified)
A nation is invaded without provocation and must defend its citizens. The cause is just, authority is legitimate, and discrimination is possible (target enemy military, not civilians).

Example 2: Nuclear Deterrence (Just War questioned)
Can nuclear weapons satisfy "discrimination"? Likely no—they kill indiscriminately across civilian and military populations. This is why many theologians reject nuclear war as justifiable.

Example 3: Pre-emptive War (Just War rejected)
A nation attacks another because it might pose a future threat. This lacks just cause (no current aggression) and violates last resort. Most Christian ethicists oppose pre-emptive wars.

Holy War and Lesser Jihad in Islamic Tradition

The Crusades (11th–13th centuries)

Christian kingdoms launched military expeditions to reclaim the Holy Land from Muslim rule. Christian justification: Holy War—wars sanctioned by the Church itself. - Pope Urban II declared it divinely ordained - Promised spiritual rewards (forgiveness of sins) - Mixed religious zeal with political/economic motives Modern assessment: Most Christian scholars reject the Crusades as perverted Just War (they lacked discrimination, involved massacres of civilians, and mixed religious with economic motives). The Crusades remain a painful example of religious justification for violence.

Lesser Jihad Conditions (Islamic)

The Qur'an permits military struggle (qital) only under strict conditions: - Defensive only: Fight those who fight you (Surah 22:39) - Clear authority: Caliph/just leader declares it - Just cause: Protect religion, life, property, intellect, family - Discrimination rules: Spare women, children, elderly, monks - No treachery: Keep treaties and agreements - Mercy: Treat prisoners humanely Modern example: Muslim scholars debated 2001 Afghanistan war. Some saw US invasion as aggression; others accepted self-defence against terrorism. All rejected 9/11 attacks on civilians as violating Qur'anic rules.

Weapons of Mass Destruction: Religious Responses

Nuclear Weapons and Christian Teaching

Catholic Position (Post-Vatican II): The Catholic Church officially opposes nuclear weapons as disproportionate and indiscriminate. Vatican II (1962–65) condemned weapons that cause widespread destruction. Pope Francis has called nuclear weapons "immoral and illegal."

Protestant Position: Most Protestant denominations reject nuclear deterrence. The "just war" criteria cannot be met: nukes kill indiscriminately (violate discrimination) and threaten total mutual destruction (violate probability of success and proportionality).

Conclusion: Modern Christianity widely rejects WMDs as immoral because they: - Kill civilians indiscriminately - Cause disproportionate harm exceeding any military goal - Violate the principle of non-combatant immunity

Islamic Responses to WMDs

Surah 2:195: "Do not cast yourselves with your own hands into destruction."

Islamic scholars unanimously reject WMDs as violations of Qur'anic rules: - Indiscriminate killing of civilians (haram/forbidden) - Disproportionate harm without just cause - Violation of mercy principles in Lesser Jihad

Major Islamic organizations (Al-Azhar, Organisation of Islamic Cooperation) have condemned nuclear weapons and biological/chemical weapons as un-Islamic.

Terrorism: Religious and Non-Religious

Definition and Distinction

Terrorism: Deliberate attacks on non-combatants (civilians) to spread fear and achieve political/religious goals. The key issue: targeting civilians.

Key point: Terrorism is NOT the same as military combat or even guerrilla warfare. Even if a cause is just, targeting civilians is universally condemned by both Christian and Islamic ethics.

Religious claim: Some groups (e.g., ISIS, al-Qaeda) justify attacks on civilians as: - Retaliation for Western bombing - Holy war against infidels - Necessary to "wake up" populations

Religious rejection: Mainstream Christian and Muslim scholars reject these justifications: - Civilians are non-combatants—their death is never morally justified - Revenge cycles are un-Islamic (contrary to Surah 42:40) - Terrorism violates Just War and Lesser Jihad principles - Both traditions teach mercy and proportionality

Quick Review: Key Terms

Discrimination (in warfare)

Definition: Just War principle requiring military forces to distinguish between combatants and non-combatants, targeting only armed enemy forces and minimizing civilian casualties. Nuclear and biological weapons violate this principle.

Proportionality (in warfare)

🔒 Tap to reveal

Definition: Just War principle requiring that the expected benefits of war must outweigh the harms caused. Excessive force beyond what's needed to achieve a just goal violates proportionality.

Lesser Jihad

🔒 Tap to reveal

Definition: Islamic concept of defensive military struggle against aggression. Permitted only under strict conditions: legitimate authority, just cause, discrimination rules, and mercy to non-combatants. Distinguished from Greater Jihad (spiritual self-discipline).

Summary: War, Justice, and Weapons

Just War criteria: Both Christian and Islamic traditions developed frameworks for assessing whether war can be morally justified. Key: just cause, legitimate authority, last resort, discrimination, proportionality.

WMDs: Weapons of mass destruction (nuclear, biological, chemical) are rejected by both traditions because they kill indiscriminately and cause disproportionate harm.

Terrorism: Deliberate attacks on civilians are condemned by mainstream Christian and Muslim ethicists as violations of core principles about protecting the innocent.

The challenge: In modern conflicts, it's harder to meet Just War criteria. Insurgencies blend with civilians. Air strikes have "collateral damage." These realities push many theologians toward pacifism.
