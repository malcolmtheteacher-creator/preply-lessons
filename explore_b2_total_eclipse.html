A cosmic coincidence

Here is a strange pair of facts. The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon. The Sun is also about 400 times farther away from us than the Moon.

SPEAK Think for a moment: what do those two numbers mean for how the Sun and Moon look in our sky?

They appear almost exactly the same size in our sky. That is why the Moon can cover the Sun perfectly — hiding the bright disc but leaving the Sun's ghostly outer atmosphere visible around the edge.

No other planet we know of gets this show. It is pure luck — a coincidence of sizes and distances. Some astronomers call Earth's eclipses the greatest free show in the universe.

How a total eclipse works

Three objects, one straight line. When the Moon passes directly between the Sun and the Earth, it blocks the sunlight and throws its shadow onto our planet.

to line upto form a straight line — "The Sun, Moon and Earth line up perfectly."
to block (out)to stop light from passing — "The Moon blocks out the Sun."
a shadowthe dark shape made when light is blocked — "Its shadow races across the Earth."

The puzzle

The Moon travels around the Earth roughly once a month. So it passes between us and the Sun roughly once a month.

SPEAK So why isn't there a solar eclipse every single month? Make a guess — any theory is welcome.

The Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5 degrees. Most months, the Moon passes a little above or a little below the Sun in our sky — and its shadow misses the Earth completely. Only when everything lines up exactly do we get an eclipse.

And even then, the shadow is tiny: the zone of total darkness — the path of totality — is usually a ribbon just 100–300 km wide, racing across the planet at over 2,000 km/h. Stand inside that ribbon and day becomes night. Stand 50 km outside it and you see only a partial eclipse — impressive, but a completely different event.

Lucky timing — in two ways

On average, any single place on Earth sees a total eclipse only about once every 375 years. Whole generations live and die without one passing overhead. That is why people cross oceans to stand in the shadow for two minutes.

The Moon is slowly drifting away from Earth — about 3.8 cm per year, roughly the speed your fingernails grow. In around 600 million years it will look too small to cover the Sun completely, and total eclipses will end forever. We happen to live in the narrow age of the universe when this show is playing.

Ninety seconds of night

People who have stood in the Moon's shadow say photos capture almost nothing of it. Let's walk through what actually happens, minute by minute. For each stage, guess first, then open it.

Through special glasses, you see the Moon take a small dark "bite" out of the Sun's edge. Without glasses you would notice… nothing at all. Life continues. Most people walking past have no idea anything is happening.

SPEAK Why do you think nobody notices this stage without glasses?

The light becomes weak and silvery — like someone slowly turning down a dimmer switch on the whole world. Colours look wrong. Shadows become unnaturally sharp. The temperature starts to fall — it can drop by 10 degrees or more. A strange wind sometimes picks up.

SPEAK Guess before you read on: how do you think animals react to this false evening?

Birds fly home to their nests and fall silent. Crickets begin their evening song. Cows lie down. Flowers start to close. During the 2017 eclipse in the USA, observers even reported fireflies switching on in the sudden darkness. The animals are not confused for long — they simply follow the light, and the light says "night".

SPEAK Which of these animal reactions would you most like to witness? Why?

Faint ripples of light and dark — shadow bands — sometimes race across the ground. Then the last point of sunlight blazes through a valley on the Moon's edge: one brilliant jewel of light on a thin silver circle. This is the famous diamond ring. It lasts a heartbeat or two.

SPEAK Why "diamond ring"? Describe the picture in your own words.

The diamond vanishes — and the sky collapses into deep twilight in seconds. Where the Sun was, there is now a black hole in the sky, surrounded by the corona: the Sun's outer atmosphere, a white crown of light invisible on every other day of your life. Bright stars and planets appear. Around the whole horizon, in every direction, glows the orange light of sunset — a 360-degree sunset.

People cheer, gasp, go completely silent — and many, without planning to, cry. Then, two minutes later, the diamond ring flashes out on the other side, and daylight floods back like a wave.

SPEAK Totality is the ONLY moment it is safe to look with bare eyes. The rest of the time, eclipse glasses — always. Why do you think the partial phases are actually the dangerous ones?

What people believed — guess before you open

Many in ancient China described a celestial dragon devouring the Sun. The response was practical: make as much noise as possible — drums, shouting, banging pots — to frighten the dragon away.

And here's the trap: it worked every single time. The noise was made, and the Sun always came back. Perfect evidence — for the wrong theory.

In Norse mythology, a giant sky-wolf called Sköll hunts the Sun across the heavens. An eclipse was the moment the wolf finally caught it. People made noise to scare the wolf into spitting the Sun back out.

According to the ancient historian Herodotus, two armies — the Lydians and the Medes — were in the middle of a battle when day suddenly turned to night. Both sides stopped fighting, took it as a sign from the gods, and agreed peace after years of war. Because astronomers can calculate eclipses backwards, we can date that battle precisely: 28 May, 585 BC — possibly the earliest exact date in human history.

Einstein's theory said gravity should bend light — but starlight passing the Sun can normally never be seen. During the total eclipse of 1919, astronomers photographed stars right beside the darkened Sun and found their light shifted exactly as Einstein predicted. Newspapers around the world carried the story, and an unknown physicist woke up world-famous. Two minutes of darkness changed science forever.

Talking about the past you can't be sure of

How do we talk about the feelings of people who died thousands of years ago? English has a precise tool for it:

must have + past participle → you're almost certain it was true
"They must have been terrified — the Sun was dying in front of them."

might have + past participle → it's possible, you're not sure
"The soldiers might have seen it as a message from the gods."

can't have + past participle → you're almost certain it was NOT true
"A dragon can't have eaten the Sun — it always came back."

In Spain the path crossed cities from A Coruña and Oviedo to Zaragoza, Valencia and Palma de Mallorca — with totality arriving in the golden light of evening, the Sun hanging just above the horizon.
