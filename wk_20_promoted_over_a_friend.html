Promoted Over a Friend

You got the job. So did she — she just didn't get it. She's been your friend for nine years, she doesn't know yet, and by this afternoon you'll be her manager.

The Scene 🎬

The email came at ten past nine. You read it three times, sat still for a bit, and then remembered the other thing — that Ruth applied too, that you both knew she was going to, and that she is going to find out today whether you tell her or not. HR sends the announcement round at four. You have until then.

This is a conversation almost nobody handles well, and the reason is that you're carrying two things at once that don't fit together. You're pleased, honestly pleased — you wanted this job and you worked for it. And you're about to hurt someone you've had nine years of Friday drinks with, someone who once drove you to hospital at two in the morning. Faced with that, most people do one of two things: they shrink the good news until it sounds like an apology, or they go brisk and businesslike and never mention it again. Both make it worse, and both take about a year to recover from.

Across the table: Ruth. Nine years. And from four o'clock, someone whose holiday requests you sign off.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Have you ever had good news you couldn't enjoy, because of who it was bad news for? What did you do with it — tell people anyway, play it down, say nothing at all?
  2. Think of a friendship that survived one of them being promoted over the other, or one that didn't. What actually made the difference, as far as you could see from the outside?

Who's who

You
The One Who Got It 📩

You're pleased and you're dreading this at the same time, and neither feeling is going to wait politely for the other. Your job today isn't to make Ruth feel better. It's to tell her the truth quickly, and then hand her some control over what happens next.

The other voice
Ruth Aiken 🥃

Your friend of nine years. She applied for the same job, she didn't get it, and she doesn't know yet. She won't shout. She'll be generous first, because that's her — and then somewhere underneath it she'll be genuinely hurt, and she won't be sure which of those she means.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't follow the plot — you already know the plot. Watch the order: how fast the news gets said, how little the speaker defends themselves, and how much of it is spent giving Ruth permission rather than reassurance.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It isn't smooth, and it isn't meant to be. Notice that the speaker never once explains why they deserved it.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Ruth says the job itself isn't really the problem. So what is, in her words, roughly?

2. She names two things she doesn't want from you. What are they?

3. She asks you not to have this conversation today. What day does she pick instead, and what reason does she give for putting it off?

1. It's the nine years, and the fact that you're now on the other side of the desk. She isn't upset about losing a job; she's upset that a friendship has quietly turned into a reporting line, which is a much harder thing to be upset about, because there's nobody to blame for it.
2. She doesn't want you being careful with her, and she doesn't want you handing her the easy jobs to make yourself feel better. Both are the same request underneath — don't manage her guiltily, because she'll notice, and it will insult her.
3. Thursday. Her reason is that today she'd say something she didn't mean and then have to live with it — which is her protecting the friendship rather than avoiding you, although from the outside those two look identical.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for good news that lands badly. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick, and this is a conversation you want to have already had once.

Say it like you mean it. The tone here is flat, warm and unhurried — the voice you'd use to tell someone their train's been cancelled, not the voice you'd use to make a toast. Don't let your voice go soft at the ends of the sentences, because that's the sound of pity and she'll hear it before she hears the words. Say the news, then stop talking and let her have the silence, however long it runs on for.

🚪 Getting it said

Phrase 1

You're about to tell her. Which opening makes it clear that you chose to come and find her, rather than being caught out by the announcement at four?

A. "Have you checked your inbox this morning?"
B. "I wanted you to hear it from me, not from the email."
C. "There's something I should probably mention before it goes round."
B — "I wanted you to hear it from me, not from the email."
It does one job before the news arrives, which is to tell her you came on purpose. A makes her find it out for herself while you stand and watch, which is unkind. C sounds like you're clearing something off a list, because "should probably mention" is what people say about a parking ticket.
Phrase 2

Now the news itself. She already knows what's coming — you can see it in her face. Which version doesn't make her do the work of decoding you?

A. "So they've, um, they've decided to go with me, apparently, for the role, if that makes sense."
B. "It looks like the decision may have gone in my favour."
C. "I got it."
C — "I got it."
Three words, and she's out of the waiting. A and B are both padded, and the padding isn't for her — it's for you, because a long sentence delays the moment you have to look at her face. Hedging bad news makes the other person work out what you're saying while they're already braced for it, which is a small cruelty dressed up as tact.

🪧 Being straight about your own side of it

Phrase 3

You're pleased. Pretending otherwise would be a lie, and she'd spot it. Put it in order:

because I am.pretendI'm not going toI'm not pleased,
"I'm not going to pretend I'm not pleased, because I am."
The two negatives are doing real work, because what you're refusing is to perform a smaller version of your own good news. If you play it down in front of her, you're asking her to comfort you about the thing that has just hurt her, which is a strange favour to ask. Owning it plainly is the more respectful move, although it takes more nerve.
Phrase 4

You want to admit that this conversation has been hanging over you since six this morning — without asking her to feel sorry for you. Complete the chunk: "I'd be ___ if I said I hadn't been dreading this."

"I'd be lying if I said I hadn't been dreading this."
"I'd be lying if I said…" is a fixed way of admitting something you'd rather not admit, and it works because it makes the honesty the point rather than the feeling. Say it once and move on. Say it twice and it turns into a request for sympathy, which is not yours to ask for today.

🫱 Leaving her room to not be fine

Phrase 5

She's just congratulated you, generously, about four seconds after finding out. You don't quite believe her, and nor does she. Which reply gives her permission rather than instructions?

A. "Don't be upset — you'll get the next one."
B. "You don't have to be nice about it."
C. "I hope this isn't going to be awkward between us."
B — "You don't have to be nice about it."
It quietly says: I can see you're being generous, and you're allowed to stop. A tells her how to feel and then promises her something you can't promise. C is the worst of the three, because "I hope this isn't going to be awkward" hands her the job of reassuring you, while she's the one who has just lost something.
Phrase 6

You want to say that she's allowed to be cool and distant with you for a while, and that you won't take it personally. Put it in order:

for a bit,be off with me.If you need tobe off with me
"If you need to be off with me for a bit, be off with me."
To be off with someone is to be cold or short with them without ever saying why — the friend who answers in one word and doesn't ask how you are. Repeating the words in the second half turns the sentence from a worry into an offer, and "for a bit" is doing something too, because it says you expect this to end.

🔄 The part where you're now her manager

Phrase 7

By four o'clock the friendship has a reporting line running through it, and you genuinely haven't worked out how that's going to work. Say it out loud, in one sentence — the honest one, with no plan attached to the end of it.

"I don't know how to be your mate and your manager yet, if I'm honest."
The strength of it is the word "yet", which admits the problem without pretending you've already solved it. Anyone who claims on day one that they've worked out how to be both is either lying or hasn't thought about it, and Ruth will know which. "If I'm honest" on the end keeps it a confession rather than an announcement.
Phrase 8

She's braced for you to start behaving like her boss this afternoon. Take that away. The missing word is the verb hiding inside your new job title: "I'm not going to start ___ you today."

"I'm not going to start managing you today."
It gives her back the rest of the day as a friend, and it's a promise small enough that you can actually keep it. Notice what it doesn't say — it doesn't say never, because never would be a lie and she'd hear the lie in it. Today is the only thing you can honestly offer, so offer today.

🎤 Handing her the pen

Phrase 9

You want her to set the terms — to name the things that would make this worse — instead of leaving her to raise them one at a time over the next six months. Which line asks her to do that?

A. "Let me know if you ever need anything."
B. "I'll try not to get on your nerves."
C. "Tell me what you don't want from me."
C — "Tell me what you don't want from me."
It's a real question with a real answer, and it puts her in charge of a situation she didn't choose. A is the open offer that everyone makes and nobody ever takes up, because it leaves all the work with her. B is a promise about yourself, dressed up as consideration for her.
Phrase 10

She's holding something back, and you'd rather have it now than in fragments over the next twelve months. Put it in order:

each otherI'd ratherSay what you want.for a year.than tiptoe roundhave it out
"Say what you want. I'd rather have it out than tiptoe round each other for a year."
To have it out with someone is to say the whole unsaid thing in one go, properly, rather than in pieces; to tiptoe round someone is to keep choosing your words carefully for months so that the subject never comes up. Naming the year is what makes it land, because that's the real alternative and you both know it.

Your Turn 🎭

Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then write them. Use the timer — the announcement goes out at four, and a conversation like this only gets worse the longer you circle it.

The Twist

🎯 The favour

You did it well. You gave her space, she asked for Thursday, you were going to give her Thursday. Then at half two she comes and finds you, and she's very quiet, and she asks you for one thing: hold the announcement till Monday, and let her tell people she withdrew her application. It costs you nothing. It's nearly true — she nearly did withdraw it. It would let her walk out of the building on Friday with her dignity in one piece. And it is, however small, a lie you'd be telling the whole floor on your first day of running it, about a decision that wasn't even yours. Start the timer. She's standing in front of you. Say yes, say no, or find the third thing — but decide out loud, to her face, and don't hide behind "it's not up to me".

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said out loud. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I got the news out in the first few seconds instead of building up to it.
  • I said I was pleased without shrinking it into an apology.
  • I let her be cold with me without trying to talk her out of it.
  • I asked her a real question about what she wants, and then I stopped talking.
  • When the favour came, I gave her a straight answer instead of blaming someone above me.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that costs you a friendship one day. Run the twist again and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

The friendship doesn't usually end in the conversation. It ends over the six months afterwards, in the meetings where you're slightly too fair to her, and the drinks she stops coming to because she can't work out what she's still allowed to complain about.

Which is why the useful thing you can do today isn't comfort, and it certainly isn't modesty. It's to say the true thing quickly, and then hand her some control — over when you talk, over how you treat her, over what she's allowed to be angry about.

Some of these friendships survive it and some don't, and a fair amount of that was never yours to decide. What's yours is whether she heard it from you, and whether you were honest in the ten minutes afterwards. That's a small thing. It's also most of it.

Two bigger questions

  1. Is it possible to manage a close friend fairly — or does one of the two relationships always quietly win? Think of someone you've watched try it. What did it cost them, and did they ever notice?
  2. When you're given good news that hurts someone near you, how much of the urge to play it down is kindness, and how much is you trying not to feel guilty? Can the other person tell the difference?
