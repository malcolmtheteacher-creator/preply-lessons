At the Hairdresser's

You know exactly what you want. The scissors have other ideas. Somewhere between the two, you'll need every polite word you own.

The Scene 🎬

You've got a big week ahead — a wedding, a job interview, a first date, take your pick — and the mirror this morning was not on your side. So you've booked in at a smart little salon two streets from home, where you've never been before and nobody knows your hair.

You know, more or less, what you want: a proper tidy-up, a little shape, but nothing that will make people ask if everything's alright at home. The tricky part isn't the haircut — it's getting the picture in your head to travel, word for word, into someone else's hands. And then, if the scissors start heading somewhere you didn't agree to, saying so before it's too late, without turning the mood sour.

In the chair beside the mirror: a stylist who is warm, chatty, quick with the scissors, and — like most good stylists — quietly convinced they know what suits you better than you do.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. What's the worst haircut you've ever walked out with — and did you say anything at the time, or smile, pay, and quietly plan never to return?
  2. Some people chat happily to their hairdresser for an hour; others would rather be left in silence with a magazine. Which are you — and why do you think the small talk in that chair feels different from anywhere else?

Who's who

You
The Customer 💺

Clear about what you want, hopeless at describing it. Determined to leave with the haircut in your head — and to stop the one you don't want before it's a fact of life.

The other voice
The Stylist ✂️

Friendly, confident, mid-story about their holiday. Genuinely wants you happy — but happy to get creative the moment your instructions go the least bit vague.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how the customer stays specific, keeps the chat easy, and lands three gentle corrections without a single sharp word.

StylistHiya! Come on through, have a seat. Right — so what are we thinking today? Big change, or just a freshen-up?
YouNothing dramatic, honestly — just a tidy-up. I'd like to keep the length, more or less, but it's got very heavy, so maybe take some weight out of it? If that makes sense.
StylistMakes perfect sense. So we keep it long, thin it out through here, tidy the ends. Were you thinking we'd touch the fringe at all?
YouJust out of my eyes, if you could — but no shorter than that, if possible. I know it won't look exactly like this, but something along these lines.
StylistOoh, lovely, that'll really suit you. Leave it with me. So — going anywhere nice on your holidays this year?
YouHa, I wish — nothing booked yet, we keep meaning to. What about you? You look like you've caught the sun.
StylistPortugal, two weeks, don't get me started, I'm not over it. Right, I'll just start taking a bit of length off the back to balance it out…
YouSorry to stop you — could we go easy on the length at the back? I'd really like to hang on to as much of it as we can.
Stylist'Course, no problem at all — I'll just take the very ends off, then. It does need it, mind, it's a bit straggly down here.
YouThat's fine, tidy away — I just panicked for a second there! Sorry.
StylistDon't apologise, better you say than sit there fretting. How's the fringe looking to you — enough out of your eyes?
YouDo you know, I think that might be a touch shorter than I had in mind. Could we leave it a little longer, if it's not too late?
StylistIt's fine, there's plenty there — I'll blend it so it sits longer. You won't notice in a day or two, I promise.
YouPerfect. Actually, now I see it in the light — would you mind taking a little more off this side, just to even it up?
StylistGood eye — you're right, it's sitting heavy. There. Better? Have a proper look while I hold the back up for you.
YouOh, that's exactly what I wanted — you've read my mind. Honestly, I'm thrilled. Thank you so much.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The customer arrives wanting to "keep the length" — yet by the end they've agreed the ends can be tidied. What changed their mind, and how did the stylist bring them round without a row?

2. The customer makes three separate corrections during the cut. Roughly what are they — and what do they all have in common in the way they're worded?

3. "I just panicked for a second there! Sorry." Why does the customer say this, and what does it do for the atmosphere in the chair?

1. The stylist explained the back was "a bit straggly" and genuinely needed the very ends off — so the customer let the ends go while still protecting the overall length. The stylist agreed to the request first ("'Course, no problem at all"), then gave the reason, so it felt like advice, not a fight.
2. One: go easy on the length at the back. Two: leave the fringe a little longer. Three: take a little more off one side to even it up. All three are softened — "Sorry to stop you…", "I think that might be…", "would you mind…?" — and all catch the problem early, before it's permanent.
3. It's a light bit of self-deprecation that takes any sting out of the interruption. By laughing at their own worry, the customer keeps the stylist on-side — the correction lands, but the warmth stays.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for saying exactly what you want — and rescuing it when it starts to slip away — without ever souring the room. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

✂️ Saying what you want

Phrase 1

You want a tidy-up, not a transformation — and you want to signal that from the first second, so the stylist doesn't get ambitious. Which line sets the right expectation?

A. "Do whatever you think is best."
B. "Nothing dramatic — just a tidy-up."
C. "I want it short."
B — "Nothing dramatic — just a tidy-up."
A hands over all control (dangerous); C is so vague it could mean anything. B draws the boundary gently but clearly. Useful cousins: "just a freshen-up", "nothing too drastic", "keep it more or less as it is".
Phrase 2

Your hair feels thick and heavy, and you want it thinned rather than shortened. There's a set phrase hairdressers know instantly: "keep the length, but ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ it."

"…take some weight out of it."
Exactly the words a stylist wants to hear — it means thin it and lighten it without losing length. Pair it with a vague-but-honest "…if that makes sense", which invites them to translate your words into their craft.
Phrase 3

You've brought a photo, but you know your hair won't magically become the photo. How do you show it while quietly lowering the stakes, so nobody's set up to fail?

"I know it won't look exactly like this, but something along these lines."
"Along these lines" (or "something in this sort of direction") says use this as a guide, not a contract. It gives the stylist room, and spares you both the awkwardness of an impossible promise.
Phrase 4

The fringe matters to you: out of your eyes, but no shorter. Add the two-word tag that turns a firm limit into a polite request: "Just out of my eyes — but no shorter than that, ___ ___."

"…if possible."
"If possible" (or "if you can") lets you set a hard limit while still sounding easy-going — you're drawing a line without making it feel like an order.

☕ Keeping the chat easy

Phrase 5

The famous question lands: "Going anywhere nice on your holidays?" You've nothing booked — but you don't want to kill the chat stone dead. What's the warm, honest deflection that keeps it alive?

"Ha, I wish — nothing booked yet. What about you?"
"I wish" answers honestly and lightly, then the question bounces straight back so they carry the conversation. Bouncing it back ("What about you?", "How about yourself?") is the engine of all easy small talk.
Phrase 6

You need to interrupt mid-snip — always a delicate moment. Put the pieces in order to make the gentlest possible cut-in:

go easycould weon the lengthsorry to stop you —at the back?
"Sorry to stop you — could we go easy on the length at the back?"
"Sorry to stop you" buys permission to interrupt; "could we…" makes it a shared decision, not a complaint. "Go easy on…" is a lovely soft way to say please don't overdo it.

🪞 When it's going wrong

Phrase 7

The fringe has gone shorter than you wanted. You need to flag it without accusing anyone. Soften "that's too short" into a hedged, blameless version — the sort that starts "I think that might be…". What's the full line?

"I think that might be a touch shorter than I had in mind."
Three cushions in one sentence: "I think", "might be", and "a touch". "Than I had in mind" keeps it about your picture, not their mistake — so nobody has to feel got-at.
Phrase 8

You want them to change course, but you're worried the moment's passed. Which version makes the request AND checks it's still fixable, most gracefully?

A. "You've cut it wrong, fix it."
B. "Could we leave it a little longer, if it's not too late?"
C. "I don't like it."
B — "Could we leave it a little longer, if it's not too late?"
A blames; C sulks without a solution. B proposes the fix ("leave it a little longer") and hands them a graceful exit ("if it's not too late") — which, oddly, makes them keener to help.

🌟 The mirror moment — and a proper goodbye

Phrase 9

Near the end you spot one side sitting heavier than the other. Build the polite fix-it request from the pieces:

off this side,would you mindto even it up?taking a little more
"Would you mind taking a little more off this side, to even it up?"
"Would you mind + -ing" is one of the most polite request frames in English. "A little more" keeps it small and doable; "to even it up" gives the friendly reason, so it reads as teamwork, not criticism.
Phrase 10

It's perfect. You want the stylist glowing as you leave — genuine delight beats a mumbled "thanks". Which closing line does that best?

A. "Yeah, that's fine."
B. "It's acceptable, thank you."
C. "That's exactly what I wanted — you've read my mind. Thank you so much."
C — "That's exactly what I wanted — you've read my mind. Thank you so much."
A is flat; B sounds like a school report. C praises the result and the person ("you've read my mind") — the kind of warm, specific thanks that gets you remembered, and looked after next time.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole visit out loud using all ten, in order — say what you want → keep the chat easy → catch it going wrong → the mirror fix → a warm goodbye. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your chair, your hair, your rules. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer — a real haircut doesn't pause while you find the words.

The Twist

💇 The cut you can't take back

One more round — no script, no safety net. It's the morning of the big event, and you asked for a trim. But you glance up mid-cut and realise the stylist has misheard you completely: a good deal more is already gone than you ever agreed to, and it can't come back. Start the timer and talk your way through it: name the problem calmly, find out what can still be done today, ask what they'd honestly recommend to make the best of it — and settle the awkward question of the bill — all without a single raised voice or a scene. One rule: the moment you turn rude or accusing, the stylist stops helping you and starts defending themselves. Stay warm, and you might just walk out looking better than you feared.

The Echo 🕰️

For hundreds of years, a Japanese samurai's haircut wasn't a matter of taste — it was his identity. The chonmage, that precise oiled topknot, announced his rank, his class and his code the moment he walked into a room. You did not improvise with a samurai's hair.

So when the modernising Meiji government told men to cut their topknots off, it wasn't just a new fashion — it felt like being asked to stop being who you were. Some men wept. A haircut, it turns out, is never only a haircut.

Which is why the stakes in that salon chair feel oddly high. You're not just choosing a length. You're handing a stranger the scissors — and asking them to hand back the person you had in mind.
