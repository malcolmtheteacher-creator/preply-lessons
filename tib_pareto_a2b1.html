The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — The man who counted

Vilfredo Pareto was born in Paris in 1848. His father was Italian, his mother was French. First he was an engineer: he worked with trains and with metal. He was clever, and he loved to argue about politics.

Then, when he was about forty-five, he changed his job completely. He became an economist — a person who studies money — at a university in Switzerland.

At that time, people argued a lot about money: who has it, and who should have it. Pareto did something different. He did not argue. He collected numbers about land and money in different countries.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Pareto's first job?

2. What did he become later?

3. What did he do that was different?

1. He was an engineer (trains and metal).

2. An economist — a person who studies money.

3. He collected real numbers instead of only arguing.

Discuss:
  • Is it better to argue with opinions or with numbers? Why?

Part 2 — A strange pattern

The numbers surprised him. In country after country, the picture was the same and very uneven: a small number of people owned most of the land and most of the money.

The famous version of the story says this: in Italy, 20% of the people owned 80% of the land.

Many books add a nice story about his garden: 20% of his pea plants gave him 80% of the peas. It is a lovely story, but there is no good evidence for it. It is probably not true.

Pareto wrote about money. He died in 1923, long before anybody said the words “the 80/20 rule”.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What pattern did Pareto find?

2. What is the famous version of the story?

3. Is the garden story true?

1. A small number of people had most of the land and money.

2. In Italy, 20% of people owned 80% of the land.

3. Probably not — there is no good evidence.

Discuss:
  • Why do people like the garden story so much?

Part 3 — The few that matter

Fifty years later, an American engineer called Joseph Juran worked in a big factory. His job was to check products for problems.

He noticed something. The problems were uneven too. A few causes made most of the problems. If the factory fixed those few causes first, most of the problems disappeared.

Juran needed a name for this idea. He remembered Pareto's numbers, and he called it the Pareto principle: the vital few and the useful many. Later, he said honestly that Pareto had never said this about everything — he had only written about money.

Today people use the rule everywhere: in shops, in sport, in schools. But remember: it is a helpful guide, not a law. Sometimes it is 70/30, sometimes 90/10.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Juran notice in the factory?

2. What name did he give the idea?

3. Is it always exactly 80/20?

1. A few causes made most of the problems.

2. The Pareto principle — “the vital few and the useful many”.

3. No. Sometimes it is 70/30 or 90/10.

Discuss:
  • Where do you see this pattern in your life?

How the tool works

Put your problems in order, from big to small. The bars show how many. The red line adds them up. The first few bars are usually most of the problem — the vital few.

Using it, step by step

  1. Count something real: problems, complaints, hours, money.
  2. Put the causes in order: the biggest first.
  3. Add the numbers until you have about 80 out of 100.
  4. Fix those first. Then count again — the picture will be different.

Try it yourself

A small shop had 200 returns (people bringing things back) last month. Which causes are the vital few — the big ones — and which are the small many?

1. Wrong size — 110 returns
2. The customer changed their mind — 12 returns
3. Broken in the post — 16 returns
4. The colour was different on the website — 50 returns
5. It arrived late — 10 returns
6. Bad quality — 2 returns

1. Vital few — more than half of everything.

2. Small many — only 12 of 200.

3. Small many — 16 of 200 — not in the top two.

4. Vital few — 50 + 110 = 160 of 200 = 80%.

5. Small many — 10 of 200.

6. Small many — only 2 — but if it is dangerous, fix it anyway!

Where the tool breaks

It is not a law. Don't push your numbers into 80/20.

Small can be serious. One dangerous problem is more important than a hundred small ones.

Be careful with people. “Most of our money comes from a few customers” can mean a shop stops caring about everybody else.

Grammar — most, a few, a lot of

This rule is about how many and how much. These are the words you need.

Countable things (chairs, people, problems): a few, many, a lot of, most.
“A few customers complained.”  “Most people agree.”
Uncountable things (water, money, time, information): a little, much, a lot of, most.
“I have a little time.”  “Most of the money went on rent.”
a few = some (it's OK).  few = not many (it's a problem).
“I have a few friends here.” 🙂  “I have few friends here.” 🙁
most or most of?
General: “Most students have a phone.”  This group: “Most of the students in my class have a phone.”
Careful: use much mainly in questions and negatives. “I don't have much time.”  “Do you drink much coffee?”
