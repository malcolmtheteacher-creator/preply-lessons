The Viva

Defend your research. Answer questions you haven't prepared for. Stay in the room.

The Room

You're sitting across from two or three examiners. You've spent 3-5 years on this research. They've read your thesis once, maybe twice. They haven't lived with it like you have. For 90 minutes, they'll ask you to defend, clarify, and contextualise what you've done. Some questions you'll have prepared for. Some you won't. Your job is to stay calm, listen carefully, and answer honestly.

Warm-up Question 1

This is the opening salvo. You have 2 minutes to summarise 3-5 years of work. The examiners want to know: (1) What's your research question? (2) Why does it matter? (3) What did you find? No detail. Just the headline.

Tell me about your research in 2 minutes
2:00

Warm-up Question 2

This catches people. Most students choke on this one. You have 90 seconds. The examiners want intellectual honesty, not defensiveness.

What's the most significant weakness in your study?
1:30

Why These Questions Matter

Examiners aren't trying to trap you. They want to know:

  • Can you articulate your contribution clearly?
  • Are you aware of your study's limitations?
  • Can you defend methodological choices?
  • Can you stay calm under pressure?

A viva is not a test of what you know — you're the expert in your research. It's a test of whether you can communicate that expertise to intelligent people who know less about your specific topic than you do.

1. The Research Summary (2-minute pitch)▼

What it is: A clear, compelling summary of your research that a smart person unfamiliar with your field can understand.

Real example

"I studied how university students develop academic writing confidence over their first year. Most research assumes writing ability develops through instruction and practice — but I found that peer feedback and community matter more than formal teaching. I interviewed 40 students across three institutions, tracking their writing confidence monthly, and analysed their feedback experiences. Key finding: students who received sustained peer feedback showed 60% greater confidence gains than those who received only instructor feedback. This challenges current university writing support models, which typically centralise expert feedback rather than peer community."

Why it works

You've stated the question (what do students develop?), the gap in existing research (we thought X, but actually Y), your method (interviews, tracking, analysis), and your key finding (with a concrete number). An intelligent non-specialist could understand every word.

2. Presenting Methodology Confidently▼

What it is: Speaking about your methods with authority and appropriate caution. You made deliberate choices. Own them.

Weak version

"I kind of did interviews? And I sort of analysed them qualitatively. Maybe thematic analysis or something."

Strong version

"I used semi-structured interviews as my primary method. The rationale was that I needed to capture the lived experience of students, not just numerical confidence scores — I needed to understand how and why confidence develops. I conducted 40 interviews across the first year, at four time points: September, December, March, June. I analysed them using thematic analysis, specifically following Braun and Clarke's six-phase model. Two coders independently coded 20% of interviews to ensure reliability."

Why it works

You've stated your method (interviews), your rationale (captures lived experience), your scope (40, four timepoints), your analysis approach (Braun & Clarke), and your quality assurance (independent coding). You sound like you know what you did and why.

3. Discussing Limitations Honestly▼

What it is: Acknowledging what your study doesn't do — without undermining its central findings.

Weak version

"My study has no limitations. It's perfect."

Strong version

"My study is limited by several factors. First, my sample was drawn from three UK universities — all Russell Group institutions — so findings may not generalise to other higher education contexts. Second, I measured confidence through self-report interviews, which is subject to social desirability bias. However, these limitations don't affect the central finding because [explain why]. What they do mean is that the mechanism I've identified — peer feedback effects — should be tested in more diverse institutional contexts before scaling recommendations."

Why it works

You've identified specific limitations (sample scope, measurement method), acknowledged what they mean (limited generalisability, potential bias), explained why they don't undermine your core finding, and suggested how future research should address them. This shows intellectual sophistication.

4. Handling Questions You Can't Answer▼

What it is: The examiner asks something you haven't thought through. You don't know the answer. What do you do? Don't panic. Don't make something up.

Weak version

Silence. Sweating. "Um... I... I guess maybe?"

Strong version

Examiner: "Did you consider longitudinal follow-up to see if confidence gains persist after graduation?"

You: "That's an interesting question, and I acknowledge I didn't include that in my design. What I can say is that my research captures confidence development across the critical first-year period, where the most significant gains occur. Whether those gains persist is a valuable next question — but it would require a separate longitudinal study tracking graduates over several years. That's beyond the scope of my current research, but it's certainly something I'd be interested in pursuing."

Why it works

You've acknowledged the question, stated what you can address (your actual scope), explained why you didn't do what they suggested (it would require a different study), and positioned it as a valuable future direction rather than a weakness. You're not defensive — you're realistic about your study's scope.

5. Defending Contested Choices▼

What it is: An examiner questions why you did X instead of Y. You need to defend that choice rationally.

Real example

Examiner: "Why did you use thematic analysis rather than grounded theory? GT would have allowed you to build theory from the ground up."

You: "That's a fair point. I chose thematic analysis over grounded theory because my research question was exploratory but not necessarily theory-generating. I wanted to understand how peer feedback shapes confidence, but I already had a theoretical framework from psychology literature — the social development theories of Vygotsky and Wenger. My goal was to apply those lenses to empirical data, not to generate entirely new theory. Thematic analysis allowed me to do that. If my goal had been to develop novel theory about confidence development, grounded theory would have been more appropriate. But given my framing, thematic analysis was the right choice."

Why it works

You've acknowledged the alternative, explained why you chose your method (matched your research question), and shown that you understand when the alternative would be better. You're not being defensive — you're being deliberate.

6. Contextualising Your Contribution▼

What it is: Explaining what your research adds to the field. What's new? What changes because of your work?

Weak version

"I studied something about writing confidence."

Strong version

"My research contributes to the field in three ways. First, it challenges the instructor-centred model of writing development — current best practice assumes that expert feedback is the engine of learning. I've shown that peer community is equally or more important, which shifts where universities should invest resources. Second, it provides empirical evidence for social learning theory in higher education contexts — we've known this theory was important, but few studies have tested it in writing development specifically. Third, it offers a practical model that universities can adopt: structured peer feedback communities are cheaper to scale than one-to-one instructor consultations and more effective. So my research is simultaneously theoretical (tests social learning theory), methodological (rigorous longitudinal evidence), and practical (offers a scalable model)."

Why it works

You've positioned your research at the intersection of theory, methods, and practice. You're not saying "this is important because I studied it" — you're saying "this matters to the field because it changes what we should do".

The Examiners' Questions

Six questions in increasing difficulty. 60 seconds each. These are the kinds of questions examiners actually ask.

1
Walk me through your choice of research methodology.

Straightforward. Explain why you chose your method. What were the alternatives? Why was your method better for your research question?

1:00
2
Your literature review doesn't engage with [important theorist/paper]. Why not?

They've caught a gap. Either you missed something, or you deliberately excluded it. Explain your reasoning honestly. If you missed it, say so — but position it productively.

3
Your sample size is relatively small. How confident are you in the generalisability of your findings?

They're testing whether you're aware of your study's limitations. Show that you are. Distinguish between generalisability to the population and transferability of ideas.

1:00
4
You've identified limitations. Which one, if fully addressed, would most significantly change your conclusions?

This is sophisticated. You need to think about causal chains: which limitation, if fixed, would actually alter your central finding?

1:00
5
Another researcher using a different methodology found the opposite of your conclusions. How do you explain that?

Conflicting evidence. Resist the temptation to dismiss their study. Instead, explore where the difference comes from: different population? Different measures? Different context?

1:00
6
What would you do differently if you started this research today?

Forward-looking. Show that you've learned. Would you change your methodology? Your sample? Your analysis? Why?

After the Viva: Reflection & Language Reference

Reflection Questions

What's the hardest question you encountered?▼

Was it about methodology? Limitations? The gap between your findings and others' research? Reflect on what made it hard — was it genuinely a weakness in your study, or just a question you hadn't anticipated? There's a difference.

Where did you feel most confident?▼

This tells you something important: you're confident when you've thought deeply about something. These are the areas where you can speak authoritatively. Build on that.

What would you prepare differently next time?▼

Would you practice your 2-minute summary more? Study the literature more deeply in contested areas? Prepare responses to predictable limitations questions? Use these insights for future presentations or defences.

Viva Language Reference

Summarising Your Research

"My research examines [question] in [context]. The gap I'm addressing is [what's missing from literature]. I used [method] with [participants], and found [key finding]. This contributes to the field by [impact]."

"In essence, I'm arguing that [claim]. The evidence for this comes from [study design and findings]. The implication is [practical/theoretical significance]."

Presenting Methodology Confidently

"I chose [method] specifically because [rationale]. The alternatives were [X and Y], but [method] was better suited to my research question because [explanation]."

"My sample consisted of [N] participants, selected through [sampling strategy]. The rationale for this approach was [reason for sample design]."

Discussing Limitations Honestly

"This study is limited by [specific limitation]. What this means is [implications]. However, this doesn't affect [central finding] because [explanation]. Future research should [next step]."

"I acknowledge that [limitation], though I would argue it doesn't undermine the core finding because [reasoning]."

Handling Uncertainty

"That's a question I hadn't fully considered, but what I can say is [related point you have thought about]."

"That's beyond the scope of my current research, but it's certainly something I'd be interested in exploring in future work."

Defending Methodological Choices

"The decision to [choice] was deliberate because [rationale]. I was aware of alternatives, but this approach was better suited to [reason]."

"Some might argue that [alternative], and that's valid. However, my choice reflects [justification based on your research question]."

Contextualising Contribution

"My research contributes to the field by [specific contribution]. This matters because [practical/theoretical implications]. It challenges the current assumption that [existing assumption], and suggests instead that [your finding]."

"The significance of this finding is that it [changes understanding/practice/theory in specific way]."

Hedging Language (Appropriate Caution)

"The evidence suggests..." (weaker claim)

"I would argue that..." (positioning your interpretation)

"This study indicates..." (what your data shows)

"It's reasonable to conclude that..." (interpretation supported by evidence)

Confidence in Findings

"Whilst my sample is limited, the consistency across [X, Y, Z] gives me confidence that [finding] holds."

"The robustness of this finding is evident because [explain what supports it]."
