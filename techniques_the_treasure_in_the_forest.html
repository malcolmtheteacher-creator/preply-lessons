The Treasure in the Forest

Two thieves follow a stolen map to hidden gold. The map shows the treasure—but not the protection left around it.

Adapted for B2/C1 from H. G. Wells's 1894 story

Memory first

Have a go without notes; uncertainty is useful here.

  1. Explain the difference between few clues and a few clues.

    Few clues emphasises that the number is insufficient. A few clues means some clues exist and gives a more positive sense.

  2. Explain the difference between little hope and a little hope.

    Little hope means almost none and sounds negative. A little hope means some hope remains.

  3. Replace “almost no water” with a precise quantifier.

    “They had very little water” or “They had hardly any water.” Both express an amount close to zero.

The story in three movements

After each movement, answer from memory before revealing the response. The turn remains hidden until you commit to a prediction.

1 · A map taken from a dying man

Evans and Hooker rowed towards the island under a white, merciless sun. Between them lay a map stained with seawater and blood.

They had taken it from a Chinese trader who knew where gold was hidden. The man had tried to explain something as he lay dying, but neither Englishman understood more than a few words. The crosses and distances on the map seemed clear enough. The smaller Chinese notes could not matter.

“Three palm trees, then forty paces east,” Evans said.

Hooker looked at the water jar. There was very little left. “We find it quickly and leave.”

The island appeared empty. Insects screamed in the trees, and the wet heat pressed against them as they entered the forest. They found the three palms exactly where the map promised.

Hooker marked trees as they passed, afraid the forest would close behind them. Evans laughed at the precaution. Every measured step matched the map, and accuracy made him trust the document more completely than its unread words deserved.

Near the roots lay a dead man.

He was Chinese and had been dead for several days. One arm stretched towards a dark opening beneath the trees. Evans recognised him as one of the trader's companions.

“He reached the gold first,” Hooker said.

“Then why didn't he take it?”

For a moment neither moved. Then sunlight touched something yellow beyond the body, and caution became much less important.

Answer from memory

Which quantities should worry them before they land?

They have little water, few translated instructions and too much confidence in incomplete information.

2 · The man beside the gold

The gold lay beneath fallen branches: heavy ingots stacked in a shallow pit. There were more than the two men could carry.

Evans laughed and dropped to his knees. He lifted one bar, tested its weight and imagined the things it would buy. A sharp pain touched his palm.

“A thorn,” he said, pulling out a tiny barb.

They worked greedily. The ingots were too heavy for the boat, so they argued over how many to take. Hooker wanted to make two journeys. Evans refused to leave any gold unguarded.

Each bar seemed to create a new problem—weight, ownership, transport—and each problem made the men less willing to abandon it. They had imagined treasure as freedom. On the ground, it was a pile of metal too heavy to move and too valuable to leave.

After several minutes Evans sat down. Sweat covered his face.

“The heat,” he muttered.

He tried to stand and fell against the pile. His hands shook; his breathing became short and violent. Hooker moved towards him, then stopped.

The dead Chinese man lay only a few steps away. One hand was swollen. Near his fingers were several thin barbs like the one Evans had removed.

Hooker looked again at the untranslated writing on the map. What if the notes were not directions? What if they were a warning?

Evans reached for him, but Hooker stepped back. Within minutes, his companion was still.

Answer from memory

How does abundance become a danger rather than an advantage?

The quantity of gold creates urgency and physical difficulty, distracting them from the few clues that explain the dead body.

3 · The untranslated warning

Hooker stood alone with the treasure.

Fear told him to leave. The gold told him that Evans had been careless. A man who knew about the danger could avoid it.

He wrapped cloth around his hands and examined the ground. Small poisoned thorns had been fixed among the branches and between the ingots. The trader had protected the treasure with a trap that required no guard.

The arrangement was simple enough to survive its maker. Rain could not wash the poison from every hidden point, and greed would make any discoverer search the pile with bare, impatient hands. The dead men around it were not accidents; they were part of the warning.

Hooker cleared several barbs with a stick. Then he selected the largest ingot and lifted it by its smooth sides.

Something pricked his thumb through a tear in the cloth.

He dropped the gold. The wound was almost invisible.

Suddenly he understood the dying trader's final urgency, the notes they had dismissed and the body beside the palms. There had been enough information to save them, but greed had translated only the part that led to gold.

Hooker stumbled towards the shore. The trees seemed to tilt around him. Behind him, the ingots remained exactly where they had been: bright, heavy and useless.

He had spent the journey believing that the map belonged to him because he could read its crosses and numbers. The island delivered its last lesson more precisely. A map is not understood when a man reads only what he wants it to say.

Answer from memory

What is the story's most important contrast of quantity?

The men find plenty of what they desire and none of what they need.

Grammar — precise quantifiers

few / a few + countable plural

Few clues = almost none; a few clues = some.

The article changes the attitude from insufficient to useful.

little / a little + uncountable

Little time = almost none; a little time = some.

Use with time, water, hope, information.

hardly any / plenty of / enough

Hardly any water; plenty of gold; not enough caution.

Flexible quantity across countable and uncountable nouns.

Mixed practice

Complete all six before revealing. Explain the choice, not only the missing words.

They found ___ footprints, which gave them a direction. (some)

They had ___ understanding of the Chinese warning. (almost none)

There was ___ gold to make them careless. (a large amount)

They paid ___ attention to the thorns. (almost none)

___ barbs were enough to kill. (a small number)

There was not ___ water and there were too ___ ingots.

  1. a few — A few means some countable items—enough to be useful.
  2. little — Little means an amount close to zero and presents it as insufficient.
  3. plenty of / enough — Plenty of clearly means a large amount; enough works if the intended meaning is sufficient gold.
  4. little / hardly any — Little fits uncountable attention; hardly any is an equivalent stronger phrase.
  5. A few — A few means a small but real number of countable barbs.
  6. enough; many — Enough fits uncountable water; many follows plural countable ingots after too.

A short analysis

Give your answer first. Then reveal a model and identify one idea you handled differently.

Poison is the physical cause, but the story repeatedly supplies evidence before it acts: untranslated notes, a corpse and scattered thorns. Greed narrows attention until the men count gold and stop counting risks. Wells makes failure a problem of interpretation before it becomes a problem of chemistry.
