Latin America: One Name, Many Worlds

"Latin America" sounds like one culture. In reality it is around twenty countries — with Indigenous, African, European and Asian roots — from Mexico to the tip of Patagonia.

The stereotype: "Latin America is basically one country where everyone is the same." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Where the name came from

The term "Latin America" spread in the mid-19th century, partly through French and Latin American writers who wanted to stress the link between the region's Spanish-, Portuguese- and French-speaking countries and "Latin" Europe. Today it usually means the countries of the Americas where Spanish or Portuguese is mainly spoken, from Mexico to Argentina and Chile — about 20 countries with more than 600 million people.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. When did the term spread?

2. Roughly how many people live there?

1. In the mid-19th century.

2. More than 600 million.

Discuss:
  • Who chooses the names of regions?

Part 2 — Many roots

Before Europeans arrived, the region was home to great civilisations such as the Maya, the Aztecs and the Inca, whose descendants still live there in their millions. Spain and Portugal ruled most of it for about 300 years, and millions of enslaved Africans were brought by force, especially to Brazil and the Caribbean. Later immigrants came from Italy, Germany, Lebanon, Japan and elsewhere: Brazil, for example, has the largest Japanese community outside Japan.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name three pre-Columbian civilisations.

2. Which country has the largest Japanese community outside Japan?

1. The Maya, the Aztecs and the Inca.

2. Brazil.

Discuss:
  • How do many roots shape one culture?

Part 3 — Very different places

Mexico City, which has more than 20 million people in its metropolitan area, is a world away from rural Bolivia, where many people speak Quechua or Aymara. Argentina and Uruguay have strong Italian influences; Peru has deep Andean and Indigenous traditions; Cuba and Colombia's Caribbean coast have strong African roots. Most countries became independent in the early 19th century, and each has developed its own politics, accent and national pride.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How big is Mexico City's metropolitan area?

2. Which countries have strong Italian influences?

1. More than 20 million people.

2. Argentina and Uruguay.

Discuss:
  • Is "Latin American" a real identity?

Grammar — Relative Clauses

To pack a lot of information into one sentence — what a place is, who lives there — we use relative clauses.

How it works:
• who for people, which for things, where for places, whose for possession.
• Non-defining clauses add extra information with commas: "Brussels, which hosts the EU, is multilingual."
• Defining clauses identify which one — no commas.

Going deeper

From outside, shared languages, Catholic traditions and news coverage make Latin America look like one place, and films often show it through the same few images. From inside, people tend to identify first with their country, and often their region. The label itself is debated: some prefer "Latinoamérica", "Ibero-America" or simply the names of their countries.

Worth noticing: Use "whose" for possession; "where/when" for places and times; commas for extra information.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Use country names. Ask people which country — and which city — they're from, and avoid assuming that someone from Peru knows all about Mexico. Learning one fact about their country's history or food is a great conversation starter.
Dig into it — talk these through:
