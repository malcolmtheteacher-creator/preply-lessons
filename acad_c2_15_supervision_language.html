The Feedback Problem

Your PhD student has been working slowly. Progress has been uneven. Now this chapter arrives—and it's significantly below the standard required for doctoral-level work. You need to give honest feedback without crushing them.

The language you choose in the next 30 minutes will shape their next six months of work. Or their choice to quit.

Your Turn Imagine you're the student. You've just heard your work is "below standard." What would you want to hear next? What would make this feedback useful rather than demoralising?
2:30

What Bad Feedback Looks Like

Too harsh: "This isn't doctoral-level work. You need to completely rewrite this."

Too soft: "There are some areas to develop. You're on the right track."

Vague: "The argument needs to be stronger. The writing could be clearer."

Deflecting: "I'm sure you'll figure it out. Let's meet again in three months."

None of these tell the student what to do.

Your Turn You're about to give feedback to a struggling student. Write down: (1) What is genuinely problematic about this work? (2) What shows promise? (3) What specific changes would move it toward doctoral standard? Be precise, not general.
3:00

Six Moves for Constructive Feedback

1 Framing Feedback Constructively +
Before you critique, establish safety. Show that feedback is an investment in their growth, not a judgment of their worth.
Blunt

"This chapter isn't doctoral-level work."

Constructive

"I can see you're working hard on this chapter. I want to give you honest feedback because I believe this can become strong work. Right now it's at a B-level for a research paper—good ideas, but not yet at doctoral standard. Let me show you specifically what needs to change to get there."

The Move

Acknowledge effort → State your intention clearly → Name the gap precisely → Offer a path forward

2 The Honest Assessment +
Don't soften honest feedback. Be clear about what's not working. But be specific: name the problem, show where it appears, explain why it matters.
Vague

"Your argument could be clearer."

Specific

"In pages 3-5, you introduce three competing frameworks but don't commit to one. Readers are left uncertain which frame you're using to analyse the data. By page 7, you're applying [framework A], but you've never explicitly said that's your choice. This confusion undermines the analysis. You need to either commit to one framework or explain why you're moving between them."

The Move

Name the problem → Show precisely where it appears → Explain the consequence → Propose a specific solution

3 Setting Clear Expectations +
Struggling students often don't know what "doctoral standard" means. Be explicit. Show them the gap between where they are and where they need to be.
Vague

"Your work needs to be at doctoral level."

Clear

"Doctoral work does three things consistently: (1) establishes why the question matters in the field; (2) engages critically with existing scholarship, not just summarising it; (3) offers an analysis that's yours—not just reporting what data shows, but interpreting what it means. Right now your chapter is strong on #1 and #3, but weak on #2. You summarise Smith and Jones, but you don't engage with their arguments. That's what needs to change."

The Move

Define the standard clearly → Show where they meet it → Show where they fall short → Connect feedback to the standard

4 Managing Emotional Responses +
Students receiving hard feedback often become defensive, shut down, or spiral. Watch for this. Name it if it's happening.
Unaddressed

[Student starts getting quiet, eyes down. Supervisor continues with feedback.]

Named and Addressed

"I notice you're getting quiet. This is hard feedback to hear. I want to pause and ask: what are you thinking right now? Is this feeling manageable, or do you need a break?"

The Move

Notice emotional shifts → Name what you observe → Ask them to share their experience → Adjust pace and tone based on their response

5 The Progress Conversation +
If progress is slow, address it directly. Don't blame. Instead, treat it as a problem to solve together.
Accusatory

"You're falling behind. You need to work faster."

Collaborative

"I've noticed your progress has slowed. This matters because [timeline/milestones]. Before I give you more feedback, I need to understand what's happening. Are there barriers I don't know about? Is the work feeling overwhelming? Are you uncertain about the direction? Let's figure this out together."

The Move

Name the observation → Explain why it matters → Ask open questions → Position yourself as collaborator, not judge

6 Recommendation Letter Language +
Years from now, you may write a reference letter for this student. The language of that letter reflects the work of supervision. Be honest but generous.
Damning

"[Name] struggled with this project and produced work below standard."

Honest and Fair

"[Name] undertook ambitious research that required significant development of new skills. Early chapters showed promise but lacked critical depth. With intensive feedback and revision, later chapters demonstrate genuine analytical sophistication. [He/She] is a careful, conscientious scholar who responds well to structured feedback."

The Move

Acknowledge the struggle → Show the growth → Highlight genuine strengths → Position them fairly for their next opportunity

The Meeting Begins

Your student arrives for the feedback meeting. You've read their chapter carefully. It's not meeting standard, but you can see potential.

Your Opening

Speak for 2 Minutes How do you begin this conversation? You want to be honest about the chapter's limitations while also affirming the student and setting a collaborative tone.
2:00

The Specific Feedback

Take 5 Minutes Prepare three pieces of specific feedback for this chapter: (1) One major problem that needs to be addressed; (2) One area of strength to build on; (3) One concrete next step.
5:00

The Action Plan

Don't end with vague "revise and resubmit." Make a concrete plan together.

The Plan Should Include

WHAT The specific task: rewrite section X, add analysis of Y, reorganise chapters
HOW The approach: talk through the argument first, draft and circulate, get peer feedback
WHEN Timeline: draft by [date], feedback by [date], revised version by [date]
SUPPORT What help are you offering: weekly meetings, reading groups, writing workshops
Speak for 2 Minutes Propose a concrete action plan. Show what needs to happen, when, and how you'll support it.
2:00

The Hard Questions

The feedback meeting continues. Your student says things that are difficult to hear. Work through these.

Student: "I worked so hard on this chapter. I spent weeks on it. And you're saying it's not good enough. What more do you want from me?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Separate effort from output. Affirm effort while naming what's missing.

Answer: "I see your effort. That matters. And it's also true that effort and output aren't the same thing. You've put in real work here. But doctoral work requires a particular kind of thinking—critical engagement with scholarship, not just reporting what you found. That's not effort-dependent; it's a skill. You can develop it. Let me show you what that looks like."

Student: "I don't think I'm cut out for this. Maybe I should quit. I'm not smart enough for a PhD."
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Distinguish between struggle and inadequacy. Show what's actually happening.

Answer: "I hear you. This is discouraging. But let me be clear: struggling with a chapter doesn't mean you're not smart enough. Most doctoral students write chapters that need major revision. I did. This is normal. What matters is: can you hear the feedback and revise? And from what I've seen, you can. Let's take this one step at a time. Not quit. Revise."

Student: "But I thought my argument was clear. The feedback makes it sound confused. How was I supposed to know?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Use this as a teaching moment about revision and audience.

Answer: "You thought it was clear—that's because you live inside the argument. You know what you mean. But readers don't have that context. This is why revision matters. What's clear in your head isn't always clear on the page. That's not a failure; it's why we have readers and feedback. Now you have feedback. Use it to make the argument clear to readers, not just to you."

Student: "You said this was promising work at the beginning of my degree. What changed? Am I getting worse, or was I always this bad?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Explain the raising of standards honestly. Show it's normal.

Answer: "Your early work was promising. And standards rise as you progress. First-year work is different from third-year work. I'm not lowering my assessment of you; I'm raising what doctoral-level work looks like. This happens to everyone in a PhD. The gap between 'promising' and 'doctoral standard' gets cleared through revision and feedback. You're in that gap right now. It's where everyone is."

Student: "I'm worried this feedback means you don't believe in me anymore. Are you going to recommend me for the position I'm applying for?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Separate feedback on this work from faith in their potential.

Answer: "Honest feedback doesn't mean I don't believe in you. It means I do. I'm telling you what you need to work on because I believe you can do it. Will I recommend you? That depends on your progress. Show me you can revise this chapter with the feedback I've given. Show me growth. If you do, absolutely. References are based on demonstrated capability, not potential. Right now, your capability is being tested. Use that as a chance to prove what you can do."

Stepping Back

Supervision is one of the most powerful forms of teaching. The feedback you give shapes not just a student's PhD, but their relationship with scholarship itself.
