The Language of Academic Supervision

Giving and receiving feedback in mentorship relationships

10:00

Supervision as Reciprocal Development

Academic supervision is fundamentally a relationship of inequality: supervisors hold institutional power, control resource access, and evaluate supervisees" work. Yet effective supervision transcends hierarchy, creating spaces where both parties develop intellectually. Supervisors learn from supervisees" fresh perspectives; supervisees benefit from supervisors" experience and institutional knowledge.

Different Supervision Models

Supervision can adopt multiple models. Directive supervision (supervisor instructs what to do) offers security but limits independence. Facilitative supervision (supervisor guides discovery) develops autonomy but can feel frustratingly slow. Most effective supervision adapts to supervisee needs: more directive when confidence is low or guidance crucial, more facilitative as competence and independence develop. Supervisors should explicitly discuss their approach and adjust based on feedback.

Managing Expectations Explicitly

Supervision relationships benefit from early conversation about expectations: How often will you meet? Who initiates meetings? What is the supervisor"s turn-around time for feedback? What kind of feedback does the supervisee want? What happens if the relationship isn"t working? These conversations prevent misunderstandings and establish psychological safety.

Power and Vulnerability in Supervision

The hierarchical relationship means supervisees cannot easily resist problematic direction without career risk. Supervisors must recognize this power differential and use it responsibly. Conversely, supervisees must navigate carefully: advocating for their perspective while respecting supervisor expertise, raising concerns without accusations, accepting criticism while maintaining intellectual autonomy.

Communicating Effectively in Supervision

Both supervisors and supervisees must develop communication skills for this relationship to flourish. The language should balance honesty with respect, clarity with encouragement.

Supervisors Giving Feedback

Effective feedback is specific: "This paragraph is unclear" is vague. "This paragraph confuses the distinction between X and Y; consider reorganizing to first explain X, then explain Y, then discuss their relationship." This gives direction. Effective feedback acknowledges context: "I see you"re working on establishing theoretical grounding. Here"s where I think the theory needs development..." This recognizes the supervisee"s direction while offering guidance. Effective feedback invites discussion: "I have a concern about this methodological choice. Help me understand your thinking here" opens dialogue rather than asserting authority.

Supervisees Receiving Feedback

Listen without immediately defending. Pause before responding—silence enables processing. Ask clarifying questions: "When you say unclear, what specifically caused difficulty?" This helps you understand rather than defend. Distinguish feedback on ideas (which is discussable) from feedback on your intelligence or character (which is inappropriate). It"s fine to say "I disagree with that assessment, but I understand your concern and will consider it."

Supervisor language: "I notice you"ve been revising this section multiple times without substantial change. Let"s talk about what you"re struggling with. What would be most helpful from me right now—more guidance on the conceptual framework, technical writing suggestions, or something else?"

Supervisee language: "I appreciate your feedback on my draft. Before I revise, I want to clarify my thinking. Do you see the conceptual problems as stemming from the framework itself or how I"ve applied it?"

Managing Disagreement

Supervisees should feel able to disagree with supervisors, though managing this carefully is wise. "I understand your perspective. I"m inclined toward [different direction] because..." This respects the supervisor"s view while asserting your thinking. Supervisors should welcome such disagreement: "I see why you chose that approach. Let me explain my concern, and then we can decide together."

Navigating Supervision Challenges

Supervision relationships sometimes become strained. Addressing difficulties early prevents resentment.

When Supervision Isn"t Working

If you feel unsupported, unclear about expectations, or unable to move forward, raise this directly. "I feel like we"re not quite clicking on how to approach my thesis. Can we discuss what we need from each other?" This opens dialogue. Similarly, if you feel your supervisor is pushing in a direction you don"t support, say "I appreciate your expertise on this, but I"m not convinced this direction is right for my work. Can we discuss my concerns?"

Supervisor Perspective: Addressing Underperformance

If a supervisee isn"t meeting expectations, address it: "I"ve noticed you haven"t submitted chapters for feedback in two months. I"m concerned about your progress. What"s happening? What support would help?" This expresses concern while inviting explanation. Avoid accusatory language ("You"re not working hard enough"). Instead, focus on observable behaviours and impact.

Negotiating Timelines and Expectations

Life happens. If you need to slow your pace, discuss it early. "I"m dealing with [challenge]. I"d like to extend my timeline from X to Y. Here"s why this is necessary and my plan for staying on track." This is a negotiation, not a confession. Most supervisors can accommodate reasonable requests when explained clearly.

Difficult Conversation Practice

Describe a supervision challenge you"ve experienced (or might face). Draft how you would address it, focusing on specific, non-accusatory language.

Using Supervision for Maximum Development

Effective supervisees are active agents in their own development. You must direct your supervision relationship toward your goals.

Being a Good Supervisee

Come to meetings prepared: have questions ready, bring drafts for feedback, reflect on previous suggestions. Engage seriously with feedback: don"t dismiss it instantly but sit with it, try revisions, then discuss results. Take responsibility for your timeline; don"t expect your supervisor to keep you on track. Ask for what you need: "I need feedback that focuses on structure before you address sentence-level issues" clarifies priorities.

Developing Academic Voice

Early supervision is often directive—supervisors shape your thinking substantially. As you develop, seek increasingly facilitative supervision where you drive direction and supervisors guide. "I"m thinking about framing my work this way. What do you see as potential problems?" positions you as primary thinker. Eventually, supervision should be more peer-like, with the supervisor as sounding board for your ideas rather than director.

Reflection: What do you want from supervision? What aspects of your work most need development? How will you communicate these needs? What would make you a more effective supervisee? Asking yourself these questions transforms supervision from something that happens to you into something you actively shape.

The Long-Term Mentorship Relationship

The best supervisor relationships often continue long after formal supervision ends. You become junior colleagues who periodically exchange feedback, celebrate successes, and support each other through challenges. Cultivating this relationship—by maintaining communication, acknowledging your supervisor"s contributions, and eventually becoming a mentor to others—extends the benefits of good supervision across your career.
