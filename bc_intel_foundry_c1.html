The Bet on Being Everyone's Factory

In 2021 Intel promised to make chips not only for itself but for the whole industry — and to take on TSMC doing it. Five years, tens of billions of dollars, a departed chief executive and a government shareholder later, the factory is running. The question is who will fill it.

Easier version: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you’ll follow one of the largest industrial bets of the decade — Intel’s attempt to become a contract manufacturer for the whole chip industry — and practise the language leaders use when the outcome is still years away: is likely to, could well, is bound to, is set to, there’s a good chance that…

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Apple and Nvidia design their own chips but do not make them. They pay a foundry, such as TSMC, to manufacture them.
a foundry (n) — in semiconductors, a company that manufactures chips designed by other companies. Originally a factory where metal is melted and cast. A chip company with no factories is fabless.
2. Each new chip factory — a fab — costs billions of dollars and takes years to build and equip.
a fab (n) — short for fabrication plant, a chip factory. Collocations: build / equip / ramp up a fab; a leading-edge fab.
3. Intel’s new manufacturing technology, called 18A, is a process node: a generation of chipmaking with smaller, denser transistors than the last.
a process node (n) — a generation of manufacturing technology (18A, 14A…). Being first to a new node is called process leadership; the most advanced nodes are the leading edge.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms from the case: an anchor customer · a subsidy · an equity stake. Say the right term, then reveal.

1. Which term means a big, early client whose commitment makes an investment worth making?
an anchor customer (also anchor tenant in property) — land / secure / win an anchor customer. Without one, a new factory may never be full.
2. Which term means money a government gives a company to support an activity it wants to encourage?
a subsidy (n) / to subsidise (v). Also a grant (money that does not have to be repaid) and an incentive. Collocations: award / disburse / claw back a subsidy.
3. Which term means part ownership of a company, held as shares?
an equity stake — take / hold / sell a 10% stake in. A passive stake comes without a seat on the board.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Intel slowed construction in Ohio “to ensure our spending is aligned with market demand.” In other words, it wanted to avoid building too much capacity. Capacity means…
  (a) the skill of the workers   (b) the maximum amount a factory can produce   (c) the price of a product
(b) — add / cut / underuse capacity; run at full capacity; spare or excess capacity; capacity utilisation.
2. Critics said Intel kept spending because it had already spent so much — a sunk-cost trap. This means…
  (a) continuing a plan because of money already spent, not future value   (b) a tax on unused factories   (c) a cheap way to borrow money
(a) — a sunk cost is money already spent that cannot be recovered. Economists say it should not affect the next decision; humans find that very hard. Related: throwing good money after bad.

D · Collocation & word building

1. When a new factory slowly increases production to full volume, we say it ___ up. Which verb?
ramps up — also a noun: the ramp, a production ramp, ramp-up costs. The opposite: wind down.
2. Make the noun from turn around (= rescue a failing company), and complete: “The new CEO was hired to lead a ___.”
a turnaround (n) — a turnaround plan / story / specialist. Note the verb is two words (turn a company around); the noun is one.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — The promise (2021–2022)

For most of its history Intel designed its own chips and made them in its own factories. By 2020 that model was under pressure: manufacturing stumbles had sent Intel’s shares down, and much of the industry now designed chips and paid Taiwan’s TSMC or South Korea’s Samsung to make them. In early 2021 Intel brought back Pat Gelsinger, an engineer who had started at the company in 1979, as chief executive.

On 23 March 2021 Gelsinger announced a strategy he called “IDM 2.0”. Intel would keep making its own chips, use outside foundries where it made sense — and become a major foundry itself, manufacturing chips for other companies from factories in the US and Europe. A new unit, Intel Foundry Services, would lead it. The first step was an estimated $20 billion for two new fabs in Chandler, Arizona. Reuters described it as a direct challenge to the only two companies that could make the most advanced chips: TSMC and Samsung.

More announcements followed. In January 2022 Intel promised more than $20 billion for two fabs in Ohio, with production planned for 2025 and room for eight fabs in total. Later that year it announced a €30 billion complex in Magdeburg, Germany, and a €5 billion plant in Wrocław, Poland. The bet was not one factory; it was a network, built ahead of demand.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What were the three parts of “IDM 2.0”?

2. Which new sites did Intel announce in 2021–2022?

1. Keep manufacturing its own chips, use outside foundries where useful, and become a major foundry for other companies (Intel Foundry Services).

2. Two fabs in Arizona (about $20bn), two in Ohio (more than $20bn), a €30bn complex in Magdeburg and a €5bn plant in Wrocław.

Discuss:
  • Why might a company announce several huge sites at once, before it has the customers? What does it gain — and what does it risk?

Part 2 — The bill (2023–2024)

Washington wanted the same thing. In November 2024 the US Commerce Department finalised a grant of up to $7.865 billion for Intel under the CHIPS Act, to support nearly $90 billion of US investment by the end of the decade, paid out as Intel reached project milestones. But the market was less patient than the policy. Intel Foundry lost about $7 billion in 2023 and about $13.4 billion in 2024, as the company paid for new technology and new factories long before outside customers arrived in volume.

The European plans were paused in 2024. In February 2025 Intel said its first Ohio factory would now be completed only by 2030 — at least five years later than first promised. By then Gelsinger was gone. On 2 December 2024 Intel announced that he had retired, effective the day before, and named two interim co-chief executives. The chair thanked him for helping to “revitalize process manufacturing” — and said the board was committed to “restoring investor confidence”.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How much did the US government award Intel under the CHIPS Act, and how was it to be paid?

2. What happened to the foundry’s losses, the Ohio timeline and the chief executive?

1. Up to $7.865 billion, finalised in November 2024, paid out as Intel completed project milestones.

2. Losses grew from about $7bn (2023) to about $13.4bn (2024); the first Ohio fab slipped to 2030; Gelsinger retired in December 2024.

Discuss:
  • Was replacing the chief executive a sensible correction, or a loss of nerve halfway through a ten-year plan? What would you need to know to decide?

Part 3 — The new terms (2025–today)

In March 2025 Lip-Bu Tan, a chip-industry veteran who had previously sat on Intel’s board, became chief executive. He changed the rules of the bet. In July 2025 Intel cancelled the German and Polish projects, said it would “further slow” construction in Ohio, and warned investors that it might pause or stop developing its next node, 14A, if it could not win a significant outside customer. Factories would follow customers, not the other way round.

Then came new money from unusual places. In August 2025, after President Trump had publicly said Tan should step down, the US government agreed to take a 9.9% stake in Intel: $8.9 billion for 433.3 million shares at $20.47 each, paid for with $5.7 billion of CHIPS grants not yet disbursed and $3.2 billion from a defence programme called Secure Enclave. The government also received a warrant to buy more shares under certain conditions — an incentive, Reuters reported, for Intel to keep control of its foundry. SoftBank invested $2 billion; in September Nvidia agreed to invest $5 billion and develop chips with Intel.

The engineering has started to deliver. Intel’s first PC chips on 18A came from its new Arizona fab in late 2025, and in July 2026 Intel reported revenue up 25% on the year — its strongest growth in more than fifteen years — while the foundry loss narrowed to about $2.1 billion for the quarter. But the same report still listed winning commitments from major outside customers for 14A as a key risk. The factory is real. The question is still open: will anyone else fill it?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Lip-Bu Tan change about the strategy in July 2025?

2. Describe the US government’s deal: how much, for what, and paid with what?

1. He cancelled Germany and Poland, slowed Ohio further, and made future investment (including 14A) depend on winning significant outside customers first.

2. $8.9bn for a 9.9% stake (433.3m shares at $20.47), funded by $5.7bn of undisbursed CHIPS grants and $3.2bn of Secure Enclave money, plus a conditional warrant for more shares.

Discuss:
  • Does a government shareholder make Intel a safer partner for customers — or a more complicated one? Who might feel each way?

Grammar — predicting & speculating

Nobody in this case knows the ending. Analysts, ministers and chief executives all have to talk about it anyway — and the words they choose signal exactly how sure they are. At C1 the skill is choosing the strength that matches your evidence, and no more.

1 · How certain? A scale from sure to doubtful
• Near-certain: is bound to + verb · is certain to · there’s no doubt that… — “Losses are bound to continue while the fabs ramp up.”
• Probable: is likely to · there’s a good chance that… · is expected to — “Intel is likely to need an anchor customer before it spends on 14A.”
• Possible: could well · may well · might — “The Ohio site could well slip again.” (could well is stronger than could.)
• Unlikely: is unlikely to · there’s little chance that… · is hardly likely to
2 · Plans and schedules — is set to, is due to, is on course to
• is set to = arranged or expected, often in news: “The government is set to become Intel’s largest shareholder.”
• is due to = scheduled for a time: “The first Ohio fab is due to be completed by 2030.”
• is on course to / on track to = progress suggests it will: “The foundry is on course to narrow its losses.”
3 · Form watch
• likely is an adjective here: Intel is likely to win or It is likely that Intel will win — never Intel likely will win in formal British English.
• bound to is for things you are confident about, not for obligations here: bound to fail = sure to fail.
• Negatives: is unlikely to is the usual choice (is not likely to is also correct); there’s little chance = almost no chance, but there’s a little chance sounds wrong — say a small chance.

1. Choose the strength

Complete each sentence with one expression: is bound to · could well · is unlikely to · is set to.

1. A new fab with no outside customers lose money in its first years — every analyst agrees.

2. The deal close next week, once the final documents are signed.

3. If a big customer signs, the 14A timetable move forward — but nobody is promising.

4. Without subsidies, a new European fab be built this decade.

1. is bound to   2. is set to   3. could well   4. is unlikely to

2. Rewrite with the same meaning

Complete the second sentence so it means the same as the first.

1. It is probable that Intel will need more outside investment. → Intel is need more outside investment.

2. It’s quite possible that customers will wait for 14A. → There’s a chance that customers will wait for 14A.

3. The factory is scheduled to open in 2030. → The factory is open in 2030.

4. I’m sure the share price will react. → The share price is react.

1. likely to   2. good   3. due to   4. bound to

3. The analyst on air — complete it, then deliver it

A television presenter asks a semiconductor analyst: “Will Intel’s foundry bet pay off?” Complete the answer, then deliver it aloud in under a minute, as if live on air.

“Honestly, it’s too early to call. The losses are to continue for another year or two — that’s simply what building fabs costs. There’s a chance that 18A keeps improving, because the yields are moving in the right direction. Whether a major customer signs for 14A is the real question; one big name well change the whole story. And with the government as a shareholder, Intel is to be allowed to fail quietly.”

bound / likely · good · could / may · unlikely

Notice the shape: a near-certainty, a probability, a possibility, an improbability. A good forecast tells the listener how confident you are at every step.

Going deeper

A note on the figures: amounts are rounded and come from the sources listed at the end of this tab. Intel restates some segment figures from year to year, so you may see slightly different loss numbers elsewhere. The case is still moving.

What the case actually tests. The first reading sees a brave strategic bet. Almost all of the world’s most advanced chips were being made by a very small number of companies, mostly in Asia. Someone had to try to change that, and only Intel had the engineers, the history and the American factories to do it. On this reading the losses are the entry fee for a business that takes a decade to build; the new 18A factory in Arizona, the revenue recovery in 2026 and the willingness of governments, SoftBank and Nvidia to invest are signs that the bet is still alive. You do not judge a ten-year plan in year four.

The second reading sees a classic sunk-cost trap. Intel announced factories on three continents before it had customers to fill them, kept spending as losses roughly doubled, and has now cancelled two sites and pushed another back by at least five years. On this reading, each new rescue — grants turned into shares, strategic investors, a government stake — makes it harder, not easier, to ask the simple question: if Intel were starting today, would it build this foundry at all?

The third point complicates both. Lip-Bu Tan’s rule — no new capacity without customer commitments — can be read as the brave bet finally becoming disciplined, or as the trap finally being recognised. The government stake can be read as patient national capital, or as a reason the business can never be allowed to stop. The facts are the same; the reading depends on what you think 14A’s first big customer will do. That is the decision the case leaves open.
Talk it through:
  • Brave bet or sunk-cost trap? Argue one side with at least two facts from the case — then name the fact that most weakens your side.
  • Gelsinger built ahead of demand; Tan says capacity must follow customers. Which approach would you choose for a business that takes five years to build a factory?
  • Should taxpayers own part of a company they are subsidising? What should they get for their money — and what should they not be allowed to do?
  • If you were TSMC, how worried would you be? What would change your answer?

Speak

All of it out loud, against the clock. A bet that has years left to run has to be explained to people who all want a different kind of certainty.

Useful language: is bound to, is likely to, there’s a good chance that, could well, is unlikely to · is set to, is due to, is on course to · it’s too early to call · capacity will follow customers · and to close: the question is not whether…, but when — and who pays until then.

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

You are Intel’s head of foundry at an investor conference. A fund manager says: “You’ve lost tens of billions, cancelled two countries, delayed Ohio by five years, and now taxpayers own ten per cent of you. Give me one reason to believe this ends differently from every other sunk-cost story.”

Answer properly: accept what went wrong, separate the past spending from the next decision, give two pieces of evidence, and make one calibrated prediction. Then do it again in ninety seconds — the compression is the exercise.

Task C · Four rooms, one delay (4 minutes)

Your company has just decided to slow the construction of a major new site until it has signed customers. Explain the decision four ways, one minute each — and notice how the strength of your predictions changes from room to room.

  • To the board: what you expect to happen, and what would make you restart.
  • To investors: why slower is safer — without sounding like retreat.
  • To the workforce at the site: what is likely, what is possible, and what you cannot promise.
  • To the press: one clear sentence about the future that you will not regret next year.
