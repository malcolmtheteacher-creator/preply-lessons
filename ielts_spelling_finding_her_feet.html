By her second year abroad, Maya was ready to find a part-time job. She saw an advertisement for a role at a small business in the city centre and filled in a long questionnaire before they invited her to an interview. Throughout it she was conscious of every word she used, careful not to exaggerate her experience. To her relief the manager was kind, and by the following Wednesday she had the job.

The office was a warm, comfortable place, though the heating was so strong that the temperature made her sleepy after lunch. Her main task was the maintenance of the company calendar — booking meetings, chasing replies, and making sure no double-booking was ever a common occurrence. She kept every receipt from work trips, because the company guarantee was that all costs would be repaid, and she quickly learned to be thorough with the paperwork.

Maya occasionally joined the team for lunch at a small restaurant around the corner. She grew close to a friendly neighbour in the next office, who drove an old vehicle that broke down almost every week. There was the odd argument about whose turn it was to make the coffee, but nothing serious. On quiet afternoons she would slip up to the company library to read about the development of the industry she now worked in.

When the internship ended, Maya did not want to disappoint the team that had taught her so much. She wrote a card to the committee that had first hired her, signing it "Yours sincerely", and promised to return the next summer. "See you tomorrow," her manager joked on the final morning — and somehow Maya knew that her real career had only just started.

Why these words catch people out

Five traps explain almost every spelling mistake in the story.

1 · Double letters where you least expect them

occurrence (double c and double r, then ‑ence), committee (double m, double t and double e), exaggerate (double g), guarantee (double e at the end). And the classic one-or-two trap: disappoint = dis + appoint (one s, two p's), tomorrow = to + morrow (one m, two r's).

2 · Silent or hidden letters

Wednesday (the silent d — say "Wed‑nes‑day" in your head), receipt (silent p: recei‑p‑t), vehicle (silent h: ve‑h‑icle), business (hidden in the word "busy": busi‑ness).

3 · The ending people guess wrong

calender → calendar (‑ar). maintainance → maintenance (‑tenance). occurrance → occurrence (‑ence). arguement → argument (no e). developement → development (no e).

4 · Tricky vowel clusters

restaurant (‑aurant), questionnaire (double n, then ‑aire), neighbour (‑eigh‑ with a silent gh; British ‑our), thorough (‑orough — not the same as through).

5 · Words people mispronounce — so misspell

temperature (say the whole thing: tem‑per‑a‑ture), library (two r's: lib‑ra‑ry, not "libary"), conscious (‑scious), comfortable (com‑fort‑able), occasionally (double c, one s, ‑ally).
