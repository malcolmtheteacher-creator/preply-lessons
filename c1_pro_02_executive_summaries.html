Writing Executive Summaries

C1 Level · Business Writing

The Executive's Dilemma

A CEO receives 200+ emails a day. A board member sits in 8+ hours of meetings weekly. An executive has 4 minutes to understand whether your proposal deserves 40 minutes of their time. They're not lazy — they're time-constrained. An executive summary is not a courtesy. It's a survival tool.

Why Executives Stop Reading

  • No clear landing: They don't know what the document is asking for in the first three sentences.
  • Too much context: They don't need the history. They need the recommendation.
  • Buried insight: The key finding is on page 12. They stopped on page 3.
  • Unclear stakes: They don't know why this matters to the business.
  • No single source of truth: Different parts of the document contradict each other. They lose trust.

What Executive Summaries Solve

A well-written summary tells an executive:

  1. What's being proposed (crystal clear, sentence one)
  2. Why it matters (business impact, risk, opportunity)
  3. What evidence supports it (key data points, not all data)
  4. What decision is needed (not vague — specific ask)
  5. What happens next (timeline, dependencies)

Attention Spans Are Ruthless

Research shows executives spend an average of 1.5 minutes per document. In that time, they need to decide: Is this important? Do I need to read the whole thing? Can I delegate? The summary determines their decision.

An executive summary is not a condensed version of the document. It's a standalone argument. Someone should be able to read only the summary, understand the recommendation, and make a decision.

Vocabulary: Summary & Analysis Terms

Concise
Expressing much in few words; brief and clear without unnecessary detail
Salient
Most noticeable or important; standing out from other elements
Distil
To extract the essential meaning or most important elements from something
Synthesise
To combine separate elements into a unified whole; to draw conclusions from multiple sources
Bottom line
The main point or final conclusion; the net result after all factors are considered
Recommendation
A suggested course of action; what you believe should be done next
KPI
Key Performance Indicator — a measurable value showing how effectively a goal is being met
Benchmark
A standard or reference point against which performance is measured

The Pyramid Principle

Most people write conclusions at the end. Executives read summaries in reverse. Use the "pyramid principle": Start with your conclusion, then justify it.

Recommendation: We should acquire Company X

Why?

  • It captures a $15M market opportunity we currently lack
  • Integration costs are $2.3M with 12-month payback period
  • Two of our three strategic partners have already committed to the acquired product

Evidence for each point:

  • Market size data, competitor analysis, gap in our portfolio
  • Cost breakdown by category, comparable acquisition case studies
  • Letters of intent from Partner A and Partner B

Key principle: Each section should answer the question "So what?" If a reader skims, they should still understand the logic.

Language for Executive Summaries

Strong language:

  • "We recommend..." (not "we believe" or "we think")
  • "This addresses three key risks" (not "this is important")
  • "Revenue impact: +$2M annually" (quantify when possible)
  • "Decision required by [date]" (be specific about timeline)

Weak language to avoid:

  • "This is really important for the business"
  • "We've done extensive analysis"
  • "In our professional opinion"
  • "This could potentially help"

Your Task: Write an Executive Summary

Scenario: The Full Report (500 words)

Full Report: Remote Work Productivity Study

Over the past 18 months, our HR department conducted an analysis of remote work patterns and their correlation with productivity metrics. The study examined 320 employees across four departments (Sales, Engineering, Finance, Marketing) with a mix of fully remote, hybrid, and office-based work arrangements.

Our baseline findings showed that remote employees logged 8.3% more billable hours weekly than office-based peers, though this may reflect incomplete tracking of office breaks. Interestingly, hybrid workers reported the highest stress levels (68% reported moderate-to-high stress) compared to fully remote (42%) and office-based (55%) cohorts.

Collaboration metrics are mixed. Virtual meetings are 22% longer on average than in-person meetings, suggesting less efficiency in achieving consensus. However, asynchronous communication channels (Slack, email) show 40% more documented decision-trails, which reduces rework. Engineering teams report 18% faster feature delivery in fully remote settings, while Sales reports 12% decline in deal velocity with fully remote arrangements.

Attrition data is instructive. Remote workers have 31% lower voluntary attrition than office-based workers over the study period. Exit interviews consistently cite flexibility and commute time as retention drivers. However, remote employees report 27% less sense of team belonging and identify fewer internal advancement opportunities during the study window.

Real estate costs are significant. Our current office footprint supports 450 employees. Modelling suggests a shift to 70% remote/30% office hybrid would reduce annual real estate costs by $3.2M, though this assumes permanent lease adjustments. Implementation would require office consolidation and hot-desking policies.

We surveyed employee preferences: 64% prefer hybrid arrangements, 22% prefer fully remote, and 14% prefer office-based. By department, Engineering skews remote (71% prefer), while Sales prefers office proximity (58% prefer at least 3 days/week).

Risks of full remote transition include client relationship impact (particularly in Sales), team culture dilution (noted by current office workers), and potential compliance complications in certain regulated teams (Finance is concerned about audit trail management in fully remote settings).

Your Brief

Write a one-page executive summary (100-150 words) for the CEO.

The CEO needs to decide: Do we shift to hybrid/remote? Do we maintain current office model? The summary should present the key insight and a recommendation.

Model Executive Summary:

"Recommendation: Adopt a hybrid model with department-specific flexibility — fully remote for Engineering, 3-day/week office requirement for Sales, flexible hybrid for Finance and Marketing.

Rationale: This approach captures the key benefits of our study: Engineering productivity gains (+18% delivery velocity), cost savings ($3.2M annually from office consolidation), and employee retention (31% lower attrition in remote settings), while mitigating Sales and compliance risks.

Key trade-off: We sacrifice some team belonging perception (27% decline reported) and office-centric culture. Mitigation: quarterly all-hands, department retreats, and mentorship programs.

Timeline: Pilot hybrid model in Q3 with two departments (Engineering and Marketing), then expand Q4 pending outcomes. Real estate optimization begins immediately."

Why this works: Leads with recommendation, then supports with data from the report. Acknowledges the trade-off (CEO already knows about team belonging concern). Gives specific timeline and scope. Takes a stand rather than saying "it depends."

Assessment Checklist

  • ✓ Recommendation is clear in first sentence
  • ✓ Three key reasons provided (not more)
  • ✓ Numbers are quoted accurately from the report
  • ✓ Trade-offs are acknowledged
  • ✓ Next steps/timeline are specific
  • ✓ Tone is confident, not hedging ("we should" not "we might")
  • ✓ Length is approximately 100-150 words

Information Asymmetry & Your Reader

You Know Too Much

After weeks of research, you've internalized all the nuance. The CFO also knows context that you don't — she knows the board is nervous about retention costs, or that competitors have already gone remote. Your job in the summary isn't to share everything you know. It's to answer the question your reader actually has.

When Detail Helps vs. Hurts

Detail helps when: It closes an objection the reader might have ("Yes, but what about compliance?" — you've addressed it). It supports an unexpected recommendation (remote model works because engineering benefits outweigh sales risk).

Detail hurts when: It buries the recommendation. It presents trade-offs as uncertainties instead of choices. It includes nice-to-know data instead of need-to-know data.

Knowing Your Audience

Different executives read differently. A CFO wants financial impact first. A product-focused CEO wants business model implications. An operational executive wants implementation feasibility. Before writing the summary, ask yourself: What does this specific leader care about most? What's their background? What decisions are they currently making?

The Summary That Gets Ignored

You've probably read a summary that felt like a condensed version of the full document — all the background, pros and cons equally weighted, no recommendation. This fails because it puts the decision-making burden on the reader. A good summary shoulders the burden: "Here's what I recommend, and here's why."

Reflection questions:

  • Think of a document you recently wrote. What would a one-page summary look like? What would you cut?
  • Who is your most senior reader for that document? What would they need to know that nobody else cares about?
  • How do you typically read documents — summary first, or straight through? What does that tell you about how to write for others?
