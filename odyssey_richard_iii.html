The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Shakespeare made him a villain.
a villain (n) — an evil character.
2. His skeleton showed scoliosis.
scoliosis (n) — a sideways curve of the spine.
3. He was not a hunchback as Shakespeare said.
a hunchback (n) — a person with a hump on the back.
4. Lord Stanley chose to betray him.
to betray (v) — to be disloyal.
5. He led a cavalry charge.
a charge (n) — a fast attack.
6. His remains were found under a car park in Leicester.
a car park (n) — a place to park cars.
7. DNA tests helped confirm his identity.
DNA (n) — genetic material.
8. He was reburied in Leicester Cathedral in 2015.
to rebury (v) — to bury again.
9. Tudor propaganda blackened his name.
propaganda (n) — biased information.
10. His reputation is still debated.
reputation (n) — what people think of someone.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The king

Richard III, brother of Edward IV, was crowned in 1483 after his nephews were declared illegitimate. His reign included legal reforms, such as bail and the translation of laws into English.

But rumours about the princes spread, and rebellions began.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What reforms did Richard make?

2. What damaged his reign?

1. Bail and laws printed in English.

2. Rumours about the princes and rebellions.

Discuss:
  • Can good laws make up for doubts about how you came to power?

Part 2 — Bosworth

On 22 August 1485 Richard faced Henry Tudor at Bosworth. The Stanleys, whose armies were watching, turned against him. Richard led a charge towards Henry and was killed — the last English king to die in battle.

Shakespeare gave him the line "A horse! A horse! My kingdom for a horse!"

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who betrayed Richard at Bosworth?

2. What was unique about his death?

1. The Stanleys.

2. He was the last English king to die in battle.

Discuss:
  • Why did loyalty matter so much in this battle?

Part 3 — The car park

In 2012 archaeologists found a skeleton under a car park in Leicester, on the site of a lost friary. It had scoliosis and battle wounds. DNA and other evidence confirmed it was Richard. He was reburied in Leicester Cathedral in 2015.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where was his skeleton found?

2. What did the skeleton show?

1. Under a car park in Leicester.

2. Scoliosis and battle wounds.

Discuss:
  • Should science change how we judge Richard?

Grammar — Relative Clauses

To pack a lot of information into one sentence — who someone was, where something happened — we use relative clauses.

How it works:
• who for people, which for things, where for places, whose for possession.
• Non-defining clauses add extra information with commas: "Edward, who was deeply religious, rebuilt Westminster Abbey."
• Defining clauses identify which one — no commas.

1. Choose who, which, where or whose

Complete each sentence about Richard III. Use the word in brackets.

1. Richard, brother was Edward IV, became king in 1483.

2. Bosworth is the place he died.

3. The Stanleys, betrayed him, changed history.

1. whose

2. where

3. who

2. Join the sentences

1. The skeleton was found. It had scoliosis. (which)

2. Shakespeare wrote a play. It made Richard a villain. (that)

3. The car park was in Leicester. The skeleton was found there. (where)

1. The skeleton, which had scoliosis, was found.

2. Shakespeare wrote a play that made Richard a villain.

3. The car park where the skeleton was found was in Leicester.

Going deeper

Richard's reputation was shaped by Tudor writers and Shakespeare. Modern research gives a more balanced picture — but the princes remain a dark question.

Worth noticing: his scoliosis was real, but he was not the hunchback of Shakespeare's play.
Learning from the past — for today: Look again. Reputations can be unfair — or deserved. When new evidence appears, be willing to update your view.
