The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — A company too big to see

By the late 1960s, General Electric was one of the biggest companies in the world. It made almost everything: light bulbs, fridges, televisions, jet engines, even nuclear power stations. Every year it sold more. But there was a problem that worried its chief executive, Fred Borch. Sales were growing much faster than profits. People inside the company had a name for it: profitless growth.

The trouble was that GE's portfolio had become too big for anyone to understand. Every division asked for money, and every division had a good story. How could the leaders in New York compare a factory making toasters with a team building computers?

In 1969 Borch asked the consulting firm McKinsey for help. Together they reorganised GE into “strategic business units” and built a simple tool to judge them. Each unit was scored on two questions: How attractive is this market? and How strong are we in it? Each answer was low, medium or high — and three times three makes nine boxes.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was “profitless growth”?

2. Why was it hard for GE's leaders to decide where to put their money?

3. What two questions did the grid ask about each business?

1. Sales were rising, but profits were not rising nearly as fast.

2. The company made so many different things that the divisions were almost impossible to compare — and every one had a good reason to ask for money.

3. How attractive is the market, and how strong is GE in that market?

Part 2 — Green, yellow, red

The grid became known as the GE–McKinsey matrix, and it worked like a traffic light. Businesses in the three top-right boxes were coloured green: invest and grow. The three boxes along the middle diagonal were yellow: hold steady. The bottom-left boxes were red: take the cash, or divest. In the 1970s and 1980s, GE sold dozens of businesses and poured money into the strongest ones.

Then somebody noticed that the same shape could be used for something very different: people. A company is a portfolio of businesses — but it is also a portfolio of employees. Instead of market attractiveness and business strength, HR teams asked two new questions: How well does this person perform now? and How much potential do they have?

Nobody is completely sure who first drew the people version. But GE became famous for its annual talent reviews, especially under Jack Welch, its chief executive from 1981 to 2001, who was candid — some said brutal — about ranking staff. By the 1990s and 2000s, the “nine-box” had spread to large companies all over the world.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did a red box mean for a GE business?

2. What were the two new questions when the grid was used for people?

3. Do we know exactly who invented the people version?

1. Take the cash from it, or sell it (divest).

2. How well does the person perform now, and how much potential do they have for the future?

3. No — its exact origin is unclear, but GE's talent reviews helped make it famous.

Part 3 — The room where careers are decided

Today, in thousands of companies, managers meet once or twice a year for a talent review. Each employee's name goes on a sticky note or a screen, and the managers argue about which box it belongs in. The top-right box — high performance, high potential — holds the future leaders. They get training, big projects and a place on the list of possible successors. The bottom-left box holds people who are underperforming and may be asked to leave.

Supporters say the grid forces managers to be honest and to talk about people they might otherwise ignore. It can reveal a quiet employee who is far more talented than anyone had noticed.

Critics see a darker side. “Performance” can be measured, but “potential” is only a guess — and guesses are where bias hides. Managers may see potential in people who look and sound like themselves. And the labels stick: someone pigeonholed as “low potential” at 30 may never get the chance to prove otherwise. Some companies have stopped using the grid; many still rely on it. The nine boxes are simple. The people inside them are not.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What happens to people in the top-right box?

2. What is one advantage of the grid, according to its supporters?

3. Why do critics say that “potential” is a problem?

1. They are treated as future leaders: they get training, big projects and are considered as possible successors.

2. It forces managers to be honest and to discuss everyone — so a quiet but talented person can be noticed.

3. Potential can't really be measured; it is a guess, and guesses can be affected by bias.

How the tool works

The grid has two axes. Along the bottom: performance — how well someone is doing the job now. Up the side: potential — how far they could grow in the future. Each axis has three levels, so every person lands in one of nine boxes. The labels vary between companies; these are typical.

Using it, step by step

  1. Decide what performance means for this job — results, quality, behaviour — and look at evidence, not feelings.
  2. Decide what potential means: can this person handle a bigger or different role in the next few years?
  3. Place each person, then argue about it with other managers. The discussion is worth more than the box.
  4. Agree an action for each box: stretch the stars, support the rough diamonds, value the trusted professionals, and have an honest talk with anyone at risk.

Try it yourself

1. Star — high performance now and high potential.

2. Rough diamond — low performance so far, but high potential.

3. Trusted professional — high performance, low potential for a bigger role — valuable, but not a future leader.

4. Talent risk — low performance and low potential.

5. Future star — medium performance, high potential.

6. Core player — medium performance and medium potential — the middle box.

Where the tool breaks

Potential is a guess. Performance can be measured; potential can't. When people guess, bias creeps in — and the grid can make a guess look like a fact.

Boxes become labels. A person placed in a low box may get fewer chances, and so never show what they could do.

It's a snapshot. Someone struggling after a difficult year may look like a “risk” when they simply need time.

Grammar — comparing with much, far, slightly

A grid is all about comparison — this person is stronger than that one, this business is less attractive. At B2, the skill is to say how much stronger or weaker. The words before the comparative do that work.

A big difference: much / far / a lot / considerably + comparative.
“Our Paris office is far more profitable than our Lyon office.”  “Life in the new team is a lot easier.”
A small difference: slightly / a little / a bit / marginally + comparative.
“The second hotel was slightly cheaper, but a bit further from the beach.”
Not + as … as (with nearly or quite):
“The sequel is not nearly as good as the first film.” (a big gap)  “My Spanish is not quite as fluent as my French.” (a small gap)
The more …, the more … — two things changing together:
“The longer you wait, the harder it gets.”
Watch out: very cannot come before a comparative (very cheaper ✗). And more is not used with short adjectives: cheaper, not more cheap.
