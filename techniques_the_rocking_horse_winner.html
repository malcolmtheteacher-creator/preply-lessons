The Rocking-Horse Winner

D. H. Lawrence's story of a boy, a whispering house, and the terrible price of being lucky.

Today: you'll tell the story of a boy who rides a toy horse to find real winners — and you'll master the grammar of escalation: the more… the more…, louder and louder — the structures English uses when things spiral.
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers — the point is to start thinking about the ideas the story plays with.
Discuss:
  • Do you believe some people are simply born lucky? Or is "luck" always something else in disguise?
  • Can a family be poor on a good income? What makes people feel there is never enough money?
  • What do children understand about their parents' worries — even the worries no one says out loud?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four short activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. Nobody ever said it aloud, yet the phrase filled every room of the house like a whisper that only the children could hear.
a whisper (n) / to whisper (v) — very quiet speech, almost without voice; here, a sound just at the edge of hearing.
2. She smiled calmly at the neighbours, but underneath she was permanently anxious — about bills, about appearances, about the future.
anxious (adj) — worried and nervous, often about something that might happen.
3. His eyes were too bright, his cheeks burned, and he could not sit still — there was something feverish about the boy that frightened her.
feverish (adj) — hot and over-excited, as if ill with a fever; unnaturally intense.

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: stake · fortune · urge. Match each definition to the right word — out loud, then reveal.

1. Which word means the money you risk on a bet?
a stake (n) — "He put his whole stake on one horse." Also: to stake money on something.
2. Which word means a very large amount of money?
a fortune (n) — "The win made him a fortune." Also in the collocation to come into a fortune (to inherit one).
3. Which word means a strong, sudden need to do something?
an urge (n) — "He felt an overpowering urge to ride." Also a verb: to urge someone to do something.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. "Uncle Oscar put a bet on the race." A bet is…
  (a) money risked on guessing a result   (b) a ticket to watch a race   (c) a prize for the winner
(a) — a bet is money you risk on a result; if you guess right, you win more back. Collocation: to place a bet.
2. "The house was haunted by the unspoken phrase." Here, haunted means…
  (a) full of actual ghosts   (b) constantly troubled by something that will not go away   (c) empty and abandoned
(b) — haunted can be literal (ghosts) or figurative: troubled by a feeling, memory or idea that keeps returning. Lawrence means it both ways at once.

D · Word building & collocations

1. You know luck (n). Make the adjective ("a ___ man"), the opposite ("an ___ man"), and the adverb ("___, nobody was hurt").
lucky · unlucky · luckily. The story turns on one sentence: "Then I am lucky." Keep the whole family ready.
2. You know to win. Complete the family: the person is "the ___", the thing you get is "the ___-nings". And what is the opposite verb — with money?
the winner · the winnings (money won). Opposite: to lose — you win money or lose it. The title puts "winner" right next to a child's toy.
3. Collocation check: which verbs go with these nouns? "___ a secret" (keep it hidden), "___ a bet" (make one), "___ a fortune" (get one by luck or inheritance).
keep a secret · place a bet · come into a fortune. All three collocations appear in today's story.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The house that whispered

There was a woman who was beautiful, who started with all the advantages, yet she had no luck. She married for love, and the love turned to dust. She had bonny children, yet she felt they had been thrust upon her, and she could not love them. Only she herself knew that at the centre of her heart was a hard little place that could not feel love — not for anybody. Everybody else said of her: "She is such a good mother. She adores her children." Only she and her children knew it was not so. They read it in each other's eyes.

The family lived in a pleasant house with a garden, and they had discreet servants, and felt themselves superior to anyone in the neighbourhood. But there was always anxiety in the house. There was never enough money. The father went into town to some office; the mother tried this and that; but their small income was nowhere near enough for the social position they had to keep up. And so the house came to be haunted by the unspoken phrase: There must be more money! There must be more money! The children could hear it all the time, though nobody said it aloud. They heard it at Christmas, behind the shining new toys. Even the rocking-horse, bending his wooden head, seemed to be listening. It whispered from the springs of the still-swaying horse; the big doll heard it; the puppy heard it: There must be more money!

One day the boy, Paul, asked his mother: "Mother, why don't we keep a car of our own? Why do we always use uncle's?" "Because we're the poor members of the family," she said. "But why are we, mother?" "Well — I suppose," she said slowly and bitterly, "it's because your father has no luck." Paul was silent for a moment. "Is luck money, mother?" "No, Paul. Not quite. It's what causes you to have money. If you're lucky you have money. That's why it's better to be born lucky than rich. If you're rich, you may lose your money. But if you're lucky, you will always get more." "Well, anyhow," Paul said stoutly, "I'm a lucky person." "Why?" said his mother, with a sudden laugh. He stared at her. He didn't even know why he had said it. "God told me," he asserted. And he went off by himself, wanting to find luck — seeking it inwardly, on his big rocking-horse, charging madly into space, riding and riding until he got there.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the secret at the centre of the mother's heart — and who knows it?

2. What does the house whisper, and where do the children hear it?

1. She cannot feel love — not for her husband, not for her children — though everyone else believes she adores them. Only she and the children know; they read it in each other's eyes.

2. "There must be more money!" — it whispers from every room, behind the Christmas toys, even from the springs of the rocking-horse itself, though nobody ever says it aloud.

Discuss:
  • "It's better to be born lucky than rich." Do you agree with the mother? What is that belief doing to her son as she says it?

Part 2 — The partnership

Paul's secret ally was Bassett, the young gardener, who had been wounded in the war and had got his present job through Uncle Oscar. Bassett was a keen follower of the racing news, and the boy talked horses with him endlessly. One day Uncle Oscar took Paul for a ride in his car and asked, casually, about the racing. He discovered, to his astonishment, that the boy and the gardener were partners — and that they were winning. Paul had started with ten shillings Bassett had put on for him; now, said the child calmly, he had three hundred pounds in reserve. "When I'm sure," Paul explained, "like about Daffodil, we go strong, for all we have. When I'm not sure, we're careful. Are you a lucky uncle, would you like to be partners?"

Uncle Oscar, half-amused and half-serious, became a partner. At the Lincoln races, Daffodil came in first at four to one. Paul's face was blazing with a strange, quiet fire. He had made ten thousand pounds. "You see," he told his uncle, "when I'm absolutely sure — then we go strong." "But how do you know?" Oscar asked. "I don't know," said the boy uneasily. "I'm absolutely sure — and then it's as if I knew, uncle; that's all." How did he get sure? Riding his rocking-horse, furiously, until the charging wooden gallop carried him to the place where the name of the winner simply — arrived.

Paul had a plan for the money. "I started it for mother," he told his uncle. "She said she had no luck, because father is unlucky; so I thought if I was lucky, it might stop the whispering." "What whispering?" "Our house. It whispers — about money. I hate it. I thought if I was lucky…" So they arranged, through the family lawyer, for the mother to receive five thousand pounds — a thousand a year on her birthday, from an unknown relative. But on the morning of her birthday, the mother's face was cold and expressionless; and by afternoon she had been to the lawyer and asked whether the whole five thousand could be advanced at once. She got it — and the house went mad. There were new furnishings, a tutor for Paul, flowers in winter, luxury everywhere. And the voices in the house did not stop. They went suddenly mad, like a chorus of frogs on a spring evening, screaming behind the sprays of mimosa and almond-blossom: There must be more money! Oh-h-h; there must be more money. Oh, now, now-w! Now-w-w — there must be more money! — more than ever! More than ever!

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Paul's system work — when do he and Bassett "go strong"?

2. What happens when the mother receives the birthday money?

1. Paul rides his rocking-horse furiously until he becomes "absolutely sure" of a winner's name. When he is sure, they bet everything they have; when he is not sure, they bet carefully. The certainty simply arrives — he cannot explain it.

2. Instead of a thousand a year, she has the whole five thousand advanced at once and spends it on luxury. The whispering doesn't stop — it grows louder and madder than ever: "There must be more money — more than ever!"

Discuss:
  • Paul gives secretly, hoping to silence the house. Why does the money make the whispering louder instead of quieter? What is Lawrence saying about wanting?

Part 3 — Malabar

The secret certainty began to fail him. He didn't "know" for the Grand National, and lost a hundred pounds. He didn't "know" for the Lincoln, and lost fifty. He grew wild-eyed and strange, as if something were about to explode in him. "Don't worry about it, son," Uncle Oscar urged. But the boy seemed not to hear. "I've got to know for the Derby!" he insisted. His mother, feeling a strange anxiety about him at last, told him he ought to be sent to the seaside, away from the racing. "Not before the Derby, mother!" he pleaded. She noticed, uneasily, how his eyes blazed; and she noticed, too, that he had had his rocking-horse — a boy of nearly fourteen — moved up to his own bedroom at the top of the house. "Surely you're too big for a rocking-horse!" she had said. "Well, you see, mother, till I can have a real horse, I like to have some sort of animal about," was his quaint answer.

Two nights before the Derby, at a big party in town, the mother was seized by one of her rushes of anxiety about the boy — anxiety so strong she could hardly speak. She rang the house: "Are the children all right, Miss Wilmot?" "Oh, yes, they are quite all right." But close to midnight she and her husband drove home, and she stole upstairs to her son's room. Outside the door she stopped and listened. There was a strange, heavy, and yet not loud noise. Something huge, in violent, hushed motion. Frozen with fear, she gently turned the door-handle. The room was dark — yet by the window she heard and saw something plunging to and fro. She switched on the light: there was her son, in his green pyjamas, madly surging on the rocking-horse. "Paul!" she cried. "Whatever are you doing?" "It's Malabar!" he screamed in a powerful, strange voice. "It's Malabar!" And he fell with a crash to the ground, unconscious, into the brain-fever that possessed him for days and nights.

Paul lay critically ill, tossing and talking of horses, while his mother sat stone-hearted with grief by his bed. Bassett — allowed in for one moment — whispered to the boy that Malabar had come in first: Paul's stake, at fourteen to one, had won him over seventy thousand pounds; with what he had before, more than eighty thousand in all. "Malabar came in all right, Master Paul." "Did I say Malabar, mother?" the boy cried, struggling up. "Do you think I'm lucky, mother? I knew Malabar, didn't I? Over eighty thousand pounds! I call that lucky, don't you, mother? … Mother, did I ever tell you? I am lucky!" "No, you never did," said his mother. But the boy died in the night. And as she sat, her brother's voice came to her: "My God, Hester, you're eighty-odd thousand to the good, and a poor devil of a son to the bad. But, poor devil, poor devil, he's best gone out of a life where he rides his rocking-horse to find a winner."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the mother find when she opens Paul's bedroom door?

2. What are Paul's last words about — and what does Uncle Oscar's final sentence mean?

1. Her son, nearly fourteen, riding his rocking-horse madly in the dark. He screams "It's Malabar!" — the Derby winner's name — and collapses into brain-fever.

2. Being lucky: "Mother, did I ever tell you? I am lucky!" — he dies still trying to win the one thing the money was for: her love. Oscar's bitter arithmetic — eighty thousand to the good, a son to the bad — names the exchange out loud: the family has traded a child for a fortune, and Oscar thinks the boy is better off dead than living in a world where such a trade is possible.

Discuss:
  • Paul's last question is "Do you think I'm lucky, mother?" What was he really asking her — and does she ever answer it?

Grammar — escalation: the more… the more… and louder and louder

This story is built on a spiral: more money brings more wanting; the harder Paul rides, the higher the stakes climb. English has two elegant structures for exactly this — things that grow together, and things that keep growing.

Pattern 1 — the + comparative…, the + comparative… (two things growing together):
• The more you practise, the better you get.
• The older I get, the less I worry.
• The harder the exam, the sweeter the result.
Structure: the + comparative + clause, the + comparative + clause. Note the fixed short form: the more, the merrier.
Pattern 2 — comparative and comparative (one thing growing on its own):
• The queue grew longer and longer.
• Prices rise higher and higher every year.
• With long adjectives: more and more expensive, more and more anxious.
Common verbs before it: get, grow, become — "it got colder and colder", "she became more and more impatient".

1. Two growing together — build the pattern

Complete each sentence about the story with the correct "the + comparative" form of the word in brackets.

1. The more money came into the house, (loud) the whispering became.

2. The (sure) Paul felt, the more they staked on the race.

3. The closer the Derby came, (desperate) the boy grew.

1. the louder (short adjective → the + -er)

2. surer (the surer Paul felt… — short adjective)

3. the more desperate (long adjective → the more + adjective)

2. One thing spiralling — comparative and comparative

Complete each sentence with the "…and…" pattern of the adjective in brackets.

1. After the five thousand was spent, the voices in the house screamed (mad).

2. As the races went by, Paul's eyes grew (wild).

3. His mother became (anxious) about the boy she thought she could not love.

1. madder and madder

2. wilder and wilder

3. more and more anxious (long adjective → more and more + adjective)

3. Retell the spiral — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap using the pattern hinted in brackets. Read the whole paragraph aloud when you finish.

It was a perfect trap: the family had, it seemed to need (much / much — two growing together). Paul rode (hard — spiralling), and the secret grew to carry (difficult — spiralling). The more he won, the house whispered (loud).

the more · the more · harder and harder · more and more difficult · the louder

Notice how the two patterns work together: "the more…, the more…" links two changes; "harder and harder" spirals one change on its own.

Going deeper

Lawrence wrote this story in 1926, near the end of his life, for an anthology of ghost stories. He delivered something stranger: a fairy tale with no magic wand, where the haunted thing is not a castle but a family's relationship with money — and with love.

The heart of it. The story runs on a terrible substitution: in this house, money has taken the place of love. The mother cannot love, so she keeps accounts; Paul cannot win her love, so he wins money — the only currency the house understands. That is why the five thousand pounds makes the whispering louder: the hunger was never for money at all, so money can never feed it. Paul's final cry — "I am lucky!" — is a child still trying to earn what should have been free. The story never explains how the rocking-horse works, and it doesn't need to: the mechanism is the metaphor. A child rides a motionless horse, harder and harder, going nowhere — and pays with everything he has.

Talk it through:
  • "There must be more money!" No one ever says it aloud — the children hear it in the house itself. What do children in real families "hear" that is never said? Give an example.
  • Whose fault is Paul's death? Rank them — the mother, the father, Uncle Oscar, Bassett, Paul himself, the "house" — and defend your order. Who could have stopped it, and when?
  • The mother defines luck as "what causes you to have money". Paul makes that definition literally true — and it kills him. What would a healthier answer to "What is luck?" have been?
  • Uncle Oscar's last words say Paul is "best gone" from such a life. Is that wisdom, cruelty, or the only honest sentence in the whole story?

The family's income was, in fact, perfectly reasonable — and never enough. Build an argument: is "enough money" a real amount, or a feeling that money itself can never produce? Use the story as your first piece of evidence, then bring in the modern world: salaries, lifestyle, social media, "keeping up". The more examples you give, the stronger your case will be.

Task C · The spiral (4 minutes)

The story is a portrait of a spiral — something that grows the more it is fed. Describe a spiral you have seen or can imagine, using today's grammar to trace how it escalates.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • A habit that grew: the more time online, the harder it became to stop.
  • Ambition at work: the higher someone climbed, the more they needed the next step.
  • A collector, a gamer, a perfectionist — someone for whom "one more" was never the last.
  • Fame or followers: the more attention arrives, the more it takes to feel seen.
  • A good spiral: the more you learn a language, the more doors open, the more you want to learn.
  • A family keeping up appearances — where did the pressure come from, and where did it lead?
