A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XXXVIII of L

The Social Contract

In which a self-taught genius declares that man is born free but is everywhere in chains — and sets the stage for revolution

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778), self-taught, brilliant, and profoundly contradictory. He declares that humans are born naturally good, yet abandoned his five children to foundling homes. He champions the general will, yet his ideas enabled tyranny. He wrote one of the most influential books ever published: The Social Contract (1762). His opening sentence is revolutionary: "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." Unlike Hobbes, Rousseau says we are not naturally selfish. Unlike Augustine, we are not naturally broken. We are naturally good. Society corrupts us. And freedom comes not through power or grace, but through the general will—the collective voice of the people. By the end, you will understand why Rousseau inspired both democracy and terror, and why a question haunts us: if humans are naturally good, why do we act so badly?

Three Anthropologies

Every philosophy rests on an answer to one question: What are humans really?

Augustine said we are broken—marked by original sin, needing grace. Hobbes said we are selfish—naturally competitive, needing power. Rousseau says we are naturally good—corrupted by society, needing freedom. These three answers lead to three different visions of how we should live. Hold all three in mind as you work through this lesson. By the end, you will see why the question matters so much.

II. The Story — The Contradiction at the Heart

Jean-Jacques Rousseau was born in Geneva in 1712, the son of a watchmaker. His mother died nine days after his birth. He was raised by his father, who filled his head with tales of ancient Rome and Geneva's republican past. His father abandoned him when Rousseau was ten. He never finished school. He was largely self-taught—a radical fact in an age of formal education.

The Wanderer and the Reformer

Rousseau wandered Europe: tutor, musician, secretary, engraver. In Paris, he became friends with Diderot. He wrote about music and sociology. In 1750, he won a prize essay contest with a radical thesis: civilization corrupts us. The noble savage is happier than civilized man. This made him famous.

In 1762, he published two masterworks simultaneously: The Social Contract and Émile, or On Education. Both were condemned. Both were banned. Both changed the world.

The Thesis: Natural Goodness

Rousseau began The Social Contract with a shocking declaration:

Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains. One thinks himself the master of others, and still remains a slave. — Jean-Jacques Rousseau, The Social Contract, Book I, Chapter 1

But notice: Rousseau does not blame human nature. He blames society. In a state of nature, humans are good. We have natural pity for others' suffering. We care for ourselves and our children. We do not make war. We do not create inequality. These are inventions of civilization. This is radically different from Hobbes, who saw the state of nature as a war of all against all. Rousseau saw it as a state of peace and innocence.

In his Discourse on Inequality (1755), Rousseau explained how this corruption happened. Originally, humans lived solitary lives in forests, guided by natural pity (compassion for others' suffering) and natural self-love (care for oneself). But when property was invented—when someone said "this is mine"—everything changed. Property created inequality. Inequality created pride and shame. And civilization, built on property and inequality, corrupted our natural goodness.

The Solution: The General Will

How do we escape the chains of society? Not through Hobbes's absolute authority. Not through the divine right of kings. Instead, through the general will—the collective voice of the people united for the common good.

When I join society, I surrender my natural freedom. But I gain something better: civil freedom, limited by the general will. I am free not because I do whatever I want, but because I obey a law I prescribe to myself—the law of the collective people.

Freedom, then, is a paradox. True freedom is obedience to the general will. This sounds strange. But Rousseau's idea is powerful: I am free when I choose to live with others according to rules we all agree to. I am enslaved when I act according to appetites that society has instilled in me (greed, vanity, jealousy).

Education and Awakening

In Émile, Rousseau imagined a child educated in freedom. Not imposed with abstract knowledge. Not forced into the mold of conventional society. Instead, allowed to develop naturally, learning from experience, from sensation, from his own curiosity. Education should awaken the human to what he naturally is—free, good, curious, capable of growth.

Rousseau's Dark Secret. Rousseau preached natural education and the goodness of parents' love for children. Yet he abandoned all five of his children to foundling homes. This contradiction destroys the common reading of Rousseau. It suggests that his philosophy was partly a fantasy—a dream of what humans could be, not a description of what we are.

The Influence and the Terror

Rousseau's ideas electrified Europe. They inspired the French Revolution. "The general will" became the watchword of the revolution. But the revolution devoured itself in terror. Robespierre claimed to represent the general will while guillotining thousands. How? Because Rousseau's general will, taken to its extreme, can justify tyranny. If the general will is supreme, and I disagree with it, am I not enslaved to a tyranny of the majority?

Rousseau died in 1778, just as the revolution was beginning. He never saw what his ideas would become.

III. The Ideas — Three Anthropologies in Conflict

Rousseau’s philosophy is built on three revolutionary ideas. Let’s explore them.

Idea 1: Natural Goodness

Humans are naturally good. Society corrupts them.

This directly contradicts Augustine (humans are naturally broken) and Hobbes (humans are naturally selfish). Rousseau says the state of nature is peaceful and good. Humans in nature have natural compassion. They live without property, hierarchy, or artificial desire. It is only civilization—property, inequality, luxury—that makes us corrupt.

This idea inspired Romanticism, progressive education, and modern environmentalism. It grounds the idea that we can reform society and awaken our true nature.

Idea 2: The General Will

Legitimate authority comes from the collective will of the people, not from God or nature or a single sovereign.

This is the philosophical foundation of democracy. I am free when I obey a law I give myself collectively with others. The general will is not my private will. It is the voice of the community united. When the general will decides, I must obey—but in obeying, I obey myself (as a member of the collective).

This is profoundly different from both monarchy (one person rules) and direct democracy (everyone votes on every question). The general will is something deeper—the spirit of the community, the voice of the people united.

Idea 3: Education as Awakening

True education does not impose. It awakens. Children should learn from nature and experience, not from books and authority.

This revolutionized pedagogy. It says education should follow the child's natural development, not force external forms upon the child. Let the child learn by doing. Let curiosity be the teacher. Let nature be the textbook. This idea lives on in Montessori schools, progressive education, and experiential learning. In Émile, Rousseau imagines a boy educated in isolation from society, learning from direct experience with the natural world. The tutor guides but does not impose. The child discovers principles through action and sensation, not through lectures and rules.

The goal of education is not to fill the child with knowledge, but to awaken the child to his own nature—to help him become fully human, free, and good. This is radical. It says childhood is not preparation for adulthood, but a valuable stage in itself, with its own logic and beauty. Respect the child.

The Thesis Seed: Three Answers to Human Nature

Now we have three anthropologies competing for truth:

Augustine: Humans are naturally broken—marked by original sin. We need grace.

Hobbes: Humans are naturally selfish—competitive and fearful. We need power and law.

Rousseau: Humans are naturally good—compassionate and free. We need liberation from corrupting society.

Which is true? The answer you give shapes everything—your politics, your ethics, your hope for human flourishing. If humans are broken, reform is limited. If humans are selfish, strong law is necessary. If humans are good, liberation is possible. Rousseau believed liberation was possible. Later history would question this belief. But his hope lives on in every movement for justice and reform.

Location 38: The Garden and the City

You arrive at a location that shows two worlds—a split vision. On one side, a garden where a child runs free, learning from nature, from his own curiosity. Wild, innocent, good. On the other side, a city where people gather in circles, speaking as one voice—the general will. Both are natural. Both are human. The contradiction between them is the heart of Rousseau.
