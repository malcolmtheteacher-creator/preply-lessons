The Social Contract

Rousseau: Natural goodness, the general will, and liberation through education

I. Before We Begin

Are humans naturally good or naturally selfish?

II. The Story

Rousseau (1712–1778) began with a radical premise: humans are naturally good, but society corrupts them. This contradicts Hobbes, Augustine, and Aristotle. It became the foundation of Romanticism and modern educational theory.

Natural Goodness

In nature, Rousseau argued, humans are solitary, compassionate, and free. We have natural pity for others' suffering. Self-interest exists, but it is balanced by compassion. Only when society is created does corruption begin. Property, inequality, and artificial desires make us selfish.

Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains. One thinks himself the master of others, and still remains a slave.— Jean-Jacques Rousseau, The Social Contract, Book I, Chapter 1

The General Will

Rousseau's solution to Hobbes: not absolute authority but the general will—the collective voice of the people united in pursuit of the common good. When I join society, I surrender my natural freedom but gain civil freedom, which is limited by the general will. Freedom is obedience to a law one prescribes to oneself.

Education and Awakening

Through education, we can awaken to our true nature. Rousseau's novel Émile describes a child educated in freedom, learning from nature and experience, not from books and tradition. Education should liberate, not constrain.

III. The Ideas

Idea 1: Natural Goodness (Against Original Sin)

Humans are born good. Society corrupts them. This reverses Augustine and the Christian tradition. It makes education and social reform possible. If humans are naturally good, we can build a good society.

Idea 2: The General Will

Legitimate authority comes from the collective will of the people. This became the philosophical foundation of democracy. Not the will of the sovereign, but the will of the people united.

Idea 3: Education as Liberation

True education awakens freedom, not constrains it. This revolutionized pedagogy. Education should follow the natural development of the child, not impose external forms.

V. Room 38 of Your Philosophy Palace

Location 38: The Garden and the City

You arrive at a location that shows two worlds. On one side, a garden where a child learns freely from nature, from sensation, from his own curiosity. On the other, a city where people gather, speaking with one voice—the general will. Both are natural. Both are good.

The 3 Images

1. A child in a garden, learning from nature, laughing, free — Natural goodness. Education as awakening.

2. People in a circle, speaking as one, their voices harmonizing — The general will. Collective freedom.

3. Chains breaking, light pouring through — Liberation. Rousseau's promise that we can be free.
