How Much Land Does a Man Need?

Leo Tolstoy (1886) — a peasant is offered all the land he can walk around in a single day

Easier version available: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Read in four parts

Tolstoy asks the question in his title and makes you wait the whole story for the answer. This is a graded retelling in four movements — read each part, pause for the discussion box, then go on. The last line answers the title. Do not skip ahead.

Part 1 — The two sisters

The elder sister had married a merchant in the town; the younger had married a peasant called Pahóm, out in the village. One evening the two sisters were sitting together, and they began to argue about which life was better. The townswoman boasted of the comforts of the town — the fine clothes, the theatres, the rich food. The peasant's wife defended the land. "We may live more roughly," she said, "but we are free of worry. You may earn more, but you may lose it all overnight."

Pahóm was lying on the stove above them, and he listened. "It is true," he thought to himself. "If I had plenty of land, I would not fear the Devil himself." Now the Devil had been sitting behind the stove, and he had heard everything. "Good," thought the Devil. "We shall have a tussle. I will give you land enough — and through that very land I will get you in my power."

Pause and discuss:
  • Pahóm says that with enough land he would not fear even the Devil. Why is that exactly the wrong thing to say?
  • The Devil treats Pahóm's confidence as an opening. What in a person does a temptation usually need before it can work?
  • Tolstoy lets us hear the bargain before Pahóm makes a single move. What does that do to how we read the rest?

Part 2 — A little is never enough

Things began to go Pahóm's way. A piece of land came up for sale nearby, and he scraped together the money and bought forty acres. He was overjoyed. He ploughed and sowed on his own land, and his harvests were good. But his neighbours' cattle kept straying into his fields, and quarrels followed, and Pahóm grew bitter. "If I had more land of my own," he kept thinking, "none of this would happen."

Then a passing dealer told him that beyond the Volga the land was richer and far cheaper. Pahóm sold up, moved his family, and took a larger holding. He prospered again — and again it was not enough. He heard that the Bashkirs, far out on the steppe, were selling vast tracts for almost nothing, and he could not rest until he had gone to see for himself.

Pause and discuss:
  • Each time Pahóm gets what he wanted, the wanting moves. Where have you seen that pattern — in a person, a company, a country?
  • Notice the rhythm Tolstoy builds: get land, prosper, grow restless, move on. Why repeat the cycle three times before the climax?

Part 3 — The Bashkirs' bargain

The Bashkirs welcomed Pahóm with kumiss and tea, and when he explained what he wanted, they laughed and named their price. For one thousand roubles, the chief said, Pahóm could have as much land as he liked.

"As much as I like? But how do you measure it?"

"We measure it by the day. Whatever land you can walk round in a single day is yours — for a thousand roubles. But there is one condition: you must return to the very spot where you started before the sun sets. If you do not return in time, your money is lost and you get nothing."

Pahóm was delighted. He would start at dawn, walk a great circle, mark his turns with a spade, and be back by sunset. "In a day," he thought, "a man can walk round an enormous tract." He could hardly sleep for excitement, and in the night he dreamed — strangely — of lying dead at the feet of the laughing chief.

Pause and discuss:
  • The deal sounds generous. Where, exactly, is the trap hidden inside it?
  • Tolstoy gives Pahóm a warning dream the night before. Why warn the character — and us — and let him go anyway?

Part 4 — The walk

At sunrise Pahóm set off. The land was so good that he kept going further than he had planned — there was always a better patch just ahead, a hollow worth including, a stretch of black earth he could not bear to leave out. By the time he turned for the second corner the sun was already high. He turned again, too late, and saw how far he still had to go. The circle he had drawn was far too big.

Now he began to be afraid. He threw away his coat, his boots, his flask — everything that weighed him down — and he ran. The sun was sinking. He could see the hillock where he had started, and the Bashkirs waving their caps, and the chief sitting there. His breath tore at his chest. The sun touched the horizon just as he reached the foot of the hill, and with the last of his strength he threw himself forward and touched the cap on the ground with his hands. "Ah, what a fine fellow!" cried the chief. "He has gained much land."

Pahóm's servant ran to lift him up — but Pahóm was dead. The Bashkirs clicked their tongues in pity. The servant picked up the spade, dug a grave, and buried him in it: six feet from his head to his heels, exactly as much land as a man needs.

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • Read the last line again. How does it answer the title — and how does it turn the whole story into a single argument?
  • Pahóm wins the land and loses his life in the same instant. Is the story about greed, about death, or about something quieter — the inability to stop?
  • Tolstoy never lets us inside the chief's head, or the Devil's, at the end. We only see the spade and the grave. Why end on an action rather than a comment?
  • Your turn: Describe a time you kept walking past the point where you had enough — in money, work, or ambition. What made you unable to turn back?

The grammar this story teaches itself

A story is a machine for arranging time. Tolstoy runs three past structures side by side: the past simple for the chain of events, the past continuous for the scene already in motion, and the past perfect for what had happened before. Learn to switch between them and you can narrate anything.

1. Past simple vs past continuous — the event and its backdrop

Use the past continuous (was/were + -ing) for the longer action already in progress, and the past simple for the shorter action that interrupts or advances it. ("The two sisters were sitting together when they began to argue.")

1. Pahóm (lie) on the stove while the women (argue) below him.

2. While he (plough) his own field, his neighbour's cattle (stray) onto it.

3. The sun (sink) as Pahóm (reach) the foot of the hill.

4. The Bashkirs (wave) their caps when he finally (appear).

1. was lying / were arguing

2. was ploughing / strayed

3. was sinking / reached

4. were waving / appeared

2. Past perfect — stepping back behind the story

Use the past perfect (had + past participle) for an action that happened before the past moment you are describing. It lets you tell events out of order without confusing the reader. ("The Devil had heard everything before Pahóm made a single move.")

1. By the time the dealer arrived, Pahóm (already / buy) his first forty acres.

2. He moved beyond the Volga because he (hear) that the land there was cheaper.

3. The Devil (sit) behind the stove, so he knew Pahóm's boast.

4. The night before the walk, Pahóm (dream) of his own death.

1. had already bought

2. had heard

3. had been sitting (past perfect continuous — duration before the past moment)

4. had dreamed / had dreamt

3. Sequencing a narrative — the connectors that move time

Choose the time-marker that fits the gap. These are the words that tell a listener whether you are moving forward, jumping back, or marking a turning point.

1. He bought forty acres. long, he wanted more. (soon after)

2. he reached the third corner, it was already too late. (by the time)

3. He ran on, throwing things away he went. (at the same time)

4. He touched the cap; , he fell dead. (in the very next moment)

1. Before (Before long = soon)

2. By the time

3. as (as he went = during)

4. the next instant / an instant later

What Tolstoy does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are why a story written in 1886 still closes like a trap.

1. The title as a question — and a delayed answer

Tolstoy puts the question in the title and then withholds the answer for the whole story. We read the entire tale as a search for the figure, and the last line supplies it — six feet — and reframes everything that came before.

2. Foreshadowing — the warning we are allowed to see

The Devil's plan in Part 1 and the death-dream in Part 3 foreshadow the ending. Tolstoy is not hiding the outcome; he is making us watch Pahóm walk toward it. The dread comes not from surprise but from inevitability.

3. The repeating cycle — structure as argument

Forty acres, then the Volga, then the steppe: Tolstoy repeats the cycle of get-prosper-tire three times. The repetition distils a whole psychology — that the wanting is in the man, not in the land. Structure does the moral work.

4. The flat final sentence — understatement

After the desperate run, the prose goes utterly calm: a spade, a grave, six feet. Tolstoy undercuts the drama with plainness, and the plainness is what makes it land. He ironises the whole chase: all that running, for a plot the length of a body.

Spot-the-technique — quick MCQ

Tolstoy tells us the Devil's plan in Part 1, long before Pahóm walks. What is this technique, and what does it do?
Pahóm buys land three separate times, prospering and then tiring each time. Why does Tolstoy repeat the cycle instead of going straight to the Bashkirs?
The final sentence reports the burial in plain, almost bored language. What is the effect of that flatness?

Three speaking tasks

Do all three. Each one builds on the last. The timer is for your benefit — it forces you to fill the time rather than escape it. Use the past tenses and effect verbs from the previous tabs.

Task A · Tell the Whole Story in Four Sentences (4 minutes)

Retell "How Much Land Does a Man Need?" using exactly four sentences — one per part. The challenge is the grammar: each sentence must contain at least one past perfect ("By the time he reached the steppe, he had already…") and the whole retelling must move cleanly from background to climax.

Then say which single sentence you think carries the moral, and why.

Task B · A goal you set too big (4 minutes)

Describe a time you took on more than you could manage — a target, a plan, or an ambition that turned out to be too much for the time you had. Tell it in order: how it started, how the finish line kept seeming just within reach, and the moment you realised you had gone too far.

Narrate using past continuous for the backdrop ("Everything was going well when…") and past simple for the turn. Use at least three effect verbs from Tab 3.

Task C · How Much Is Enough? (4 minutes)

Tolstoy's answer is grim: six feet. Give your own. For two minutes, argue what "enough" actually looks like for a person — in money, space, work, or recognition. For two more, argue the opposite case: that the restless wanting Pahóm shows is also what builds farms, cities and discoveries, and that "enough" can be its own kind of trap.

Speak both sides honestly. Use comparatives ("more than I need", "less than I feared") and at least one structure of contrast ("Whereas Pahóm…", "On the other hand…").

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The Devil never forces Pahóm to do anything. Every step is Pahóm's own choice. Does that make the story more comforting or more frightening?
  2. Is the problem greed, or is it the inability to stop and say "this is enough"? Are they the same thing?
  3. Tolstoy wrote this as a moral tale. Do you read it as a warning, or as a description of how progress actually happens?
  4. If you could give Pahóm one sentence to read at the third corner, before he turned too late, what would it say?
