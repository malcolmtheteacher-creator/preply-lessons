Effective Onboarding

C1 Level · People Development

First Impressions Matter

The first 90 days of a new hire matter far more than most people realise. This is when they decide if they've made the right choice. When they form opinions about the team, the culture, the work.

Reflect on your first day at a job:

  • What made it great or terrible?
  • What did you feel anxious about?
  • Who helped you succeed?
  • What information or clarity would have helped?
  • When did you feel like you truly belonged?

The First Day: Foundation

A good first day is simple: they feel welcome, they're not lost, and they know what happens next. A bad first day? They sit waiting for someone to check on them. Their welcome email has typos. No one's arranged their desk. Worse: it signals the organisation is disorganised and doesn't care.

The cost of a bad first-day experience: people start job hunting within weeks. Seriously.

The First Week: Overwhelm

New hires are drowning in information: systems, passwords, people, processes, history, context. Some organisations think: throw them in the deep end, they'll figure it out.

This is wrong. The brain can only absorb so much. Without structure, information goes in one ear and out the other. They feel confused and incompetent.

Better: structured information in digestible chunks. First day is logistics and welcome. First week is context and key people. First month is actual projects with support. This isn't coddling—it's smart.

The First 90 Days: Real Integration

By 90 days, someone should be productive and integrated. They understand the work, they know the players, they feel like part of the team. This doesn't happen by accident. It requires planning.

The 30-60-90 framework: clarity on what success looks like at each phase. At 30 days: understand the role, know key people. At 60 days: starting to deliver, understanding politics and priorities. At 90 days: fully ramped, contributing, clear about career path.

Think about your team: How long does it actually take a new hire to become fully productive? Is it tracked? Is it the same for everyone?

Vocabulary & Frameworks

Master the language and concepts of effective onboarding.

Onboarding Process of integrating a new employee into the organisation; includes training, socialisation, and productivity ramp-up.
Induction Initial orientation covering company policies, procedures, systems, and culture; the formal introduction phase.
Buddy System Pairing new hires with experienced team members for informal mentoring, informal learning, and social integration.
Ramp-up Period where someone goes from unproductive (onboarding) to fully productive in their role; varies by role and person.
Probation Trial period (usually 3-6 months) where employer and employee evaluate fit before permanent employment begins.
Orientation Introduction to organisation, building, facilities, systems, and key policies; the physical and logistical introduction.
Expectations Clear understanding of what success looks like, what the role requires, and what's needed to thrive.
Integration Becoming part of the team; forming relationships, understanding culture, feeling like you belong.

The 30-60-90 Framework

30 Days (Learning Phase): Focus on understanding. Learn the organisation, the role, the key people and relationships. Listen more than you talk. Ask questions. Read documentation. Start small projects with clear guardrails.

Deliverables: Understand org strategy, your role within it, key processes, who are the key stakeholders, initial project ideas.

60 Days (Contributing Phase): Now you're starting to deliver. You know enough to take on real projects. You're integrating more with the team. You're getting feedback and adjusting.

Deliverables: Complete initial projects, contribute to team goals, demonstrate competence, show cultural fit.

90 Days (Integration Phase): You're a member of the team. You're productive. You understand what you're doing and why. You have relationships. You know what comes next career-wise.

Deliverables: Full productivity in role, proven competence, clear understanding of career development path, integrated into team and culture.

Scenario: Design Your 90-Day Plan

You're bringing on a senior hire (a direct report or peer). They're strong, they're moving from another organisation, and you want to set them up for success. Design a 90-day onboarding plan you'd present to HR.

10:00

Part 1: First 30 Days

What do they need to learn and accomplish in the first month? Be specific: meetings, people, documents, projects. What are your week-by-week priorities?

Week 1: Orientation and logistics. Ensure desk/tech are ready. Meet the team. Get keys and access. Learn org structure, strategy, key priorities.

Week 2: Meeting phase. One-on-ones with key stakeholders. Understand current projects, politics, relationships. Start reading documentation.

Week 3: Deep dives. Understand your specific role and responsibilities in detail. Sit in on key meetings. Understand what success looks like.

Week 4: First small project. Something low-risk where they can contribute quickly and get success, feedback, integration.

30-day checkpoint: One-on-one with manager. How is it going? Do they understand the role? Any major gaps? Adjust as needed.

Part 2: Days 30-60

Now they're starting to deliver. What projects should they lead or contribute to? How do you build their credibility and relationships? What's the progression?

30-60 days focus: Take on a real project (with guardrails). Lead a small initiative or contribute meaningfully to a larger one. Present work/learning to the team. Get peer feedback.

Relationship building: Informal coffees with peers and stakeholders. Participate in team social events. Start mentoring/collaborating with direct reports.

60-day checkpoint: Broader feedback session. Not just from you, but from team members, peers, stakeholders. How are they integrating? Where are they strong? Where do they need development?

Part 3: Days 60-90

Final phase. They should be fully integrated and productive. What does that mean for your role? What are you handing off to them? How do you confirm they're ready?

60-90 days focus: Full productivity. They own projects without guardrails. They're representing your team externally. They're contributing to strategic thinking.

Career conversation: Discuss what comes next. How do they see their role developing? What do they want to learn? Career path over next 12-24 months?

90-day evaluation: Formal assessment. Are they meeting expectations? Ready for full load? Where do they shine? What development support do they need?

Beyond 90 days: Transition to regular management cycle. They're a full team member now. Continue to develop them, but they're past onboarding.

Building Belonging

Effective onboarding isn't just about productivity. It's about belonging. Making someone feel like they made the right choice, like they fit, like they're part of something.

Reflection questions:

  • When you join a new team, what makes you feel like you belong?
  • What's the relationship between onboarding quality and retention?
  • How much of onboarding is about systems/processes vs. relationships/belonging?
  • Who's responsible for onboarding—HR, your manager, the team, you?
  • How do you know when someone's been successfully onboarded?

Remote Onboarding Challenges

Virtual onboarding is harder. There's no informal learning at the water cooler. No casual conversations that build relationships. No spontaneous help.

To make it work:

  • Structure it more: Schedule everything. Don't assume informal integration will happen.
  • Buddy system: Assign someone to check in, answer questions, include them socially.
  • Camera on: More face-to-face in early phase builds connection better than just video calls.
  • In-person time: If possible, have them come in-office for first week or month. Relationship-building is faster in person.
  • Documented processes: Write everything down. Remote workers can't guess; they need clarity.

Information Overload

Most organisations dump information on new hires: 20 links to read, 15 systems to learn, 50 people to meet. Within three days, none of it stuck.

Better: structured information. What do they need day one? Week one? Month one? Sequence it. Repeat it. Make it digestible.

A document that clearly spells out "Your first week looks like this: Monday you'll learn X, Tuesday you'll meet Y people, etc." is worth far more than a chaotic overwhelming pile of materials.

The Long-Term Perspective

Onboarding isn't a one-time event. It's continuous. By three months, someone's ramping up. By six months, they're established. By a year, they're integrated.

Good managers maintain check-ins through all of this. They're asking: "How are you really doing? Are you getting what you need? How's the team dynamics feeling? Where do you want to go next?"

This investment pays off in retention, performance, and culture. Onboarded people stay. People left to figure it out alone often leave.

Action step: Think of your newest team member. How would you rate their onboarding experience on 1-10? What could have been better? What's one thing you could improve for the next new hire?
