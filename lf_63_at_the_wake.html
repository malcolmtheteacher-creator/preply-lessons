At the Wake

A function room above a pub, a queue of people waiting to shake her hand, and about forty seconds when you get to the front. You worked with David eleven years ago. You have no idea what to say to her — and that's fine, because the useful thing to say is short.

The Scene 🎬

There are about nine people in front of you and you still haven't decided what you're going to say.

You worked with David for three years, a long time ago. He trained you, more or less — you were twenty-four and useless and he was patient with you in a way nobody else in that building was. You hadn't seen him since 2019. You came today because when you saw the notice you found yourself crying at your desk, which surprised you.

Now you're in a queue in a function room above a pub, holding a glass of orange juice you don't want, and at the front of it is Bernadette. She has been standing there for an hour and a half shaking hands. She has no idea who you are.

Almost everyone in this queue is frightened of the same thing: saying the wrong thing, upsetting her, being the person who makes it worse. So most of them will say "sorry for your loss" and move on quickly. There is something better available, and it takes about thirty seconds.

Bernadette David's widow. She has shaken two hundred hands today and is running on autopilot. Exhausted, gracious, and hearing the same sentence over and over.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the front of the queue — short, plain, and useful to someone who has heard two hundred versions of the same sentence today. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Slower and quieter than you think, and don't fill the pauses. If she cries, the correct response is to stand there and let it happen — not to apologise, not to reach for a tissue, not to change the subject. Most people rush at this moment because the silence is uncomfortable for them. Being the person who can stand still in it is most of the skill.

🖤 Opening

Phrase 1

You're at the front. You have nothing clever and you shouldn't reach for anything clever. Which opening is best?

A. "I'm so sorry. I don't really know what else to say."
B. "He's at peace now, at least. That's something to hold on to."
C. "I can't imagine what you must be going through right now."
A — "I'm so sorry. I don't really know what else to say."
B offers her a consolation she may not believe and has heard all day, and it asks her to agree with you about something enormous. C sounds sympathetic but puts the focus on your imagination rather than her husband. A is honest and takes about two seconds. Admitting you have no words is not a failure — it's the truth, everybody else's words haven't helped either, and it frees you both from the pretence that the right sentence exists.
Phrase 2

She doesn't know who you are and is too polite to ask outright. Rescue her from that immediately. Put the pieces in order:

I worked with DavidYou won't remember me —years ago.
"You won't remember me — I worked with David years ago."
A small act of mercy. She is trying to place two hundred faces while grieving, and "you won't remember me" removes the obligation entirely — it's not a complaint, it's permission. Follow it immediately with how you knew him, because that's the actual information she needs. Never make a grieving person work out who you are.

🎁 Giving her something

Phrase 3

Now the part that makes you different from the queue: say what he was to you, honestly and in proportion. Complete the missing words:

"I only knew him at work, but he was very ___ ___ me when I started."

"I only knew him at work, but he was very good to me when I started."
"I only knew him at work" is important — it's honest about the limits of your claim, which matters in a room where some people have lost a husband and a father. And "very good to me" is specific enough to mean something without inflating your relationship. To be good to someone means to treat them with particular kindness, often without obligation, which is exactly what happened.
Phrase 4

Here is the gift — a fact about her husband that she did not have. Tell her something he said or did that she'd have no way of knowing. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "He talked about you constantly, you know."
This is what she'll remember from the entire afternoon, and she says so — "that's the first new thing anyone's told me all day." Everyone else is offering sympathy, which she has in abundance and cannot use. You are offering information about the person she has lost, from a part of his life she never saw. If you knew the person at all, you almost certainly have one of these. It is the most valuable thing you can bring.

🤐 Not asking the daft question

Phrase 5

She's welling up. The English instinct is to fill the gap. Do the opposite — give her room. Complete the missing word:

"Take your time, honestly, there's no ___."

"Take your time, honestly, there's no rush."
Six words that mean you are allowed to cry in front of me and I am not going anywhere. Notice what it isn't: it isn't "don't cry", it isn't "sorry", it isn't a change of subject. All three of those tell a grieving person that their grief is inconvenient. This is the one moment in the conversation where saying almost nothing is the whole skill.
Phrase 6

Refuse to ask the question every single other person has asked — and say why you're not asking it. Put the pieces in order:

it's a daft questionI won't asktoday.how you are —
"I won't ask how you are — it's a daft question today."
"How are you?" forces a bereaved person to perform an answer — "bearing up", "surviving" — for the comfort of the asker, and she's done it nine hundred times. Announcing that you won't ask does two things at once: it releases her from the script, and it shows you've thought about her day rather than your own awkwardness. Daft is a mild British word for silly — light enough to be affectionate rather than critical.

🫱 Offering something real

Phrase 7

Everyone says "let me know if there's anything I can do", and nobody ever gets taken up on it. Ask better — narrower, and about today. Which version might actually get used?

A. "If there's anything at all I can do, just say the word."
B. "Is there anything at all you need doing today?"
C. "You must let me know if you need anything, any time, day or night."
B — "Is there anything at all you need doing today?"
A and C are kind and useless: they put the work on her to think of a task, find you, and ask — which no grieving person does. B is narrow enough to be answerable. "Today" and "doing" point at real things — lifts, chairs, someone to take the sandwiches back. She may still say no, as she does here, but it's a question she can actually process, and offering a small definite thing is worth more than offering everything.
Phrase 8

You're aware of the queue behind you. Say so — it shows you're thinking about her afternoon, not just your turn. Complete the missing words:

"I don't want to ___ ___, there's a queue behind me."

"I don't want to keep you, there's a queue behind me."
To keep someone is to detain them longer than they can spare. It's a graceful way of ending your turn without it feeling like you're leaving — you're releasing her rather than escaping. It also quietly tells her that you know she has ninety more of these to do, which is another small sign that you're thinking about her rather than performing sympathy at her.

📞 The bit that matters later

Phrase 9

Everyone is kind this week. In a month the phone stops ringing. Ask now for permission to be there then — as a question, and with a reason that isn't pity. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Would it be alright if I rang you in a few weeks, when everyone's gone home?"
The most useful sentence in the lesson, and almost nobody says it. Grief has a shape: overwhelming attention for a fortnight, then silence. Asking permission now — while you're in front of her — makes the later call possible, and "when everyone's gone home" shows you understand the shape. Giving a reason that isn't concern ("I've got a lot of him I could tell you") matters too: it makes the call something she might want rather than something she has to endure.
Phrase 10

Close with something about him rather than about her. Complete the missing word — the one that makes it affectionate rather than solemn:

"It was a lovely service. He'd have ___ the fuss."

"It was a lovely service. He'd have hated the fuss."
This is the line that makes her laugh, and laughing is allowed. Talking about the dead person as a person — with opinions, irritations, a personality — is a relief after an afternoon of reverence. It also proves you knew him: only someone who did could say what he'd have made of his own funeral. Warm, slightly irreverent, and true.

🔑 Make them yours

These work at any funeral, wake, or first meeting after a death — and most of them work for any bad news at all. Pin three of them to your own life. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"He talked about you constantly, you know." — think of someone you've lost, or someone else has. What could you tell their family that they wouldn't know?

"Is there anything at all you need doing today?" — what small, definite thing could you offer, instead of offering everything?

"Would it be alright if I rang you in a few weeks?" — who's had a hard thing happen where the attention has now stopped?

⚡ The twist

You do ring, five weeks later, as you promised. She answers — and she is not the composed woman from the function room. She's flat, and slightly cold, and about ninety seconds in she says: "Sorry — who is this again? I'm not being rude, I've just got no idea who you are."

You are now a stranger telephoning a grieving woman who doesn't remember agreeing to it. What do you say — and do you stay on the line or get off it?

The Bigger Picture 🔭

The queue at a wake is a strange piece of theatre. Two hundred people, each with about forty seconds, all reaching for a sentence that will help — and almost all landing on the same one, because there isn't a good one and everybody knows it.

What the bereaved person is actually short of isn't sympathy. It's information: what he was like at work, what he said about them when they weren't there, the twenty minutes of his life you saw and they didn't. Sympathy is the one thing they have too much of.

And the other shortage arrives later. Everyone is kind for a fortnight and then the house goes quiet, which is roughly when it gets hard. The question worth asking at the front of that queue is not how she is today. It's whether you can ring in a month.
