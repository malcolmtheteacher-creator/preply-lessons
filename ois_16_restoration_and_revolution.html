The Bridge: A Nation in Two Halves

By 1649, after years of civil war, England is divided. Parliament has won. They have captured the king. And now they will do something unprecedented in European history:

They will try him for treason against the people.

They will execute him.

What comes next? A kingdom without a king. A military general in power. A ban on Christmas. Theatres shut. A Puritan dream turned to stone.

And then? A restoration so sudden and so beloved that the king who returns will be called the Merry Monarch.

II. Cromwell's Commonwealth & the Restoration

The Death of a King (1649)

Charles I was tried and found guilty of treason. On 30 January 1649, outside the Palace of Whitehall, in front of thousands, he was beheaded. It was the most shocking moment in British history.

When the King walked to the scaffold, there was a great silence among the people. Many wept. No English king had ever been put to death by law before. Some thought this a just punishment for his treachery. Others thought it a terrible sin that would bring God's judgment upon the nation. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Ch. LXXVIII

The Commonwealth Years: Cromwell as Lord Protector (1649–1658)

With the king dead, Parliament had to create something new: a republic. They declared England a Commonwealth, ruled by a council of parliament and a military commander. That commander was Oliver Cromwell.

Oliver Cromwell was a man of strong will and strong faith. He believed God had chosen him to lead England. But instead of a crown, he took the title Lord Protector. He was a soldier, not a king. And his England would be ruled by soldiers, not by courtiers in silks and jewels. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Ch. LXXX

The Puritan Experiment

Cromwell was a Puritan — a Christian who believed the Church of England was still too grand, too full of ceremony and colour. Under his rule, England became a much grimmer place.

Christmas was forbidden. The theatres were shut. Music and dancing and merry-making were seen as sins. The Puritan soldiers would march through the streets on a feast day, making sure no one was enjoying themselves. Maypoles were cut down. The joy seemed draining from the nation, drop by drop. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Ch. LXXX

Yet Cromwell was no tyrant. He was honest, fearless, and genuinely believed he was serving God. He even offered to be crowned king — and refused.

The historian's note: Cromwell remains one of the most controversial figures in English history. To his supporters, he was a godly military genius who saved Parliament from tyranny. To his opponents (especially Irish Catholics and Royalists), he was a fanatical killer. The truth is complex: Cromwell was a remarkable leader and strategist, but he also carried out brutal military campaigns in Ireland and Scotland. His Commonwealth briefly made England a republic and a force in Europe. But it was too austere to last.

Cromwell's Death and the Collapse (1658–1660)

Cromwell ruled for nine years. In 1658, he died. His son Richard inherited the title of Lord Protector, but he lacked his father's force of will. The military fractured. Parliament weakened. The nation fell into chaos.

And then, General Monk — who had been Cromwell's trusted general in Scotland — made a fateful decision: it was time to bring back the king.

The Restoration of Charles II (1660)

Charles II had spent his exile in France, living in poverty, selling his jewels to eat. Now, after eleven years, he was returning home.

The roads leading to London were lined with people cheering. Charles came back to his throne not as a conqueror, but as the beloved son returning home. The nation had tried to rule without a king. It had failed. The nation had tasted the iron rule of a soldier-king. It remembered the joy that had been forbidden. Now, it wanted laughter and light again. And Charles II was ready to give them that. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Ch. LXXXI

The Merry Monarch

Charles II earned the name "Merry Monarch" for good reason. He loved theatre, music, art, and pleasure. He married Catherine of Braganza, a Portuguese princess. He patronised scientists (he founded the Royal Society). And he made people smile again after the grey years of Puritan rule.

But Charles II was also cunning. He had learned a hard lesson from his father's fate: never let Parliament feel threatened. Keep them happy. Keep the nobles content. Never push too hard, even when you disagree with them.

The historian's note: The Restoration was not a return to absolute monarchy. Parliament had tasted power. The civil war had shown that the king could not rule alone. What emerged was a negotiation — the king would rule, but Parliament had rights. This balance would take many years to stabilise. Charles II's reign saw the birth of political parties, the first steps of cabinet government, and the emergence of the idea that power must be divided.

III. The Great Plague, the Great Fire & the Glorious Revolution

The Great Plague of London (1665)

Just five years into the Restoration, catastrophe struck. A plague — the bubonic plague, the same disease that killed millions in the Middle Ages — broke out in London. Within months, tens of thousands were dead.

The plague came like a shadow creeping through the streets. At first, only a few fell ill. Then it became a flood. Entire families disappeared. The watchers came at night, marking the doors of the infected with a red cross. No one dared enter those houses. The dead were carried away in carts, piled high like cordwood, and thrown into great pits outside the city. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Ch. LXXXII

The wealthy fled. The king left London. Scientists like Isaac Newton went home to Cambridge to escape the contagion. Those left behind — the poor, the sick, the brave — endured.

The historian's note: The Great Plague killed roughly 100,000 people — perhaps one-quarter of London's population. Samuel Pepys, who stayed in London, kept a famous diary of the horror. The plague faded as mysteriously as it had come, dying out by early 1666. Historians believe colder weather, the death of infected rats, and the isolation of the sick finally broke the chain of transmission.

The Great Fire of London (1666)

If the plague was not enough, London faced an even more catastrophic disaster. On 2 September 1666, a fire started in a bakehouse in Pudding Lane. But it did not stop there.

The fire spread like a living thing, jumping from roof to roof, devouring the wooden houses as if they were dry straw. The noise was like thunder. The heat was unbearable. People stood in the streets, watching their homes burn, watching their livelihoods turn to ash. The King himself came to London to command the firefighting, but the fire seemed unstoppable. It raged for three days and nights. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Ch. LXXXII

The fire destroyed much of the medieval City of London. But from the ashes came something new. A young architect named Christopher Wren was commissioned to rebuild the city. The result was a masterpiece: a new London of straight streets and classical buildings, crowned by a new St Paul's Cathedral — which still stands today, more than 350 years later.

The historian's note: The Great Fire of 1666 destroyed 13,200 houses, 87 parish churches, and countless businesses. Yet in a way, it freed the city. Medieval London — a maze of narrow, dark streets — was replaced by a more rational, healthier design. The new stone and brick buildings were safer than the old wooden ones. Wren's plan for a grand rebuilding was never fully realised (the merchants wanted to rebuild quickly to reclaim their land), but his St Paul's Cathedral remains one of the greatest works of English architecture.

James II and the Crisis of Conscience (1685–1688)

Charles II died in 1685 without a legitimate heir. The throne passed to his brother, James II. This was dangerous. James had secretly converted to Catholicism.

In England, Catholics meant suspicion and fear. England had fought Spain (Catholic) and France (Catholic). Catholics were seen as foreign, as agents of the Pope. And now, a Catholic king sat on the throne.

James believed in the Divine Right of Kings — that God had chosen him to rule, and that no one had the right to question him. But Parliament, and the people, remembered what happened to a king who believed in Divine Right. They had executed his father. They were not ready to bow to another absolute monarch, least of all a Catholic one. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Ch. LXXXV

The Glorious Revolution (1688)

In 1688, James II made a fatal mistake. His wife gave birth to a son — a Catholic heir to the throne. This was the moment Parliament had feared. They faced the prospect of a Catholic dynasty ruling forever.

But this time, they did not call for war. Instead, they made an unprecedented move: they invited James's own daughter, Mary, and her Dutch Protestant husband, William of Orange, to come and take the throne.

William sailed from Holland with a small fleet. When he arrived, James II found his own generals deserting him, his own supporters melting away. Without a shot fired, without a battle, the king fled to France. It was called the Glorious Revolution — a revolution with no blood shed, an invasion with no fighting. A new king and queen had arrived to rule by the consent of Parliament, not by Divine Right. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Ch. LXXXVI

The Bill of Rights (1689)

Now came the key moment. Parliament did not simply hand the crown back to a king. Instead, they made William and Mary accept a contract: the Bill of Rights.

This document said:

  • The king cannot suspend laws.
  • The king cannot levy taxes without Parliament's consent.
  • Parliament must be called regularly.
  • Elections must be free.
  • Subjects have the right to petition the king.
  • Standing armies cannot exist without Parliament's approval.

Constitutional Monarchy is Born

The Glorious Revolution changed the nature of monarchy itself. No longer would kings rule by Divine Right. No longer could they claim to answer only to God. Now, the king answered to the law — and Parliament made the law.

This was not democracy. Parliament was still small, and only men with property could vote. But it was a crucial step toward democratic government. And it established a principle that would spread around the world: no one is above the law, not even the king.

The historian's note: The Glorious Revolution is sometimes misnamed — it was not particularly glorious for everyone. Catholics faced new restrictions. James II and his supporters (called Jacobites) fought on in Ireland and Scotland for years. William brought Dutch soldiers and Dutch influence, which some English resented. But it was truly bloodless in England itself — the only revolution of its kind in history — and it established principles of limited monarchy that have influenced democracies ever since. This is why the British system is called a "constitutional monarchy": the crown exists, but within the limits of a constitution and the rule of law.

VI. Your Turn: Discuss

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Could Cromwell have been a good king?

He refused a crown, saying only God could rule. But he acted like a king, and his rule was harsh. Should he have accepted the crown? Would he have been a better monarch than Charles II?

Why does joy matter?

After Cromwell's grim years, England wanted laughter and music again. Charles II gave them theatres, art, and pleasure. Is a king who brings joy better than a stern ruler who may be more just?

What caused the Great Plague and Fire?

People in 1665 believed the plague was God's judgment on the nation's sins. People believed the Fire was divine punishment. How do we explain disasters today?

Was the Glorious Revolution truly glorious?

It was bloodless — which was remarkable. But Catholics lost rights, Scots and Irish had their own wars, and change was slow. For whom was it "glorious"?

Can a revolution be bloodless?

1688 saw no battles in England. But it was possible partly because James II chose to flee. What if he had chosen to fight? Would that have changed everything?

What does the Bill of Rights mean today?

It says the king cannot levy taxes without Parliament, elections must be free, and the king must obey the law. How have these ideas changed over 330 years?

2. The Commonwealth and Its Lesson

What did Cromwell's Commonwealth teach the English people about government?

The Commonwealth taught that you cannot rule a nation by fear and austerity alone. People need joy, culture, and hope. They also taught that one man, even a brilliant military leader, cannot rule without consent. And they showed that replacing a bad king with a stern general does not create a just society. The lesson was hard-earned: England would eventually learn that stable government requires both a limit on power (through law) and the consent of the governed (through Parliament).

3. The Glorious Revolution

Why was 1688 called "glorious"? What was truly revolutionary about it?

It was glorious because it was bloodless. A king was removed and replaced without warfare or public execution. What was revolutionary was the principle it established: the king is not above the law. The Bill of Rights made it clear that the monarch serves at the pleasure of Parliament, that power is shared and limited, and that even the highest person in the realm must answer to the law. This was genuinely revolutionary — most European monarchs still claimed Divine Right and absolute power.
