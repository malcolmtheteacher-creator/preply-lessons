The Bridge

Here's what changed: Alfred defended Britain from invaders and built a kingdom. Edward I expanded that kingdom—Wales, Scotland, strength. But now—a young king inherits a united England. And through his own restlessness, ambition, and passion, he will break the unity that centuries had forged. Not through foreign conquest, but through his own will.

II. The King & His Wives

Henry VIII ascended to the throne in 1509 as one of the most promising young monarchs in Christendom. Marshall begins his portrait with unusual directness:

Henry was fond of music and poetry, and hunting and all the pleasures of the court. He was clever, and at first, when he came to the throne, he was well-loved by his people. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXII

But this favourable beginning would not last. The king's personality was volatile—brilliant, but dangerous. And his appetite for power extended to every realm: political, religious, and intimate.

The Six Wives of Henry VIII

Henry married six times in 38 years. Marshall tells each story with unflinching brevity:

1. Catherine of Aragon (m. 1509, d. 1536)

Marriage: 24 years

Divorced, then died in exile

Daughter of Isabella and Ferdinand of Spain. Could not give Henry the male heir he desperately wanted.

2. Anne Boleyn (m. 1533, d. 1536)

Marriage: 3 years

Executed (beheaded)

The reason Henry broke with Rome. Gave him Elizabeth, but no son.

3. Jane Seymour (m. 1536, d. 1537)

Marriage: 1 year

Died in childbirth

Gave Henry his longed-for son, Edward. She alone he never publicly criticized.

4. Anne of Cleves (m. 1540, d. 1557)

Marriage: 6 months

Divorced, lived comfortably afterward

A political marriage that Henry found repugnant. The only union he avoided bloodshed to end.

5. Catherine Howard (m. 1540, d. 1542)

Marriage: 2 years

Executed (beheaded)

Henry's fifth wife, married the same year he divorced Anne of Cleves. Barely twenty when she died.

6. Catherine Parr (m. 1543, d. 1548)

Marriage: 4 years

Outlived Henry, remarried, died in childbed

A widow, intelligent and patient. Marshall notes her charity toward Henry's children.

Marshall's account of Anne Boleyn contains one of the most damning sentences in the entire book:

The King soon tired of Anne Boleyn, and, when people told him that she was a wicked woman, he was quite willing to believe them. He put her into prison and presently cut off her head. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXIV

On Jane Seymour, Marshall's tone shifts—almost to approval:

The very next day he married another lady called Jane Seymour. This lady was good and gentle, but she did not live very long after she was married to Henry. He was very sad at her death, and for two years he did not marry any one else. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXIV

And of Catherine Howard, Marshall's moral clarity cuts through the romance:

This time, the King soon discovered that he had married a wicked woman. She was not any more wicked than Henry was himself, but he did not think of that. To punish her, he cut off her head and the heads of several of her friends as well. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXIV

Catherine Parr, his last wife, was "a good woman":

About a year later Henry married his sixth and last wife, a lady called Catherine Parr. She was a good woman, and it is wonderful that she should have been willing to marry so bad a man, and one who was so fond of cutting off the heads of his wives. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXIV
The historian's note: Marshall's portraits are vivid but selective. Anne Boleyn was likely innocent of the charges against her (adultery, incest, witchcraft). She was brilliant, ambitious, and politically threatened—a dangerous combination for a woman in Henry's court. Catherine Howard was indeed caught with another man, but she was also a pawn in court factions and barely more than a child. Henry's own behaviour—his cruelty, his serial marriages, his infidelity—was far worse than theirs. Yet it was the women who paid with their heads.

III. The Reformation & the Break with Rome

Henry VIII's six marriages were not the cause of the Reformation in England—they were a symptom. The root cause was Henry's absolute conviction that he should rule all realms: political and spiritual. When the Pope would not give him what he wanted, Henry simply made himself Pope.

Why Henry Broke with Rome

In 1527, Henry asked Pope Clement VII to annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon. She had not borne him a son, and Henry believed a male heir was essential. But Catherine was the aunt of the Emperor Charles V, who was powerful and opposed the annulment. The Pope refused.

Henry would not accept refusal. In 1533, he secretly married Anne Boleyn and broke with the Roman Church entirely. Marshall explains the theological cover:

Henry did not like to acknowledge that the Pope was the head of the Church. He said that the Bible did not say so, and he refused to pay money to Rome any longer. The people of England agreed with him, and the Pope's power in England came to an end. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXIII

The Act of Supremacy (1534)

In 1534, Parliament passed the Act of Supremacy, declaring Henry VIII to be the "Supreme Head of the Church of England." This was the formal break. England's monasteries, churches, and priests now answered to the King, not Rome.

One man refused to accept this: Thomas More, the Lord Chancellor and one of the finest minds in Christendom. Marshall honours him:

Sir Thomas More, a good and clever man, refused to say that the King was the head of the Church, and although the King had liked him very much, and had made him a great man, Henry had him put in prison and afterwards cut off his head. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXIII

The Dissolution of the Monasteries (1536–1541)

Between 1536 and 1541, Henry ordered the systematic destruction of England's monasteries. These were not just places of worship—they were schools, hospitals, refuges for the poor, centres of learning, and keepers of ancient manuscripts. For nearly a thousand years, monks had copied books, preserved knowledge, sheltered the sick and homeless.

In less than a decade, almost all were gone. Marshall describes the devastation with restrained sorrow:

The monasteries had been very rich and powerful. Henry seized all their lands and treasure for himself. In doing so, he destroyed many beautiful buildings, and a great deal of learning that had been kept safe in the monasteries was lost. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter LXIII
The historian's note: The monasteries were not without corruption—some were complacent, some were wealthy beyond need. But the destruction was staggering. Thousands of manuscripts were burned or scattered. Precious art was smashed. Young men who might have become scholars or healers lost their training grounds. Villages lost their social safety net. The poor, the sick, the orphaned—who had relied on monastic charity—were cast out into the world. England never fully recovered the intellectual and spiritual infrastructure that was destroyed in those ten years. What Marshall calls "learning that had been lost" represented centuries of accumulated knowledge, gone in an afternoon of pillage.

Why This Mattered

The Reformation in England was not primarily about theology or biblical scholarship (though those mattered to some). It was about power. Henry used religious authority to consolidate political control, to seize wealth, and to escape the constraints of the Pope's judgment. In doing so, he:

  • Severed England from a thousand years of Christian unity
  • Made the English Church subordinate to the monarchy—a dangerous precedent
  • Destroyed the medieval welfare system and centres of learning
  • Set England on a course of religious conflict that would last for generations

Yet Henry also gave England something: independence from Rome, and ultimately (through Elizabeth) a distinct English church and culture.

BRITISH HISTORY PALACE – ROOM 13

Henry VIII & the Reformation (1509–1547)

You are halfway through your palace. Rooms 1–12 lie behind you. Ahead, Rooms 14–20 await. Room 13 holds the Tudor revolution—the moment a king rewrote the rules of power and faith.

Place These 5 Images in Room 13

1. Henry VIII on his throne, massive and powerful — Young and athletic at 18, but as he ages, he grows fat, angry, commanding. His eyes are sharp, his will iron. Place him at the entrance to your Room 13, dominating the space.

2. Six women in a row — Catherine of Aragon, Anne Boleyn, Jane Seymour, Anne of Cleves, Catherine Howard, Catherine Parr. Two of their heads are missing (Anne Boleyn, Catherine Howard). Place them along one wall, each one increasingly afraid.

3. A monastery being pulled down — Stone walls crumbling, monks fleeing, manuscripts burning in a bonfire, bells and roof timbers crashing to the ground. The destruction is systematic and total. Place this at the window, as if the ruin is happening outside in the countryside all around.

4. Thomas More's head on a spike — Grim but necessary. He refused to bow to the King's supremacy. His martyrdom is one of the few moments of moral clarity in this dark chapter. Place it on a high shelf, honourable even in death.

5. An English Bible chained to a church lectern — For all the destruction, Henry also allowed the Bible to be printed in English. Ordinary people could read God's word. Place it at the heart of the room, glowing with light—the one redemption in a brutal era.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Why six marriages?

Henry wanted a son. But he also seems to have been chronically unsatisfied, moving from wife to wife. What does this pattern tell us about him as a person and as a ruler?

The cost of one man's will

Henry broke with Rome, destroyed monasteries, and executed anyone who opposed him—all to satisfy his personal desire for an annulment. How much power should one person have?

Were the women guilty?

Marshall tells us Anne Boleyn and Catherine Howard were "wicked"—but then adds that Catherine Howard was "not any more wicked than Henry was himself." Do you agree? What evidence do we really have?

Thomas More's choice

More refused to accept Henry as head of the Church and died for it. Was he a martyr or a man who refused to accept political reality? What would you have done in his place?

Was the Reformation necessary?

The Roman Church was corrupt in some ways, powerful in others. Henry used reform as a cover for grabbing wealth and power. Did England need to break with Rome? Could it have happened differently?

The cost to the poor

The monasteries fed the hungry, sheltered orphans, and trained scholars. Their destruction left these people with nowhere to turn. Who paid the real price for Henry's ambition?

5. Catherine Howard (m. 1540–1542): Beheaded (accused of infidelity)

6. Catherine Parr (m. 1543–1547): Outlived Henry (a good woman, the historians agree)

3. The Break with Rome

Why did Henry break with the Pope? What was the Act of Supremacy? What happened to Thomas More?

Why: Henry wanted an annulment from Catherine of Aragon. The Pope refused. Henry made himself Supreme Head of the Church of England instead (1534).

Act of Supremacy: Parliament declared Henry (and future monarchs) to be the head of the English Church, not the Pope.

Thomas More: Refused to accept the King as head of the Church. Was imprisoned and then executed for this refusal. He was a martyr to conscience.

4. The Dissolution

What was destroyed? What was lost? Who suffered?

What was destroyed: Almost all of England's monasteries, their buildings, lands, wealth, and libraries.

What was lost: Centuries of preserved manuscripts, centres of learning, schools, hospitals, shelter for the poor.

Who suffered: The poor, the sick, the orphaned, the hungry—and all future generations without access to monastic libraries and teaching.
