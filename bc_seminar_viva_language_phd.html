Seminar & Viva Language

The phrases that let you ask, push back, defend, and survive — with grace

The seminar question — your most underrated tool

A good question in a seminar does three things: it shows the speaker you've engaged with their work, it raises an issue worth raising, and it opens a conversation rather than starting an argument. Bad questions do the opposite — they showcase the asker, miss the point, or close the conversation down.

💬 Honest reflection

When you're at a seminar, do you usually ask a question, think of one but not ask it, or not have one?

Most early-stage PhDs are in the middle category — they think of questions but don't ask them. We're going to change that.

Weak vs strong question

⚠ Weak
"I just wanted to say that I really enjoyed your talk and I had a question — well, more of a comment really — about the part where you mentioned the framework, and I was thinking about it because in my own work I've been looking at..."

Apologetic, self-centred, no question. Audience patience evaporates.

✓ Strong
"Your framework distinguishes A from B on the basis of X. Have you considered cases where X doesn't hold — does the distinction still do useful work?"

Names the move, surfaces a tension, asks a real question. Speaker can answer in 60 seconds.

💬 The structure of a good question

What three elements does the strong question have that the weak one doesn't?

  1. It names the speaker's move accurately ("Your framework distinguishes A from B on the basis of X") — shows you listened.
  2. It surfaces a real tension (cases where X doesn't hold) — gives the speaker something to work with.
  3. It ends with a question, not a statement — invites response rather than performs expertise.

Three question shapes that almost always work

  • The clarification: "Could I check my understanding of [specific point] — are you arguing X, or X-with-qualification?"
  • The boundary case: "Where does your framework apply, and where does it stop applying? What would be a case it can't handle?"
  • The methodological probe: "How would your argument look if you'd used [alternative method] instead — would the conclusions hold?"
✍ Now you try

Think of a recent paper or talk in your field. Write a question of each type — clarification, boundary case, methodological probe — about that paper.

⚡ The hidden lesson

Good questioners are remembered. The reputation you build at seminars in years 1-3 is the reputation that gets you cited, invited, and supported in years 4-10.

Pushing back politely — the diplomatic disagreement

Academia rewards intellectual disagreement. But the form matters: you can disagree forcefully and still be seen as collegial. The trick is in the language, not the strength of the position.

💬 Watch for this

You disagree with the speaker. Your instinct is to start with "I think you're wrong because..." What's the problem with that opener?

It's confrontational, it's personal, and it tells the audience your goal is to win, not to advance the discussion. The speaker becomes defensive — even if your point is right. Better: lead with what you accept before stating where you depart.

The disagreement template — concede, depart, redirect

1. Concede: Name what you accept in their argument.

2. Depart: State where your view differs and why.

3. Redirect: Open a constructive next step or invite their response.

Three useful phrases for each move

Concede

  • "I take your point that..."
  • "Your argument that X is well-supported by Y, and I find that persuasive."
  • "There's clearly merit in the position that..."

Depart

  • "...where I'd push back is on the assumption that..."
  • "...although I'd want to qualify the claim about X by..."
  • "...my hesitation is that this seems to overlook..."
  • "...I'm not yet persuaded that..."

Redirect

  • "How would you respond to that?"
  • "Is that something you've considered, or would the framework need extending?"
  • "I'd be interested to hear how you'd handle [specific case]."
💬 Build a full disagreement aloud

Pick a position from your literature you don't fully accept. Concede something true about it, depart on a specific point, and redirect with a question. Say it out loud — the rhythm matters.

✍ Write it
⚡ The principle

Disagreement is the engine of academia. Diplomatic disagreement is the engine that keeps academic communities working. You can hold a strong position and still leave the room with allies.

Presenting your work — the seminar paper

A seminar paper is not a chapter read aloud. It's a 20-minute argument with a clear claim, a clear stake, and a clear ask of the audience. The audience should walk away knowing what you're arguing, why it matters, and what you want them to think about.

💬 What's the difference?

A seminar paper and a thesis chapter are NOT the same. What changes between them?

The thesis chapter has time to build. The seminar paper has 20 minutes. So:

  • The seminar paper leads with the claim, doesn't bury it.
  • The seminar paper picks ONE argument — not the whole chapter.
  • The seminar paper invites response — what do you want feedback on?

The 5-move structure

  1. The hook (60 seconds). A real-world tension, a striking finding, or a contradiction in the literature that the audience can't dismiss.
  2. The claim (60 seconds). One sentence. What you're arguing today.
  3. The argument (12-15 minutes). Three or four moves that build toward the claim. Each move signposted clearly.
  4. The stake (60 seconds). Why this matters — for the field, for practice, for the next research question.
  5. The ask (30-60 seconds). What do you want from the audience? Specific feedback on a specific issue.
💬 Of the five, which is most often skipped?

Take a guess.

The ask. Most PhD presenters end with "thank you" and stop. Smart presenters end with: "What I'd particularly value your view on is whether the framework holds when [specific scenario]." That single sentence transforms the Q&A from random to focused — and you get the feedback you actually need.

Signposting phrases for each move

Hook

  • "There's a tension at the heart of [field] that hasn't been adequately addressed..."
  • "Recent work on X has produced a curious finding..."

Claim

  • "My argument today is that..."
  • "What I want to suggest is..."
  • "The claim I'll defend is..."

Move-by-move signposts

  • "To see this, consider..."
  • "This raises a second question..."
  • "Building on that..." / "By contrast..."

Ask

  • "What I'd particularly value your view on is..."
  • "The piece I'm most uncertain about is X — I'd welcome pushback on that."
  • "I'm especially interested in feedback on [specific section] before I take it forward."
✍ Draft your hook + claim + ask (3 sentences)

For a piece of your own work, draft the three sentences that would open and close a 20-minute talk.

⚡ The unwritten rule

Audiences remember the first 60 seconds and the last 60 seconds. The middle 18 minutes evaporate. Make the open and close work — that's where reputations are made.

The viva — six question types and how to handle each

The viva is not a test of memory. It's a test of defensible judgement. Examiners want to know that the choices in your thesis were made by you, with reasons, and that you can hold them under pressure.

Six question types cover almost everything you'll be asked.

🎓 Type 1 — The opener

"Could you tell us, in your own words, what your thesis argues?"

💬 The trap

What is the wrong way to answer this question?

Reciting the abstract. The examiners have already read the abstract. They want to hear your voice — what you actually think your thesis does, said freshly. Aim for 90 seconds, three moves: (1) the central claim; (2) the contribution it makes to the field; (3) the limit of the claim — what you're NOT arguing.

🎓 Type 2 — The contribution probe

"What's the original contribution your thesis makes? How is it different from what's been done before?"

💬 What examiners listen for

They listen for whether you can name the contribution in one sentence. Hesitation means you're not sure where it sits.

"Where the literature has done X and Y, my thesis does Z — specifically by [the methodological or conceptual move that makes this possible]."

One sentence. Memorise it. Adapt it on the day if you need to. But have it ready.

🎓 Type 3 — The methodological challenge

"Why did you choose method X when method Y has been more common in this area?"

This question is covered in detail in the Defending Methodological Choices lesson. The short version: name the rationale, name the alternative considered, name what you'd lose by switching to it.

🎓 Type 4 — The literature challenge

"Why didn't you engage with [theorist or tradition X]?"

💬 What this question tests

Two things: did you make a principled boundary, or an accidental omission? Can you name what's lost by your boundary AND why the boundary still holds?

"I considered [X] and made a deliberate boundary. The reason is [methodological or scope-based reason]. I acknowledge this means [what's lost], but the boundary was necessary because [thesis-coherence reason]. If [different research question], engaging with X would be essential."

🎓 Type 5 — The limitations probe

"What are the limitations of your study? What couldn't you do?"

⚠ Watch for this

The trap is to either downplay limitations (which makes you look defensive) or to over-state them (which makes the examiner ask if the thesis still stands). The strong answer names real limitations with real reasons — and shows that you've thought about what work the limitations leave for future research.

🎓 Type 6 — The future-work probe

"Where would you take this research next?"

💬 What this signals

This question is mostly friendly — examiners ask it to give you space to show your forward thinking. But it also tests whether you see your thesis as a complete object OR as a stage in a longer trajectory. The right answer treats it as a stage — names two or three concrete next directions.

✍ Draft your answer to Type 1

"Could you tell us, in your own words, what your thesis argues?"

Aim for 90 seconds. Three moves: claim → contribution → limit.

🎓 Practice 2 — A diplomatic disagreement

Imagine the examiner says: "Surely your argument is simply a restatement of [established theorist]'s position?" Push back diplomatically using concede → depart → redirect.

"I take your point — there's a clear lineage from [theorist]'s work, and I'd be unwise to deny that. Where my argument departs is on [specific move]: [theorist] treated X as Y, whereas my framework treats X as Z, which has consequences for [downstream claim]. So I'd describe the relationship as building on rather than restating — though I'd be interested to hear if you see the departure differently."

Notice the moves: concede the lineage, name the departure with specificity, invite the examiner's response. Confidence + collegiality.

🎓 Practice 3 — The seminar opening (3 minutes)

Imagine you're presenting a 20-minute seminar on a chapter of your thesis. Speak the first three minutes: hook → claim → outline of your three main moves.

💬 What to listen for in your own voice

Are you signposting clearly? Did you START with the hook, or did you apologise for the speaker before you? Did the claim arrive within 90 seconds? Are you using "I think" too often (B2 hedging) instead of "what I want to suggest is" (C1 register)?

The viva is the day your thesis becomes a public object. Every seminar and supervision is rehearsal for that day. Treat them as performances of judgement, not tests of memory.
