The Client Who Won't Decide

The proposal went over six weeks ago. Since then: 'leave it with me', 'crazy week', 'circling back soon'. The client is friendly, engaged, and completely undecided — and every week of drift costs you both. Today's call gets a decision. Even if the decision is no.

The Scene 🎬

The hardest client isn't the one who says no. It's the one who says nothing, warmly, for six weeks.

You quoted the Marek job in May: a clear proposal, two options, a price. Mr Marek loved it. Since then you've had four phone calls of genuine friendliness and zero decisions — and meanwhile you're holding capacity for a project that may never exist, materials prices are creeping, and the start date you promised is quietly dying.

Here's what experience teaches about the undecided client: the delay is almost never about you. Something you can't see is jamming the works — a nervous business partner, a budget that isn't approved, a fear they can't name, or simply a decision too big to face whole. Pushing harder produces polite retreat. Waiting politely produces more waiting. The way through is neither: it's making the decision easier to make — smaller, dated, or taken off their plate entirely.

And underneath it all, one professional truth that changes the whole conversation once you accept it: a fast no is the second-best outcome you can get. It releases your capacity, keeps the relationship, and beats a slow maybe by miles. The only bad outcome is the drift.

  1. What's the biggest decision you've ever put off? What was the delay actually about — and what finally moved it?
  2. Why do we chase a "maybe" for months when we'd accept a "no" in a day?
YouQuoted in May, held capacity since, still friendly — and done with drift. Today's goal: a decision with a date on it, any decision.
Mr MarekGenuinely wants the work done. Also genuinely stuck — for a reason he hasn't said out loud, possibly not even to himself.

The Conversation 💬

Thursday, 10am. Call five. Mr Marek answers with his usual warmth.

Mr MarekAh! I know why you're calling. Look — it's been a crazy few weeks, the proposal's right here on my desk, top of the pile—
YouMr Marek, I believe you, and let me say it straight: I'm not chasing you — I'm protecting the date. You wanted this finished before your busy season. That's still possible today. In two weeks it genuinely isn't, and I'd rather tell you that now than surprise you with it.
Mr MarekNo, I appreciate that. It's just — you know how it is. Timing, moving parts…
YouCan I ask directly? What's actually holding this up? In my experience there's usually one thing — not five. One.
Mr Marek…Honestly? *a pause* My brother-in-law had almost the same work done last year — different firm — and it ran forty percent over the quote. Forty percent. My wife brings it up every time I mention starting. I look at your proposal and I like it, and then I hear Tomas's forty percent.
YouThere it is. Six weeks, one sentence — and it's a completely fair fear, and I wish you'd told me in May, because it's exactly the kind I can do something about. Before I do: which option are you leaning towards, even slightly? The full job or the phased one?
Mr MarekThe full one, if I'm honest. The phased one is just… the nervous version. That's me negotiating with Tomas's forty percent.
YouThen let's deal with the forty percent, not the nerves. Here's what I can offer: a fixed price on everything in the proposal's scope — the number you signed is the number you pay, and I'll put that in writing. If something outside the scope turns up once we open the wall, work stops, you get a written option, and you decide before a cent is spent. Your brother-in-law's forty percent came from open-ended day rates. This is the opposite structure.
Mr MarekThe number is the number… and anything extra, I approve first, in writing. That's — hm. That's the thing Tomas didn't have.
YouIt is. And would it help if I made it a recommendation instead of a menu? Because I'll happily own one: take the full job at the fixed price. You said yourself the phased option is just the fear wearing a suit — and phasing costs you eight percent more overall for the privilege.
Mr Marek*laughing* "The fear wearing a suit." I'm using that on my wife. Look — I want to say yes. I do. Let me just — leave it with me over the weekend?
YouOf course — a real weekend of thinking is fair, six more weeks of "soon" isn't, and I don't think you want that either. So: can I put a date on it? Monday, noon, I call you — and whichever way it lands is fine. And I mean that part: is the real answer maybe no? Because no is fine — no I can work with. It's the maybe that's expensive, for both of us.
Mr MarekNo — it's not a no. If it were a no I'd have said so weeks ago; I'm not a coward, I'm just married to Tomas's sister. It's a yes that needed… whatever this call was. Monday, noon. And bring the fixed-price letter.
YouIt'll be in your inbox tonight, so the weekend conversation has it on the table. One more thing — should your wife be on the Monday call? No irony intended: she's clearly the other half of this decision, and I'd rather answer her questions than have Tomas answer them for me.
Mr Marek*a long pause* …Ten years, nobody's suggested that. Yes. Monday, noon, both of us. And — thank you for asking the direct question. Six weeks, and it turns out I was one honest sentence from deciding.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for anyone whose income waits on other people's decisions — trades, freelancers, sales, agencies. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The tone of this whole call is warm and unhurried — pressure is what the last four calls already proved doesn't work. What's different today is direction: every sentence moves towards a decision with a date on it. Friendly about everything, vague about nothing.

🧭 Naming the drift

Phrase 1

The job: reframing the chase. Complete it:

"I'm not chasing you — I'm protecting the ___."

"I'm not chasing you — I'm protecting the date."
The fifth call can't open like the fourth. This reframe changes whose interest the call serves: you're not a supplier wanting money, you're the guardian of something HE wants — the before-busy-season finish — that drift is quietly killing. Follow it with the honest deadline ("possible today, genuinely not in two weeks") and the nudge becomes information. Nobody resents being told the door is closing; they resent discovering it closed.

Take it anywhere: "I'm not chasing you — I'm protecting ___." The date, the price, the slot, the option. Name what drift destroys.
Phrase 2

The job: the direct question, finally asked. Complete it:

"What's actually holding this up? There's usually ___ thing — not five. ___."

"What's actually holding this up? There's usually one thing — not five. One."
Six weeks of "crazy weeks" and "moving parts" dissolved in one direct question — because vague delays are almost always one specific fear wearing camouflage. "There's usually one thing" does the work: it gives permission to skip the excuses and name the real one, and it's true often enough to feel like insight. Note what surfaced: nothing you could have guessed. You cannot answer the blocker you haven't heard.

Take it anywhere: "What's actually holding this up? There's usually one thing." For every stalled decision, deal, or house move in your life.
Phrase 3

The job: finding which way they're already falling. Complete it:

"Which option are you ___ towards, even slightly?"

"Which option are you leaning towards, even slightly?"
Leaning asks for a tilt, not a verdict — which is why it gets answered when "so what have you decided?" gets deflected. "Even slightly" lowers the bar to nearly nothing, and people step over low bars. What it buys is direction: once you know he's leaning to the full job, every remaining sentence can serve that lean instead of re-selling both options. Undecided people are usually decided people who haven't said it out loud yet.

Take it anywhere: "Which way are you leaning, even slightly?" Clients, hiring panels, teenagers choosing universities.

🔧 Making the decision smaller

Phrase 4

The job: answering the fear with structure, not reassurance. Complete it:

"The number you signed is the number you ___ — and I'll put that in ___."

"The number you signed is the number you pay — and I'll put that in writing."
"Don't worry, we're not like that firm" is reassurance — and reassurance bounces off a fear with a forty-percent story behind it. Structure penetrates: a fixed price, in writing, plus the stop-and-approve rule for anything outside scope. You're not asking him to trust your character; you're removing the mechanism his fear runs on. When you can't do a fixed price, the same principle holds — caps, checkpoints, approval gates. Convert your trustworthiness into paperwork.

Take it anywhere: "The number is the number — in writing." And for scope: "Anything extra, you approve before a cent is spent."
Phrase 5

The job: taking the weight of choosing off their shoulders. Which version?

A. "Well, both options have their pros and cons, it's really up to you."
B. "Would it help if I made a recommendation and owned it?"
C. "Honestly, whichever — it's the same money to me either way."
B — "Would it help if I made a recommendation and owned it?"
A hands the undecided man his indecision back with a bow on it — "up to you" is exactly what he can't do. C is worse: it says his choice doesn't matter. B offers what stuck people actually lack: someone willing to carry part of the decision's weight. Note the form — permission first ("would it help"), then a real recommendation with reasons and skin in it. Experts who won't recommend are menus. Clients don't need another menu.

Take it anywhere: "Would it help if I made a recommendation and owned it?" Then make one, with the reasoning shown.
Phrase 6

The job: converting "leave it with me" into a fixture. Put the pieces in order:

can I put"Leave it with me" —a date on that?
"'Leave it with me' — can I put a date on that?"
The five most expensive words in business, finally given a clock. The move grants the request completely — a real weekend of thinking is fair — and attaches the one thing that stops it becoming six more weeks: a specific moment when thinking ends. Monday, noon, you call (you, not him — never leave the follow-up in the drifter's hands). "Leave it with me" without a date is just drift in a nicer coat.

Take it anywhere: "Can I put a date on that?" After every "let me think", "I'll get back to you", and "we should discuss this properly".

💰 The truths that end drift

Phrase 7

The job: naming what the delay costs — without threatening. Complete it:

"Every week this sits, it costs us the ___ — that's not pressure, it's the ___."

"Every week this sits, it costs us the start date — that's not pressure, it's the calendar."
Undecided clients believe, on some level, that the decision will wait for them at no cost — and often nobody has told them otherwise, because it sounds like a sales tactic. The way to say it is to make the cost concrete and external: the start date, the materials price, the booked crew. "Not pressure, the calendar" marks the line you're standing on: you didn't invent the deadline, you're just refusing to hide it. Real costs, honestly named, move people that urgency-theatre never will.

Take it anywhere: "Every week this sits costs ___ — that's not pressure, it's the calendar." Only with true costs. The phantom version is Julian's two other couples.
Phrase 8

The job: making no a safe thing to say. Complete it:

"Is the real answer no? Because no is ___ — no I can ___ with."

"Is the real answer no? Because no is fine — no I can work with."
The bravest sentence in the call, and the most liberating — for both sides. Many long maybes are noes wearing politeness, held by clients who dread the awkwardness of saying it; offering the exit with genuine warmth either gets you the fast no (second-best outcome — capacity released, relationship intact) or, as here, flushes out the truth that it ISN'T a no, which changes everything. "It's the maybe that's expensive" is the honest economics, said out loud.

Take it anywhere: "Is the real answer no? No is fine." Proposals, invitations, relationships — the question that ends the expensive maybe.
Phrase 9

The job: finding the missing person in the decision. Complete it:

"Who else needs to be in the ___ for this to get decided?"

"Who else needs to be in the room for this to get decided?"
Half of all stuck decisions are stuck because the person you're talking to can't actually make them alone — there's a spouse, a partner, a board, a Tomas. The question surfaces the real decision unit, and the follow-through is the masterstroke of this call: inviting the wife onto Monday's conversation. "I'd rather answer her questions than have Tomas answer them for me" — because absent decision-makers get their information from whoever IS in the room, and it's never you.

Take it anywhere: "Who else needs to be in the room?" Then get them in it. Deals, projects, family decisions.
Phrase 10

The job: closing the call so Monday actually happens. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "The letter's in your inbox tonight, I call Monday at noon, both of you on the line — and whichever way it lands is fine."
The close is a checklist read back as a promise: what arrives (the fixed-price letter, in time for the weekend conversation), when the decision happens (Monday, noon, you dialling), who's present (both deciders), and the safety rail ("either way is fine") repeated once more so it holds over the weekend. Every stalled deal that finally moved, moved because somebody built this exact scaffolding around one specific moment.

Take it anywhere: "___ arrives tonight, I call ___ at ___, and whichever way it lands is fine." The scaffold for any decision you need to actually occur.

The Echo 🕰️

Napoleon's marshals learned to dread one thing more than any enemy: his speed of decision. While opposing councils of war debated — gathering more information, waiting for perfect certainty, leaving it with themselves — he decided, moved, and arrived where nobody was ready. "I may lose a battle," he said, "but I shall never lose a minute." At Austerlitz, faced by two emperors and a larger army, he won largely because the allied command spent the critical days doing what committees do: almost deciding.

His actual method is the useful part — because it wasn't recklessness. He decided fast because he'd done the thinking BEFORE the moment: worst cases mapped, options pre-weighed, so that when the moment came the decision was already small. And he understood the ledger most people never draw up: the cost of deciding wrong is usually visible and survivable; the cost of not deciding — the melted advantage, the lost season, the drift — is invisible and total. Empires, he proved in both directions, are lost to maybe.

Your client isn't facing Austerlitz; he's facing a quote and his brother-in-law's forty percent. But the ledger is the same one, and your job on the phone is Napoleonic in miniature: shrink the decision until it's already made, put a clock on it — and never lose a minute to a warm, friendly, six-week maybe.
