Warm-up

Before anything else — out loud, no writing yet.

SPEAK

Think of someone at work or in your family. Describe them in one sentence starting with "That's the person who..." Say it out loud.

Listen in on this conversation

Sam: Did you meet the new manager yet — the one whose office is next to reception?

Priya: Briefly. She's actually the person I sent that report to last week.

Sam: The one you spent three days on?

Priya: That's the one — ten pages, most of which I ended up rewriting anyway.

Sam: Rough. Anyway, we're in the meeting room with the chair fixed on Monday. Still wobbles.

Priya: Of course it does.

Look at what Sam and Priya do without even noticing. Three different ways of packing extra, precise detail about a person or thing into a sentence. Can you spot all three? Say them before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "whose office is next to reception" — whose, for showing something belongs to someone.
2. "most of which I ended up rewriting" — a quantity + of which, for saying more about part of a group already mentioned.
3. "the chair fixed on Monday" — a shortened clause (it really means "which was fixed"), dropping the extra words when they're not needed.

This is why these matter: they let you add one sharp extra fact about a person or thing without stopping your sentence to start a new one.
Today: three ways to add precise extra detail to what you say — whose, a quantity + of which/of whom, and shortened clauses like "the people living..." or "the report written..." — plus knowing when to keep it natural and when to make it more careful and formal.

Whose Office Is It?

Showing that something belongs to someone, and being precise about a preposition that belongs to the clause.

Spot the pattern

  • "That's the woman whose car got towed last week."
  • "He's the client I've been dealing with." → more carefully: "He's the client with whom I've been dealing."
  • "This is the drawer I keep my passport in." → more carefully: "This is the drawer in which I keep my passport."

What's each one doing, and what changes between the natural and the careful version? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
whose + noun shows possession — "her car", "his office" — and works for people and things alike.

When a preposition belongs to the clause ("dealing WITH the client", "keep it IN the drawer"), natural spoken English leaves it at the end. Careful or formal English moves it to the front — and once it's at the front, you must use whom for a person, or which for a thing. "Who" never follows a preposition directly.
whose + noun  (possession — people or things)
preposition + whom  (people, careful/formal)
preposition + which  (things, careful/formal)
natural spoken: ...(who / that / — ) ... + preposition  (at the end)
One trap: after a preposition it's always whom, never "to who". But at the end of a natural spoken sentence, "Who did you give it to?" is completely standard.

Quick check

1. Your new neighbour's car has an alarm that goes off constantly. Combine that into one sentence about the neighbour:

"My new neighbour has a car alarm goes off every night."

2. Natural version: "She's the colleague I always turn to for advice." Rewrite the gap in the more careful, formal way — move the preposition to the front:

"She's the colleague I always turn for advice."

3. Natural version: "This is the shelf I put my keys on every day." Rewrite the gap the careful, formal way:

"This is the shelf I put my keys every day."

Three of Which Were Late

Adding one more fact about part of a group you've just mentioned.

Spot the pattern

  • "We interviewed twelve candidates, three of whom had no relevant experience."
  • "My uncle owns four cafés, one of which is right next to the station."
  • "I sent out twenty invitations, twelve of which got no reply."

What comes before each one, and what job is "of which/of whom" doing? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Each sentence first mentions a whole group (candidates, cafés, invitations), then adds a comma and a quantity + of which/of whom to say something extra about PART of that group — of whom for people, of which for things. The verb that follows agrees with the quantity, not the original number: "three of whom had", not "has".
[group mentioned], quantity + of which/of whom + verb...  (some, several, many, most, none, a number)
This only works once a plural group has already been named — you can't start a sentence with "of which".

Quick check

1. Your sister has six kids. Four of them are still at school. Combine that into one sentence:

"My sister has six kids, are still at school."

2. The library has thirty computers. Ten of them are already broken. Combine that into one sentence:

"The library has thirty computers, are already broken."

3. You texted five old friends. Not one of them replied. Combine that into one sentence:

"I texted five old friends, replied."

Now say it

SPEAK

Tell me about a group you belong to — your team, your class, your family — and describe part of it using of whom or of which. ("...ten of whom...", "...three of which...")

The People Living Upstairs

Dropping the pronoun and the verb "to be" when the meaning stays completely clear.

Spot the pattern

  • "The people living upstairs just moved in." (= who are living)
  • "The report written last week is already out of date." (= which was written)
  • "Anyone wanting a refund should speak to the manager." (= who wants)

What's dropped in each one, and how do you choose which participle to use? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
The relative pronoun and "to be" both disappear, leaving just a participle stuck onto the noun. If the noun is doing the action, use the -ing form (living, wanting). If the action is done to the noun, use the past participle (written, sent, taken) — a passive meaning without spelling out the full passive.
noun + verb-ing  (active — the noun does it)
noun + past participle  (passive — it's done TO the noun)
A report doesn't write itself — so "the report writing last week" is wrong. It's written: something was done to it.

Quick check

1. A man is right outside your building this moment, waiting for someone. Describe him:

"The man outside is waiting for my flatmate."

2. Yesterday, someone put an important message about the meeting straight into your inbox — you still haven't opened it. Describe it:

"The email yesterday about the meeting is still unread."

3. Full clause: "Anyone who needs extra help can email the office." Shorten it:

"Anyone extra help can email the office."

Now say it

SPEAK

Look around you right now. Describe three things or people using a shortened clause — noun + -ing, or noun + past participle. ("The chair standing in the corner...", "The letter left on the table...")

What Do You Remember?

A quick reminder first

1. Basic relative pronoun for a thing: "That's the shop just opened on the corner."

2. In your own words: what's the difference between "the man who called is my brother" and "my dad, who called earlier, is coming for dinner"? Say it out loud, then check.

Check your answer CLICK
The first one identifies which man you mean — no commas, essential information. The second one already knows who "my dad" is; the clause just adds extra information, marked off with commas.
