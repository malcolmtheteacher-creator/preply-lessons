Do we have rights simply because we are human, or only because a government gives them to us?

You buy a new phone, and the company says it may read your messages. Your boss says you can't post political opinions online, even at the weekend. Is that just unfair, or does it break something deeper: your rights? Over three hundred years ago, an English doctor argued that some rights belong to you from birth, and no king can take them away.

Today: You'll meet John Locke and his idea of natural rights, so you can talk about rights with confidence and explain why he thought a government must protect them or be replaced.

Meet Locke

🩺

The quiet doctor

John Locke was born in Somerset, England, in 1632, the same year as Spinoza. He studied at Oxford, where he became interested in medicine and the new science. In 1667 he went to live with the politician later known as Lord Shaftesbury, as his doctor and adviser. According to the usual story, Locke saved Shaftesbury's life by organising a dangerous operation. Through Shaftesbury, Locke was pulled into the risky politics of his time.

How did Locke become involved in politics?

He became the doctor and adviser of Lord Shaftesbury, a powerful politician, and was pulled into the dangerous politics of the time.
⛵

Escape and return

In the early 1680s, Shaftesbury fell into serious trouble with the King, and Locke was in danger too. In 1683 he escaped to the Netherlands and lived there for several years. In 1688 King James II was forced from the throne, and William and Mary took his place. Locke came home in 1689 and published his two most important books. One said that a newborn baby's mind is like a 'blank slate': everything we know comes from experience.

What is the 'blank slate'?

Locke's idea that a newborn baby's mind is empty, and everything we know comes from experience.
📜

Life, liberty and property

The other book, Two Treatises of Government, was about power. Locke said every person is born with natural rights to life, liberty and property. These rights do not come from kings; they belong to us as human beings. A government exists to protect them, and it rules only with the people's consent. If it attacks their rights, the people may replace it. Locke died in 1704. Later, his ideas helped shape the American Declaration of Independence.

According to Locke, what can people do if a government attacks their rights?

They may replace it, because a government rules only with the people's consent, and its job is to protect their rights.
Talk: Locke said people may replace a government that attacks their rights. Is that a dangerous idea, or a necessary one?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. Prisoners lose their liberty: they cannot go where they want or see who they want.
liberty (n) — the freedom to live, think and act as you choose, within the law. Example: "The new law protects the liberty of the press."
2. You can't use someone's photo in an advert without their consent; you have to ask first.
consent (n) — permission or agreement to let something happen. Example: "Doctors need a patient's consent before an operation."
3. After ten years with the company, she was entitled to an extra week of holiday, so she took it.
entitled (adj) — having the right to have or do something (used with 'to'). Example: "Full-time workers are entitled to four weeks' paid holiday."
4. The fence marks the edge of our property; the field beyond it belongs to the farmer.
property (n) — things that someone owns, such as land, a house or money. Example: "Please don't leave any personal property on the train."
5. The election was free and fair, so most people accepted the new government as legitimate.
legitimate (adj) — fair and acceptable according to the law or to good reasons. Example: "Is a sick cat a legitimate reason to miss work?"
6. Babies seem to have an innate fear of falling: nobody has to teach them.
innate (adj) — present from birth, not learned. Example: "Some people believe musical talent is innate."
7. A government that abuses its power may start arresting people who criticise it.
abuse (v) — to use power or a position in a wrong or harmful way. Example: "The manager abused his position by giving jobs to his friends."

See it in action

Here is the idea in action. A town council is elected to keep the streets safe and fix the roads. Then it starts taking people's houses without paying and arresting anyone who complains. Locke would say: the council has broken the agreement. Its power came from the people's consent, so that it could protect their rights. Now that it attacks those rights, it is no longer legitimate, and the people are entitled to replace it.

Connect

Compare

Hobbes and Locke: Hobbes and Locke both said people agree to have a government. But Hobbes said we can never take that power back, while Locke said we can replace a government that abuses its power. Why do you think they disagreed?
Hobbes feared chaos above everything, so he wanted a ruler whose power could never be questioned. Locke feared a ruler who abused power, so he said the people keep their natural rights and may replace a bad government. Hobbes put safety first; Locke put rights first.
Plato and Locke: Plato believed we are born already knowing important truths, so learning is really remembering. Locke said the mind starts as a blank slate. Which seems closer to the truth to you?
For Plato, knowledge is already inside us, and good questions help us remember it. For Locke, everything comes from experience, so education and our surroundings shape who we become. Many people today think the truth is somewhere in between: we are born with some abilities, but most knowledge is learned.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From Baruch Spinoza

What was Spinoza's big idea about God and Nature?

That they are the same thing: one infinite whole. Everything that exists, including us, is a part of it.
From René Descartes

What was the one thing Descartes could not doubt?

That he was thinking, and so that he existed: "I think, therefore I am."
From Averroes (Ibn Rushd)

What did Averroes believe about faith and reason?

That truth cannot contradict truth: real faith and careful reasoning must agree, and if they seem to clash, the words of scripture should be interpreted more deeply.
From Epicurus

According to Epicurus, what is the secret of a happy life?

A calm mind and a body without pain: want only simple things that are easy to get, and enjoy them with friends.
