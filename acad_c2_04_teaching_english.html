Teaching Through English (EMI)

Delivering academic content in English to multilingual students

English as Medium of Instruction (EMI)

EMI presents a distinctive pedagogical challenge: you must simultaneously teach disciplinary content and manage language demands for non-native English speakers. Unlike English language teaching, where language is the object, EMI treats English as the vehicle. This creates tension: slowing down for language clarity may compromise content depth; pushing forward with content density may exclude struggling students.

The Content-Language Trade-Off

Research on EMI reveals persistent tensions. Students report understanding individual words but struggling with conceptual integration. They hesitate to contribute because formulating ideas in English requires cognitive effort that leaves little capacity for complex thinking. This doesn't mean students lack capability; rather, the linguistic load exceeds their current processing capacity.

Inclusive Practice in EMI Contexts

Effective EMI requires deliberate scaffolding: building support structures that enable access without diluting content. This involves pre-teaching key vocabulary, using visual representations alongside verbal explanation, providing written summaries, and creating opportunities for peer discussion in students' first language before contributing to whole-group dialogue.

The Confidence Paradox

Non-native speakers often underestimate their own understanding. They may be thinking clearly in their native language yet reluctant to express ideas in English. As the instructor, you must create psychological safety where hesitation to speak reflects language processing, not conceptual confusion. Explicitly acknowledge this: "Take your time—I know translating complex ideas takes mental effort."

EMI Vocabulary

Scaffolding Temporary support structures that enable students to access content beyond their independent capability; gradually removed as competence increases.
Cognitive load Mental effort required for a task; in EMI, managing language demands prevents overload that impairs learning.
Comprehensible input Language that is slightly beyond students' current level but made understandable through context and support.
Code-switching Alternating between English and L1; often strategically employed to clarify concepts or reduce cognitive load.
Disciplinary register Specialized vocabulary and discourse patterns particular to an academic discipline.
Negotiation of meaning Interactive process where speaker and listener work together to achieve mutual understanding.
Wait time Pause following a question, allowing students time to formulate responses; particularly important in multilingual contexts.
Affective filter Emotional state (anxiety, confidence) that influences language acquisition and classroom participation.

Strategic Simplification Without Condescension

Your classroom English must balance accessibility with academic integrity. This requires conscious choices about word selection, sentence structure, and explanation density.

Principles of Accessible Academic Language

Use high-frequency words with complex ideas: Rather than "we shall elucidate the mechanisms underlying this phenomenon," say "we will explain how this works." The simpler structure enables focus on the concept. Signpost your thinking: "First, I'll define the key term. Then I'll show you examples. Finally, we'll apply this to your research." This scaffolds understanding through explicit structure. Repeat key terminology: Use crucial discipline-specific terms multiple times in varied contexts so students develop secure understanding.

Checking Comprehension Authentically

Avoid "Does everyone understand?" (students always nod). Instead: "Who can summarize what I just explained?" "What question does this answer?" "Give me an example from your field." These activities reveal actual comprehension and provide opportunities for language practice.

Example comprehension check: Instead of asking "Did you understand the statistical method I described?" ask "Walk me through the steps of this analysis—what comes first, second, third?" This reveals whether they grasped the concept or merely heard English words.

EMI Classroom Language Patterns

Making vocabulary explicit: "I'm using the word 'paradigm' here, which means a framework—a way of looking at a problem."

Signposting: "We're moving from theory to practice now. Pay attention to how the ideas change."

Encouraging elaboration: "Can you say more about that? Give me an example." (provides practice while demonstrating genuine interest)

Confirming understanding: "Let me rephrase that to check you got it. I said [simpler version]. Is that what you understood?"

Using wait time: Ask question. Count silently to 10 before accepting any answer. This allows non-native speakers time to formulate thoughts.

Validating effort: "I notice you're working hard to express this in English. Take your time—accuracy is less important than trying."

Designing EMI Lessons

Effective EMI lesson design explicitly manages language demands while maintaining content integrity.

The Pre-Teach Strategy

Introduce key vocabulary before the main lesson. This reduces cognitive load during instruction; students recognize terms rather than encountering them for the first time. Pre-teaching can be brief (5-10 minutes) but profoundly affects comprehension and participation.

Multimodal Representation

Present concepts through multiple modalities: verbal explanation, visual diagrams, written text, and if possible, practical demonstration. Research shows this redundancy supports learning, particularly for non-native speakers. Visual aids should represent meaning, not merely decorate slides.

EMI Lesson Design Checklist

Key disciplinary vocabulary identified and pre-taught
Content explained through multiple modalities (verbal, visual, textual, practical)
Opportunity for peer discussion before whole-group contribution
Clear signposting of lesson structure (what we're doing and why)
Assessment activities reveal genuine comprehension, not merely English recognition
Built-in wait time for responses (10+ seconds)
Authentic comprehension checking throughout

Developing Your EMI Practice

Analyze a lesson you teach or have taught recently. Work through these reflections:

Language Demands

What vocabulary or syntactic complexity might challenge non-native speakers? How will you address this?

Cognitive Load Management

Where might students struggle to simultaneously process language and content? How can you reduce this load?

Inclusive Participation

How do you create safety for students to contribute even if their English is imperfect? What signals do you send about the role of language in your classroom?

The Broader Purpose

EMI is fundamentally about equity: ensuring that non-native speakers can access disciplinary knowledge and participate fully as scholars. This doesn't mean lowering standards; it means designing instruction with their linguistic realities in mind. As you develop this practice, remember that your accommodation of language differences benefits all learners—clarity, multimodal representation, and explicit structure support everyone's understanding.
