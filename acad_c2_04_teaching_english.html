What Just Happened?

You've moved beyond the anxiety that teaching in English means being less of an expert. Your credibility isn't linguistic—it's intellectual. Students don't respect you less because you occasionally pause for a word. They respect you more when you handle the pause with grace.

EMI isn't about fluency. It's about clarity, signposting, checking understanding, and recovering when communication breaks. These are moves that serve complex thinking—in any language.

Your Moves

These are the specific language and delivery moves that make complex content accessible without losing sophistication.

1 Signposting for Clarity +

You make the structure of your thinking explicit. You signal where you are and where you're going. This helps non-native listeners follow complex reasoning.

When to use it:

Every lecture. Between major ideas. When pivoting to a new concept.

Without signposting

"This is related to X. So when we look at Y, it becomes clear that Z. But there's another way to think about it..."

With signposting

"I've explained X. Now I'm going to show you how X connects to Y. This connection is important because it challenges the traditional view of Z. I'll make three points: first... second... third..."

2 Buying Thinking Time +

You acknowledge that you need a moment to find the right word or think through the question. This is not weakness—it models intellectual honesty.

When to use it:

When a student asks an unexpected question. When you're searching for precise terminology. When you need to collect your thoughts.

The move

Student asks a complex question. You: "That's a really good question. Let me think about how to answer it clearly. The key issue is X... I think what you're asking is whether... Yes, here's my answer."

Avoid

Silence and panic, OR rapid rambling to fill the void.

3 Reformulating for Non-Native Listeners +

You state a complex idea once in rich language, then immediately say it again more simply. Not dumbing down—accessing the idea from different angles.

When to use it:

When you've introduced a difficult concept. When you're using specialized vocabulary. When you want to ensure understanding before moving on.

The move

"The phenomenon of epistemological relativism—which is the idea that we can't access objective truth, only interpretations of truth—has significant implications. In simpler terms: if knowledge is interpretation, then experts and students are not as far apart as we think."

4 Managing Questions You Don't Understand +

You ask for clarification without embarrassment. You model that confusion is not a personal failing—it's data about communication.

When to use it:

When a student's question is unclear to you. When you don't know if you're misunderstanding the language or the concept.

The move

Student asks unclear question. You: "I want to give you a good answer, so let me make sure I understand. Are you asking whether X affects Y, or whether X causes Y? Those are different questions."

Benefit

You get clarity AND you model intellectual engagement for your students.

5 Checking Understanding Without Infantilizing +

You ask students to apply the concept, not to parrot it back. You treat them as thinkers, not receivers.

When to use it:

After you've presented a complex idea. When you want to gauge whether students can work with the concept.

Weak check

"Do you all understand? Does that make sense?"

Strong check

"Can anyone give me an example of where you see this principle at work in your own field?" OR "How would you explain this concept to someone who disagreed with it?"

6 Recovering From Confusion +

When communication breaks—you misspeak, a student misunderstands, an explanation didn't land—you acknowledge it, restart, and move forward. You show resilience.

When to use it:

Whenever there's confusion. This is continuous.

The move

You realize you've said something that confused students. You: "I think I explained that poorly. Let me start again. The key point is..."

Effect

Students see that mistakes are normal and correctible. Your confidence increases because you're not defending confusion—you're clearing it.

Your Situation

You're 20 minutes into your first lecture. You've been explaining a complex theory. You see some students nodding, others looking confused. Then a hand goes up. A student asks:

"I don't understand the difference between what you said before about X and what you're saying now. Is it the same thing?"

You realize they've misunderstood a key point from 5 minutes ago. The confusion cascades forward. You need to decide: go back and clarify, or move forward?

Your Task: Prepare Your Response

You have 2 minutes to think. Consider:

  • Should you address this immediately or note it for later?
  • How do you clarify without making the student feel bad?
  • How do you explain something differently without insulting their intelligence?
  • What's your actual move here—not the ideal version, but what you'd really do?
