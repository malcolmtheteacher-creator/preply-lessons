The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — A true story told as a comic

Marjane Satrapi was born in 1969 and grew up in Tehran, the capital of Iran. As an adult she settled in France, and between 2000 and 2003 she published the story of her own childhood and youth in French, as a comic in four volumes. She called it Persepolis, the Greek name for the ancient Persian capital.

It is a memoir — a true account written from memory — but it looks nothing like a traditional one. The pages are made of small black-and-white drawings in boxes called panels, with short captions and speech bubbles. A first-time reader may expect something light. Few expect what follows.

The book reached English readers in the early 2000s and became one of the most widely read graphic memoirs in the world. In 2007 Satrapi helped turn it into an animated film, which shared a Jury Prize at Cannes and was nominated for an Oscar.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did Satrapi grow up, and where did she later live?

2. Why is the book called a memoir?

3. What happened to the story in 2007?

1. She grew up in Tehran in Iran and later settled in France.

2. Because it is a true account of her own life, written from memory.

3. It became an animated film that shared a Jury Prize at Cannes and was nominated for an Oscar.

Discuss:
  • Would you have picked this book up if you had seen it in a shop? What would have drawn you in, or put you off?

Part 2 — What the story is about

The story begins when Marji, the narrator, is about ten and living in Tehran. Her country is in the middle of a revolution: the old government falls, a much stricter one takes its place, and almost everything about daily life changes — what girls must wear, what books are allowed, what can be said out loud.

Then comes a long war with Iraq. Marji grows up through bombing, fear and loss, until, at fourteen, her parents send her to Vienna to keep her safe. The second half of the book follows her through loneliness abroad, a difficult return to Tehran and a final decision to leave again.

It sounds heavy, and it is. But it is also funny, sharp and full of ordinary life: a girl who loves music, argues with her teachers and dreams of being a prophet. That mixture is what many readers remember.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How old is Marji when the story begins?

2. What happens to her at fourteen?

3. Why do many readers say the book is not only heavy?

1. About ten.

2. Her parents send her to Vienna to keep her safe.

3. Because it is also funny and full of ordinary life, not only fear and loss.

Discuss:
  • Can a story about something terrible be funny without making light of it? Where is the balance?

Part 3 — Why a comic can hit so hard

A comic works differently from a novel. Words and pictures arrive together, and the reader has to join them up. In the white spaces between the panels, nothing is drawn, and the mind fills the gap. That small act of imagination can make a scene feel more personal than a page of description.

Satrapi's style is deliberately stark: bold black lines, simple faces, no shading. Some readers feel this makes the book more honest; others say the simplicity lets them see themselves in the story, whatever country they come from. Because the drawings are so plain, the sad moments can feel sharper, and the funny ones lighter.

That is why a comic can be poignant in a way that surprises people. It does not tell you how to feel. It shows you a scene, leaves a gap, and lets you arrive at the feeling yourself.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What happens in the white spaces between panels?

2. How do some readers explain the effect of the simple drawings?

3. Why can a comic feel more personal than a long description?

1. Nothing is drawn; the reader's mind fills in the gap.

2. They say it feels honest, or it lets readers see themselves in the story.

3. Because the reader must join words and pictures and make the connection themselves.

Discuss:
  • Think of a film or a book that did not tell you how to feel but still moved you. How did it manage it?

How the tool works

Most of us stop at "I enjoyed it" or "I didn't". A good book conversation goes further. The Response Wheel gives you four moves you can use on any book — and any one of them can start a discussion.

Using it, step by step

  1. Notice. Say one thing you actually observed: a detail, a pattern, something you'd have missed on a first read. I noticed that…
  2. React. Say how it affected you, in more than one word. What struck me most was… / I found it genuinely moving when…
  3. Question. Ask something real that you'd like others to think about. Why do you think the author…? Did anyone else…?
  4. Connect. Link it to your own life, another book or something in the news. It reminded me of… Then hand the conversation back.

Try it yourself

These comments could all be said in a book discussion. Which move on the wheel is each one?

1. I noticed how few words there were on some pages.
2. Honestly, the ending left me quite shaken.
3. Why do you think she chose to draw herself as a child?
4. It reminded me of my grandmother's stories about the war.
5. The black-and-white drawings never change, even in the happy scenes.
6. I was laughing one minute and almost in tears the next.
7. Did anyone else find the school scenes hard to read?
8. My own family moved country when I was nine, so the Vienna chapters felt very close.

1. Notice — it points to something observed in the book.

2. React — it reports how the book affected the speaker.

3. Question — it asks the group to think about the author's choice.

4. Connect — it links the book to the speaker's own life.

5. Notice — it observes a pattern across the book.

6. React — it describes a feeling, not a fact about the book.

7. Question — it invites others to respond.

8. Connect — it connects the story to the speaker's own experience.

Where the tool breaks

It's not a script. Four moves are a way in, not an order to follow. Real conversations jump between them.

"Notice" must be specific. "It's really well written" is a judgement, not an observation. Name the page, the line or the detail.

A reaction is not a verdict. Someone who was bored has still reacted. Say so, and say why — it is more interesting than polite praise.

Talk it through:
  • Which move do you find easiest — and which do you usually skip?
  • Try the four moves on a film you saw recently. Which one gives you the most to say?

Language — Putting a reaction into words

"I liked it" ends a conversation. Better reaction language is precise, varied and a little personal. Three tools help: the -ed / -ing adjectives, intensifiers, and the "what struck me was…" structure.

1 · -ed or -ing? The -ing adjective describes the thing that causes the feeling; the -ed adjective describes the person who feels it. “The ending was moving.” “I was moved.” Pairs: shocking / shocked, fascinating / fascinated, disturbing / disturbed, amusing / amused.
2 · Intensifiers that fit. Do not overuse very. Try genuinely, deeply, quite, rather, strangely, unexpectedly: “I found it genuinely moving.” Some adjectives do not take very at all: fascinating and shocking are already strong, so say absolutely fascinating.
3 · The cleft sentence. To put the focus on your reaction, begin with What or The thing that: “What struck me most was how calmly it is told.” “The thing that surprised me was the humour.” “It was the ending that stayed with me.”
4 · Hedging a personal view. I found it… / To me it felt… / I'm not sure everyone would agree, but… makes your view open to discussion, which is what a group wants.
5 · Traps. I am boring (I am dull) → “I am bored.” The film was very fascinated → “The film was fascinating.” What surprised me it was the humour → “What surprised me was the humour.”
