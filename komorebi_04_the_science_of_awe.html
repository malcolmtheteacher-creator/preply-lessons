The Science of Awe

B2/C1 Level · The feeling that makes us small — in a good way

The feeling that floors you

There is a peculiar feeling that everyone has had once or twice and almost no one has a good word for: the feeling of being knocked slightly off your axis by something far larger than you. A starry sky in a quiet place. A great piece of music heard in good company. The sea, in certain weathers. A newborn child. Scientists, who came late to all this, have settled on a single, slightly grand-sounding name: awe. And they have been finding, in the past fifteen years, that it is rather more useful to us than anyone expected.

Think about this:

1. When were you last properly awed by something — not impressed, not amused, but knocked slightly off balance?

2. Some people seem to feel awe often; others rarely. What do you think makes the difference?

3. Why might a feeling that makes us feel small actually be good for us?

Quick prediction

Researchers have measured the awe response across many situations. Which two of the following do they consider the strongest reliable triggers in everyday life, and why?

a) standing under a starry sky   b) reading the news   c) walking through an old-growth forest   d) watching short funny videos   e) hearing a piece of music that genuinely moves you

Show Answer
(a) a starry sky and (c) an old-growth forest are the most reliable triggers, because both combine genuine vastness with something old, slow and natural — a combination the brain finds difficult to fully process. (e) music can produce awe, but typically in shorter bursts. (b) and (d) tend to produce engagement or amusement, rarely the small loss-of-self that defines awe.

Reading: A Word the Laboratory Came Late To

Scientists have a slightly grand-sounding name for the feeling that floors us — the kind we get standing under a starry sky, watching a thunderstorm move across a valley, or holding a newborn for the first time. They call it awe. For most of human history, awe was the territory of priests, poets and painters, far outside the laboratory. In the last fifteen years, however, that has begun to change. Researchers have started measuring it. They have, it turns out, a great deal to say.

Phrasing · read this paragraph aloud and break it into natural thought groups. Pause briefly at each comma; the long sentences are designed to be chunked.

At the heart of the modern science of awe lies a beautifully simple definition, due largely to the American psychologist Dacher Keltner. Awe, he argues, has two ingredients. First, there must be a sense of vastness — physical, conceptual, or social. And second, the experience must resist assimilation: it must not quite fit into the categories you already carry. The combination is what stops a person mid-thought, what brings them up short, and what produces the curious, oddly pleasant feeling of the diminished self.

Sentence stress · read aloud and underline the three or four content words in each sentence that take the heaviest stress. Function words (the, of, with, are) should slip past almost unnoticed.

There is also, it turns out, a body. Functional MRI studies show distinct brain patterns during awe, and a growing pile of evidence links repeated experiences of awe with lower levels of inflammation, lower stress markers, and a measurable rise in pro-social behaviour — helping strangers, donating, taking the time to listen. Subjectively, people in awe tend to feel that time slows, even though no clock has actually moved. They walk away with a quiet recalibration of what matters, and a slightly looser grip on the small grievances that fill most of our days.

Linking · notice how, in fluent speech, words bleed into each other — "lower_levels", "an_awe response", "time_actually". Practise the linking; don't separate every word.

The trouble is that modern life has, in many small ways, engineered awe out of itself. We spend our hours indoors, bathed in artificial light. The stars are dimmed by our cities. The slow, large, ancient things that historically produced awe — old forests, vast skies, a great piece of music heard in good company — have been crowded out by faster, smaller, brighter inputs. Few of these are bad in themselves. But almost none of them produce goosebumps; almost none make a person feel small in any useful way.

Intonation · this paragraph turns on a contrast — what we have, set against what we have lost. Listen for the falling intonation on each side of the pivot, with a small rise on the contrast word itself.

That, perhaps, is the most quietly hopeful finding of the whole field. Awe is not, it turns out, the property of the lucky few. It is recoverable, almost on demand, by people who deliberately seek it out — a long walk in an old place, a slow look at the night sky, a piece of music attended to seriously. The sensation seems trivial. The effects last for days. And as far as anyone can tell, it remains the only emotion we know of that puts our troubles in perspective while doing the body real, measurable good.

Shadow reading · try reading this paragraph aloud half a beat behind your teacher, matching pace, stress and intonation. The aim is rhythm, not perfection — lock onto the music of the sentence.

1. According to Dacher Keltner, what two ingredients make an experience awe-producing?

Show Answer
First, a sense of vastness — physical, conceptual or social. Second, the experience must resist assimilation; that is, it must not quite fit the categories you already carry. Both ingredients must be present; vastness alone is not enough.

2. What measurable physical effects does repeated awe seem to have?

Show Answer
Lower levels of inflammation, lower stress markers, and a measurable rise in pro-social behaviour. Subjectively, people also feel that time slows, and walk away with a recalibration of what matters and a looser grip on small everyday grievances.

3. How has modern life "engineered awe out of itself"?

Show Answer
We spend our hours indoors, under artificial light; cities dim the stars; and the slow, large, ancient things that historically produced awe have been crowded out by faster, brighter, smaller inputs that engage attention without producing the body's awe response.

4. Why does the writer describe the science of awe as "quietly hopeful"?

Show Answer
Because awe is not, it turns out, the privilege of the lucky few. It is recoverable, almost on demand, by anyone willing to walk in an old place, look slowly at the sky or attend seriously to a piece of music. The conditions are accessible to almost everyone.

5. Inference: what does the writer mean by "a slightly looser grip on the small grievances that fill most of our days"?

Show Answer
That awe seems to dissolve, at least temporarily, our normal preoccupation with small everyday irritations. The diminished self that awe produces makes our usual grievances feel proportionally smaller — and that effect, modest as it sounds, may be the most useful gift of the whole experience.

Inversion for emphasis

Awe is the perfect topic for inversion — the structure where we move a negative or restrictive adverbial to the start of the sentence, and flip the auxiliary verb in front of the subject. "Never have I felt anything quite like it." sounds three times more vivid than "I have never felt anything quite like it." Inversion is the literary register of intense experience. Use it sparingly, and it lifts an entire paragraph.

Negative adverbials

Never, rarely, seldom, hardly, scarcely, little, no sooner… at the start ⇒ auxiliary before subject.

Never had I felt so small.

Rarely does anything stop me in my tracks.

Not until… / Only when…

Time-restrictive openings also trigger inversion.

Not until I stood under that sky did I understand awe.

Only when you stop and look does the scale of it land.

"Little did I…"

A favourite literary opener — "little" + auxiliary + subject. Used to introduce something the speaker didn't anticipate.

Little did I know that the climb would change me.
