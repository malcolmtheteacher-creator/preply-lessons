Power and Control

C1 Level · IB Poetry Analysis · Browning, “My Last Duchess”

The situation

Ferrara, Italy, in the 1500s. A powerful duke is showing a visitor around his palace. The visitor is an envoy, a messenger arranging the duke’s next marriage. Behind a curtain hangs a portrait of the duke’s last wife. The duke does all the talking.

SPEAK: Predict: what might a man like this say about a dead wife’s portrait? What would make you suspicious?

Something odd

The poem is written in rhyming couplets (pairs of rhyming lines), but it hardly sounds like it. The sentences keep running past the line ends, so the rhyme is easy to miss.

SPEAK: Why might a poet hide the rhyme in a poem about control?

The speaker wants to sound relaxed and natural, as if he is just chatting. The strict form underneath mirrors the control he hides beneath his polite surface.

Your analysis toolkit

For each one: name it, point to it, explain the effect. Say the effect before you reveal it.

Dramatic monologue

One speaker talks to a silent listener, and gives himself away.

SPEAK: What does the reader know that the listener might not?

The duke reveals far more than he intends. This gap is dramatic irony: we see the cruelty he thinks he is hiding behind politeness.

Enjambment and caesura

Sentences run over line ends; punctuation breaks lines in the middle.

SPEAK: Look at the line where he says “How shall I say?” What does the interruption do?

The pauses make him seem to search for a delicate word, yet he is choosing his words carefully to justify himself. It sounds spontaneous but is calculated.

Possessive language and imagery

Words of ownership, art and objects.

SPEAK: Find three phrases that treat the Duchess as a possession.

The portrait “hangs on the wall” behind a curtain only he draws; he is proud of the “nine-hundred-years-old name”, of the sculpture of Neptune “taming” a sea-horse. People and things are both to be owned, controlled and shown off.

Euphemism and tone

Soft words that hide harsh facts.

SPEAK: What does “all smiles stopped together” really mean?

The calm, almost casual phrase hides what the reader fears: he had her killed or silenced. The mild tone makes it more chilling than an open confession.

Check: which tool?

“The duke uses a gentle phrase to cover up a violent act.”

“The reader understands the danger, while the listener may not.”

“Sentences spill over line ends, hiding the strict rhyme.”

Close reading

Round 1: Model, then you. Spot the difference before you read on.

Weak: The Duke is jealous and it shows he is controlling.

Strong: By complaining that the Duchess’s smile was given “somewhere else” as readily as to him, the duke reveals a possessive insecurity: he wants not her love but her exclusive, controlled attention, which positions marriage as ownership.

SPEAK: Tell me three things the strong version does better.

It points to a precise moment, explains what it reveals, uses an exact phrase of analysis (possessive insecurity), and links the detail to a larger idea about marriage and power.

Four key moments

Short extracts for close reading. Find them in context in the poem.

A. “…but who passed without / Much the same smile? This grew; I gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together.”
B. “I choose / Never to stoop.”
C. “Notice Neptune, though, / Taming a sea-horse, thought a rarity…”
D. “…Will’t please you sit and look at her?”

SPEAK: Pick one. Name the technique, explain the effect, and connect it to the theme of power.

A: Short, abrupt clauses and the sudden, flat “I gave commands” show power exercised in secret; the semicolons keep the tone controlled. B: “Never to stoop” shows pride so absolute that communication, or compromise, is impossible. C: Neptune taming a sea-horse mirrors the duke’s own relationship to the Duchess. D: The final invitation shows he is still staging the portrait: a woman is now a controlled, silent exhibit.

Point: Browning presents power as…

Technique: The use of… creates…
Effect: This makes the reader feel…
Link: Therefore the poem suggests that unchecked power…

SPEAK: Rank from weakest to strongest and defend your order.

  1. The duke is a bad man.
  2. Browning uses the duke’s own polite voice to expose how power can disguise cruelty as good manners.
  3. The poem is about a painting.
Number 2 is strongest: specific, arguable, linked to technique and theme. Number 1 is a judgment without analysis; number 3 only describes the surface.

Key words

dramatic monologue (noun) — a poem in which one character speaks to a silent listener and reveals their true nature. Example: "The poem is a dramatic monologue spoken by a duke."
dramatic irony (noun) — when the reader understands more than the character or listener does. Example: "Dramatic irony makes the duke's boast sound sinister."
caesura (noun) — a pause in the middle of a line of verse. Example: "The caesura makes him sound as if he is choosing his words."
couplet (noun) — two lines of verse that rhyme and follow each other. Example: "The couplets are hidden by the long sentences."
euphemism (noun) — a mild phrase used instead of a harsh one. Example: "Passed away is a common euphemism for died."
possessive (adjective) — wanting to own or control another person completely. Example: "His possessive attitude shows in how he talks about her."
sinister (adjective) — seeming evil or threatening. Example: "The calm tone is more sinister than shouting."
imply (verb) — to suggest something without saying it directly. Example: "The poet implies that the duke ordered her death."
