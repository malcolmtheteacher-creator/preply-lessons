The Matrix

A man learns his whole world is fake — made by machines. Old philosophers had a similar strange idea, long before computers.

The film, in three parts

The Wachowskis' The Matrix (1999). Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one. (There are spoilers — that is fine, we want to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A strange choice

Neo is a normal man with a computer job. But at night he is a hacker who asks: "Is something wrong with the world?" A woman called Trinity finds him. Then a man called Morpheus tells him a big secret. He says nothing in the world is real. He gives Neo a choice: a blue pill (Neo forgets everything) or a red pill (Neo learns the truth). Neo takes the red pill.

Neo wakes up inside a strange machine. His real body was always sleeping there. Morpheus tells him: this whole world was a computer program called "the Matrix." Machines made it. The machines use human bodies for energy, like batteries. Neo's whole "life" before this was not real — it was only a program in his mind, made to keep people calm and asleep.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What choice does Morpheus give Neo?

2. What is "the Matrix," really?

1. A blue pill, so he forgets everything, or a red pill, so he learns the whole truth. He chooses the red pill.

2. A computer program that makes people believe a fake world is real, while machines use their bodies as a source of energy.

Talk about it:
  • Would you want to know if your life was not real? Or is it better not to know?

Part 2 — Learning new rules

Neo must learn to use his real body — he has never really moved it before. Morpheus's friends teach him new skills very fast, like a computer program going straight into his brain. Morpheus says: the Matrix has rules, like a computer game. If you truly understand this, you can break the rules.

A wise woman called the Oracle talks to Neo. She says he is not "the One" — a special person who can save everyone — but maybe she is only testing him. A boy shows Neo a spoon and says, "There is no spoon." He means: nothing is really there at all. Only your mind makes it feel real.

One man on the team, Cypher, is tired of the hard, real world. He makes a secret deal with the enemy programs, the Agents. He wants to go back into the Matrix and forget everything. He tells the Agents where to find Morpheus.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the boy mean when he says "there is no spoon"?

2. Why does Cypher betray his friends?

1. The Matrix isn't really there — it's only in your mind — so if you understand this, you can change how it works.

2. He is tired of the hard, real world, so he tells the Agents where Morpheus is, in exchange for a comfortable life back inside the Matrix, with no memory of the truth.

Talk about it:
  • Cypher chooses a comfortable lie instead of a hard truth. What would you choose?

Part 3 — Neo believes

The Agents catch Morpheus. Neo and Trinity go back into the Matrix to save him. They succeed — but then Neo meets a dangerous Agent, Agent Smith, in a train station. Agent Smith shoots Neo, and Neo's body stops working.

Outside the Matrix, Trinity does not believe Neo is dead. She loves him, and an old prophecy said she would love "the One." She tells him this. Suddenly, Neo's heart starts again! Now Neo can see the Matrix in a new way — as computer code, not a real, solid world. Bullets cannot hurt him anymore. He beats Agent Smith easily. At the end, Neo promises to show everyone the truth and to free their minds.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happens when Trinity tells Neo she loves him?

2. How does Neo change after this moment?

1. Neo's heart starts again, and he comes back to life.

2. He can now see the Matrix as computer code, not a real world, and the Agents' bullets can no longer hurt him.

Talk about it:
  • Neo becomes strong when he truly believes the Matrix is not real. Do you think believing in yourself can really make you stronger?

Grammar: the first conditional

This film is full of choices and their results: if you do this, that will happen. English has an easy structure for a real, possible future.

if + present simple, ... will + base verb = a real, possible future result: "If you take the red pill, you will learn the truth." "If Neo doesn't believe, he will not be able to stop the bullets."
Remember: we use the present simple after if — never will in the if-part. "If it rains" (not "if it will rain").

1. Complete with the correct form

Read the sentence and complete both gaps.

1. If Neo (take) the red pill, he (learn) the truth.

2. If Trinity (not / help) Neo, he (die) in the station.

3. If people (not / ask) questions, they (never / find) the truth.

1. takes · will learn

2. doesn't help · will die

3. don't ask · will never find

2. Make the sentence

Put the verb in the correct base form after will. Then read it out loud.

1. If you believe completely, you will (be) strong, like Neo.

2. If the machines control everything, people will (not / know) the truth.

3. If Neo doesn't stop the Agents, they will (catch) him.

1. be

2. not know

3. catch

3. Complete the short text

Fill each gap with the correct form. Read it aloud when you finish.

If people (not / ask) hard questions, they (never / learn) the truth about their world. Neo believes: if he (trust) himself completely, nothing (stop) him.

don't ask · will never learn · trusts · will stop

Remember: present simple after if, and will + base verb in the result.

The Real Story — is any of this true?

The Matrix uses very old philosophy ideas — some over two thousand years old!

🏛 Plato's Cave — a very old story

A long time ago, the Greek thinker Plato told a story about people chained inside a cave. They could only see shadows moving on a wall in front of them. They thought the shadows were the real world! One day, one prisoner escapes and sees the real world outside for the first time. It is difficult, but it is true. This is very similar to Neo's story: he thinks his world is real, but it is only "shadows" made by machines.

Can we ever know what's real? (Descartes and Bostrom)

In 1641, a philosopher named René Descartes asked: how do I really know anything is true? He imagined an evil "demon" tricking his mind about everything. He decided that one thing was certain: "I think, therefore I am." Much later, in 2003, another philosopher, Nick Bostrom, asked a very modern question: could we actually be living inside a computer simulation right now? He could not prove it — but he showed that it is not a crazy question to ask.

Talk about it:
  • Which idea do you like more: Plato's cave, or Descartes' question?
  • Do you think you could ever really know if your world was fake?
  • Would you rather know an uncomfortable truth, or believe a comfortable lie?
