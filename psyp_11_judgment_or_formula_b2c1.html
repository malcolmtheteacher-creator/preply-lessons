Expert or Formula?

Judgement or formula: true or false?

In 1954, Paul Meehl challenged his own profession. Here are six claims about the research on expert judgement and simple rules. Decide which are true.

1. In most comparisons of this kind, experts outperform simple rules.
False. In most comparisons the rule did at least as well.
2. Kahneman and Klein agreed that intuition is more likely to be reliable in regular environments with fast, clear feedback.
True. Chess and firefighting are examples.
3. An algorithm cannot inherit bias, because it only follows a rule.
False. A rule built on biased data can entrench the bias.
4. Meehl compared clinical prediction, in which an expert combines information by judgement, with statistical prediction, in which the information is combined by a fixed rule.
True. His short book was a challenge to his own profession.
5. Human judgement is noisy: it varies from day to day and from person to person, even with the same information.
True. A rule, by contrast, gives the same answer every time.
6. Kahneman and Klein concluded that expert intuition can never be trusted.
False. They agreed it can be reliable in some settings.

The evidence, in three steps

Before each part, make a prediction out loud. Then read, and check yourself.

Part 1 — Meehl's challenge

Before you read: Meehl compared expert judgement with a fixed rule across twenty studies. Which do you expect came out ahead, and how clearly?

In 1954, the psychologist Paul Meehl published a short book that challenged his own profession. He compared clinical prediction, in which an expert integrates information by judgement, with statistical prediction, in which the same information is combined by a fixed rule. He reviewed twenty studies and found that, in almost all of them, the statistical method was at least as accurate as the clinical one.

The pattern has held remarkably well. A 2000 meta-analysis by William Grove and colleagues of 136 studies found that mechanical prediction was equal to or better than clinical prediction in about 94% of comparisons, and substantially better in roughly a third to a half of them. The studies spanned predictions of academic success, recidivism, medical diagnosis and response to treatment.

Part 2 — Why simple rules win

Before you read: Why might two experts looking at identical information disagree, or one expert disagree with herself?

One reason is noise. Human judgement varies from day to day and from person to person, even when the information is identical. Experts have been shown to give different assessments of the same case when it appears twice, and to be affected by mood, fatigue and the order in which cases arrive. A formula, by contrast, is perfectly consistent.

Another is that experts tend to overweight vivid but weak cues and to be more confident than their accuracy justifies. Even simple 'improper' models, such as an equal-weight sum of a few predictors, have been shown by Robyn Dawes to do surprisingly well. People often feel that they add value by taking account of subtle factors that a formula ignores; but the evidence suggests that, on average, these exceptions add more error than accuracy.

Words from this part — what does each word in bold mean here? Say your idea, then reveal.

“One reason is noise.”
noise (n) — unwanted, random variation in judgements of the same information. Example: "Two judges giving very different sentences for the same crime is an example of noise in the system."
“Another is that experts tend to overweight vivid but weak cues and to be more confident than their accuracy justifies.”
overweight (v) — to give something more importance than it deserves when making a judgement. Example: "Investors often overweight recent news and forget the long-term trend."
“People often feel that they add value by taking account of subtle factors that a formula ignores; but the evidence suggests that, on average, these exceptions add more error than accuracy.”
subtle (adj) — small and hard to notice, but possibly important. Example: "There was a subtle change in her tone when the subject of money came up."

Part 3 — When intuition deserves trust

Before you read: Under what conditions would you trust an expert's intuition over a rule?

The picture is not entirely one-sided. In 2009, Daniel Kahneman, a sceptic of intuition, and Gary Klein, who studies expert decision-making in settings such as firefighting, published a joint paper. They concluded that expert intuition can be trusted when two conditions are met: the environment must be regular enough to be predictable, and the expert must have had a long chance to learn the regularities through rapid, clear feedback. Chess, firefighting and anaesthesia meet these conditions. Predicting the long-term behaviour of a person does not.

The practical implication is not that professionals should be replaced. A structured tool, such as the Apgar score used in obstetrics for the past seventy years, can combine with judgement. Critics add important cautions: algorithms can entrench biases in their training data, can be opaque, and may be applied outside the populations on which they were tested. The question is therefore not 'expert or formula?' but 'which parts of the decision should be structured, and how will we check?'

Words from this part — what does each word in bold mean here? Say your idea, then reveal.

“Critics add important cautions: algorithms can entrench biases in their training data, can be opaque, and may be applied outside the populations on which they were tested.”
entrench (v) — to fix something, such as an attitude or a practice, so firmly that it becomes very hard to change. Example: "Years of unequal pay can entrench the gap between men and women at a company."
“Critics add important cautions: algorithms can entrench biases in their training data, can be opaque, and may be applied outside the populations on which they were tested.”
opaque (adj) — difficult to understand or see into; not clear about how it works. Example: "The rules for getting a refund were completely opaque, so I gave up."

Prediction and likelihood

When we predict, we choose how strongly to commit. Precise language shows how likely something is.

be likely / unlikely to + verb — The formula is likely to outperform the expert.
there is a chance / risk / possibility that… — There is a risk that the tool will entrench bias.
be bound to / be certain to + verb — An unstructured interview is bound to be a weak predictor.
be expected to / be set to + verb — The tool is expected to reduce errors.

Build it

Make one sentence for each one. Type it, say it, then reveal a model.

1. It is probable that the formula will outperform the expert. → The formula is …
The formula is likely to outperform the expert.
2. Perhaps the tool will entrench existing bias. → There is a risk …
There is a risk that the tool will entrench existing bias.
3. I am almost certain that an unstructured interview will be a weak predictor. → An unstructured interview is bound …
An unstructured interview is bound to be a weak predictor.
4. Hospital managers predict that the new score will reduce errors. → The new score is expected …
The new score is expected to reduce errors.

Trust the formula?

Five decisions. Choose whether to rely on the expert, the formula or both. Then give your prediction in one sentence with a likelihood structure, such as is likely to or there is a risk that. Then reveal the evidence or one idea.

1. A hospital wants to predict which patients are likely to be readmitted within a month. Who should make the call? be likely to
Structured risk scores usually do at least as well as clinical judgement at this kind of prediction, and they are consistent from case to case.
2. A chess player has to choose a move in a complex position with seconds on the clock. Who do you rely on? be likely to
Kahneman and Klein's two conditions are met: chess is a regular environment with rapid, clear feedback, so trained intuition can be trusted here.
3. A company must decide which of two candidates will perform better over the next five years. Who should decide? be unlikely to
Unstructured interviews are weak predictors of job performance. Structured interviews, with the same questions scored in the same way, and work samples predict considerably better.
4. A court plans to use an algorithm to guide bail decisions. It was trained on past data from a system with known bias. Who should decide? there is a risk that
Idea. Both, with caution. The algorithm may entrench the bias in its data, so it needs regular auditing, and a person must be able to review and overturn its advice.
5. A clinician has strong concerns about a client who scores low on a risk tool. Whose view should count?
Idea. Both. Feelings are information, but not a reason to set the tool aside. Write the concern down, look for what the tool may have missed, and review the cases where the two disagree.
