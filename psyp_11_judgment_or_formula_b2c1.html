Expert or Formula?

For seventy years, a simple formula has matched or beaten expert judgement in many fields. Why do professionals still resist it?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — Meehl's challenge

In 1954, the psychologist Paul Meehl published a short book that challenged his own profession. He compared clinical prediction, in which an expert integrates information by judgement, with statistical prediction, in which the same information is combined by a fixed rule. He reviewed twenty studies and found that, in almost all of them, the statistical method was at least as accurate as the clinical one.

The pattern has held remarkably well. A 2000 meta-analysis by William Grove and colleagues of 136 studies found that mechanical prediction was equal to or better than clinical prediction in about 94% of comparisons, and substantially better in roughly a third to a half of them. The studies spanned predictions of academic success, recidivism, medical diagnosis and response to treatment.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Meehl compare?

2. What did the 2000 meta-analysis find?

1. Clinical prediction, based on an expert's judgement, and statistical prediction, based on a fixed rule.

2. Mechanical prediction was equal to or better than clinical prediction in about 94% of 136 comparisons.

Discuss:
  • Why might this result be so hard for professionals to accept?

Part 2 — Why simple rules win

One reason is noise. Human judgement varies from day to day and from person to person, even when the information is identical. Experts have been shown to give different assessments of the same case when it appears twice, and to be affected by mood, fatigue and the order in which cases arrive. A formula, by contrast, is perfectly consistent.

Another is that experts tend to overweight vivid but weak cues and to be more confident than their accuracy justifies. Even simple 'improper' models, such as an equal-weight sum of a few predictors, have been shown by Robyn Dawes to do surprisingly well. People often feel that they add value by taking account of subtle factors that a formula ignores; but the evidence suggests that, on average, these exceptions add more error than accuracy.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is noise?

2. Why do experts do worse than they expect?

1. Unwanted variability: the same expert may judge the same case differently on different occasions.

2. They overweight vivid but weak cues and are more confident than their accuracy justifies.

Discuss:
  • When is it justified to override a formula?

Part 3 — When intuition deserves trust

The picture is not entirely one-sided. In 2009, Daniel Kahneman, a sceptic of intuition, and Gary Klein, who studies expert decision-making in settings such as firefighting, published a joint paper. They concluded that expert intuition can be trusted when two conditions are met: the environment must be regular enough to be predictable, and the expert must have had a long chance to learn the regularities through rapid, clear feedback. Chess, firefighting and anaesthesia meet these conditions. Predicting the long-term behaviour of a person does not.

The practical implication is not that professionals should be replaced. A structured tool, such as the Apgar score used in obstetrics for the past seventy years, can combine with judgement. Critics add important cautions: algorithms can entrench biases in their training data, can be opaque, and may be applied outside the populations on which they were tested. The question is therefore not 'expert or formula?' but 'which parts of the decision should be structured, and how will we check?'

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. When can intuition be trusted?

2. What are the cautions about algorithms?

1. In regular environments, where the expert has had rapid, clear feedback.

2. They can entrench bias, can be opaque and may be used on populations they were not tested on.

Discuss:
  • Which of your own decisions are made in a regular environment with fast feedback, and which are not?

Grammar — prediction and likelihood

Professionals have to say what they expect and how sure they are. English offers a wide range of structures for this, from tentative to strong.

A scale of likelihood:
• is bound to / is certain to / will definitely (very strong)
• is likely to / will probably / is expected to (strong)
• may well / could well (a real possibility)
• might / may / could (possible)
• is unlikely to / is not expected to (weak)
Other useful forms:
• The chances are that…, It seems probable that…, There is a good chance that…
• will have + past participle (future perfect), for a prediction about something that will already be finished: "By 2040, algorithms will have replaced many routine assessments."
• be set to / be due to / be about to for scheduled or imminent events.

Trust the formula?

Five cases. Say how far you would trust a formula and how far the expert. Then reveal.

1. Predicting which patients are likely to be re-admitted to hospital.
Formula helps. Structured risk scores usually do at least as well as clinical judgement, and they are consistent.
2. Choosing the best move in a chess position.
Expert. A regular environment and fast feedback make intuition reliable.
3. Deciding which of two job candidates will perform better in five years.
Structure helps. Unstructured interviews are weak predictors; structured assessments and work samples are stronger.
4. An algorithm is used to decide bail, trained on past data from a system with known bias.
Caution. It may entrench the bias; it needs to be audited, and humans must be able to review and appeal.
5. A clinician has strong feelings about a client who scores low on a risk tool.
Document and examine. Feelings are information, but they are not a reason to ignore a tool; compare the cases where the two disagree.
