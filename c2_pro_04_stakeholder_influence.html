C2 Level · Strategic Communication

Mapping Influence: Internal Politics & Buy-in

Getting an idea funded or approved within an organisation is a political process. It is not purely meritocratic. The best ideas fail without buy-in; mediocre ideas succeed with sufficient coalition support. Understanding internal influence networks is essential.

Every significant decision in large organisations requires alignment across multiple stakeholders with competing interests. The CFO cares about cost. The CTO cares about technical feasibility. HR cares about talent. The board cares about shareholder value and risk. Marketing cares about brand. Each has legitimate concerns; each possesses veto power.

Sophisticated leaders don't fight this system; they navigate it. They build coalitions before formal approval processes begin. They understand who genuinely matters versus who must simply be informed. They frame proposals to address each stakeholder's primary concern, not a generic version.

Consider: Recall an initiative you wanted implemented. Who were the formal decision-makers? Who actually held power? Where did resistance emerge? Looking back, how might you have built better coalition before the formal presentation?

Stakeholder Mapping & Influence Strategies

The Power/Interest Grid is a fundamental tool for understanding stakeholder dynamics. Plot each stakeholder on two axes: the power they hold to advance or block your initiative, and their interest in the outcome.

Vocabulary Cards

Buy-in Agreement and commitment; the genuine support of key stakeholders, not mere acceptance.
Coalition An alliance of parties with shared interests; the group formed to support an initiative.
Alignment Agreement on goals and direction; the state achieved when diverse stakeholders move together.
Advocacy Active support and promotion of a cause or position; stakeholders who advocate become your champions.
Escalation Moving a decision to higher authority; used when lower-level consensus cannot be achieved.
Vested Interest A stake in the outcome; the personal or professional benefit a stakeholder derives from success or failure.
Consensus General agreement; in sophisticated organisations, consensus is the goal, though unanimity is often impossible.
Mandate Authority to act; claims to mandate are often constructed through accumulated buy-in.

The Power/Interest Grid & Strategy

High Power / High Interest (Key Players): These are your primary targets. They can make or break your initiative and care deeply about the outcome. Strategy: Engage extensively, involve them in solution design, ensure they feel ownership.

High Power / Low Interest (Gatekeepers): They control resources but don't particularly care about your issue. Strategy: Keep them satisfied with minimal disruption to their domain. Make it easy to say yes.

Low Power / High Interest (Advocates): They care deeply but lack formal authority. Strategy: They become your internal champions. Equip them with language and arguments to advocate on your behalf within their networks.

Low Power / Low Interest (Observers): Inform them for completeness, but don't expend political capital here. They may become relevant later if dynamics shift.

Your strategy shifts based on where each stakeholder sits. A CFO is likely High Power / Low Interest on an initiative you care about deeply. Rather than presenting emotional appeal (they don't care), present financial impact and risk mitigation. A potential user of your product is High Interest / Low Power. Empower them to advocate amongst peers.

Building the Coalition: Budget Approval Campaign

Scenario: You've identified a strategic initiative requiring budget approval from three resistant stakeholders: the CFO (cost-conscious), the Chief Operating Officer (concerned about operational disruption), and the Board Member representing investor interests (focused on shareholder return). Each has legitimate concerns.

Your Campaign Checklist

Pre-approach: Research each stakeholder's priorities, recent decisions, known concerns, relationship networks
For the CFO: Present financial case with clear ROI, cost phasing, and risk of not investing
For the COO: Address operational burden with implementation timeline, resource planning, and minimal disruption strategy
For the Board Member: Frame initiative as competitive advantage, market positioning, and long-term shareholder value
Tailor messaging to each stakeholder's concern; don't present identical pitch three times
Identify potential advocates in each stakeholder's orbit; brief them before formal engagement
Schedule one-on-one conversations, not group meetings (prevents coalitions against you)
Listen more than pitch; ask questions about their concerns and incorporate feedback
10:00

Your Task

Conduct three 2–3 minute pitches—one to each stakeholder. Customise messaging based on their role and concerns. If possible, work with a peer to role-play each stakeholder; their objections will be more authentic. At the end, reflect on which approach was most effective and whether you built sufficient coalition to secure approval.

Reflection: Persuasion, Manipulation & Long-term Trust

There is a fine line between ethical persuasion and manipulation. Persuasion presents information and reasoning designed to change someone's mind; they remain free to reject it. Manipulation obscures information, misrepresents intent, or coerces without genuine choice.

Ethical Questions: Is tailoring your message to each stakeholder's concerns persuasion or manipulation? If you emphasise benefits to the CFO and benefits to the COO separately, is that deception? Or is it simply smart communication?

The distinction lies in honesty and respect for autonomy. Tailoring messaging is ethical if all versions are truthful. You can emphasise financial benefits to the CFO and operational benefits to the COO without lying about either. Both are true; you're simply highlighting what matters to each audience.

Manipulation would involve hiding downsides, misrepresenting timelines, or omitting relevant information. It would involve exploiting personal relationships or using pressure tactics that leave someone feeling bullied rather than persuaded.

Long-term organisational influence depends on reputation. A leader known for honesty can request and receive buy-in repeatedly. A leader known for distorting information will face scepticism forever, even when truthful. Building genuine coalitions—where stakeholders have actual ownership—creates lasting support beyond any single initiative.
