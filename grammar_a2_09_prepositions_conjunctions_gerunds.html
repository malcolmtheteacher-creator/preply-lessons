Look at Sam's answer again. Small words like "along" and "through" don't just say WHERE something is — they show HOW something moves. Why do you think English needs different words for that? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
Because "the park is near my flat" (a fixed position) and "I go through the park" (movement, with a start and an end) are two different jobs. You already know words like in, on, at for fixed positions. Today is about the other job: words that describe a journey or a route.
Today: words that describe getting somewhere and moving around — to, into, out of, through, along, across, past, over, up, down — so you can explain your journey and give someone directions.

Getting There: "to", "arrive in/at"

For saying where you're going, and where you've arrived.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "I cycle to work every day."
  • "We arrived in Lisbon at midnight."
  • "The bus finally arrived at the terminal."

Before you check: why "in" for Lisbon but "at" for the terminal?

Check the pattern CLICK
go / walk / cycle / drive + to + a place shows the direction you're moving in.
arrive in + a big place (a city, a country, a town). arrive at + a specific point (a station, an airport, a building). English never says "arrive to" — that combination doesn't exist.
go / walk / drive / travel + to + place
arrive in + city / country / town
arrive at + station / airport / building
get to + place  |  get home (no preposition before "home")

In, Out and Through

For entering a place, leaving a place, and going in one side and out the other.

Spot the pattern

Three things that happened:

  • "The cat jumped into the box."
  • "Water was pouring out of the broken pipe."
  • "We walked through the tunnel and came out near the station."

What's the direction of movement in each one? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
into = movement from outside to inside. out of = movement from inside to outside. through = in one side of an enclosed space, out the other.
into + place  (outside → inside)
out of + place  (inside → outside)
through + place  (in one side → out the other)
Careful: "She was walking in the park" (no movement into anywhere — she's already inside, walking around) is different from "She walked into the park" (she went from outside to inside) and "She walked through the park" (she entered one side and came out the other).

Round Town: along, past, across, over, up, down

The words you need to give someone directions around a town.

Spot the pattern

Directions to a café:

  • "Walk along this street until you reach the traffic lights."
  • "Go past the chemist's — it's the next building."
  • "Then walk across the square and you'll see it."

What's the difference between these three? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
along = following the length of something (a street, a river). past = going by one point without stopping there. across = from one side of a surface to the other (a square, a road, a field).
along + street / river  (follow its length)
past + a point  (go by it)
across + a surface  (one side to the other)

Three more directions, this time about height:

  • "Go up the hill and the castle is at the top."
  • "The path goes down towards the beach."
  • "You have to go over the bridge to reach the old town."
up + a higher point  |  down + a lower point  |  over + across the top of something with height (a bridge, a wall, a hill)

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: in is for something enclosed (a box, a room, a city) and on is for a surface (a table, a wall, a street). Both describe a fixed position — nothing is moving. Today's words are different: they describe movement to or through a place.
