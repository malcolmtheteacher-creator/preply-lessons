Part 1 — The man who optimised spaghetti sauce

The modern snack was not discovered; it was calculated — and much of the calculating traces to one cheerful American market researcher named Howard Moskowitz, a Harvard-trained experimental psychologist who, in the 1970s and 80s, turned taste into mathematics. Food companies hired him to answer a precise question: at what exact level of sweetness (or salt, or fat) does pleasure reach its maximum? Plot pleasure against sugar and you get a curve that rises, peaks and falls; the summit — enough to delight, not enough to satisfy — is the bliss point, and Moskowitz became its cartographer, optimising drinks, sauces and snacks product by product.

His most famous insight sounds like philosophy and reshaped supermarkets. Hired to fix a struggling spaghetti sauce, he ran vast taste tests and found that people didn't cluster around one ideal: they split into groups — plain, spicy, and a third of Americans who craved extra-chunky, a sauce that didn't exist. His conclusion became a business commandment: there is no perfect product; there are only perfect products. The company launched extra-chunky, took in hundreds of millions, and the age of the segmented supermarket began — thirty variants of everything, each tuned to a tribe's bliss point.

Around the same toolkit grew the rest of the engineering: the crunch calibrated to sound fresh, the melt that makes calories vanish before the brain counts them, the sugar-salt-fat trinity balanced so that no single taste ever tires. None of it was secret villainy — it was excellent product development, done by people optimising exactly what they were paid to optimise: how much you buy and how little you can stop. The one number nobody's bonus tracked was what it all added up to inside the customer. Measuring that would take a scientist of a different kind, and a locked ward.

Part 2 — The locked ward

For decades the argument stalled at correlation: ultra-processed diets travelled with weight gain and illness, but poorer people eat more UPF and carry more of every burden — maybe the food was innocent and poverty guilty. The category itself came from São Paulo, where the nutritionist Carlos Monteiro noticed something odd in Brazil's data: families were buying less sugar and oil as ingredients, yet obesity was climbing — because the sugar had moved out of the kitchen cupboard and into the factory formulations. His NOVA system (1 to 4, from fresh food to ultra-processed) gave the world a new axis: judge food not by its nutrients but by what has been done to it. Nutrition science split over it and remains split.

The experiment that moved the argument came in 2019, from Kevin Hall's team at the US National Institutes of Health, and its power was its cruelty to excuses. Twenty adults lived locked on a research ward for a month. For two weeks they were served ultra-processed meals; for two weeks, unprocessed ones — with the menus matched, meal by meal, for calories, sugar, fat, salt and fibre on offer, and the instruction simply: eat as much or as little as you like. If UPF were innocent, the fortnights should have matched. They did not. On the ultra-processed diet, the same people ate roughly five hundred calories more per day — faster, without noticing, while reporting the meals no more delicious — and gained weight; on the unprocessed diet they lost it. Something about the formulation itself, beyond its nutrients, was driving consumption. It was a small study and Hall himself is careful about how far it stretches — but it did what no supermarket correlation could: it showed causation, in a locked room, with the excuses controlled away.

Around it, the population numbers kept arriving: in Britain and the United States, over half of the average diet is now ultra-processed — for children, often more — and study after study links high-UPF diets to obesity, diabetes and heart disease. The industry answers, fairly, that the category is crude (it puts factory wholegrain bread near confectionery), that reformulation is underway, and that its products are safe, legal and demanded. Both things can be true: each product safe by itself; the diet they add up to, another matter.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Monteiro notice in Brazil, and what did NOVA change?

1. Families bought less sugar and oil yet obesity rose — the sugar had moved into factory products. NOVA reclassified food by degree of processing, not nutrients: a new axis that split nutrition science.

2. Twenty adults, locked ward, two matched fortnights (UPF vs unprocessed), free portions. On UPF the same people ate ~500 calories more daily — faster, unknowingly, no tastier — and gained weight. Small study, honestly hedged, but causal: the formulation itself drives eating.

Part 3 — The black octagons

The counter-attack's laboratory turned out to be Chile — by the mid-2010s one of the world's biggest consumers of sugary drinks, with childhood obesity to match. In 2016 it enacted the boldest food law anywhere: black octagonal warning labels — HIGH IN SUGAR, HIGH IN CALORIES — stamped on the front of qualifying packets; cartoon mascots banned from children's products (the tiger left the cereal box); junk advertising banned from children's television; the products expelled from schools. The industry predicted ruin and fought hard. The evaluations that followed found purchases of the worst-labelled drinks falling by double digits, children pressuring parents away from octagon products, and companies reformulating to escape the labels — arguably the point all along. A dozen countries copied the octagons; the idea that the label's job is to warn, not inform politely, had crossed an ocean.

Elsewhere the fight found other fronts: sugar taxes (Britain's levy quietly reformulated the nation's soft drinks before collecting much tax at all), school-food rules, and a rising scientific and political argument — pushed by campaigners and bestselling doctors — for treating the UPF pattern the way a previous generation learned to treat another engineered product whose industry also once had a bliss point of sorts. That comparison — to tobacco — is contested and probably unfair in places; food, unlike cigarettes, is necessary, and demonising dinner helps nobody. But one echo is precise: in both stories, the industry knew its optimisation worked on human biology, measured it carefully, and left the adding-up to someone else.

The story ends, as this dashboard's stories tend to, at your own table — but with the guilt pointed accurately. If you cannot eat one crisp, that is not weak character meeting strong snacks; it is amateur biology meeting professional engineering, the attention economy's asymmetry (from this dashboard's phone lesson) restaged in the kitchen. The defences rhyme too: not willpower but friction and defaults — what enters the house, what sits at eye level, what the label is allowed to whisper versus shout. And the closing question is the same one every Big Conversation here has ended on: in the contest for your appetite, your evening and your attention, somebody is doing the optimising. Is anybody doing it on your side — and could it be you?

1. Black warning octagons on packets, cartoon mascots and child-targeted ads banned, products out of schools (2016). Results: double-digit falls in worst-labelled drink purchases, children policing parents, mass reformulation to dodge the labels — and a dozen countries copying it.

2. Fair: an industry that measured its products' effect on biology and optimised it, leaving the consequences unaccounted. Unfair: food is necessary, most eating is fine, and demonising dinner is neither science nor help.

Grammar — quantity: much, many, little, few & friends

Food talk is quantity talk, and English splits the world into what you can count (calories, snacks) and what you can't (sugar, time). Every quantity word picks a side.

The two teams:
• Uncountable → much, (a) little, less, a great deal of, how much: "too much sugar," "very little fibre," "less salt."
• Countable plural → many, (a) few, fewer, how many: "too many snacks," "fewer additives."
• Both teams: a lot of, lots of, plenty of, some, any, enough, no.
Watch the food traps: bread, rice, meat, fruit, advice, information — uncountable, however much of them there is.
The meaning-changing article:
• little / few = almost none, negative: "There is little real food in it." · "Few children escape the marketing."
• a little / a few = some, positive: "a little olive oil," "a few simple ingredients."
One letter — opposite mood.

Formality ladder: in statements, plain much/many sound formal ("There is much debate"); everyday English prefers a lot of — but much/many rule questions and negatives: "How much…? / not many…"

Going deeper

A note on the record: Moskowitz's story is documented in Michael Moss's Pulitzer-winning reporting; the Hall trial is real, small, and honestly hedged by its own author; the UPF debate is live science with serious people on both sides — and this lesson deliberately keeps both on stage.

What the story actually teaches. First, the optimisation lesson — this dashboard's recurring spine. Nobody in this story is a villain: the taste scientists optimised pleasure, the companies optimised sales, the shoppers optimised budgets and time — and the sum optimised nobody's health. When every actor locally optimises and the total misfires, the fix is never better individuals; it is changed defaults, which is exactly what Chile understood: don't lecture the shopper, re-engineer the shelf.

Second, the measurement lesson. For decades the correlation excuse held ("it's really poverty / lifestyle / choice") — until one small, brutal experiment closed the exits by controlling them. Hold that method: whenever a debate loops forever on confounded data, ask what the locked-ward version of the question would look like, and whether anyone has run it. Often the argument persists precisely because nobody wants it run.

Third, the fairness lesson, stated plainly. Cheap engineered calories are dense in food deserts and thin in farmers' markets; time to cook from scratch is a luxury unevenly distributed; and lecturing tired, broke people about wholefoods is the nutritional version of blaming willpower for the attention economy. The honest policy question — Chile's question — is not "why do people choose badly?" but "who designed the choices, and who is allowed to redesign them?"
