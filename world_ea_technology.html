The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Some factories use cutting-edge robots.
cutting-edge (adj) — using the newest and most advanced ideas. Example: “Some factories use cutting-edge robots.”
2. The first bullet train opened in Japan in 1964.
bullet train (n) — a very fast passenger train. Example: “The first bullet train opened in Japan in 1964.”
3. China became a nearly cashless society in many cities.
cashless (adj) — using cards or phones instead of money. Example: “China became a nearly cashless society in many cities.”
4. Japan has millions of vending machines.
vending machine (n) — a machine that sells drinks or snacks when you put in money. Example: “Japan has millions of vending machines.”
5. Robotics is one of South Korea's strongest industries.
robotics (n) — the science of designing and using robots. Example: “Robotics is one of South Korea's strongest industries.”
6. An ageing population means fewer young workers.
ageing (adj) — having a growing share of older people. Example: “An ageing population means fewer young workers.”
7. China has invested heavily in high-speed rail infrastructure.
infrastructure (n) — the basic systems a country needs, such as roads, railways and networks. Example: “China has invested heavily in high-speed rail infrastructure.”
8. Many shops were slow to adopt card payments.
adopt (v) — to start to use something new. Example: “Many shops were slow to adopt card payments.”

The stereotype: “East Asia is a high-tech world of robots, bullet trains and neon — everyone lives in the future.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Really high-tech

Japan's first Shinkansen bullet train opened in 1964, and its average delay is often well under a minute. China has built the world's largest high-speed rail network, more than 45,000 kilometres long. South Korea has among the fastest internet connections in the world and the highest density of industrial robots, about 1,000 for every 10,000 factory workers. In China, mobile payment through phone apps is used for everything from taxis to street food.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. When did Japan's first bullet train open?

2. Which country has the highest density of industrial robots?

1. In 1964.

2. South Korea.

Discuss:
  • Which of these achievements impresses you most?

Part 2 — But not everything

Japan is still a country where cash is widely used, and fax machines survive in many offices. In 2022 Japan's digital minister announced a campaign to end the use of floppy disks in government. Vending machines are everywhere, but they often take only coins and notes. For visitors, the mix can be surprising: a bullet train that is never late, and a restaurant that only accepts cash.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Japan's digital minister announce in 2022?

2. What do many Japanese vending machines still take?

1. A campaign to end the use of floppy disks in government.

2. Coins and notes.

Discuss:
  • Why might a society keep old technology even when new technology exists?

Part 3 — Behind the neon

Not everyone lives in a futuristic city. In rural areas populations are ageing and some villages are shrinking. Robots are mostly found in factories, not homes. And technology brings its own questions: long working hours, online privacy and what happens to jobs. The “future” is something that is built by choices — government investment, company strategy and what people are willing to change.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where are most robots found?

2. What is happening to some rural villages?

1. In factories.

2. They are ageing and shrinking.

Discuss:
  • What are the best and worst effects of new technology in the place where you live?

Grammar — The Passive & Reporting Passives

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
The passive moves the focus to the thing that is done:
• be + past participle: “The first bullet train was opened in 1964.”
• reporting passives: “It is said that Japan has millions of vending machines.” / “Korea is believed to have the most robots per worker.”
• add by only when the agent matters: “…built by a government agency.”

1. Choose the right form

Complete each sentence about this topic.

1. The first bullet train (open) in 1964.

2. It (say) that Japan has millions of vending machines.

3. Korea (believe) to have the most robots per worker.

1. was opened

2. is said

3. is believed

2. Now try these

1. China's high-speed rail network (build) over twenty years.

2. Cash still (use) widely in Japan.

3. Many robots (find) in factories, not in homes.

1. has been built (or was built)

2. is still used

3. are found

Going deeper

The picture of East Asia as “the future” appeared strongly in the 1980s, when Japanese electronics and later films such as Blade Runner made neon cities a symbol of the next century. It is a flattering image, but it hides the ordinary parts of life — and the fact that every country has some areas that are cutting-edge and others that are not. Rural Japan, rural China and cities that are full of high technology exist side by side.

Worth noticing: Passives: be + past participle; reporting passives: It is said / believed that… and X is said / believed to…; the agent is added with by only when it is important.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Carry a little cash and a card when you travel in Japan, and a phone with a payment app set up if you go to China: both systems work best if you plan for them. Don't assume the technology is the same in every city or village.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Which is more important for a country: leading in technology or keeping traditions?
  • Are you happy to depend on a phone for almost everything?
  • What changes will robots bring to jobs where you live?
