Listen in on this conversation

Jamie: Did you talk to the landlord?

Alex: Yeah, I called him this morning. He said the boiler would be fixed by Friday, and he said he'd already ordered the part.

Look again at what Alex says the landlord told him. The landlord's actual words were probably "I'll fix it by Friday" and "I've already ordered the part." What happened to will and have already ordered? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
will moved back to would, and have already ordered moved back to had already ordered. Every verb takes one step further into the past when you report it later — that's called backshift, and it happens with continuous forms, perfect forms, and modals like will, can and must too.
Today: report what people said using the full range of backshifts — was/were + -ing, had + done, would, could, had to — plus the time and place words that shift with them (today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, here → there) — so you can retell conversations, plans and instructions accurately.

Telling the Story Later

What was happening, and what had already finished, when someone spoke.

Spot the pattern

Three things people actually said, and how they got reported later:

  • "I'm cooking dinner," Sam said. → Sam said he was cooking dinner.
  • "The train is arriving," the announcer said. → The announcer said the train was arriving.
  • "I already ate breakfast," Priya said. → Priya said she had already eaten breakfast.

Before you check: what happens to "am/is/are + -ing"? What happens to a past simple verb like "ate"?

Check the pattern CLICK
Present continuous (am/is/are + -ing) moves back to was/were + -ing.
Past simple (ate) moves back to had + past participle — this is called the past perfect, and it shows the action finished before the person spoke.
was / were + -ing  (present continuous → past continuous)
had + past participle  (past simple → past perfect)

Promises, Ability & Rules

Reporting offers, promises, ability and obligation — will, can and must.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday examples:

  • "I'll fix it tomorrow," the mechanic said. → The mechanic said he would fix it the next day.
  • "I can meet you at six," Nina said. → Nina said she could meet me at six.
  • "You must wear a helmet," the instructor said. → The instructor said we had to wear a helmet.

What happens to will, can and must? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
will → would, can → could, and must → had to (for an obligation or rule). The verb after the modal never changes its form — it stays in the base form: fix, meet, wear.
will → would
can → could
must → had to

"I'll fix it tomorrow."

Today Becomes That Day

Time and place words shift too — and sometimes the verb itself is allowed to stay exactly as it was.

Spot the pattern

  • "I'll meet you here tomorrow," Ben said. → Ben said he would meet me there the next day.
  • "I'm free today," Ella said. → Ella said she was free that day.
  • "My brother lives in Berlin," Omar said — and it's still true right now. → Omar said his brother lives (or lived) in Berlin.

What happened to "here", "today" and "tomorrow"? And why does the third sentence allow two different answers?

Check the pattern CLICK
today → that day, tomorrow → the next day, here → there — because "today" and "here" only make sense at the moment someone said them.
But if the fact is still true when you report it (a brother who still lives in Berlin), English lets you keep the present tense OR backshift — both are correct.
today → that day
tomorrow → the next day
here → there
Still true right now, or a general fact? You can keep the present tense instead of backshifting: "She said she lives in Berlin" and "She said she lived in Berlin" are both fine.
