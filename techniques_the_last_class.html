The Last Class

Alphonse Daudet's story of a boy who never wanted to study — until the morning he found out it was the last lesson.

Today: you'll tell the story of the most famous school lesson in French literature — and you'll learn the exact language of regret: I wish I had studied, if only I'd known.
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • Is there something you're putting off learning because you assume there's plenty of time? What is it?
  • How many languages are spoken where you live — including dialects and regional languages? Are they all treated equally?
  • What would actually be lost if a language stopped being spoken?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four short activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. Normally at that hour you could hear desks opening and closing, voices repeating lessons, and the teacher's ruler tapping the table. That morning the room was completely still.
still (adj) — silent and without movement. "The house was still." (Different from the adverb still = up to now.)
2. He wore the green coat and the black silk cap that he only ever put on for prize days and official inspections. It was clearly a solemn occasion.
solemn (adj) — serious, formal, without any lightness. "A solemn promise." Adverb: solemnly.
3. The villagers came in quietly, sat on the back benches with their hats on their knees, and looked around the schoolroom as if they were seeing it for the last time. Their faces were full of regret.
regret (n/v) — the feeling of being sorry about something you did or failed to do. "My one regret is that I didn't start earlier."

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: notice · participle · trumpet. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means an official announcement put up in public for everyone to read?
a notice (n) — "A crowd was reading the notice on the wall." Also: to give notice, to take notice of.
2. Which word means the form of a verb used in tenses like "I have written" or "it was written"?
a participle (n) — the past participle (written, gone, kept) and the present participle (writing, going). Franz has not learned his.
3. Which word means a brass instrument you blow, used by soldiers to give signals?
a trumpet (n) — "The trumpets sounded in the square outside."

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Franz was tempted to spend the morning outside instead of going to school; the fields were warm and the birds were loud. Tempted means…
  (a) forced   (b) attracted to doing something you probably shouldn't   (c) frightened
(b) — tempted (adj) = pulled towards something you know you should resist. Noun: temptation.
2. The teacher said the villagers had put off learning to read for forty years, always meaning to start next winter. To put something off is to…
  (a) delay it until later   (b) refuse it completely   (c) finish it quickly
(a) — to put off = to postpone. Noun for the habit: procrastination. "Stop putting it off."

D · Word building & word partners

1. You know mother tongue. Which of these means the same: native language · second language · foreign language? And what do we call a language spoken by only a small group in a country?
native language = mother tongue. A language spoken by a small group is a minority language. Related: fluent in, to lose a language, to pass a language on.
2. One verb fits all three: to ___ a language alive · to ___ a promise · to ___ something in mind. And the phrase for holding on tightly to something important: to ___ fast to it.
keep — keep a language alive, keep a promise, keep something in mind, keep fast to something (hold on to it firmly).

Now use them — out loud

Talk it through before we read:
  • Using notice, solemn, regret and put off, predict: why might a whole village come to sit at the back of a children's classroom?
  • Which of today's words belong to school, and which to a serious public occasion? Sort them out loud.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Late for school

Franz was very late that morning, and he was afraid of being scolded, particularly because Monsieur Hamel had said he would ask them about participles and Franz did not know the first word about them. It was such a warm, bright morning. He could hear birds in the wood, and out in the field behind the sawmill the soldiers were drilling. All of it was far more tempting than grammar. But he ran on.

As he passed the town hall there was a small crowd in front of the notice board. For two years every piece of bad news had arrived there. Franz did not stop; he thought only about getting to his desk without being seen. The blacksmith called something after him about not needing to hurry. Franz thought he was making fun of him, and ran into the schoolyard out of breath.

Usually at the beginning of school there was a noise you could hear in the street: desks opening and closing, lessons repeated out loud, the teacher's ruler tapping the table. Franz had been counting on the noise to get to his seat unnoticed. But that day everything was still, as still as a Sunday morning. Through the window he could see his classmates already in their places, and Monsieur Hamel walking up and down with the iron ruler under his arm. Franz had to open the door and cross the silent room. He went red to the ears. And to his astonishment, his teacher looked at him without any anger at all and said gently, "Go to your place quickly, little Franz. We were beginning without you."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is Franz afraid of going to school that morning?

2. Name three things that are unusual when he arrives.

1. He is late, and the teacher has promised to test them on participles, which Franz has not learned at all.

2. Any three: the crowd at the notice board, the total silence instead of the usual noise, the class already seated, the teacher's fine green coat and silk cap, and the fact that he is not scolded for being late.

Discuss:
  • Everything in the first paragraph is ordinary — birds, sunshine, homework not done. Why does the story spend so long on an ordinary morning?

Part 2 — The announcement

Then Franz noticed two more strange things. His teacher was wearing the beautiful green coat and the little black silk cap that he only ever wore on inspection days and prize days. And at the back of the room, on the benches that were always empty, the village people were sitting: old Hauser with his ancient reading book open on his knees, the former mayor, the old postman, and several others. They all looked sad.

Monsieur Hamel went up to his chair and said, in the same gentle serious voice, that this was the last lesson in French he would give them. The order had come that from tomorrow only the new language would be taught in the schools of the district. The new master arrived the following day. This, he said, was their last French lesson, and he begged them to pay close attention.

Franz felt the words go through him. That was what they had been reading on the notice board. His last French lesson — and he could hardly write. He thought of all the mornings he had spent hunting birds' nests and sliding on the river instead of studying, and suddenly his books, which had been so heavy and so boring an hour ago, felt like old friends he could not bear to part with. When his name was called for the participles he stood up and could not get through the first line. He stood there holding the desk, with his heart beating, not daring to look up.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is sitting at the back of the classroom, and why?

2. What does Monsieur Hamel announce, and how does Franz react?

1. The village people — old Hauser with his old reading book, the former mayor, the postman and others — who have come to be present at the last French lesson.

2. That this is the final French lesson; from tomorrow only the new language will be taught. Franz is stricken with regret for all the time he wasted, and cannot recite his participles when called.

Discuss:
  • His schoolbooks change from a burden into old friends in a single moment. What actually changed — the books, or Franz?

Part 3 — "Hold fast to your language"

Monsieur Hamel did not scold him. He said that Franz was punished enough already, and that this was how it always went: people say to themselves that there is plenty of time, that they will learn tomorrow — and then the day comes when it is too late. He blamed himself too. How often, he said, had he sent a pupil to water his garden instead of studying, or given a holiday when he wanted to go fishing?

Then he talked about the language itself. It was the clearest and the most beautiful language in the world, he said, and they must guard it among themselves and never forget it, because when a people are held down, as long as they hold fast to their language they hold the key to their prison. He opened a grammar book and read to them, and Franz was astonished to find how easily he understood every word. Then came a writing lesson: new sheets had been prepared, and on each of them, in a beautiful round hand, were the words of their country's name, over and over. The whole room bent over the paper, and there was no sound but the scratching of pens. Old Hauser at the back spelled the letters out with the little children, and his voice shook so much that everyone wanted to laugh and cry at once.

At noon the church bell rang, and the trumpets of the soldiers returning from drill sounded under the windows. Monsieur Hamel stood up in his chair, very pale. He began, "My friends, I —" and something stopped his voice. He turned to the blackboard, took a piece of chalk, and wrote in the largest letters he could, two words about his country. Then he stood with his head against the wall, and without speaking he made a sign with his hand: that is all — you may go.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Monsieur Hamel blame himself for?

2. What happens at noon, and how does the lesson end?

1. For sending pupils to water his garden instead of teaching them, and for giving holidays when he wanted to go fishing — he says the fault is shared by everyone, including himself.

2. The church bell rings and trumpets sound outside. He tries to speak, cannot finish his sentence, writes two words about his country on the board in the largest letters he can, and dismisses the class with a gesture.

Discuss:
  • He says that a people who keep their language keep the key to their prison. What does that actually mean — and do you think it is true?

Grammar — wish & regret

This is a story about wishing you had done something earlier. English has a precise set of forms for that feeling, and they are among the most useful sentences you will ever learn to say.

Three wishes, three times:
• wish + past simple — about now: "I wish I knew the answer." (I don't.) · "I wish I were taller."
• wish + past perfect — about the past, the real regret: "I wish I had started earlier." (I didn't.)
• wish + would — about other people's annoying habits: "I wish he would stop interrupting." (Not used for yourself.)

The shape is always one step further back in time than the thing you are talking about. That backwards step is what makes it unreal.
The other ways to say it:
• If only — stronger and more emotional, same grammar: "If only I'd known."
• should have + past participle — self-criticism: "I should have paid attention."
• regret + -ing: "I regret not asking her." · It's too late to…: "It's too late to change it now."

The classic mistake: ✗ I wish I would have studied. ✓ I wish I had studied.

1. Now or the past?

Put the verb in brackets into the correct form after wish.

1. Franz wishes he (learn) his participles the week before.

2. He wishes he (can) read the grammar book as easily every day.

3. Monsieur Hamel wishes he (not / give) so many unnecessary holidays over the years.

1. had learned (a past regret)

2. could (about now — "can" steps back to "could")

3. hadn't given (past regret, negative)

2. Say it another way

Rewrite each regret using the word in brackets. Write only the missing words.

1. I didn't pay attention in class. → I wish attention in class. (wish)

2. We didn't know it was the last lesson. → If only . (only)

3. He didn't study when he had the chance. → He when he had the chance. (should)

1. I had paid   2. we had known   3. should have studied

Three different structures, one feeling — and all of them step back into the past perfect.

3. The village looks back — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the correct form, then read the whole paragraph aloud.

Sitting at the back of the classroom, the old men wished they (come) to school more often when they were young. If only they (understand) how quickly things can be taken away. Franz wishes he (not / waste) so many mornings hunting birds' nests; he knows now that he (should / listen) to his teacher. And Monsieur Hamel, who says the fault belongs to everyone, wishes he (be) a stricter man than he was. It is too late (change) any of it — but not too late to begin.

had come · had understood · hadn't wasted · should have listened · had been · to change

Every one of them steps back into the past perfect. That step is the grammar of regret.

Hold on to these forms — in the Speak tab you'll use them about your own learning, where they are needed constantly.

Going deeper

Daudet published this in 1873, shortly after the region of Alsace changed hands at the end of a war between France and Prussia, and schools there were required to switch to German. It became one of the most widely read school stories in Europe, and generations of children learned it by heart. It is also, inevitably, a story told from one side of a border where many people had spoken both languages for centuries — which is part of what makes it worth arguing about.

The heart of it. Notice how small the story keeps everything. There is no battle in it, no politics explained, nobody making speeches about nations. There is a boy who has not done his homework, a teacher in his good coat, some old men on the back benches, and a lesson that ends at twelve o'clock. The enormous thing happens entirely offstage, and reaches us as a notice on a wall and a trumpet in the square. And notice the teacher's most surprising move: when he finally speaks about what has been lost, he blames himself and the parents as much as anyone — the fishing trips, the garden, the years of "there's always next winter". Then the story does something very brave for a piece of patriotic writing: it gives its hero nothing. Franz does not save anything. He simply understands, at the exact moment when understanding is no longer any use, what he has been given and has not taken.

Talk it through:
  • The teacher says people always assume there is more time. Where in your own life is that happening right now?
  • Why does Daudet make the teacher blame himself as well? What would be lost if he only blamed the soldiers?
  • A language is a key to a prison, he says. Is that a fair description of what a language does for a people — or is it too romantic?
  • This story was written from one side of a contested border. If a boy on the other side wrote his own version, what might be in it?

Speak

This is the heart of the lesson — do all of it out loud. The timer is there to keep you going: fill the time with detail rather than racing through it.

Useful language: lean on today's forms — I wish I had…, if only I'd known…, I should have…, I regret not…, it's too late to…. For talking about learning: "What helped me most was…", "Looking back…", "The thing nobody tells you is…".

Task B · Should a school teach in a minority language? (4 minutes)

Take a side and argue it properly. Some say children should be taught in the main national language so that they can compete for jobs anywhere; others say a school that drops the local language helps to kill it. Give three reasons, answer the strongest objection to your view, and propose what you think a fair rule would actually look like.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Franz understands the value of his lessons at the exact moment they end. Is that always how understanding works?
  2. The teacher blames himself as much as anyone else. Does that make him more convincing, or is he letting the real culprits off?
  3. You are studying a language right now, by choice, in a lesson nobody is taking away from you. Does this story change anything about how that feels?
