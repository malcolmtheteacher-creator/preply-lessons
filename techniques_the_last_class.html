The Last Class

Alphonse Daudet's story of a boy who never studied, and the morning the lesson ran out.

Today: you'll read the most famous school lesson in French literature — and you'll command the full grammar of regret: I wish I had, if only I'd known, I should have, it's too late to.
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. He wore the green coat and the black silk cap that came out only for prize days and official inspections, and he stood at the front without moving. The whole occasion was unmistakably solemn.
solemn (adj) — serious, formal and weighted with significance. A solemn promise · a solemn silence. Adverb: solemnly.
2. The villagers filed in and took the empty benches at the back with their hats on their knees, looking round the schoolroom as though seeing it for the last time — which they were. They came in homage.
homage (n) — public respect shown to something or someone. To pay homage to. A slightly formal, ceremonial word — exactly what the scene requires.
3. For two years every announcement that ruined somebody's life had appeared on that board, so a crowd in front of it was never a good sign. It was the town's notice board.
a notice (n) — an official public announcement. Also: to give notice (formal warning), at short notice, to take notice of.

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: complacency · mother tongue · reproach. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means the comfortable assumption that everything will continue as it is, so nothing need be done?
complacency (n) — self-satisfied inaction. Adjective: complacent. It is precisely what the teacher accuses the whole village of, himself included.
2. Which phrase means the first language you learned as a child, at home?
mother tongue — also native language, first language. Related: a minority language, to pass a language on, language loss.
3. Which word means an expression of blame or disappointment, often gentle?
reproach (n/v) — "a note of reproach in his voice". Useful phrase: beyond reproach = faultless. The teacher's reproach is aimed mostly at himself.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. He was sorely tempted to spend the morning in the fields instead of at his desk. Tempted means…
  (a) compelled   (b) attracted towards something you know you should resist   (c) forbidden
(b) — tempted (adj). Noun: temptation. Note the construction: tempted to do something.
2. The village had put off learning to read for forty years, always intending to begin next winter. To put something off is to…
  (a) refuse it outright   (b) postpone it repeatedly   (c) finish it in a hurry
(b) — to put off = to postpone. The formal noun for the habit: procrastination. Also: to defer, to shelve.

D · Word building & collocation

1. The teacher says that a people who hold on to their language hold the key to their prison. Complete the collocations: to ___ a language alive · to ___ a language on to your children · to ___ fast to something.
keep a language alive · pass a language on · hold fast to something. Note also language death, language revival, a dying language.
2. You know to regret. Give the adjective describing something that makes you sorry, and complete the two patterns: I regret ___ (say) that · I regret ___ (inform) you that… — why are they different?
regrettable (adj). I regret saying that = I am sorry about a past action. I regret to inform you = a formal announcement of bad news now. Gerund looks back; infinitive looks forward.

Talk it through before we read:

  • Sort today's words into the language of ceremony and the language of self-blame. Which does reproach belong to?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — An ordinary morning, late

Franz was very late that morning, and he had good reason to be afraid of the consequences, since Monsieur Hamel had promised to examine them on participles and he did not know the first word about them. It was a bright warm morning. Birds were audible at the edge of the wood, and in the field behind the sawmill the soldiers were drilling. All of it was considerably more attractive than the rules governing the agreement of the past participle, and for a moment he considered spending the day out of doors. Then he ran on.

Passing the town hall he saw a small crowd gathered at the notice board. For two years every piece of bad news had reached the village from that board, and he had learned to associate a crowd in front of it with something disagreeable. He did not stop. The blacksmith, watching him hurry past, called out that there was no need to run so fast — he would get there in plenty of time. Franz assumed he was being mocked, and arrived in the schoolyard out of breath.

Ordinarily the beginning of school produced a noise audible from the street: desks opened and shut, lessons repeated in unison, and the master's iron ruler rapping the table for order. Franz had been counting on that noise to cover his entrance. Instead there was a stillness like a Sunday morning. Through the window he could see the class already in their places and Monsieur Hamel walking up and down with the ruler under his arm. There was nothing for it but to open the door and cross the silent room, scarlet to the ears. And his master, instead of the expected storm, looked at him without any anger at all and said gently that he should take his place quickly, as they had been about to begin without him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is Franz afraid of arriving at school, and what nearly stops him going?

2. List the details that tell Franz something is wrong before anyone explains anything.

1. He is late and has not learned the participles he is to be examined on. The warm morning, the birds and the soldiers drilling nearly tempt him to stay outside.

2. The crowd at the notice board; the blacksmith's odd remark; the complete silence instead of the usual noise; the class already seated; and his master's gentleness instead of anger.

Discuss:
  • Daudet spends the whole first part on an ordinary morning and an unprepared boy. Why build the story that way rather than opening with the announcement?

Part 2 — The announcement, and what it costs

Two further oddities presented themselves. Monsieur Hamel was wearing the fine green coat and the small black silk cap reserved for inspection days and prize-givings. And on the benches at the back, always empty, the village had assembled: old Hauser with an ancient spelling book open on his knees, the former mayor, the old postman, and others besides. Every one of them looked stricken.

Monsieur Hamel took his chair and said, in the same gentle voice, that this was the last lesson in French he would give them; the order had come that only the new language was to be taught in the schools of the district; the new master arrived tomorrow. He asked them to give him their full attention.

Franz felt the words go through him. So that was what they had been reading on the notice board. His last French lesson — and he could barely write. Every wasted morning came back to him at once: the birds' nests, the ice on the river, the mornings he had preferred to grammar. His books, which an hour earlier had been a nuisance to be carried, became suddenly things he could not bear to part with. When his name was called for the participles he stood up and could not get through the opening line, and stood there gripping the edge of the desk, not daring to raise his head, his heart going hard.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is sitting at the back, and what has brought them?

2. How does Franz's relationship with his own schoolbooks change, and at what moment?

1. The villagers — old Hauser with an old spelling book, the former mayor, the postman and others — who have come to sit in on the final French lesson as an act of homage.

2. The instant he learns there will be no more lessons. Books he had resented carrying become objects he cannot bear to give up.

Discuss:
  • Nothing about the books has altered — only their availability. What does the story suggest about how we assign value to what we have?

Part 3 — "The key to their prison"

Monsieur Hamel did not scold him. He said that Franz was punished enough, and that this was how it always went: we tell ourselves there is time in abundance, that we shall learn tomorrow, and then the day arrives when it is too late. Nor did he exempt himself. How often, he asked, had he sent a pupil to water his garden instead of setting him to work, or given a holiday because he wanted to go fishing? The complacency, he said, belonged to all of them.

Then he spoke about the language itself — the clearest and most beautiful in the world, he called it — and told them that they must guard it among themselves and never let it go, because a people held down keeps the key to its own prison for as long as it keeps its language. He read to them from the grammar, and Franz was astonished to find that he understood every word: it had never seemed easy before, and he could not tell whether that was the reading or the attention. Then came the writing lesson. New copies had been prepared, and on each sheet, in a beautiful round hand, the name of their country was written over and over. The room bent over the paper and there was no sound but the scratching of pens; at the back, old Hauser spelled the letters out with the smallest children, his voice shaking so badly that everybody wanted to laugh and cry at once.

At noon the church bell rang, and under the windows the trumpets of the soldiers returning from drill answered it. Monsieur Hamel stood up in his chair, very pale. He began, "My friends, I —" and something took hold of his voice and stopped it. He turned to the blackboard, took a piece of chalk, and wrote in the largest letters he could manage two words about his country. Then he stood with his head against the wall, and without speaking made a sign with his hand: that is all — you may go.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who does Monsieur Hamel blame, and how widely does he spread the blame?

2. What happens at noon, and how does the lesson end?

1. Everyone: the parents who sent children to work, the children who postponed, and himself — for the garden, the fishing trips, the holidays he granted for his own convenience.

2. The church bell rings and the soldiers' trumpets sound outside. He tries to address the class, cannot finish the sentence, writes two words about his country on the board in the largest letters he can, and dismisses them with a gesture.

Discuss:
  • "As long as a people hold fast to their language, they hold the key to their prison." Is that a serious claim about power, or a consoling idea for the defeated?

Grammar — regret & the unreal past

This story is one long act of hindsight, and English has an unusually rich set of structures for it. They share one mechanism: to talk about something unreal, the verb steps one tense further back than the time you mean.

wish — three times, three forms:
• wish + past simple → about now: "I wish I knew the answer." (I don't)
• wish + past perfect → about the past: "I wish I had started earlier." (I didn't)
• wish + would → about other people's habits, with irritation: "I wish he would stop apologising." Not used about yourself.

if only takes the same grammar with more feeling: "If only I'd known."
The rest of the system:
• should have + past participle — self-criticism or criticism of others: "They should have started teaching it years ago."
• needn't have + past participle — it was done and it was unnecessary: "You needn't have waited." Compare didn't need to = it wasn't necessary, so it probably wasn't done.
• regret + -ing (looking back) vs regret + to-infinitive (formal bad news now): "I regret leaving school early." / "We regret to inform you…"
• It's too late to… · There's no point (in) …-ing · I'd give anything to have…

The classic error: ✗ "I wish I would have studied." ✓ "I wish I had studied."

1. Now or the past?

Put the verb in brackets into the correct form after wish or if only.

1. Franz wishes he (learn) his participles when he had the chance.

2. If only the villagers (understand) how little time was left.

3. He wishes he (can) read the grammar as easily every day of the year.

1. had learned (past regret)   2. had understood (past regret)   3. could (about now — "can" steps back to "could")

2. Say it three ways

Express the same regret using the structure indicated. Write only the missing words.

1. He did not pay attention in class. → He attention in class. (should)

2. The village postponed learning to read for forty years. → If only they it for so long. (if only)

3. Monsieur Hamel gave holidays he should not have given, and now he is sorry about it. → He so many holidays. (regret + -ing)

1. should have paid   2. hadn't postponed   3. regrets giving

Three structures, one feeling — and note that only the third does not step back into the past perfect.

3. The village looks back — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the correct form, then read the whole paragraph aloud.

Sitting at the back with their old spelling books, the villagers wished they (come) to school more often as boys. If only they (realise) how quickly a thing can be taken away. Franz wishes he (not / waste) so many mornings by the river; he knows he (should / listen) years ago. Monsieur Hamel, who refuses to exempt himself, wishes he (be) a stricter man, and regrets (send) his pupils to water the garden. It is too late (undo) any of it — though not, he tells them, too late to begin.

had come · had realised · hadn't wasted · should have listened · had been · sending · to undo

Every unreal past steps back into the past perfect; only "regret + -ing" and "too late to + infinitive" behave differently.

Going deeper

Daudet published this in 1873, two years after the Franco-Prussian War transferred Alsace and part of Lorraine to Germany and German became the language of instruction there. It was reprinted in school anthologies for a century, learned by heart by generations of French children, and is one of the most effective pieces of patriotic writing ever produced. It should also be read with one eye open: it is told from one side of a border where a great many people had spoken German or Alsatian at home for centuries, and where "the mother tongue" was never a simple matter.

The craft. Notice the scale Daudet works at. There is no battle, no politician, no explanation of the war — the enormous event reaches us as a crowd round a notice board and a trumpet under a window. Everything else is furniture: a green coat, an old spelling book, the scratching of pens. Notice too the story's most surprising move. Given a chance to blame the conquerors, Monsieur Hamel blames the village and himself — the parents who preferred wages to schooling, the master who wanted an afternoon's fishing. That refusal to take the easy target is what stops the story becoming a poster. And notice the ending: the teacher is not given a speech. He is given a broken sentence, a piece of chalk, and a gesture. Daudet understood that the unfinished sentence would outlast anything he could have written in its place.

Talk it through:
  • Monsieur Hamel accuses himself alongside everyone else. Does that strengthen the story's argument or soften a criticism that should have gone elsewhere?
  • The story teaches that you value what you are about to lose. Is that an insight or an excuse — does it actually change anybody's behaviour?
  • Daudet writes from one side of a contested border. Write the version a boy on the other side might tell, and say what would be true in both.
  • Is a language worth defending as a political matter, or only as a private inheritance? Where does the story stand, and where do you?

Task B · Should schools teach in minority languages? (4 minutes)

Build an argument. One position: children need the dominant national language to compete for work, and school hours are finite. Another: a school that abandons the local language accelerates its death, and the cost is not measurable in wages. Take a side, give three reasons, deal with the strongest objection, and propose a policy you could actually defend to both a parent and a minister.

Then argue the opposite for one minute.

Task C · Time, complacency and what we postpone (4 minutes)

Take the story's argument into your own experience — or keep it entirely general if you prefer.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Something you wish you had started years earlier. What did you assume at the time?
  • Address a class of eleven-year-olds on why the subject they find pointless may matter later — and be honest about the cases where it won't.
  • A language, dialect or accent in your country with fewer speakers than fifty years ago. What happened, and does it matter?
  • If you had one final lesson in a subject you love, how would you spend the hour, and what would you leave out?
  • Is "it's too late" ever literally true, or is it usually a way of avoiding an inconvenient start?
  • What would you write on the blackboard, in the largest letters you could manage, at the end of something that mattered to you?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Franz understands the value of his lessons at the precise moment they cease to be available. Is that the only way understanding ever arrives?
  2. The teacher blames himself as freely as anyone else. Generous, or a way of letting the real culprits out of the frame?
  3. You are studying a language now, voluntarily, in a lesson nobody is taking away. Does this story change how that feels — and should it?
