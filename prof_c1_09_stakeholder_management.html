Stakeholder Management Vocabulary

Click each card to reveal the definition.

Stakeholder Anyone affected by or who can affect a project or decision (clients, employees, investors, partners)
Power/Interest Matrix A tool that maps stakeholders based on their power (influence) and interest in a project
Engagement The level of involvement and communication with stakeholders; keeping them informed and included
Buy-in A stakeholder's agreement and commitment to support a project or decision
Sponsor A senior stakeholder who champions a project and has authority to allocate resources
Champion An influential supporter who advocates for a project and influences others positively
Resistor A stakeholder who opposes or creates obstacles to a project
Influence Map A visual representation of power relationships and influence networks among stakeholders
Expectation What a stakeholder believes will happen or should happen; critical to manage explicitly
Escalation Bringing a conflict or issue to a higher authority for resolution

The Power/Interest Matrix

This matrix helps you understand how to engage each stakeholder based on their power (ability to influence the project) and their interest (how much they care about the outcome).

Power / Interest Grid

HIGH POWER / HIGH INTEREST
MANAGE CLOSELY

Example: Project sponsor, key client, executive stakeholder

Strategy: Regular direct communication, involve in decision-making, keep fully informed, address concerns immediately

HIGH POWER / LOW INTEREST
KEEP SATISFIED

Example: Senior leadership not directly involved, board members

Strategy: Regular updates (but not excessive detail), involve strategically, demonstrate progress and ROI

LOW POWER / HIGH INTEREST
KEEP INFORMED

Example: End users, team members, engaged customers

Strategy: Regular communication, listen to feedback, involve in design or implementation, make them feel heard

LOW POWER / LOW INTEREST
MONITOR

Example: Tangential stakeholders, passive observers

Strategy: General awareness, minimal communication, track in case interest or power increases

Building Your Stakeholder Map

Steps to Create Your Map:

  1. List all stakeholders: anyone affected by or who can affect the project
  2. Assess their power: How much influence do they have over resources, timeline, approval?
  3. Assess their interest: How much do they care about the project's success?
  4. Place them in the matrix
  5. Plan your engagement strategy for each quadrant

Key Insight: Resistors

Resistors often have high power but low interest (or negative interest). They may resist because:

  • They fear losing control or status
  • They don't understand the benefits
  • The change threatens their department's budget or role
  • They prefer the status quo

Strategy: Acknowledge their concerns, involve them early, show how the project benefits them, find common ground.

Tailoring Communication for Different Stakeholders

For Sponsors (High Power / High Interest)

What they care about: ROI, timeline, risks, whether it aligns with business strategy

Communication style: Direct, concise, outcome-focused

Sample language:

  • "The project is on track to deliver $2M in annual savings by Q3."
  • "We're seeing one moderate risk with supplier lead times. Here's our mitigation plan."
  • "I want to update you on the decision we made yesterday and get your input before we proceed."

For Executives / Leaders (High Power / Low Interest)

What they care about: Business impact, strategic alignment, any major blockers

Communication style: Executive summary, minimal jargon, focus on implications

Sample language:

  • "Project Status: Green. On time and budget."
  • "Key milestone achieved: Platform approved for launch."
  • "One issue to monitor: regulatory approval is taking 4 weeks longer than expected. We're managing scope accordingly."

For End Users / Team Members (Low Power / High Interest)

What they care about: How it affects their daily work, training, support

Communication style: Detailed, empathetic, two-way dialogue

Sample language:

  • "Here's what changes for you, and here's why."
  • "Your feedback matters. Let's make sure this works for your team."
  • "We're offering training sessions on X and Y. Which time works best for you?"

For Resistors (Any Power / Negative Interest)

What they care about: Their concerns, how they're affected, whether they'll be listened to

Communication style: Listen first, address specific concerns, find alignment

Sample language:

  • "I hear your concern about X. Let me address that directly."
  • "What would need to happen for you to feel confident about this change?"
  • "Here's how this actually protects your team's interests."

Practice: Stakeholder Update for a Delayed Project

Time Remaining: 10:00

The Situation

You're managing a software migration project. The original deadline was March 15, but you've hit a technical blocker. The project will now launch on April 5—three weeks late. This affects:

  • CFO (sponsor): Budgeted for Q1 savings; the delay costs $50K
  • VP of Operations (high power): Needs the system to support the new workflow they're rolling out
  • Support Team (end users): Trained for the old system; dreading retraining
  • IT Director (resistor): Was skeptical about the new vendor from the start

Your Task: Write Stakeholder Updates

Update 1: For the CFO (Sponsor)

Write a short email (5-7 sentences) communicating the delay. Your tone: professional, accountable, solution-focused.

"Subject: Project Update – Launch Date Adjustment

I wanted to inform you directly of a development we encountered this week. We've identified a data integration issue that requires additional testing to ensure system stability. As a result, we're moving the go-live from March 15 to April 5. This represents a three-week extension and will push our projected ROI timeline by the same period. However, launching with confidence is critical; a rushed deployment would expose us to greater risks. We're implementing mitigation steps to minimize the financial impact and are exploring opportunities to capture some Q1 benefits. I'll have a detailed impact analysis to you by end of week. I appreciate your partnership on this."

Update 2: For the VP of Operations (High Power / Low Interest)

Write a brief update (3-4 sentences) focused on business impact and next steps.

"Update: Software Migration Launch – April 5. We encountered a technical issue with data integration that required us to extend the timeline by three weeks. This pushes your workflow rollout by the same timeframe. We're adjusting your deployment plan to maintain the synergy between system launch and workflow implementation. You'll receive a revised timeline by Friday. Is there anything in your schedule we need to account for?"

Update 3: For the Support Team (Low Power / High Interest)

Write an update (4-6 sentences) that acknowledges their effort and explains the delay in user-friendly terms.

"Team Update: Launch Delayed to April 5. I know you've all been preparing for the March 15 launch, and this extension may feel frustrating. Here's what happened: during testing, we discovered that one part of the new system wasn't communicating correctly with our database. Rather than launch with that issue and deal with problems afterward, we're taking three weeks to fix it properly. This actually works in your favor—you'll have more time to practice with the new system before real customers use it. We're scheduling additional training sessions next month. Your preparation so far has been excellent and sets us up for a smooth launch."

Update 4: For the IT Director (Resistor)

Write an update (4-5 sentences) that addresses their skepticism constructively and shows how this delay actually validates some of their concerns.

"Update: Software Migration – Timeline Adjusted to April 5. During rigorous testing, we identified a data integration issue that you raised concerns about in the planning phase. This discovery actually validates the thoroughness of your team's initial review. Rather than proceed with a risky deployment, we're taking the time to address this properly—which aligns with your feedback about vendor reliability. The extended timeline shows why careful implementation matters. Your expertise has been invaluable in catching this early. Can we schedule a debrief this week to discuss what you're seeing and ensure we're aligned on quality standards for launch?"

Key Principles for Stakeholder Updates

  • Be proactive: Don't wait for them to hear from someone else. Tell them first.
  • Be honest: Acknowledge the problem. Don't sugarcoat bad news.
  • Show accountability: Take responsibility. Don't blame vendors, team members, or "circumstances."
  • Provide context: Explain why the delay happened. Help them understand the reasoning.
  • Focus on their interests: Show how you're managing impact for them specifically.
  • Provide next steps: What happens now? When will they hear from you again?
