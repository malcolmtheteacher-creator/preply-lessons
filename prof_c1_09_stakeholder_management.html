The Stakeholder Matrix

Everyone wants something different. You have one project. Navigate.

Warm-up: Name Your Stakeholders

Think of a project or initiative you're involved in right now. Name three people who have a stake in its outcome. What does each of them want? Do their interests conflict?

Your speaking task: In 60 seconds, name your project, identify three stakeholders, and explain what each one needs from it. Be specific.

Start your timer:

1:00
What you just did: You mapped a stakeholder landscape. Every project has one. The people who hold decision-making power, budget, approval, information, or personal investment — they all need different things. Your job isn't to make them all happy. It's to understand what each one needs, and navigate the trade-offs explicitly.

5 Stakeholder Navigation Moves

Click each card to see the move and a real-world example.

1. The Stakeholder Map

Plot your stakeholders on a 2x2 grid: Influence (high/low) × Interest (high/low). Each quadrant gets a different strategy:

  • High Influence + High Interest: Manage closely. Regular updates, clear expectations, real consultation.
  • High Influence + Low Interest: Keep satisfied. Monthly updates, no surprises, escalation plan if they become interested.
  • Low Influence + High Interest: Keep informed. Share context, include them where possible, but don't assume they drive decisions.
  • Low Influence + Low Interest: Monitor. Annual update is probably enough.

The point: Same project, four completely different communication rhythms and depths. The map shows you which are which.

2. The Tailored Update

Same project news. Three different audiences. Three completely different versions.

For an executive: "We're on track. Budget is 3% under. Delivering on time. One dependency on Finance's team — I've flagged it to them."

For a technical colleague: "The API integration is proving more complex than the initial spec. We're restructuring the data contract. This adds two weeks to development, but keeps us within the overall project timeline because we've compressed testing."

For an affected department: "The system change will mean you'll see the new interface in six weeks. Here's what changes for you: three reports you run weekly will become automated. One process — the monthly reconciliation — will work differently. We're running training in week 5."

The point: Tailoring isn't dishonesty. It's showing each stakeholder why this project matters to them.

3. The Expectation Contract

The most underused tool in stakeholder management. A conversation, not a document. But structured:

"What I can commit to is..." — be specific. Scope, timeline, quality level, communication cadence. Don't over-promise.

"What I need from you is..." — decisions by X date, approval from Y, budget Z, or agreement not to change scope mid-flight.

"By [date] I'll have..." — the concrete deliverable or decision point.

Example: "What I can commit to is weekly status updates and any requested changes within two weeks. What I need from you is decision-maker approval by Friday, and no changes to the scope once we start building. By month-end, you'll have the working prototype."

4. Navigating Conflict

Two stakeholders want opposite things. You can't give both. What you do:

Name it explicitly. Don't hide the conflict and hope no one notices.

Show you understand both sides. "I understand you need X. [Other stakeholder] needs Y. Here's how I'm thinking about balancing those..."

Make a decision and explain why. "We're prioritising [one thing] because [business reason]. But here's what we're doing about the other need..."

Example: "You want this done in two weeks. Engineering says they need four to build it right. I understand both constraints. We're doing a phased release — core features in three weeks, polish in week four. That meets your timeline and their quality bar."

5. The Bad News Update

Something has changed. Something your stakeholder won't like. Three rules:

Be proactive. Tell them before they hear it somewhere else. First messenger gets credibility.

Be specific. Not "there's a delay." "We found a technical blocker that needs two extra weeks. Here's what it is. Here's how we found it."

Come with a plan. Not "things have changed." "Things have changed, and here's what we're doing about it. By [date], we'll have [solution or update]."

Example: "I need to tell you something that affects your timeline. The client feedback in week two revealed the scope was bigger than we thought. Instead of eight weeks, we're looking at twelve. But we're restructuring phase one to deliver core value in week six. You'll have something to work with earlier than originally planned, and the full build in twelve weeks."

Stakeholder Updates: Deliver 3 Times, 3 Different Ways

Pick a project scenario below. Then deliver the same project news to three completely different stakeholders — each in 60 seconds. Same information. Totally different communication.

Pick your project:
  • A software upgrade that's been delayed by 3 weeks
  • A new performance management system being rolled out across the company
  • A process redesign that eliminates one person's main responsibility
  • A merger that creates uncertainty about team structure
Your news: The project is delayed by 3 weeks. Reason: stakeholder feedback changed the requirements. No budget impact. Team is aligned on the new direction.

Update 1: To an Executive

What does an executive care about? Timeline. Budget. Risk. Risk mitigation.

Update 2: To a Technical Team Member

What do they care about? The work itself. Why requirements changed. What it means for their part.

Update 3: To an Affected Department

What do they care about? When they can use it. What changes for them. How it helps them.

Challenge Round: 6 Difficult Moments

A stakeholder says one of these things to you. You have 45 seconds to respond. Click "Next challenge" to reveal each one.

Challenge 1

Stakeholder: "You told me this would be done by end of month. It's end of month."

The tension: They want accountability. They might be angry, or just checking whether the commitment held.

0:45

Challenge 2

Stakeholder: "Why am I hearing about this from someone else and not from you?"

The tension: You failed the "no surprises" rule. They learned something important from the rumour mill, not from you. Trust is damaged.

0:45

Challenge 3

Stakeholder: "I don't think my concerns are being taken seriously."

The tension: Somewhere, they raised a problem and felt unheard. Your job: show them where their input changed things, even if it didn't change what they wanted.

0:45

Challenge 4

Stakeholder: "You're prioritising [other stakeholder's] needs over mine. Why?"

The tension: They're right. You are. But you're not going to change it. Can you explain why without making them feel like a lower priority?

0:45

Challenge 5

Stakeholder: "Can you just give me a straight answer — is this project on track or not?"

The tension: Maybe it's yes, maybe no, maybe it's complicated. They don't want nuance. They want clarity. But you need to be honest.

0:45

Challenge 6

Stakeholder: "I've lost confidence in this project. What would make me feel more confident?"

The tension: This is your chance to rebuild trust. Don't get defensive. Ask what they need to see, and then commit to showing it.

0:45

Stakeholder Communication Language Bank

Tailoring Communication

"What I think matters most to you here is..."

"For your part of the business, the impact is..."

"I know you care about [specific thing]. Here's how this affects it."

Setting Expectations

"What I can commit to is... What I need from you is..."

"Here's what success looks like: [specific]. Here's the timeline: [date]."

"By [date], you'll have [deliverable]. That gives you [time] to [use it]."

Navigating Conflict

"I understand you need X. They need Y. Here's how I'm balancing those..."

"We're prioritising this because... That means we're deferring... Here's when you'll get that."

"Both concerns are valid. This is the trade-off I'm making and why."

Bad News Delivery

"Something has changed. Let me tell you what it is, why it happened, and what we're doing about it."

"We discovered [problem]. It affects [timeline]. Here's the plan to address it."

"This isn't ideal, but here's what you can expect next."

Rebuilding Trust

"I should have told you sooner. Here's what I'm doing differently."

"I hear that I've lost your confidence. What would help rebuild it?"

"You're right to push back. Here's how I'm addressing your concern."

Mapping Stakeholders

"You have high influence and high interest in this — let's check in weekly."

"You care deeply about this, but the decision sits elsewhere. Here's how I'll keep you informed."

"This affects your area — you should know what's coming."
