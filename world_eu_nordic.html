Cold Nordics, Happy Nordics?

Nordic people are said to be cold, silent and reserved — yet Finland keeps being named the happiest country in the world. How can both be true?

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Finns are often described as reserved.
reserved (adj) — not showing feelings or opinions openly.
2. Small talk is less common in Finland.
small talk (n) — light, polite conversation.
3. The report measures well-being.
well-being (n) — feeling healthy and content.
4. High trust in others is typical of Nordic societies.
trust (n) — a belief that people are honest.
5. People are expected to be modest.
modest (adj) — not talking proudly about yourself.
6. Finland tops the ranking.
a ranking (n) — a list ordered by position.
7. Life satisfaction is high.
satisfaction (n) — the feeling of being pleased.
8. Equality is a core Nordic value.
equality (n) — the same rights and chances for everyone.

The stereotype: "Nordic people are cold and unfriendly — and they're always happy." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The quiet stereotype

Finns are often joked about for silence: standing far apart at bus stops, avoiding small talk, and being comfortable saying nothing. The Finnish cartoonist Karoliina Korhonen made a popular series, "Finnish Nightmares", about a shy character called Matti — and many Finns found it funny because it felt familiar. But reserve is not the same as coldness: many Finns say that when they talk, they mean what they say.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is Matti?

2. Is reserve the same as coldness?

1. The shy character in "Finnish Nightmares".

2. No.

Discuss:
  • Is small talk necessary?

Part 2 — The happiness rankings

Since 2018, Finland has been ranked first in the World Happiness Report every year up to at least 2025, with Denmark, Iceland and Sweden usually near the top. The report is based mainly on people being asked to rate their lives on a scale from 0 to 10, and the results are linked with things such as income, health, social support, freedom and trust. It measures life satisfaction — not how much people smile.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How is happiness measured in the report?

2. Does it measure smiling?

1. People are asked to rate their lives from 0 to 10.

2. No — life satisfaction.

Discuss:
  • Is "satisfied" the same as "happy"?

Part 3 — Trust and modesty

Nordic societies are known for high trust. In a 2013 Reader's Digest experiment, wallets were "lost" in cities around the world, and in Helsinki 11 out of 12 were returned. Modesty is valued too: the Danish-Norwegian writer Aksel Sandemose described the "Law of Jante" in a 1933 novel — rules like "don't think you're better than us". Some Nordic people say Jante encourages equality; others say it discourages ambition.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many wallets were returned in Helsinki?

2. What is the Law of Jante?

1. 11 out of 12.

2. Rules against thinking you're better than others, from Sandemose's 1933 novel.

Discuss:
  • Is modesty a strength or a weakness?

Grammar — The Passive & Reporting Passives

When we talk about beliefs and reputations, we often don't know or care who says them. That is the job of the passive.

How it works:
• be + past participle: "The survey is carried out every year."
• Reporting passives: "Finns are said to be shy." / "It is often claimed that…"
• Add by only when the doer matters.

1. Make it passive

Complete each sentence about Cold Nordics, Happy Nordics?. Use the word in brackets.

1. Finns often (describe) as reserved.

2. Finland (rank) first every year since 2018.

3. People (ask) to rate their lives from 0 to 10.

1. are … described

2. has been ranked

3. are asked

2. Choose the right passive form

1. Nordic people (say) to be cold.

2. The Law of Jante (describe) by Sandemose in 1933.

3. (it / often / claim) that Finns dislike small talk.

1. are said

2. was described

3. It is often claimed

Going deeper

The "cold Nordic" stereotype comes from comparing conversational styles: in cultures where small talk and enthusiasm are polite, silence feels unfriendly. The "happy Nordic" stereotype comes from rankings that actually measure trust, security and satisfaction. Both pictures miss the same thing: quiet contentment, not cheerful chatter.

Worth noticing: Reporting passives — "X is said to be…", "It is claimed that…" — let you mention a reputation without saying you believe it.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Read silence differently. With Nordic colleagues, don't rush to fill pauses; give people time to think, keep promises, and be modest about achievements. Fewer, more meaningful words will earn more trust than lots of enthusiasm.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Can a quiet culture be warm?
  • What would make your country happier?
  • How do you build trust at work?
