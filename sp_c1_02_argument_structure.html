Now: The Problem

You can FRAME a response beautifully. But what comes next? Many advanced speakers stumble at the same point: they have good ideas but no visible structure. The listener can't follow the logic.

The Core Skill

Persuasion isn't about stronger opinions — it's about visible structure. When listeners can follow your logic in real-time, you convince them. When they're lost, even brilliant ideas fail.

Consider two speakers answering: "Should companies prioritise profit or purpose?"

Same opinion. Different structure.

Speaker A: "Well, both matter. Companies need to make money obviously, but people care about values now. Some companies do well with purpose. But it's hard. Times are changing..."

Speaker B: "My position is that purpose and profit aren't opposites — they're interdependent. The evidence suggests that purpose-driven companies have higher employee retention and customer loyalty. What this means in practice is that purpose becomes a competitive advantage, not just ethics."

Same thinking. Radically different impact. The difference? Visible argument structure — and the signposts that make it audible.

Today you'll learn 5 POSITION strategies — the second step in the Discourse Flow Sequence. These are the phrases that make your argument architecture visible: Claim → Evidence → Implication → Defence → Depth.

Language Focus A: The Argument Chain

Three strategies that form the core of any persuasive argument: Claim → Evidence → Implication. Click each one, study it, then try the speaking task before looking at examples.

📍 "My position is..." Click to expand
Function
States your claim explicitly; frames what follows as a structured argument
Register
Professional / Authoritative — owns the stance openly
When to Use
Opening your argument or clarifying your stance when challenged
In a business debate:

"My position is that remote work has fundamentally shifted what we can expect from physical offices. Let me explain why..."

Variations: "My view is..." / "I'd argue that..." / "I'd contend that..." / "My take is..."

Now You Try

State a clear position on whether universities should be free. Use this signpost to open your argument. Speak out loud — don't just think it.

🔬 "The evidence suggests..." Click to expand
Function
Bridges from claim to supporting detail; signals you're grounding opinion in fact
Register
Academic / Analytical — projects credibility and rigour
When to Use
Moving from claim to evidence; supporting your position with reasoning
After stating a position:

"The evidence suggests that companies with strong ESG commitments have lower employee turnover and stronger stakeholder trust..."

Variations: "The data shows..." / "Research indicates..." / "Studies demonstrate..." / "In practice, we see..."

Now You Try

You've just stated your position on remote work. Now introduce evidence using this signpost. Speak for at least 30 seconds.

💡 "What this means in practice is..." Click to expand
Function
Translates evidence into real-world implication; makes the stakes clear
Register
Professional / Practical — shows you think beyond theory
When to Use
After evidence; strengthening impact; making your argument tangible
Completing the logical chain:

"What this means in practice is that organisations need to redesign their office spaces to focus on collaboration and culture, not just desk occupancy."

Variations: "In concrete terms..." / "Practically speaking..." / "The implication is..." / "So the real issue is..."

Complete the full chain: position → evidence → implication. Choose any topic you care about. The chain should feel like one flowing argument, not three separate boxes.

Language Focus B: Defence & Depth

Two more strategies — these are your tools for handling pushback and building layered, resilient arguments.

🛡 "I take your point, however..." Click to expand
Function
Defends against challenge while showing good faith and intellectual honesty
Register
Professional / Respectful — maintains credibility under pressure
When to Use
Responding to objections, counterarguments, or challenges to your position
Responding to a challenge:

"I take your point that AI poses real risks. However, the evidence suggests that regulation is catching up faster than many predict. What matters is how we shape that transition."

Variations: "I see where you're coming from, but..." / "That's a fair observation, though..." / "I hear the concern, and yet..."

Now You Try

Someone says: "Free universities would bankrupt governments." Acknowledge the point, then defend your position using this strategy. The skill is in the pivot — validating without conceding.

🏗 "To strengthen that argument..." Click to expand
Function
Adds depth; shows your argument is layered and considered, not brittle
Register
Professional / Thoughtful — demonstrates intellectual confidence
When to Use
Building on points; adding a second layer; anticipating objections
Reinforcing your position:

"To strengthen that argument, we should also consider the psychological dimension — people are demonstrably more engaged in work they find meaningful. That's not just corporate speak; it's backed by decades of research."

Variations: "To build on that..." / "To extend that logic..." / "I'd also add that..." / "And crucially..."

Now You Try

Make a point about education, technology, or your industry. Then add a second layer that strengthens it. The goal: your argument should feel deeper, not just longer.

Deep Dive: Defending Your Position

Speak for 5-7 minutes on one challenge below. The goal: build a coherent argument AND defend it convincingly when challenged. Use both FRAME strategies (from Lesson 1) and POSITION strategies together.

7:00

Choose Your Challenge

The Nuanced Position

Argue that something widely considered "bad" has unexpected benefits — OR something widely seen as "good" carries hidden costs. State your position clearly, support it with evidence, and explain what it means. Be ready to defend against "but surely..." objections.

Target: strong claim, multiple evidence points, clear implication, handling challenge gracefully

The Industry Argument

Your industry or field is facing a major shift. Take a clear stance on what's happening, why it matters, and what should be done. Build your argument layer by layer. Then imagine someone challenges a core assumption — how would you defend it?

Target: expert-level reasoning, evidence grounding, thoughtful defence

The Values Argument

Take a clear position on a values-based question (success, fairness, responsibility, loyalty). Argue it rigorously: What's your position? What evidence shapes it? What does it imply about how people should act? Then defend it against someone who disagrees fundamentally.

Target: sophisticated reasoning about values, multiple layers, principled defence

Self-Monitoring

As you speak, track: Is my structure visible? Can a listener follow my logic? Am I defending or just restating? Where do I feel processing pressure? These moments reveal where your argument architecture needs strengthening.

The Story So Far
