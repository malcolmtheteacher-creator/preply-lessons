The Framework: Structure That Persuades

A strong argument isn't about stronger opinions — it's about visible structure. When listeners can follow your logic in real-time, you persuade them. When they're lost, even brilliant ideas fail.

The Architecture

Claim → Evidence → Implication is the skeleton of persuasive discourse. But it's the signposting that makes it visible.

Why This Matters

At C1/C2, you can construct complex ideas. But structure separates commanding speakers from merely fluent ones. Consider:

Both speakers answer: "Should companies prioritise profit or purpose?"

Unstructured: "Well, both matter. Companies need to make money obviously, but people care about values now. Some companies do well with purpose. But it's hard. Times are changing..."

Structured: "I'd argue that purpose and profit aren't opposites — they're interdependent. My position is that companies pursuing genuine purpose outperform long-term. The evidence suggests that purpose-driven companies have higher employee retention and customer loyalty. What this means in practice is that purpose becomes a competitive advantage, not just ethics."

Same opinion. Radically different impact. The difference? Visible argument structure.

Today's Focus

You'll learn to signal your argument structure as you build it — so listeners always know where you are, why you're saying it, and where you're going. This lets you handle challenges, defend positions, and think in real-time without losing coherence.

"My position is..." Click to expand
Function
States your claim explicitly; frames what follows
Register
Professional / Authoritative — owns the stance
When to Use

"The evidence suggests..." Click to expand
Function
Bridges from claim to supporting detail; signals reasoning
Register
Academic / Analytical — grounds opinion in fact
When to Use
Moving from claim to evidence; supporting your position
After stating a position:

"The evidence suggests that companies with strong ESG commitments have lower employee turnover and stronger stakeholder trust..."

Variations: "The data shows..." / "Research indicates..." / "Studies demonstrate..." / "In practice, we see..."

"What this means in practice is..." Click to expand
Function
Translates evidence into real-world implication; makes stakes clear
Register
Professional / Practical — shows you think beyond theory
When to Use
After evidence; strengthening impact of your argument
Completing the logical chain:

"What this means in practice is that organisations need to redesign their office spaces to focus on collaboration and culture, not just desk occupancy."

Variations: "In concrete terms..." / "Practically speaking..." / "The implication is..." / "So the real issue is..."

"I take your point, however..." Click to expand
Function
Defends against challenge while showing good faith
Register
Professional / Respectful — maintains credibility under pressure
When to Use
Responding to objections or counterarguments
Responding to challenge:

"I take your point that AI poses real risks. However, the evidence suggests that regulation is catching up faster than many predict. What matters is how we shape that transition."

Variations: "I see where you're coming from, but..." / "That's a fair observation, though..." / "I hear the concern, and yet..."

"To strengthen that argument..." Click to expand
Function
Adds depth; shows your argument is layered, not brittle
Register
Professional / Thoughtful — demonstrates intellectual confidence
When to Use
Building on points; anticipating objections
Reinforcing your position:

"To strengthen that argument, we should also consider the psychological dimension — people are demonstrably more engaged in work they find meaningful. That's not just corporate speak; it's backed by decades of research."

Variations: "To build on that..." / "To extend that logic..." / "I'd also add that..." / "And crucially..."

Deep Dive: Defending Your Position

Now speak for 5-7 minutes on one challenge below. The goal: build a coherent argument AND defend it convincingly when challenged.

7:00

Choose Your Challenge

The Nuanced Position

Argue that something widely considered "bad" has unexpected benefits — OR something widely seen as "good" carries hidden costs. State your position clearly, support it with evidence, and explain what it means. Be ready to defend against "but surely..." objections.

Target: Strong claim, multiple evidence points, clear implication, handling challenge gracefully

The Industry Argument

Your industry or field is facing a major shift. Take a clear stance on what's happening, why it matters, and what should be done about it. Build your argument layer by layer. Then imagine someone challenges a core assumption — how would you defend it?

Target: Expert-level reasoning, evidence grounding, thoughtful defense

The Values Argument

Take a clear position on a values-based question (success, fairness, responsibility, loyalty, etc.). Argue it rigorously: What's your position? What evidence shapes it? What does it imply about how people should act? Then defend it against someone who disagrees fundamentally.

Target: Sophisticated reasoning about values, multiple layers, principled defense

Self-Monitoring

As you speak, track: Is my structure visible? Can a listener follow my logic? Am I defending or just restating? Where do I feel pressure? These moments reveal where your argument is weakest.
