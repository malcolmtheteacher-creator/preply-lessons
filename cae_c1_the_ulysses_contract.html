Show Answers

1. B (Drawing) — the collocation is "to draw on a story / source / example" meaning to use it as a basis. "Building on" works too, but "Drawing on" is the more idiomatic CAE-register choice here.

2. A (argued) — "to argue that X" is the academic-register collocation. "Discussed / talked / said that" don't carry the same sense of putting forward a position.

3. A (case) — "the case for X" = the argument in favour of X. A fixed collocation. "Reason for" doesn't take "stronger / weaker" naturally.

4. A (resist) — "to resist temptation" is a strong collocation. "Avoid / deny / refuse" each have temptation-adjacent meanings but don't form the standard pairing.

Use of English · Part 2

Open cloze

For questions 5–8, read the text below and think of the word which best fits each gap. Use only one word in each gap. There is an example at the beginning (0).

Shortened practice — the real CAE Part 2 has 8 questions.

The concept (0) of self-binding has been with us (5) the time of Homer. Ulysses, knowing that the Sirens' song would be impossible (6) resist on his own, ordered his men to lash him (7) the mast of his ship — and to ignore him, no matter (8) he later begged or shouted in the moment.

Show Answers

5. since — "since the time of Homer" — preposition of time. ("From" is just acceptable but less natural here.)

6. to — "impossible to resist" — adjective + infinitive.

7. to — "lash X to Y" — phrasal collocation (lash someone to something).

Key word transformations

13. "I regret not signing up for the policy earlier." WISH

Show Answer

wish I had signed up (5 words inc. WISH). The "wish + past perfect" pattern expresses regret about an earlier action.

14. "If he hadn't been bound to the mast, he would have leaped into the sea." NOT

been bound to the mast, he would have leaped into the sea.

Show Answer

Had he not (3 words inc. NOT). Inverted third conditional, formal register.

15. "The framework was specifically designed to prevent ad hoc exceptions." PURPOSE

The framework preventing ad hoc exceptions.

Show Answer

was designed with the purpose of (6 words inc. PURPOSE). Note the preposition "of" before the -ing form.

16. "We won't reopen this question, regardless of any pressure." MATTER

We won't reopen this question, pressure we receive.

Show Answer

no matter what (3 words inc. MATTER). Concessive structure "no matter what X" = "regardless of X".

Reading · Part 7

Gapped text

You are going to read an extract from an article about pre-commitment. Four paragraphs have been removed from the article. Choose from the paragraphs A–E the one which fits each gap (1–4). There is one extra paragraph which you do not need to use.

Shortened practice — the real CAE Part 7 has six gaps and seven options.

Lashed to the Mast: An Old Story for a New Problem

The image is a famous one. Ulysses, sailing past the island of the Sirens, knew that their song would lure him to his death — and he also knew that no amount of present resolve would survive contact with that voice. So he had himself bound to the mast of his ship and ordered his men, ears stuffed with beeswax, to ignore any future command he gave.

Gap 1

The mechanism behind the story is what philosophers and economists today call pre-commitment. The present self, while still thinking clearly, sets a binding rule that the future self — under pressure, fatigue, or temptation — will be unable to undo. The Greeks called this style of moral foresight phronesis. Modern decision theorists have a slightly more clinical term.

Gap 2

A growing body of research suggests that the conditions for pre-commitment to make sense have not weakened with modernity but, on the contrary, intensified. Where the temptations of earlier centuries arrived occasionally, often awkwardly, and rarely with much sophistication, the temptations of the present arrive constantly, smoothly, and in forms specifically engineered to overcome resistance.

Gap 3

The skill of designing good pre-commitments is, accordingly, one of the quietly important capacities a thoughtful adult can develop. The classic forms — the locked savings account, the deleted application, the public promise, the binding policy — share a single underlying logic: they remove an option from a future self who cannot be trusted to refuse it in real time.

Gap 4

Yet the picture is not entirely settled. Critics of pre-commitment, of whom there have always been some, argue that the device is a form of self-mistrust dressed up as wisdom — that the future self has rights, knowledge, and contexts which the past self cannot adequately anticipate. The debate, philosophically, remains live.

Missing paragraphs — pick which fits each gap. One is not needed.

A The trick worked. The ship passed safely. Ulysses heard the song, raved, begged to be released, and was deliberately not released. The voyage continued. Two and a half thousand years later, the story is still being told because it captures something we have all noticed about ourselves: our knowledge of what is right does not always survive the moment.
B Whatever we call it, however, the underlying move is the same: limit your future options now, while you can still reason clearly about which ones deserve to survive.
C Faced with an attention economy designed to undermine resistance, the case for pre-commitment is, on this view, no longer a matter of personal taste. It has quietly become the basic operating discipline of any serious working life.
D None of these forms is automatically wise. A binding policy that has outlived its reason can become a small tyranny; a locked savings account designed for a problem that no longer applies can produce harm rather than protection.
E Modern psychology has spent decades studying the temperamental differences between people who naturally make good pre-commitments and those who do not, with results that remain inconclusive at best.

Writing · Part 1

Essay (220–260 words)

You must answer this question. Write your answer in 220–260 words in an appropriate style.

Your class has just listened to a panel discussion about pre-commitment — the practice of setting rules for one's future self. You have made the notes below.

Some opinions expressed in the discussion:

"Pre-commitment is a confession of weakness — strong people don't need rules."
"In modern conditions, designed temptations make pre-commitment essential, not optional."
"The real danger of pre-commitment is rigidity — past selves controlling future selves who know more."

Write an essay discussing two of the three opinions in your notes. You should explain which opinion you find more convincing, giving reasons in support of your answer.

You may, if you wish, make use of the opinions expressed in the discussion, but you should use your own words as far as possible.

Time remaining: 40:00

Planning frame — 5 minutes before you write

  • Paragraph 1 — Introduction: paraphrase the topic. Briefly introduce the two opinions you've chosen. Do not give your full opinion yet.
  • Paragraph 2 — Opinion A: explain the position, then evaluate it (concede what's strong, challenge what's weak).
  • Paragraph 3 — Opinion B: same shape — explain, then evaluate.
  • Paragraph 4 — Your conclusion: state clearly which of the two you find more convincing, and give your main reason.

Useful C1-register chunks for the essay

  • It is sometimes argued that… / There is a widespread view that…
  • Those who hold this view would point out that…
  • While there is some force to this position, it overlooks the fact that…
  • A more convincing argument is that…
  • In modern conditions, where…
  • It seems reasonable to suggest, however, that…
  • On balance, I find the second view more persuasive, primarily because…
  • To conclude, …

Higher-band grammar to reach for

  • Inversion: Not only does pre-commitment reduce friction, it also…
  • Mixed conditional: Were the future self always wiser, the case against binding would carry more weight.
  • Concessive: However compelling the case for flexibility, …
  • Cleft: What makes modern conditions different is…
  • Mandative subjunctive: It is essential that the policy not be reopened lightly.

Speaking · Part 2

Long turn (about 1 minute)

In this part of the test, the examiner gives you two pictures and asks you to compare them and answer a question. You speak for about a minute without interruption.

Examiner

"Here are your pictures. They show people in different situations involving rules they have set for themselves. I'd like you to compare two of the pictures, and say what difficulties these people might face in keeping to their rules, and how they might overcome them. You have about a minute."

Picture A — a person at home, deleting a social media application from their phone in the evening.

Picture B — a group of senior people in a meeting room, signing a long document with serious expressions.

Picture C — a person on a long walk through a forest, with a phone visibly switched off in their pocket.

Time remaining: 1:00

Useful chunks for Part 2 (the long turn)

  • Comparing: "Both pictures show…" / "Whereas the first picture…, the second…" / "What both situations have in common is…"
  • Speculating: "He might be…" / "She could possibly be…" / "It looks as though…" / "I would imagine that…"
  • Difficulties: "The most obvious difficulty would be…" / "What I think they might struggle with is…"
  • Overcoming: "One way to handle this would be…" / "What they could do is…"
Speaking · Part 3

Collaborative task (about 3 minutes)

In Part 3, you and your partner are given a task to discuss together. You have about two minutes to talk about the options, then about a minute to make a decision together.

Examiner

"Now I'd like you to talk about something together. Here are some things people commonly do to set rules for their future selves.

First, talk together about how effective each of these is in modern life. You have about two minutes."

  • physically removing an option (e.g. deleting an application)
  • making a public promise to others
  • locking money away in a long-term account
  • agreeing a binding rule with a partner or colleague

"Now you have about a minute to decide which one is the most effective in modern life."

Useful chunks for collaborative discussion (Part 3)

  • Opening: "Shall we start with…?" / "What do you think about…?"
  • Agreeing: "I see what you mean — and on top of that…" / "Exactly, and the other thing is…"
  • Disagreeing diplomatically: "I take your point, but I'd say…" / "I'm not sure I agree, because…"
  • Reaching agreement: "So shall we say…?" / "On balance, I'd go for… What do you think?"
Speaking · Part 4

Discussion (about 4 minutes)

In Part 4, the examiner asks you more abstract questions related to the topic of Part 3. Your answers should be developed and analytical, not short.

Examiner questions
  1. Do you think we now need more pre-commitment than previous generations did? Why, or why not?
  2. Is it sometimes wrong to bind your future self? When?
  3. Some companies have very strict rules; others have almost none. From your experience, which works better, and why?
  4. Should governments do more to make it easier for people to bind their future selves — for example, in saving or in screen-time?
  5. If you had to teach a younger person one rule about setting rules for themselves, what would it be?

How to build a Part 4 answer (aim for > 4 sentences each)

  • Position: "Personally, I would argue that…"
  • Reasoning: "The main reason for this is that…"
  • Example or evidence: "Take, for instance, … / A good example would be…"
  • Counter and concession: "Of course, some would say… ; however, …"
  • Hedge: "It does seem reasonable to suggest, though, that…"
