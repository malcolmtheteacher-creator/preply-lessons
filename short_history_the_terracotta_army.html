The Terracotta Army

An emperor who conquered a world he could not keep — and built a second one to rule forever.

1. The emperor sat above kings and generals alike; one word from him could raise a city or flatten it, and across his vast lands no one ranked higher.
an emperor (n) — the supreme ruler of an empire, ranking above kings.
2. Terrified of growing old, he sent men to the ends of the earth to find a potion that would grant him immortality — so that, like the gods, he might never die.

3. Bent over his bench for ten hours a day, the artisan shaped the clay by hand, fired it in the kiln, and painted every detail until the figure looked almost alive.

5. The statue was life-sized: when the visitor stood beside it, the two of them were exactly the same height, eye to eye.

6. After the funeral they buried the chest deep in the earth, covered it over with soil, and left no marker, so that no one would ever find what lay beneath.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The man who made China

He was born in 259 BC and named Ying Zheng, the son of a king of Qin, one of seven states that had been at war with one another for centuries. He became king of Qin as a boy of thirteen. For the next twenty-five years, his armies fought their way across the land, swallowing rival after rival, until in 221 BC the last of them fell. For the first time in its history, China was one country, and one man ruled all of it.

He gave himself a new title to match: Qin Shi Huang — the First Emperor. And he ruled as ruthlessly as he had conquered. He standardised the writing, the coins, the weights and even the width of cart axles, so that the whole empire ran the same way. He linked together the northern walls of the old states into an early version of what would become the Great Wall. He also burned books he disliked and crushed anyone who questioned him. China was united, but it was held together by fear.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Ying Zheng change China by 221 BC, and what new title did he take?

2. Name two things he standardised across the empire.

1. His armies conquered the six rival states and united China into one country for the first time; he took the title Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor.

2. Any two of: the writing system, the coins, the weights, the width of cart axles. (He also joined up the northern walls.)

Discuss:
  • He unified a whole civilisation but ruled through fear and censorship. Can a leader do great and terrible things at the same time — and how should history judge such a person?

Part 2 — The fear of dying

Having won everything a man could win, the First Emperor wanted one thing more: never to lose it. He became obsessed with living forever. He sent expeditions out to sea in search of a magical island said to hold the secret of immortality, and he swallowed potions his physicians promised would keep death away. Some of those potions, it is thought, contained mercury — the very poison that may have been quietly killing him.

If he could not live forever in this world, he would rule forever in the next. So, from almost the moment he took the throne, he had begun preparing a tomb on a scale no one had ever attempted. Beneath an artificial hill near his capital, an underground palace was dug out, large enough to be a city of the dead. According to the historian Sima Qian, writing a century later, the tomb held a model of his whole empire, with the great rivers and the sea recreated in flowing mercury, and the heavens painted on the ceiling above. To guard this buried kingdom, the emperor ordered an army — not of men, but of clay.

By the time the emperor died in 210 BC, an estimated 700,000 labourers had been working on his tomb for nearly forty years. He was buried inside it, in secret. To keep its layout hidden, many of those who had built it are said to have been sealed in with him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the emperor do to try to escape death?

2. According to Sima Qian, what was inside the tomb?

1. He sent expeditions to find a magical island and the secret of immortality, and took potions meant to keep death away — some thought to contain mercury.

2. A model of his whole empire: the rivers and sea made of flowing mercury, the heavens painted on the ceiling, and an army of clay to guard him.

Discuss:
  • He poured the wealth of an empire and the lives of thousands into preparing for death. What does a person's tomb — or how they want to be remembered — reveal about how they lived?

Part 3 — Out of the ground

For more than two thousand years the army stood in the dark, forgotten. The dynasty the emperor had built to last "ten thousand generations" collapsed within four years of his death. The location of the tomb faded out of memory, and the fields above it were ploughed and planted like any other.

Then, in March 1974, a group of farmers digging a well near the city of Xi'an struck something hard. As they cleared the earth, they began to unearth pieces of fired clay — and then a head, staring up at them out of the ground. Archaeologists who came to investigate could hardly believe what they found. The figures had been standing in the dark for over two thousand years before those farmers stumbled on them. Pit after pit was opened, revealing an entire army: an estimated 8,000 life-sized soldiers, along with horses and wooden chariots, drawn up in battle formation.

What astonished the world was the detail. From a distance the ranks looked uniform, but no two faces are the same. Each soldier has his own features, his own expression, his own hairstyle and rank. Once, they had been brightly painted; much of the colour faded within minutes of meeting the air. The central tomb itself, under its hill, has still never been opened — partly out of respect, and partly because soil tests around it show dangerously high levels of mercury, just as the ancient historian described. The emperor who longed to rule forever did, in a sense, get his wish: 2,200 years on, his silent army still stands guard, and the world has never stopped staring back.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How was the army rediscovered, and when?

2. Give two reasons the central tomb has still never been opened.

1. In March 1974, farmers digging a well near Xi'an struck the buried figures and unearthed the first pieces of clay, including a head.

2. Out of respect for the burial, and because soil tests show dangerously high levels of mercury around it (matching Sima Qian's account).

Discuss:
  • The painted colours vanished within minutes of being exposed to the air. If digging deeper might destroy what's there, is it ever better to leave the past buried?

Grammar — Past Perfect Continuous (had been doing)

This story is full of things that went on for a very long time before something else happened — labourers working for forty years, soldiers standing in the dark for twenty centuries. To say that cleanly in English, you reach for the past perfect continuous: had been + -ing.

What it does, in one line:
The past perfect continuous describes an action that had been going on up to a point in the past — and it puts the spotlight on how long it lasted (its duration).
• "By the time the emperor died, thousands of labourers had been working on his tomb for nearly forty years."
• "The figures had been standing in the dark for over two thousand years before farmers stumbled on them in 1974."
Form: had been + verb-ing — the same for every subject (I/you/he/we/they had been working).
Watch for these signals: for (forty years, two thousand years), since, and especially by the time… / before…, which mark the later point in the past that the long action ran up to.
Don't confuse it with the past perfect simple (had done):
• Continuous (had been doing) stresses the activity and its length: "the artisans had been working for years" — we picture the ongoing effort.
• Simple (had done) stresses the completed result or how many: "the artisans had made 8,000 figures" — we picture the finished total.
Rule of thumb: how long? → had been doing. finished / how many? → had done.

1. Make the past perfect continuous

Put the verb into had been + -ing. Notice the duration each sentence is pointing to.

1. By 221 BC the states of China (fight) one another for centuries.

2. When he finally died, the emperor (search) for the secret of immortality for years.

3. The clay soldiers (stand) underground for over two thousand years before anyone found them.

4. Before the well was dug, the farmers (work) the same fields for generations.

1. had been fighting ("for centuries" → duration up to 221 BC)

2. had been searching ("for years" up to the point he died)

3. had been standing ("for over two thousand years" before being found)

4. had been working ("for generations" up to the moment of digging)

2. Continuous or simple? (had been doing vs. had done)

Choose the form the meaning needs — duration of the activity, or the completed result.

1. By the time the tomb was sealed, the workers (build) it for nearly forty years.

2. By the time the tomb was sealed, the workers (make) some 8,000 figures.

3. The colour faded fast because the paint (dry) underground for two thousand years.

4. Archaeologists realised the farmers (uncover) something no one had ever seen.

1. had been building (how long? → continuous)

2. had made (how many / finished total → simple)

3. had been drying (duration of the activity → continuous)

4. had uncovered (a completed discovery → simple)

3. Put it together — narrate a paragraph

Fill each gap with the past perfect continuous or the past perfect simple, whichever the meaning needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

By the time Qin Shi Huang came to the throne, the warring states (tear) China apart for hundreds of years. Once he was emperor, he (already / decide) how he wanted to be buried. For decades his labourers (dig) and shaping the underground city, and by 210 BC they (complete) an army of clay. The figures (wait) in the dark for over twenty centuries before a farmer's spade finally (find) them.

had been tearing (duration) · had already decided (completed) · had been digging (duration) · had completed (finished result) · had been waiting (duration) · found (the single later event — past simple)

Notice the pattern: when you want the length of an ongoing action, use had been doing; when you want a finished result, use had done; and the moment that ends it is usually plain past simple.
