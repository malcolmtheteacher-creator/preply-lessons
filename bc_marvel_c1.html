The Heroes in the Basement

A bankrupt comic publisher sold its most famous characters to survive — then borrowed against the ones nobody wanted and built the most successful film series ever made.

Easier version: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll work through a comeback that began in a bankruptcy court — and you'll practise participle clauses, the structure that lets a report or a pitch pack cause, time and background into one tight sentence: having lost the rights…, backed by a bank…, not owning its best-known heroes…
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • Have you ever had to sell or give up something valuable to keep a project alive? Would you make the same choice again?
  • Is it better to own a small thing completely, or a share of something much bigger that someone else controls?
  • Think of a product or team that everyone considered second-best. What would it take to make it the main event?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Collectors were buying five copies of the same comic, sure the prices would keep rising. Publishers printed special covers to feed the demand. When buyers realised the copies were not rare at all, prices collapsed. It had been a bubble.
a bubble (n) — a period when prices rise far above real value because people expect them to keep rising, followed by a sudden collapse. A speculative bubble, the bubble burst, the dot-com bubble.
2. The company owed hundreds of millions to banks and bondholders. In court, these creditors fought over who would control what was left of the business.
a creditor (n) — a person or organisation that is owed money. The opposite is a debtor. Collocations: to repay creditors, creditor protection, a meeting of creditors.
3. The bank agreed to lend over half a billion dollars. But if the films failed to earn enough, it would be able to take the film rights to ten of the characters. The rights were the loan's collateral.
collateral (n, uncountable) — an asset a borrower pledges to a lender, which the lender can take if the loan is not repaid. To put up collateral, a loan secured against / backed by property.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: franchise · intellectual property · box office. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means creations of the mind — characters, stories, brands, designs, inventions — that the law allows someone to own?
intellectual property (n) — usually shortened to IP in the entertainment and technology industries: “The studio owns the IP.” Protected by copyright, trademarks and patents.
2. Which term means the money a film earns from cinema ticket sales?
box office (n, often used as an adjective) — box-office takings, a box-office hit / flop, the film took $500 million at the box office.
3. Which term means a series of connected films, books or products built around the same characters or brand?
a franchise (n) — in entertainment, a series that can be extended again and again. Careful: in retail, a franchise is a licence to run a business under another company's brand, like a fast-food restaurant.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Unable to pay its debts, the company filed for Chapter 11 in December 1996. Filing for Chapter 11 means…
  (a) closing the business and selling everything   (b) asking a US court for protection from creditors while the business reorganises   (c) being taken over by the government
(b) — Chapter 11: the section of US bankruptcy law that lets a company keep operating while it restructures its debts. Closing and selling everything is Chapter 7, or liquidation. Outside the US, people say administration or insolvency proceedings.
2. Critics said the studio was releasing so many films and series that none of them felt special any more. They warned of brand dilution. Brand dilution is…
  (a) the weakening of a brand's value because it is spread across too many products or of uneven quality   (b) selling part of a brand to a competitor   (c) changing a brand's name in a new market
(a) — brand dilution (n). Also used in finance: share dilution is when new shares reduce the percentage owned by existing shareholders. Verb: to dilute.

D · Collocation & word building

1. The famous heroes had gone to other studios. The characters left were what fans called second-___. Complete the compound, and give its opposite.
second-tier (adj) — not in the top level; less important or less well-known. Opposite: top-tier or first-tier. Business uses: tier-one suppliers, second-tier cities, a two-tier system.
2. A minor character from one film was given his own series. It was a spin-___. Complete the word, and say how the same word is used for companies.
a spin-off (n) — a new production based on characters from an existing one. In business, a spin-off is a new company created from a division of an existing one: “The consumer division was spun off as a separate company.”

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — The bubble and the court

In the early 1990s, comic books became an investment. Collectors bought multiple copies of the same issue, expecting them to become valuable; publishers answered with special covers, numbered editions and endless relaunches. Marvel, owner of Spider-Man, the X-Men and hundreds of other characters, rode the boom — and, under the financier Ron Perelman, borrowed heavily to buy a trading-card company and its own distribution business.

Then the bubble burst. By the mid-1990s buyers had realised that comics printed in millions were never going to be rare. Sales fell sharply, thousands of specialist comic shops closed, and Marvel's attempt to control distribution went badly wrong. In December 1996 the company filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection. For almost two years, powerful financiers and creditors fought in court over who would own it. The fight ended in 1998 with a merger between Marvel and Toy Biz, the toy company that held a licence to make its action figures.

Throughout the crisis, Marvel had been doing what companies in trouble do: selling what others would pay for. The film rights to its most famous characters had been licensed to Hollywood studios — Spider-Man to Sony, the X-Men and the Fantastic Four to Fox, and others elsewhere. When those films finally arrived, some were enormous hits. Spider-Man, in 2002, took around 800 million dollars worldwide. Marvel, which owned the character, received only a small share of that money and had no control over the films.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What caused the collapse of the comic-book market in the mid-1990s?

2. Why did the success of films like Spider-Man not rescue Marvel's finances?

1. A speculative bubble: collectors bought comics as investments, publishers flooded the market with special editions, and when buyers realised mass-printed comics would never be rare, prices and sales collapsed. Marvel's heavy borrowing and distribution problems made it worse.

2. The film rights had been licensed to other studios, so the studios kept most of the revenue and all the creative control; Marvel received only a small share.

Discuss:
  • Selling your most valuable assets can keep you alive and still leave you poorer for decades. When is that trade worth making? Think of an example from your own sector.

Part 2 — Borrowing against the leftovers

By the mid-2000s, Marvel's leaders — including the toy executive Isaac Perlmutter and the producer Avi Arad — had a radical idea. Instead of licensing characters to others for a small fee, Marvel would make its own films and keep the profits. The problem was money: a single superhero film cost well over a hundred million dollars, and Marvel did not have it.

In 2005 Marvel arranged a loan of around 525 million dollars from the investment bank Merrill Lynch. The collateral was the film rights to about ten characters, including Captain America and the Avengers. If the films failed, the lenders could take those rights. In effect, Marvel was pledging a large part of its remaining future on the ability of a studio with no film-making experience to turn heroes that most cinema-goers had never heard of into stars.

The first film was Iron Man, released in May 2008. Its star, Robert Downey Jr., was a gifted actor whose career had been damaged by well-publicised personal problems years earlier; the director, Jon Favreau, was known mainly for comedies. The film took around 585 million dollars worldwide. A short scene after the credits hinted that Iron Man would one day join a team of heroes. Under a young producer, Kevin Feige, the studio planned its films as chapters of one connected story. In 2009 Disney agreed to buy the whole of Marvel for around four billion dollars.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Marvel use as collateral for its 2005 loan, and what was the risk?

2. Why was Iron Man a risky first film, apart from the money?

1. The film rights to about ten characters, including Captain America and the Avengers. If the films did not earn enough, the lender could take those rights — Marvel could lose valuable characters permanently.

2. The character was not widely known outside comic readers, the lead actor's career had suffered from past personal problems, the director was known for comedies, and Marvel had never made a film itself.

Discuss:
  • Marvel built its future on its “second-tier” characters because the famous ones were gone. Where could a constraint in your organisation become an advantage if you were forced to work with what is left?

Part 3 — The universe that got too big

What followed was the most commercially successful film series in history. The Avengers, in 2012, took around one and a half billion dollars. By the time Avengers: Endgame was released in 2019, earning around 2.8 billion, the connected series had made more than twenty films. Slowly, the lost characters came home: Sony agreed in 2015 to share Spider-Man with Marvel's studio, and in 2019 Disney's purchase of most of Fox's film business brought back the rights to the X-Men and the Fantastic Four.

Then the model started to strain. Disney launched its streaming service and asked Marvel for a steady flow of television series alongside the films. Output rose sharply. Some projects were praised; others were criticised for rushed special effects and stories that seemed to require viewers to have watched everything else. In 2023 The Marvels became the lowest-earning film in the series. Disney's chief executive said publicly that the studio had been making too much, and output was cut back. In 2024 Deadpool & Wolverine, built around two of the returned characters, took well over a billion dollars.

So the comeback has two lessons that seem to pull in opposite directions. The first: a crisis forced Marvel to build a system around modest assets, and the system turned out to be worth more than any single famous hero. The second: the same system, fed too fast, became a burden — every new release felt like homework, and the brand risked becoming ordinary. The question now is whether a franchise that became a universe can become special again, or whether it has simply been used up.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Marvel recover the rights to characters it had licensed away?

2. What problems did the studio face in the 2020s, and how did the company respond?

1. Through a 2015 agreement with Sony to share Spider-Man, and through Disney's 2019 purchase of most of Fox's film business, which returned the X-Men and the Fantastic Four.

2. A sharp rise in output for streaming led to uneven quality and a feeling that audiences had to watch everything; a film in 2023 earned the least in the series. Disney's chief executive admitted the studio had made too much, and output was reduced.

Discuss:
  • Many successful products are extended until customers get tired of them. How does your organisation know when to stop extending something that is still making money?

Grammar — participle clauses

Reports, investor letters and news summaries all have the same problem: too much background, too few sentences. Participle clauses solve it. They let you put the reason, the timing or the condition into the front of a sentence without because, after or which was — and they sound instantly more formal.

1 · Present participle (-ing) — same time, or reason
• “Knowing that the market was weak, the board delayed the launch.” (= because it knew)
• “Working with half the usual budget, the team finished on time.” (= while it was working)
• Negative: “Not wanting to lose the contract, the supplier cut its price.”
2 · Past participle (-ed / irregular) — passive meaning
• “Backed by a private-equity fund, the chain expanded into six countries.” (= because it was backed)
• “Asked about the delay, the spokesperson declined to comment.” (= when she was asked)
3 · Perfect participle (having + past participle) — the earlier action
• “Having lost its biggest client, the agency cut a third of its staff.”
• Passive: “Having been rejected twice, the proposal was rewritten.”

The trap — the dangling participle. The participle clause must describe the subject of the main clause. “Walking into the meeting, the numbers looked terrible.” The numbers did not walk. Correct: “Walking into the meeting, I saw the numbers — and they looked terrible.”

1. Combine the sentences

Write only the missing words so that the new sentence has the same meaning.

1. Marvel had sold the rights to its best-known characters. It had to rely on lesser-known heroes. → the rights to its best-known characters, Marvel had to rely on lesser-known heroes.

2. The studio was financed by a large bank loan. It could make its own films for the first time. → by a large bank loan, the studio could make its own films for the first time.

3. The producers realised that audiences enjoyed the links between films. They planned one connected story. → that audiences enjoyed the links between films, the producers planned one connected story.

3. The investor letter — complete it, then read it aloud

A studio in a similar position writes to its investors. Complete the paragraph using the verb and form in brackets. Then read it aloud as if presenting it on a results call.

“ (emerge — earlier action) from a long period of financial difficulty, the company now controls its own future. (secure — passive) against the rights to ten characters, the new financing allows us to produce our own films. (not / own) the characters audiences know best, we will build a connected series around the ones they will come to know. (plan — passive) as one story, each film will support the next. And, (learn — earlier action) from a decade of licensing our heroes to others, we intend to keep their value inside the company.”

Going deeper

A note on the figures: box-office takings, loan amounts and purchase prices are rounded and taken from public reporting, and they are not adjusted for inflation. The studio's strategy is still developing.

What the case actually tests. The first reading is that the crisis created the strategy. Having licensed its famous heroes away, Marvel could not make a traditional big-star superhero film. It was forced to invest in the system instead of the individual: a single producer with control over the whole series, characters designed to connect, and stories that made audiences care about the next film as much as the current one. The lesser-known heroes were a weakness that became a design principle — no single character was bigger than the universe.

The second reading says the gamble could easily have failed and is being remembered backwards. Pledging the rights to ten characters as collateral put the company's future in the hands of a first film with an unknown hero, a risky casting choice and a first-time studio. If Iron Man had disappointed, the same decisions would now be taught as a warning about reckless borrowing. The success also depended on things no strategy can guarantee: the right actor, the right tone, and a gifted producer. Survivorship bias is strong here — we do not study the studios that borrowed against their characters and lost them.

A third point is that the model's strength became its weakness. A connected universe needs every new product to protect the whole, but a parent company with a streaming service to fill needed volume. What had been scarce and planned became constant and rushed. The lesson may be that a system can protect a brand from any single failure but not from over-production — and that the most dangerous moment for a comeback is when it is finally rich enough to do everything.
Talk it through:
  • Constraint or luck? Take one side and argue it using at least two facts from the case.
  • Which of your organisation's assets would a lender accept as collateral? Would you ever pledge them — and who would have to agree?
  • Marvel built value in the connections between products rather than in any single one. Where could your organisation do the same — and what would it have to stop doing?
  • Every successful formula is eventually over-used. What early warning signs would tell you that your own best product is starting to feel like homework to customers?

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

It is 2005. You are presenting the financing plan. A lender's risk officer says: “You want over half a billion dollars to make films about heroes nobody outside comic shops has heard of, and you're offering us your remaining characters if it fails. Your company was bankrupt eight years ago. Why is this not the same mistake with bigger numbers?”

Answer properly: acknowledge the history, separate the old mistake from the new plan, explain how the risk is limited, and end with a clear reason to lend. Then do it again in ninety seconds — the compression is the exercise.

Task C · Four rooms, one big bet (4 minutes)

Take one high-risk plan to rebuild after a failure — real or invented — and present it four ways, one minute each. Try to open each version with a participle clause.

  • To the lenders: what they are exposed to, and what protects them.
  • To the board: why this risk, now, is better than slow decline.
  • To the creative team or staff: why they should stay, and what will be different this time.
  • To customers or fans: what they will get, without promising what you cannot control.
