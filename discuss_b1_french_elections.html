What actually happens next tap to reveal

No. A is not president. In France, 34% is not enough. You need more than half the country — over 50%.

So two weeks later the country votes again, and this time there are only two names on the paper: A and B. C and D are gone.

Which means every single person who voted for C or D now has to choose between two people they didn't want. That second vote is where French elections are really decided.

And here's the strange part

Since French voters started choosing their president directly in 1965, this has happened every single time. Not one president has ever won it outright in the first vote. Not once in sixty years.

The next presidential election is in spring 2027, followed by parliamentary elections that June.

The machine, in four numbers

500

Signatures to get on the paper

Before anyone can even appear on the ballot, 500 elected officials — mostly mayors — have to sign for them, spread across at least 30 different departments. Plenty of people who want to run never get that far.

2

Rounds of voting

Round one: everybody who made the ballot. Round two, a fortnight later: only the top two. Win round two and the job is yours, even by a single vote.

5 × 2

Five years, twice, and that's it

A president serves five years. They can be re-elected once. After two terms in a row they have to step down — no matter how popular they are.

577

Seats in the National Assembly

The president isn't the whole story. A few weeks after the presidential vote, the country elects 577 members of parliament — also in two rounds. Whoever controls those seats controls what actually gets passed.

The problem with talking about politics in a second language

You know what you think. But in English it comes out flat and hard — "That's wrong." — and suddenly you're in an argument you didn't want.

Compare these. Same opinions. Very different effect.

Lands like a punch

"Politicians are all liars."

"Nobody my age cares."

"That's wrong."

"The system is stupid."

Opens a conversation

"The way I see it, it's hard to trust anything said in an election year."

"I tend to think most people my age have given up expecting much."

"I see what you mean, but I'm not sure the numbers say that."

"Fair enough, but I'd change one part of it."

What is the right-hand column doing that the left-hand one isn't? Work it out before you open this. tap to reveal

It marks the opinion as yours, not as a fact about the world. "The way I see it" is a small flag that says: this is my view, you're allowed a different one.

And on the disagreements, it gives the other person something first — "I see what you mean" — before the "but". You lose nothing. You just don't corner them.

In a conversation about politics, that's the difference between a discussion and a row.

Six phrases. That's all.

Giving your view without sounding certain

The way I see it, ... I tend to think (that) ... As far as I know, ...

Disagreeing without a fight

I see what you mean, but ... That's true up to a point, but ... Fair enough, but ...

Careful with one: "As far as I know" is for facts you're unsure of — "As far as I know, the second round is in April." It doesn't work for opinions. You can't say "As far as I know, voting should be compulsory."

You're the journalist

A candidate is standing in front of you. You get three questions. They will try to answer without saying anything — that's the job.

Your job is the opposite: when an answer is vague, don't let it go. Use one of the six phrases and push again.

Explain it back

Speak

Imagine explaining this to someone who has never heard of any of it.

  • Why does France use two rounds instead of just giving the job to whoever comes first? What problem is that solving?
  • What does the system cost you? Every design has a downside — what's this one's?
One way of putting it — open this after you've had your own go tap to reveal

The problem it solves: with one round, someone can win with 34% while 66% of the country wanted somebody else. Two rounds forces the winner to be acceptable to more than half.

The cost: by round two most people are choosing the least bad option rather than the one they wanted. Some argue that's not really a choice at all — and in 2002 the second round produced a winner with more than 82%, not because he was hugely popular, but because voters from every side united against the other option.

Both things are true at the same time. That's what makes it worth arguing about.

Odd one out

Which phrase doesn't belong with the others, and why? Say your reason before you open it.

The way I see it  ·  I tend to think  ·  As far as I know  ·  If you ask me

Check your reason tap to reveal

As far as I know. The other three introduce an opinion. This one introduces a fact you're not certain of.

"As far as I know, the election is in April" works. "As far as I know, the system is unfair" doesn't — that's an opinion, and you can't be uncertain about whether you hold it.

Three different ways to talk about the future in English — and when you'd use each. tap to reveal

will — a decision made now, or a prediction: I'll vote for her.

going to — a plan you already had, or evidence you can see: I'm going to register next week.

present continuous — a fixed arrangement: The election is taking place in April.

What's the difference between "I've been to Paris" and "I went to Paris"? tap to reveal

I've been to Paris — some time in my life, no date attached; the point is the experience.

I went to Paris — a finished moment in the past, usually with a when: I went to Paris last summer.

Add a finished time and English forces the past simple. That's why "I've been there last year" is wrong.

Past simple and past participle of: choose, win, speak, hold. tap to reveal

choose → chose → chosen
win → won → won
speak → spoke → spoken
hold → held → held

Finish it in the second conditional, out loud: "If I could change one law, I ..." tap to reveal

The shape is: If + past simple, would + infinitive.

"If I could change one law, I would lower the voting age to sixteen."

The past tense here isn't about the past — it marks the whole thing as imaginary.
