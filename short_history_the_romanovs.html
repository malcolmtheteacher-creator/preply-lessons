The Romanovs

Three hundred years of empire, ended in a single Siberian night — and a mystery that outlived them all.

Today: you'll tell the story of how a 300-year dynasty fell in a single year — and explore the regrets and "what ifs" that still hang over it, using conditionals and the language of wishes.

Key words — guess first, then sort

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were. The words are in a mixed order on purpose.

1. For three hundred years the same dynasty sat on the throne, the crown passing quietly from grandfather to father to son.
a dynasty (n) — a line of rulers who all come from the same family.
2. A trusted adviser can hold so much influence at court that he quietly shapes decisions far beyond his own rank.
influence (n) — the power to affect what people think or do, often quietly and indirectly.
3. His haemophilia turned a scraped knee into a crisis; the family held its breath for days, watching to see whether the bleeding would finally stop.
haemophilia (n) — an inherited illness in which the blood does not clot properly, so bleeding is hard to stop.
4. Weeds pushed through the cracks and the roof sagged year by year, until the old mansion began to crumble, dropping bricks into the long grass.
to crumble (v) — to break apart slowly and fall to pieces; to collapse bit by bit.
5. When the people of the capital rose up, the revolution swept away the old order and put entirely new men in power.
a revolution (n) — a sudden, forced overthrow of a government or social order, often by the people.
6. For two long years the prisoners lived in captivity, locked behind a high fence, allowed into the yard only when an armed guard stood at the gate.
captivity (n) — the state of being kept as a prisoner; not being free.
7. With his generals against him and the crowds at the gates, the exhausted king signed the paper and chose to abdicate, handing the crown to his brother before sunset.
to abdicate (v) — to formally give up a throne or position of power.
8. From his very first birthday the little boy was dressed in miniature uniforms and taught to bow, for everyone at court knew he was the heir to the throne.
an heir (n) — the person who has the legal right to inherit a title, throne or property.
9. Down the centuries, more than one impostor has impersonated a vanished royal, telling the story so convincingly that many believed it.
to impersonate (v) — to pretend to be another person in order to deceive others.
10. Scientists can compare remains found in a hidden grave with living relatives to confirm exactly who lies buried there.
remains (n, plural) — the body, or parts of the body, of a person who has died.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The last of the line

By 1894, the Romanovs had ruled Russia for almost three hundred years. The dynasty had begun in 1613, and across the centuries it had produced figures as towering as Peter the Great and Catherine the Great. The new emperor, Nicholas II, inherited the largest land empire on earth — and almost none of his ancestors' iron will. Gentle, devoted to his family, happiest away from politics, he was, by most accounts, a kind man and a poor ruler, who clung to the idea that God alone had made him an absolute autocrat.

He married Alexandra, a German princess and a granddaughter of Britain's Queen Victoria. Theirs was a genuine love match, but Alexandra was shy, intense and deeply religious, and the Russian court never warmed to her. The couple had four daughters — Olga, Tatiana, Maria and Anastasia — and at last, in 1904, the son they had longed for: Alexei, the heir.

But the boy had inherited something else from Victoria's bloodline — haemophilia. His blood would not clot, and a bruise or a fall could threaten his life. The parents kept his condition a state secret, and a desperate Alexandra turned to anyone who seemed able to help. Into that fear stepped Grigori Rasputin, a wandering peasant mystic who appeared, again and again, to calm the boy's bleeding when doctors could not. To the empress he became indispensable — and through her, this strange, unwashed holy man gained an extraordinary influence over the affairs of an empire.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What kind of ruler was Nicholas II, and what did he inherit?

2. How did a peasant mystic come to hold such influence over the imperial family?

1. He inherited the vast Russian empire and the role of absolute autocrat, but he was a gentle, family-loving man and a weak, indecisive ruler, convinced his power came directly from God.

2. Their only son and heir, Alexei, had haemophilia; Rasputin seemed able to calm his bleeding when doctors could not, so the desperate Empress Alexandra came to trust him — and he gained influence through her.

Discuss:
  • A loving father can still be a disastrous ruler. Should we judge a leader by their private virtues, or only by their decisions in power?

Part 2 — The year everything fell

When Russia entered the First World War in 1914, the strain was more than the empire could bear. Millions of poorly equipped soldiers were killed; the cities went hungry; the railways and the economy buckled. Disastrously, Nicholas left the capital to take personal command of the army — which meant that when things went wrong at the front, the blame now fell on him directly. He left Alexandra, and behind her Rasputin, effectively running the government. By the end of 1916 a group of nobles, hoping to save the monarchy, murdered Rasputin. It changed nothing.

In March 1917, bread queues in the capital turned into strikes, and strikes turned into a revolution. The soldiers sent to crush the crowds joined them instead. Within days the unthinkable had happened: Nicholas, isolated and abandoned even by his generals, abdicated the throne — not only for himself but for his sick son. After three centuries, the Romanov dynasty had simply ceased to rule. The family were placed under house arrest while the new Provisional Government decided what to do with them.

That government did not last either. In October 1917 the Bolsheviks, led by Lenin, seized power in a second revolution and pulled Russia out of the war. For the former emperor and his family, this was catastrophic. They were moved east — first to Tobolsk in Siberia, then, in 1918, to a merchant's house in Yekaterinburg in the Urals. They were now in the hands of men who saw them not as people to be protected, but as a dangerous symbol that must not be allowed to fall into enemy hands.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the First World War help bring down the monarchy?

2. What happened to the family between the two revolutions of 1917?

1. The war brought huge military losses, hunger and economic collapse; when Nicholas took personal command of the army, the failures fell on him directly, and the hardship at home boiled over into the February/March revolution.

2. After the first revolution Nicholas abdicated (for himself and his son) and the family were put under house arrest; after the Bolsheviks seized power in October, they were moved east to Tobolsk and then to Yekaterinburg.

Discuss:
  • A 300-year dynasty collapsed in a matter of days. Do great institutions fall because of one final push, or because they were already hollow inside?

Part 3 — The grave and the ghost

In the early hours of 17 July 1918, with anti-Bolshevik forces closing in on the city, the family and the few servants who had stayed with them were woken, taken to a cellar room, and killed. Nicholas, Alexandra, their four daughters and their thirteen-year-old son all died that night. The bodies were taken away in secret and buried in the forest, and for decades the new Soviet state said almost nothing about exactly what had happened.

That silence left room for one of the twentieth century's strangest legends. Because the burial was hidden, rumours spread that someone — most often the youngest daughter, Anastasia — had somehow survived. In 1920 a young woman in Germany began to claim that she was Anastasia. Known as Anna Anderson, she impersonated the lost princess for the rest of her life, won the belief of some who had known the family, and inspired films, books and decades of legal argument. She was almost certainly a Polish factory worker, but while she lived, the mystery could not quite be closed.

In the end, science closed it. After the fall of the Soviet Union, a forest grave near Yekaterinburg was opened in 1991, and DNA testing — using samples from living relatives, including Britain's Prince Philip — confirmed that these were the Romanovs. Two children were still missing, fuelling the legends a little longer, until their remains were found nearby in 2007 and identified too. The same testing showed that Anna Anderson had no Romanov blood at all. The family that had ruled for three centuries was, at last, accounted for — and the ghost of Anastasia was finally laid to rest.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the family's fate a mystery for so long?

2. How were the rumours of a survivor finally put to rest?

1. The family were killed in secret in July 1918 and buried in a hidden forest grave, and the Soviet state revealed little — so for decades no one could be sure exactly what had happened, leaving room for rumours of survivors.

2. After 1991 the grave was opened and DNA testing (using living relatives) confirmed the remains were the Romanovs; the last two children were found in 2007, and the tests also proved Anna Anderson was not Anastasia.

Discuss:
  • For seventy years, people wanted to believe a princess had survived. Why do we so often prefer a hopeful legend to a hard fact?

The grammar this story runs on: conditionals & wishes

Few stories invite the words "if only" like this one. To talk about what might have been — and the regret that hangs over it — English uses conditionals and the language of wishes.

The tools:
• Second conditional — an unreal present or general situation: "If Nicholas were a stronger ruler, the empire might be safer." (if + past simple, would/might + verb)
• Third conditional — an unreal past; things we can no longer change: "If Nicholas had reformed sooner, the dynasty might have survived." (if + past perfect, would/might have + past participle)
• Wish / if only + past perfect — regret about the past: "The family wished they had left Russia." / "If only they had accepted Britain's offer."

1. Third conditional — the great "what ifs"

Use if + past perfect and would/might have + past participle for a past that can no longer be changed.

1. If Nicholas (reform) the country sooner, the dynasty (might / survive).

2. If the family (leave) Russia in 1917, they (would / escape) the cellar in Yekaterinburg.

3. If the burial (not / be) hidden, the legend of Anastasia (might / never / begin).

1. had reformed / might have survived

2. had left / would have escaped

3. had not been / might never have begun

2. Wish & if only — the language of regret

For regret about the past, use wished / if only + past perfect (had + past participle).

1. Looking back, the family wished they (accept) Britain's offer of safety.

2. Many Russians later felt that if only the emperor (listen) to his people, so much suffering could have been avoided.

3. Alexandra surely wished she (never / trust) Rasputin so completely.

1. had accepted

2. had listened

3. had never trusted

3. Second vs third — present unreal or past unreal?

Choose the form the meaning needs. The second conditional imagines a present or general situation; the third reaches back into a finished past. Read each aloud once you've finished.

1. If absolute power (be) a wise way to rule, history would look very different. (general, unreal — second)

2. If the war (not / break out) in 1914, the monarchy (might / last) longer. (unreal past — third)

3. If we (have) no DNA science today, the mystery (would / remain) unsolved. (present unreal — second)

1. were (second conditional)

2. had not broken out / might have lasted (third conditional)

3. had / would remain (second conditional)

Notice: the past perfect after "if" is the signal of an unreal past; the simple past after "if" is the signal of an unreal present.
