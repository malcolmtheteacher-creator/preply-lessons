👤 First Impressions

The science of snap judgments

C1 Advanced

How fast do you really judge someone?

"You never get a second chance to make a good first impression."
— Will Rogers, American humorist

We've all heard this advice. But here's the uncomfortable truth: science shows that by the time you've walked into a room, people have already made up their minds about you.

100 milliseconds
The time it takes your brain to form a first impression — that's 1/10th of a second
The Science: In 2006, Princeton psychologists Janine Willis and Alexander Todorov conducted a groundbreaking study. They showed people photographs of faces for just 100 milliseconds — barely enough time to consciously register what you're seeing. Yet participants had already formed clear judgments about trustworthiness, competence, and likeability. Most remarkably? When given more time to look, their opinions barely changed. They just became more confident in their snap judgments.
7 seconds
The time a job candidate has to make a positive first impression

This creates some fascinating — and troubling — consequences.

Consider this: In a 2005 study published in Science, Todorov and his colleagues showed that snap judgments of politicians' faces could predict election outcomes. Participants who had never seen the candidates before looked at photos for just a second and rated them on "competence." Those ratings predicted the actual winners about 70% of the time. Voters weren't choosing policies — they were choosing faces.
55% / 38% / 7%
First impressions are 55% visual, 38% vocal tone, and only 7% your actual words
The Interview Problem: Research shows that 33% of hiring managers decide whether to hire someone within the first 90 seconds of an interview. One study found that 55% of recruiters base their decision partly on a candidate's handshake, and 40% are influenced by whether or not they like the candidate's smile. Before a single question about qualifications is asked, the decision may already be forming.
Think about it: When was the last time you formed a strong impression of someone — and later discovered you were completely wrong? What had misled you?

Language for Talking About Impressions

Click each expression to learn more and practise using it.

1. "to come across as [adjective]"
Formality: Neutral — works in any context
Meaning: To give a particular impression to others. This is how other people perceive you, which may or may not match how you see yourself. Often used to talk about the gap between intention and perception.
"She's actually very friendly, but she can come across as reserved when you first meet her."

"I was trying to be confident, but I think I came across as arrogant."
NOW YOU TRY: How do you think you come across to strangers? Is that different from how your close friends see you?
2. "to give off [noun] vibes" / "to give the impression of being..."
Formality: "give off vibes" = informal/modern; "give the impression of" = neutral/formal
Meaning: To project a certain feeling or energy. "Vibes" (short for vibrations) refers to the subconscious signals someone sends out. Both phrases describe what others sense about you before they know the facts.
"He gives off really positive vibes — people feel comfortable around him immediately."

"She gives the impression of being very competent, even before she speaks."
NOW YOU TRY: Think of someone you know who gives off really strong vibes (positive or negative). What creates that impression?
3. "to judge someone by [noun/gerund]"
Formality: Neutral — can be used critically or neutrally
Meaning: To form an opinion of someone based on a specific factor. Often used to criticise superficial judgments, but also used neutrally to describe what influences our assessments.
"Don't judge a book by its cover" — but research shows we judge people by their faces in a tenth of a second.

"Recruiters often judge candidates by their handshake and eye contact."
NOW YOU TRY: What do YOU judge people by when you first meet them? Be honest — what's the first thing you notice?

More Language for First Impressions

4. "first impressions can be misleading/deceiving"
Formality: Neutral to formal — excellent for balanced discussions
Meaning: Acknowledges that our initial judgments are often wrong. "Misleading" suggests the impression leads you to wrong conclusions; "deceiving" is slightly stronger, suggesting the impression actively tricks you.
"I thought he was cold and unfriendly, but first impressions can be misleading — he was just nervous."

"She seemed disorganised, but first impressions can be deceiving. She runs a very successful business."
NOW YOU TRY: Tell me about a time when your first impression of someone was completely wrong. What had misled you?
5. "to be a good/bad judge of character"
Formality: Neutral — used in both casual and professional contexts
Meaning: To have (or lack) the ability to accurately assess someone's true personality and intentions. Interestingly, research suggests our first impressions are accurate about 76% of the time — but that means we're wrong nearly a quarter of the time.
"My grandmother is an excellent judge of character — she can tell within minutes if someone is trustworthy."

"I used to think I was a good judge of character, but I've been fooled too many times."
NOW YOU TRY: Do you consider yourself a good judge of character? Can you think of a time when you got it right — or got it spectacularly wrong?
Grammar Note: Notice how "come across as" is followed by an adjective (confident, arrogant, friendly), while "give the impression of" is followed by a gerund (being confident, being arrogant) or noun (competence, warmth).

🎭 The Real You

⚖️ The Interview Dilemma

🌍 Cultural Differences
What creates a good first impression in YOUR culture? Is it eye contact? A firm handshake? A modest demeanour? How might these expectations mislead people from other cultures?
📱 Digital First Impressions
In the age of LinkedIn profiles and video calls, how have first impressions changed? Do you judge people differently online than in person? What do YOU look at first?
🎯 Accuracy vs. Speed
Research suggests our first impressions are accurate 76% of the time. Is that good enough? When does it matter most to get it right — and what are the costs of the 24% when we're wrong?
🔄 Second Chances
If first impressions form in 100 milliseconds, can they ever really be changed? Tell me about someone who completely changed your opinion of them — or vice versa.

Timed Speaking Challenge

Choose a challenge below. You'll have 3-5 minutes to speak. Try to use the expressions from this lesson!

Challenge 1: The Personality Assessment

Look at the personality adjectives you selected earlier. Describe yourself using 6-8 of them. For each one, explain: Why do you think this word fits you? Do other people see this quality in you, or is it hidden? Use: "I think I come across as..." and "People might judge me by..."

Challenge 2: Argue Both Sides

First, argue FOR trusting first impressions: "We should trust our gut instincts about people because..." Then argue AGAINST: "First impressions can be misleading because..." Which side do you actually believe?

Challenge 3: The Wrong Impression

Tell the story of someone you completely misjudged at first. What impression did they give off? What had you judged them by? What changed your mind? Use: "At first, they came across as..." and "My first impression was misleading because..."

Challenge 4: Design Better Hiring

If snap judgments are so unreliable, how should companies hire people? Design a fairer system. What would you keep? What would you change? How would you prevent bias while still assessing personality?
