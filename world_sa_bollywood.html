The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The choreography of a film song can take weeks to prepare.
choreography (n) — the planned movements of a dance. Example: “The choreography of a film song can take weeks to prepare.”
2. A masala film mixes romance, comedy, action and drama.
masala (n) — a mixture of spices; a film style that mixes several genres. Example: “A masala film mixes romance, comedy, action and drama.”
3. The blockbuster broke records at the box office.
blockbuster (n) — a very successful and expensive film. Example: “The blockbuster broke records at the box office.”
4. Actors often lip-sync to a recorded singer's voice.
lip-sync (v) — to move the lips in time with recorded words. Example: “Actors often lip-sync to a recorded singer's voice.”
5. Studios dub films so audiences in other regions can enjoy them.
dub (v) — to replace the original speech in a film with another language. Example: “Studios dub films so audiences in other regions can enjoy them.”
6. Many cinemas in India have an interval after the first half.
interval (n) — a break in the middle of a film or play. Example: “Many cinemas in India have an interval after the first half.”
7. The film industry employs hundreds of thousands of people.
industry (n) — all the businesses that make a particular kind of product. Example: “The film industry employs hundreds of thousands of people.”
8. Films are made for a different audience in each region.
audience (n) — the people who watch or listen to something. Example: “Films are made for a different audience in each region.”

The stereotype: “Indian cinema is just Bollywood — three hours of singing and dancing, and all the films are the same.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — What “Bollywood” means

“Bollywood” is the name for the Hindi-language film industry based in Mumbai, which used to be called Bombay, and the word combines “Bombay” and “Hollywood”. But India makes films in more than twenty languages, and in some years it releases about two thousand films, more than any other country. Telugu, Tamil, Malayalam, Bengali and Marathi cinemas each have their own stars and styles, so Hindi films are only one part of a very large picture.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does “Bollywood” combine?

2. Which other languages have film industries in India?

1. “Bombay” and “Hollywood”.

2. Telugu, Tamil, Malayalam, Bengali, Marathi and more.

Discuss:
  • Which film industries in your own country make films in different languages?

Part 2 — Why the songs?

Songs have been part of Indian films since the first sound film in 1931. Old traditions of storytelling, theatre and folk music made music a natural part of a story. Actors usually lip-sync to recorded voices, as playback singers perform the songs, and these singers can be as famous as the stars. A typical film is long, with an interval, and mixes romance, comedy, drama and action: this is called a masala film, after the spice mixture.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. When was the first Indian sound film made?

2. What does “masala” mean for a film?

1. In 1931.

2. A mix of genres, like romance, comedy, action and drama.

Discuss:
  • Do songs make a story stronger or weaker for you?

Part 3 — Beyond the stereotype

Indian cinema is far more varied. The Bengali director Satyajit Ray made quiet, realistic films such as Pather Panchali in 1955, and many modern films deal with serious subjects. In 2023 the Telugu action film RRR won the Oscar for Best Original Song with “Naatu Naatu”. Southeast Asia also has a lively cinema: Indonesian action, Thai horror and Philippine drama reach audiences around the world. Calling it all “Bollywood” misses most of the picture.

2. Which film won an Oscar for Best Original Song in 2023?

1. Satyajit Ray.

2. RRR, with “Naatu Naatu”.

Grammar — Participle Clauses

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
Participle clauses shorten sentences:
• -ing (active): “Based in Mumbai, the industry makes Hindi films.” → “Working with famous singers, studios record the songs first.”
• -ed (passive): “Made in Telugu, the film won an Oscar.”
• Having + past participle: “Having seen the film, I understood the songs.”

1. Choose the right form

Complete each sentence about this topic.

1. in Mumbai, Bollywood makes films in Hindi. (Base / Based)

2. in Telugu, RRR became famous around the world. (Made / Making)

3. the film once, I wanted to see it again. (Having seen / Seen having)

1. Based

2. Made

3. Having seen

2. Now try these

1. the first sound film in 1931, Indian cinema soon added songs. (Releasing / Having released)

2. The songs, by famous playback singers, are often hits. (sung / singing)

3. to the story, the audience laughed. (Listened / Listening)

1. Having released (or Releasing)

2. sung

3. Listening

Going deeper

Hollywood films often keep songs for musicals. In India, songs work like emotional chapters of a story, so a love scene or a moment of joy can become a dance. Because India has so many languages, films are often dubbed or given subtitles, and audiences in the south and north can enjoy each other's cinema. Streaming has made this easier: a Telugu or Korean film can now reach a worldwide audience overnight.

Worth noticing: Participle clauses: -ing for active meaning (“Based in Mumbai, …”), -ed for passive meaning (“Made in Telugu, the film…”), having + past participle for an earlier action.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: If you are invited to a film from India, ask which language it is in and whether it is a musical, a drama or an action film. If you would like to try one, start with a famous director or a film that won an award, and read the subtitles carefully: the songs often tell the story.
