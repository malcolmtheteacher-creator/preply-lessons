a dummy (n) — a model or fake object made to look like the real thing, especially a person.

a raft (n) — a simple flat floating structure used as a boat, often made by tying materials together.

The story, in three parts

Part 1 — The Rock

A mile and a half off the shore of San Francisco sits a small, rocky island the Spanish named for its pelicans: Alcatraz. Cold water surrounds it on every side, and the currents that sweep through the bay are strong, treacherous and bitterly cold. First it held a fort, then a military prison. Then, in 1934, the United States government turned it into something new — a federal penitentiary built for one purpose: to hold the men no other prison could control.

This was the age of the notorious American gangster, and the authorities wanted a place from which escape was simply unimaginable. Alcatraz would be that place. There was one guard for roughly every three prisoners — an extraordinary ratio. The men were counted more than a dozen times a day. Talking was, at first, forbidden. And beyond the walls lay the island's greatest guard of all: the freezing, churning water that everyone reckoned no swimmer could survive.

To fill its cells, the government shipped in its most incorrigible inmates — the escape artists, the troublemakers, the famous. Al Capone, the most powerful gangster in America, arrived in 1934, his empire already broken by a conviction for tax evasion. On The Rock he was just another number, scrubbing floors and, in time, quietly declining. Alcatraz was designed not to reform men but to break the will to resist. For nearly thirty years, its reputation held: no one, the warders insisted, had ever escaped and lived.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the US government choose this particular island, and what made it so hard to escape from?

2. Who was the most famous prisoner sent there, and what had brought him down?

1. It sits a mile and a half offshore, ringed by freezing, treacherous currents; it had heavy security, constant head-counts and a very high guard-to-prisoner ratio, so escape seemed impossible.

2. Al Capone, the powerful Chicago gangster, whose empire had already collapsed after he was convicted of tax evasion.

Discuss:
  • The prison was designed to break a man's will, not to reform him. Is that a legitimate purpose for a prison? Where should the line be?

Part 2 — The plan

In 1960, a bank robber named Frank Morris arrived at Alcatraz. He was quiet, exceptionally intelligent, and utterly determined never to stay. In a nearby cell block he met two brothers, John and Clarence Anglin, and a fourth man, Allen West. Slowly, patiently, over many months, the four of them built one of the most ingenious escape plans in criminal history.

The weakness they found was the concrete. The walls around the small ventilation grille at the back of each cell had grown damp and soft over the years. Night after night, hidden by the noise of the music hour, the men scraped away at the concrete with spoons stolen from the dining hall and a crude drill made from a stolen vacuum-cleaner motor. Behind the cells ran an unguarded utility corridor, and above it, an open roof space they could use as a secret workshop.

What they built up there was remarkable. From more than fifty stolen raincoats they stitched and glued an inflatable raft and life-vests, using the hot pipes to seal the rubberised seams. They fashioned a pump from a concertina-style musical instrument. And, most famously of all, they made lifelike dummy heads — moulded from soap, toilet paper and cement, painted with flesh tones from the prison art kit, and topped with real human hair collected from the barbershop floor. Each night, a dummy on the pillow would tell the guards, at a glance, that all was well.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What weakness in the building did the men exploit, and how did they work at it without being caught?

2. What were the dummy heads made from, and what were they for?

1. The concrete around the ventilation grille at the back of each cell had softened; they scraped it away with spoons and a home-made drill during the noisy music hour, working in a hidden roof space above an unguarded corridor.

2. Soap, toilet paper, cement, paint and real human hair from the barbershop; placed on the pillow, they made the guards believe the men were asleep in their bunks.

Discuss:
  • The escape took more than a year of patient, secret work. What does a plan like this reveal about the human mind under pressure?

Part 3 — The night, and the mystery

On the night of 11 June 1962, the moment finally came. Morris and the two Anglin brothers slipped their dummy heads onto their pillows, squeezed through the widened grilles at the backs of their cells, and climbed to the roof. Allen West, the fourth man, could not free his own grille in time and was left behind — he would later tell investigators everything. The three others lowered themselves down a drainpipe, crossed to the water's edge, inflated their raincoat raft, and pushed off into the black, freezing bay.

And there the trail goes cold. The men were never seen again. In the morning, the fake heads fooled the count just long enough; by the time the alarm was raised, the three were long gone. One of the largest manhunts in the country's history followed, but it found nothing conclusive — no raft on the far shore, no confession, no arrests. Some paddles and a few sealed bags with personal effects were recovered from the water. The FBI concluded that the men had almost certainly drowned in the merciless currents.

But no bodies were ever found, and doubt has never quite gone away. Over the decades, there have been reported sightings, an unsigned letter claiming to be from Morris, and family members who swear the brothers survived. The FBI closed its file in 1979; the US Marshals, remarkably, keep the case open to this day. Alcatraz itself closed in 1963 — too expensive, too crumbling to maintain — leaving behind an empty fortress and a question no one has ever answered: did three desperate men beat the escape-proof prison, or did the water win after all?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to the fourth man, and how did the escape unfold on the night of 11 June 1962?

2. Why is the fate of the three escapers still a mystery, and what happened to the prison afterwards?

1. Allen West could not free his grille in time and was left behind. The other three left dummy heads on their pillows, climbed out through the widened grilles to the roof, went down a drainpipe and launched their raincoat raft into the bay.

2. They were never seen again and no bodies were found; the FBI thinks they drowned, but sightings, a disputed letter and family claims keep the doubt alive. The prison closed in 1963 because it was too costly and run-down.

Discuss:
  • More than sixty years on, we still don't know if the men lived or died. Why do unsolved stories like this hold on to us so strongly?

Grammar — Adverbs & adverbials

The Alcatraz story lives in its details: how the men worked, how hard the water pulled, and the narrator's judgement that it was all remarkable. That's the work of adverbs and adverbials — the words and phrases that tell us how, how much, and what the speaker thinks. Control them and you control the colour of every sentence.

Three types worth knowing:
• Adverbs of manner — how something is done: "They tunnelled patiently." "He scraped the wall silently." Usually formed with -ly, and often at the end of the clause.
• Adverbs of degree — how much: "an extraordinarily clever plan", "the water was bitterly cold", "they were almost free". These usually sit before the word they strengthen.
• Comment (sentence) adverbs — the speaker's judgement on the whole idea: "Remarkably, they were never caught." "Astonishingly, the dummies fooled the guards." These usually open the sentence and are followed by a comma.
Position matters:
• Manner adverbs normally go after the verb or object: "They worked carefully," not "They carefully worked the whole night" (though mid-position is possible for emphasis).
• Degree adverbs hug the word they modify: "almost impossible", "bitterly cold", "very nearly drowned".
• Comment adverbs stand apart, judging the whole clause, usually at the front with a comma: "Incredibly, no bodies were ever found."
An "adverbial" can also be a whole phrase doing the same job: "They escaped in the dead of night" (manner/time), "to everyone's amazement, the raft held" (comment).

Task B · Should we ever call a prison "escape-proof"? (4 minutes)

Alcatraz was built to be impossible to escape — and its whole legend rests on the fact that it may not have been. Should a society try to build a prison no one can ever leave? Build an argument: think about security, cost, human dignity and the purpose of prison itself, and decide where you stand.
