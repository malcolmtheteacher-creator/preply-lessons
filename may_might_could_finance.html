May, Might & Could

Talking about possibility in finance — forecasts, risk and reporting

B1–B2 · Modals of Possibility for Finance

When a Number Isn't Certain

When we are not sure about a number or a result, we use may, might and could. In finance you use them all the time: forecasts, cash flow, risks and audit findings are rarely 100% certain. These words help you sound careful and professional — "costs could rise" is safer than "costs will rise."

Opening Questions

Talk these through (use: might, may, could):

  • What might happen to your costs next year?
  • Where could the company find savings?
  • Why might a budget go over?
  • What may happen if a big client pays late?

How the Grammar Works

Select each example to see the explanation.

MAY — Possibility (more formal)

~50% Possible Something is possible (formal, careful)

"We may close the accounts early this month."
✓ It's possible — about 50/50. "May" sounds formal, good for reports and meetings.
"The board may not approve the budget."
✓ A negative possibility — it's possible they won't approve it.
"May I take you through the figures?"
✓ A polite, professional way to start a meeting or presentation.

MIGHT — Possibility (a little less certain)

~30-50% Possible Something is possible (a bit less sure)

"We might miss the forecast this quarter."
✓ It's possible, but you're not sure. A careful way to warn people.
"The auditor might not finish on time."
✓ It's possible they won't finish — but it isn't certain.
"You might be right about that figure."
✓ A polite way to half-agree. Maybe you're correct, but I'm not sure yet.

COULD — Possibility (an option or a risk)

Any % Possible Something is possible, or one option among several

"That cost could rise next year."
✓ A possible risk to think about.
"Where's the difference?" "It could be in the payroll numbers."
✓ Suggesting one likely place to look.
"We could cut costs or raise prices."
✓ Giving two possible options — both are possible.

The Key Difference

Look at these three. How does the feeling change?

  • "Costs may rise next quarter."
  • "Costs might rise next quarter."
  • "Costs could rise next quarter."

They are close — but the certainty and the tone change a little. Which would you write in a board report? Which would you say in a quick chat?

May vs Might vs Could

At a Glance

Modal Certainty Use Example
may ~50% — Possible Formal possibility "The board may approve it."
might ~30-50% — Less certain A bit less sure "We might miss the target."
could Any possibility An option or a risk "That could work."
will 100% — Certain A firm promise "I will send the report by Friday."

Talking About the Past

may have, might have, could have + past participle

Present / Future Past
"The invoice may be paid." "The invoice may have been paid."
"They might make an error." "They might have made an error."
"We could save more." "We could have saved more."

Reading the Numbers

In finance you often have to explain a result before you have all the facts. Look at each situation and speculate about the cause — using may have, might have, could have, could be.

Situation 1: The Late Payment

The facts: A reliable client who always pays on time is now three weeks late. They are not answering your emails.

What might be happening?

Speculate aloud:

  • They may have...
  • They might have...
  • It could be that...

Situation 2: The Missing Report

The facts: The month-end report is two days late. The analyst who prepares it is not answering messages.

What might explain it?

Use these patterns:

  • There could be...
  • They might have...
  • Something may have...

Situation 3: The Numbers Don't Match

The facts: The bank balance and the ledger are different by a large amount. Last month they matched perfectly.

What could cause the difference?

Think of at least five possibilities:

  • Someone might have...
  • A payment could have...
  • The system may have...

Situation 4: The Cash Flow Drop

The facts: Cash flow fell sharply this month. Sales look normal and costs look normal.

What could be behind it?

Explore different explanations:

  • Customers might have...
  • A large bill could have...
  • The timing may have...

    Common Mistakes

    ❌ WRONG: "The invoice may paid."

    ✅ RIGHT: "The invoice may have been paid." (may + have + been + past participle)

    ❌ WRONG: "Costs might to rise next year."

    ✅ RIGHT: "Costs might rise next year." (no "to"!)

    ❌ WRONG: "We could has saved more."

    ✅ RIGHT: "We could have saved more." (could + have + past participle)

    May vs Might — In Practice

    A useful note:

    In modern English, may and might are very close in meaning. The difference is small:

    • May is a little more formal and a little more certain (~50%) — good for written reports.
    • Might is a little less sure (~40%) — good for a soft warning or a careful idea.
    • In everyday speech, people often use them in the same way.
    • For a polite request, "may" is the natural choice: "May I...?"

    To Take Away

    To practise between sessions:

    • Record a two-minute forecast for the year, using may, might and could where you're not sure.
    • Write three careful sentences you could use in a board report (for example: "Costs could rise if...").
    • Take one strong claim ("This will work") and make it softer ("This could work, if...").

    Where this helps you

    May, might and could are the everyday words for forecasts, risk and reporting — talking about what is possible, warning carefully, and giving options without promising too much.
