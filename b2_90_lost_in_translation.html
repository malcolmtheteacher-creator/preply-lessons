Lost in Translation

Beautiful words that English simply doesn't have

A word that doesn't exist

Every language has words that are almost impossible to translate into English. Not because they describe something exotic — but because they describe something so specific, so human, that English just never found a word for it.

You probably already know some. And you probably have feelings that you can describe perfectly in your first language but struggle to express in English.

Think about this: Is there a word or expression in your language that you wish existed in English? Something you keep trying to explain but can never quite capture?

Some you might recognise

English has actually borrowed words from other languages because it had no equivalent. These are now used in everyday English:

Schadenfreude (German) — pleasure at someone else's misfortune

Je ne sais quoi (French) — an indefinable, appealing quality

Wanderlust (German) — a deep desire to travel

Hygge (Danish) — a warm, cosy feeling of contentment

Discussion: Why do you think English borrowed these words specifically? What does it say about English-speaking cultures that they didn't already have them?

Which word fills the gap?

1. She walked into the meeting room and immediately sensed the tension. Nobody had to say anything — her ______ told her everything she needed to know.

nunchi — the ability to read the room without anyone speaking.

2. He wore the suit like he'd thrown it on without thinking, but somehow looked impeccable. That kind of effortless elegance — pure ______.

sprezzatura — the art of making effort look effortless.

3. Walking through the forest alone, the only sounds were birds and her own footsteps. That peaceful solitude — the Germans would call it ______.

waldeinsamkeit — the feeling of being alone in the woods.

4. It had been twenty years since she left her hometown, and there were days when the longing was almost physical — not sadness exactly, but a bittersweet ache. A kind of ______.

saudade — a deep longing for something absent.

5. The audience sat in complete silence as the final note faded. Nobody moved. They were all lost in ______ — that feeling when music takes you somewhere you can't describe.

tarab — a state of musical ecstasy.

Does language shape how we think?

There's a famous idea in linguistics: the language you speak doesn't just reflect how you see the world — it actually shapes what you can think and feel.

If English doesn't have a word for saudade, do English speakers experience that feeling less? If Japanese has komorebi, do Japanese speakers notice the sunlight through leaves more than others?

What do you think? Do you feel differently about things depending on whether you're thinking in English or in your first language? Can you give an example?

The thought experiment

Imagine you could add ONE word from any language to the English dictionary. Which word would you choose, and why? How would it change the way English speakers communicate?

Final reflection: Has learning English changed the way you think about anything? Are there ideas that feel different — or clearer — when you express them in English versus your first language?

Some beautiful English words to take away

English may borrow a lot, but it has a few untranslatable gems of its own:

Serendipity — finding something wonderful by accident, when you weren't looking for it

Petrichor — the smell of earth after rain

Hiraeth (borrowed from Welsh) — a deep longing for a home you can't return to, or one that maybe never was

Ineffable — too great or beautiful to be expressed in words
