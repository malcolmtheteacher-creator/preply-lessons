The Berlin Wall

A city cut in two overnight — and the night, twenty-eight years later, when the world watched it come down.

Today: you'll tell the story of a wall that split a city — and its people — for a generation, and then fell almost by accident; and you'll master be used to and get used to, the structures English uses for becoming accustomed to a new normal, however strange.

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. After the war the country was divided into two halves, each with its own government, its own money and its own set of rules.
divided (adj) — split into separate parts that are kept apart from each other.
2. To cross from one side to the other, you had to stop at a checkpoint, where armed guards examined your papers before deciding whether to let you through.
a checkpoint (n) — an official place on a border where people and vehicles are stopped and inspected before they may pass.
3. Hundreds of families tried to flee the country before the border closed, packing a single suitcase and slipping away in the night.
to flee (v) — to run away quickly from danger, trouble or an oppressive place. (past tense: fled)
4. The government defected soldiers were hunted mercilessly, because anyone who abandoned the state and escaped to the West was seen as a traitor.
to defect (v) — to abandon your country or side, usually for political reasons, and go over to the other one.
5. Tanks and troops lined up on either side of the crossing in a tense standoff, each waiting for the other to move first, neither willing to back down.
a standoff (n) — a situation in which two opposing sides face each other and neither will give way.
6. Anyone caught trying to cross illegally could be shot; guards in the watchtowers were under orders to enforce the border with deadly force.
to enforce (v) — to make people obey a rule or law, if necessary by force.
7. For years the two halves of the city were kept apart, but after the wall fell they moved slowly toward reunification — becoming one country again.
reunification (n) — the process of joining together again something that was split apart, especially a country.
8. The crowd surged toward the barrier, and a huge throng of people pressed forward until the guards simply stepped aside.
a throng (n) — a very large, densely packed crowd of people.
9. Living under constant surveillance, people learned to watch their words: the state was listening, and a careless sentence could cost you your job.
surveillance (n) — close, continuous watching of people, often secretly, by police or the state.
10. When the barrier finally opened, strangers embraced in the street; the sheer euphoria of that night is hard to describe to anyone who wasn't there.
euphoria (n) — an intense feeling of joy and excitement, often shared by many people at once.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The city that woke up divided

When the Second World War ended in 1945, the defeated Germany was carved up between the victors. The country was split into a democratic West and a communist East, and its capital, Berlin — stranded deep inside the eastern half — was itself divided into sectors. On paper it was an orderly arrangement between former allies. In practice, it placed a free, prosperous West Berlin like an island in the middle of a communist state, and turned the city into the tensest frontier of the Cold War.

Through the 1950s, the difference between the two Germanys grew impossible to hide. Shops in the West were full; life in the East was grey, watched and controlled. So people voted with their feet. By 1961, some 3.5 million East Germans — many of them young, skilled and educated — had simply walked into West Berlin and kept going. The East was bleeding its own future away, and its leaders decided they could not allow it.

The answer came in the early hours of 13 August 1961. While the city slept, soldiers rolled out barbed wire and tore up streets along the border. Berliners woke to find their city cut in two. Overnight, neighbours were separated, workers were locked out of their jobs, and families found themselves on opposite sides of a line they could no longer cross. The barbed wire soon hardened into concrete: the Berlin Wall.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was West Berlin such an unusual place after the Second World War?

2. What was happening through the 1950s that made East Germany's leaders build the Wall?

1. It was a free, prosperous, Western-controlled island sitting deep inside communist East Germany — the tensest frontier of the Cold War.

2. Millions of East Germans, especially the young and skilled, were fleeing to the West through Berlin; the East was losing its own people and future.

Discuss:
  • The Wall was built to keep people in, not out. What does it tell you about a government when it has to imprison its own citizens?

Part 2 — Life in the shadow of the Wall

For twenty-eight years the Wall stood, and both sides slowly grew accustomed to it. It was never a single wall but a deadly system: two concrete barriers with a floodlit "death strip" between them, patrolled by guards, dogs and watchtowers, with orders to shoot anyone attempting to cross. The most famous crossing point, Checkpoint Charlie, became a symbol of the standoff — the place where, in 1961, American and Soviet tanks once faced each other muzzle to muzzle.

Yet people still tried to escape, and their ingenuity was extraordinary. They dug tunnels beneath the death strip. They flew over it in a homemade hot-air balloon. They crashed through checkpoints in reinforced cars, hid in tiny compartments, and swam across canals. Many made it. Others did not: well over a hundred people are known to have died at the Wall. The first, just days after it went up, was a young man who leapt from an apartment window into the West; the image of others being carried away wounded stayed with the world for decades.

Behind the Wall, ordinary life went on — but under a watchful eye. The East German secret police, the Stasi, built one of the most complete surveillance states in history, recruiting neighbours to inform on neighbours. People got used to guarding their words, to shortages, to a life with the horizon walled off. In the West, the Wall became the backdrop to defiant speeches — an American president standing beside it to declare, in effect, that this was what a failed system looked like.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the Wall far more than just "a wall"? Describe how the border system worked.

2. Give two examples of how people tried to escape, and one way the state controlled those who stayed.

1. It was two concrete barriers with a floodlit "death strip" between them — guards, dogs, watchtowers and orders to shoot anyone trying to cross.

2. People dug tunnels, flew a hot-air balloon over it, crashed cars through checkpoints or swam canals; those who stayed lived under the Stasi's surveillance, with neighbours informing on neighbours.

Discuss:
  • People risked their lives for something we take for granted — the freedom to travel. Is there any freedom you have that you think you undervalue?

Part 3 — The night the Wall fell

By 1989, the communist world was cracking. Across Eastern Europe, reform was in the air and old certainties were dissolving. In East Germany, huge peaceful protests filled the streets, and the government, losing its grip, scrambled to offer concessions. On the evening of 9 November 1989, a flustered official read out new travel rules at a press conference. Asked when they would take effect, he shuffled his papers and said, in effect, "immediately, without delay." It was a mistake — the rules were meant to be phased in — but the words were out, and the television cameras carried them across the city.

East Berliners heard it and walked toward the crossings. A throng gathered at the checkpoints, thousands strong, demanding to be let through. The guards had no orders for this and no wish to fire on their own people. At Bornholmer Street, overwhelmed, an officer finally lifted the barrier. The crowd poured through. Strangers embraced; people climbed onto the Wall itself and danced along the top; others attacked the concrete with hammers and chisels, chipping away souvenirs of a barrier that had ruled their lives. The euphoria of that night was seen around the world.

The Wall's fall opened the road to something once unthinkable: German reunification, completed less than a year later, on 3 October 1990. The Cold War, which had frozen Europe in place for four decades, was ending. Getting used to a united city and country took far longer than one joyful night — old divisions of wealth and mindset lingered for years. But the image endures: ordinary people, with their bare hands and borrowed tools, taking apart the most feared border on Earth.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the mistake at the press conference on 9 November 1989, and what did it set off?

2. What did the fall of the Wall lead to, and why did the author say the celebrations were only the beginning?

1. An official said new travel rules applied "immediately", though they were meant to be phased in; East Berliners heard it and flooded the crossings until the guards opened the barriers.

2. It led to German reunification on 3 October 1990 and the end of the Cold War; getting used to a united country took years, as old divisions of wealth and mindset lingered.

Discuss:
  • The Wall fell partly because of a confused answer at a press conference. How much of history do you think turns on accidents and small human mistakes like this?

Grammar — be used to / get used to (+ -ing)

The whole story of the Wall is a story of people becoming accustomed — to a divided city, to guards and shortages, and then, all over again, to a reunited country. English has two neat structures for exactly this, and the trick is telling them apart.

The two structures, side by side:
• be used to + noun / -ing — describes a state: something is already normal and familiar to you. "After years, East Berliners were used to living under surveillance." "I am used to the noise now."
• get used to + noun / -ing — describes the process of becoming accustomed; the change from strange to normal. "It took time to get used to seeing a wall down the middle of the city." "You'll get used to it."
Two things people mix up:
• The word after to is a noun or an -ing form, never a bare infinitive: "used to living" (✓), not "used to live" (✗ — that's the different past-habit structure).
• be used to = the finished state (it's normal now). get used to = the journey to that state (it's becoming normal). "She is used to it" vs. "She's still getting used to it."

Task B · Walls and freedom (4 minutes)

The Berlin Wall was built to stop people leaving. Around the world, walls and fences are still built along borders today — but usually to stop people coming in. Do walls ever solve the problem they are built for? Build an argument, drawing on any examples you know, and decide where, if anywhere, a border barrier can be justified.

Task C · Getting used to a new normal (4 minutes)

The story is really about adjusting to enormous change. Talk about a time when life changed suddenly and people had to grow accustomed to a new reality. Lean on the grammar: what people were used to before, and what they had to get used to afterwards.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • A big change in your city or country — a new government, a new border, a new set of rules — and how people adapted.
  • A moment in history when a whole society had to get used to something overnight.
  • A technology everyone had to get used to, that once felt strange and now feels normal.
  • A famous divided place — a country, a city, a family — and what keeps or kept it apart.
  • A time a rule or restriction was suddenly lifted, and how it felt to be free of it.
  • A "wall" of another kind — between people, groups or ideas — and whether it ever came down.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The Wall stood for twenty-eight years, and people got used to it. Is there danger in getting used to things that are wrong — and how do you keep noticing them?
  2. The Wall fell almost peacefully, through crowds rather than tanks. Why do you think this ending was so different from how it began?
  3. Reunification looked like a fairy-tale ending, but the two halves stayed unequal for years. Can a divided people ever truly become one again — and what does it take?
