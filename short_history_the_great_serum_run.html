The Great Serum Run

Twenty drivers, one hundred and fifty dogs, and a race across the ice to save a dying town.

Today: you'll tell the true story of the 1925 race to save the town of Nome — and of the two dogs history rewarded very differently — and you'll master so … that and such … that, the structures English uses to turn extreme facts into drama.

The words for today's story

Ten words, four short challenges. Say your answer out loud before each reveal.

1 · Work it out from the sentence

The mountain road was treacherous that night: it looked passable, but under the thin snow lay black ice, and one careless turn meant a fall of a hundred metres.
treacherous (adj) — full of hidden dangers; looking safer than it really is. (From the same root as treachery — betrayal: treacherous ground "betrays" you.)
The firefighters had to brave the smoke and the heat to reach the family on the top floor.
to brave (v) — to face and endure something dangerous or unpleasant in order to do something. "They braved the storm to deliver the supplies." You know the adjective — here it works as a verb.
For the cut-off villages, the single railway line was a lifeline: food, medicine and news all came down that one track, and without it they would not survive the winter.
a lifeline (n) — the one connection or means of help that survival depends on. Literal (a rope to a drowning person) or figurative ("the phone calls were her lifeline").

2 · I give you the meaning — you find the word

Which word (starting with o) means the sudden start of a disease spreading through a community?
an outbreak (n) — "an outbreak of measles" · "the outbreak of war". The moment something bad breaks out.
Which word (starting with r) describes a place far from towns, roads and people — difficult to reach?
remote (adj) — "a remote village in the mountains" · "one of the most remote places on Earth".
Which word (starting with f) names the injury that extreme cold does to fingers, toes and faces — freezing the flesh itself?
frostbite (n) — "He lost two fingers to frostbite." The word is literal: bitten by frost.

3 · Complete the chunk

No single runner could cover the distance, so they organised a ______ : each person ran one section, then passed the package to the next, like a baton in athletics.
a relay (n) — a chain of people (or teams) who each do one stage of a task and hand over to the next. "a relay race" · "they worked in relays".
Complete the phrase: the doctor decided to ______ the package to the most experienced driver — it was too precious to give to anyone else.
to entrust (v) — to give something valuable to someone you trust to look after it. Pattern: entrust something to somebody / entrust somebody with something.

4 · Choose the meaning

The children could only be saved by an injection of serum. Is serum… (a) a machine for keeping medicine cold, (b) a liquid medicine, made from blood, containing the antibodies that fight a disease, or (c) a herbal drink for fever?
(b) — serum (n): a blood-based liquid carrying antibodies, used to treat or prevent disease; in this story it is the antitoxin against diphtheria.
A perilous journey is… (a) extremely dangerous, (b) extremely long, or (c) extremely expensive?
(a) — perilous (adj): full of peril — grave danger. A more literary, dramatic word than dangerous: "a perilous crossing".

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A town sealed off

In the winter of 1925, Nome was about as remote as an American town could be. A former gold-rush boomtown of around 1,400 people on the edge of the Bering Sea, just below the Arctic Circle, it spent seven months of every year sealed off from the world: from November the sea froze solid, and no ship could reach it until summer. There was no railway and no road. The open-cockpit aircraft of the day were useless in an Alaskan winter — their engines simply would not run at fifty degrees below zero. The only land link was the dog-sled trail to the railhead at Nenana, 674 miles away — a mail journey that normally took the best part of a month.

The town had one doctor, Curtis Welch, and a small hospital with four nurses. In December and January, children began coming to him with sore throats and fever. He treated them for tonsillitis — until a three-year-old boy died, and then another child, and Welch, examining a sick girl's throat, saw the grey membrane he had been dreading. It was diphtheria: a bacterial disease, wildly contagious, that chokes its victims — and it kills children first.

Welch had antitoxin serum on his shelves, but it had expired; the fresh supply he had ordered had not arrived before the sea froze. Without serum, his own estimate was catastrophic: the disease could sweep through Nome and the surrounding Native villages and kill thousands. On 22 January 1925 he sent a radio telegram that stopped the country in its tracks: an epidemic of diphtheria was almost inevitable, and he needed a million units of serum — immediately. The town was quarantined, the newspapers seized the story, and across America people asked the same question: how do you get medicine to a place that nothing can reach?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was Nome unreachable in winter — by sea, by air, and by land?

2. What exactly was Dr Welch's problem with the serum?

1. The sea froze from November to summer; the era's open aircraft couldn't fly in the extreme cold; and there was no road or railway — only a 674-mile dog-sled trail that normally took nearly a month.

2. He had serum, but it had expired — and the fresh supply he'd ordered hadn't arrived before the sea froze. Without it, he expected the epidemic to kill thousands.

Discuss:
  • Welch's telegram turned a local emergency into a national drama that everyone followed by radio and newspaper. Why do rescue stories grip whole countries — and does that attention help or distort the rescue itself?

Part 2 — The relay

Serum was found — 300,000 units in a hospital in Anchorage. The railway could carry it as far as Nenana, but that still left 674 miles of frozen wilderness. The serum was usable for days, not weeks, and the normal mail run took twenty-five. The governor made his decision: a relay of the best dog-sled drivers in the territory, running day and night, each covering one leg of the trail and handing the serum on like a baton. Twenty drivers and around a hundred and fifty dogs took their positions along the route.

They ran into one of the worst cold snaps in memory. "Wild Bill" Shannon took the first leg out of Nenana at night, in temperatures around fifty below zero; he arrived with his face blackened by frostbite, and several of his dogs did not survive the run. Others drove through winds so violent that they flipped sleds and through whiteouts so total that the drivers could not see the lead dog — they navigated from memory, trusting the animals to hold the trail. The whole country tracked their progress; the serum, warmed at each roadhouse, moved up the trail hour by hour.

The longest and most perilous leg belonged to Leonhard Seppala, the territory's champion driver, and his lead dog Togo — twelve years old, small, and famously intelligent. To save a day, Seppala took the shortcut no one else would risk: straight across the frozen surface of Norton Sound, at night, in a gale, on sea ice that could crack and drift out into open water with the wind. Togo held the line through the darkness and brought the team across. Counting the outward run to meet the serum, Seppala's team covered some 260 miles — more than four times the distance of any other driver — and crossed the deadliest ice in Alaska twice.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was a relay necessary at all — why not one driver, or the ordinary mail run?

2. What made Seppala and Togo's leg different from everyone else's?

1. The serum would only last days, and the normal 674-mile mail run took about twenty-five. Only a chain of fresh teams running day and night could be fast enough.

2. It was by far the longest — some 260 miles including the run out — and the most dangerous: they crossed the cracking sea ice of Norton Sound at night, in a gale, a shortcut no one else would risk.

Discuss:
  • Seppala gambled the serum — and his life — on the sea-ice shortcut, and it paid off. When is that kind of risk brave, and when is it irresponsible? Who should get to decide?

Part 3 — Balto gets the statue

The final leg fell to Gunnar Kaasen and a lead dog named Balto. They drove into a blizzard so ferocious that Kaasen at times could not see the dogs in front of him; at one point a gust flipped the sled, and the serum cylinder vanished into a snowdrift. Kaasen tore off his gloves and dug through the snow bare-handed until his fingers found it. At half past five in the morning on 2 February 1925, his team limped down Front Street in Nome and he handed Dr Welch the serum — frozen solid, but intact and, once thawed, still good. The relay had covered 674 miles in five and a half days, through the worst weather in twenty years. The quarantine held, the serum worked, and the epidemic was stopped; the official death toll stayed in single figures, though the true number among the Native villages was likely higher.

Then came the strange afterlife of the story. Balto — the dog who happened to cross the finish line — became one of the most famous animals on Earth: newsreels, a national tour, and, before the year was out, a bronze statue in New York's Central Park, engraved Endurance · Fidelity · Intelligence. Togo — older, smaller, and responsible for the longest and deadliest stretch of the entire run — got almost nothing. Seppala carried the injustice bitterly for the rest of his life: the papers, he felt, had crowned the wrong dog.

History has slowly been correcting itself. Today, mushers and historians generally regard Togo as the true athletic hero of the Serum Run, and he has his own statue, his own films, and a place of honour in the record. The run itself is commemorated by the Iditarod, the great annual sled race across Alaska. And the deeper legacy is medical: the drama of 1925 helped push governments to fund immunisation, and within a generation, diphtheria — the child-killer that had terrorised Nome — had almost vanished from the map.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened on Kaasen's final leg, and in what condition did the serum arrive?

2. Why do many people say the fame went to the wrong dog?

1. He drove through a blizzard he could barely see in; the sled flipped and he dug the lost serum cylinder out of the snow with bare hands. It arrived frozen but intact — and still worked once thawed.

2. Balto only ran the short final leg but got the statue and the world's attention; Togo ran the longest, most dangerous stretch — including the Norton Sound ice — and was largely overlooked at the time.

Discuss:
  • The world celebrated the dog that finished, not the dog that did the most. Why do we so often reward the visible last step over the invisible hard miles — and where else does that happen?

Grammar — so … that / such … that

This story runs on extremes: cold beyond imagining, storms beyond seeing through. English has a precise pair of structures for saying this extreme → that result — and the choice between them is pure grammar.

The two structures, side by side:
• so + adjective/adverb + that — "The cold was so intense that engines would not run." "The storm hit so suddenly that no one was ready."
• such + (a/an) + adjective + noun + that — "It was such an intense cold that engines would not run." "They faced such violent winds that sleds flipped over."
• With quantities: so much / so many + noun + that — "There was so much snow that the trail disappeared."
The rule of thumb: look at what follows. Just an adjective (or adverb)? → so. A noun phrase (even with an adjective in front)? → such. And in fast speech the that often drops out — "It was so cold, the sea froze" — but keep it when you write.

1. so or such (a/an)?

Complete each sentence. Look at what follows the gap before you choose.

1. The blizzard was fierce that Kaasen could not see his own dogs.

2. It was dangerous shortcut that no other driver would attempt it.

3. There was much snow that the serum cylinder vanished completely.

4. Togo was intelligent dog that he could hold the trail in total darkness.

1. so (followed by the adjective "fierce" alone)

2. such a (followed by adjective + noun: "dangerous shortcut")

3. so (so much + uncountable noun)

4. such an (adjective + noun: "intelligent dog" — and "an" before the vowel)

2. Join the facts into drama

Each item gives you two plain sentences. Complete the single dramatic sentence with the missing word.

1. The ice was thin. It could break at any moment. → The ice was so thin it could break at any moment.

2. It was a terrible winter. Old drivers remembered nothing like it. → It was a terrible winter that old drivers remembered nothing like it.

3. The stakes were high. The whole country held its breath. → The stakes were high that the whole country held its breath.

1. that (so + adjective + that + result)

2. such (such + a + adjective + noun)

3. so (so + adjective + that)

3. Build the drama — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with so, such a, or that. Read the finished paragraph aloud — with feeling.

The cold was extreme that aircraft engines simply refused to run. Crossing Norton Sound was desperate gamble that no one else would take it. And the final blizzard blew so hard the driver could not see the team pulling his own sled.

so (+ adjective "extreme") · such a (+ adjective + noun "desperate gamble") · that (completing "so hard that…")

Notice how each structure turns a measurement into a story: not "-50°" but "so cold that engines refused to run".
