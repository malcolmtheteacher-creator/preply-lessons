Making a Complaint

Something's gone wrong, it wasn't your fault, and you want it put right. No shouting required — just the right words, warmly delivered.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

Ten days ago you treated yourself to a rather expensive coffee machine — the sleek one you'd been eyeing for months. This morning it made a promising gurgle, flashed every light on the front, and died. Not so much as a dribble.

So here you are, back at the shop, box under your arm, receipt in your pocket, and a small speech rehearsed in your head. You're not angry, exactly — you're disappointed, and you'd quite like your money's worth. The trick is that the person behind the counter will meet you with a wall of very reasonable-sounding reasons why none of this is their problem. Your job is to be so pleasant, and so immovable, that saying "no" to you becomes more trouble than saying "yes".

Behind the returns desk: an assistant who has heard "it just stopped working" fifteen times today, and whose first instinct — through no fault of their own — is to send you somewhere else.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last have to complain about something you'd bought? Did you get what you wanted — and how did you go about it?
  2. Some people go straight to shouting; others never complain at all and quietly put up with it. Which are you closer to — and which one usually gets the better result?

Who's who

You
The Customer 📦

Polite, calm, and completely unwilling to leave without a fair result. You bought something faulty; you want it sorted; you will smile the whole way through.

The other voice
The Shop Assistant 🧾

Not unkind — just tired, and armed with a script of policies, windows and "you'll have to contact the manufacturer". Fair, in the end, if you make saying yes the easy option.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how the customer stays warm while every "no" quietly loses ground.

AssistantMorning — who's next? Right, what can I do for you?
YouMorning. I'm afraid I've got a bit of a problem with something I bought here — this coffee machine. I'm sure it's nothing you've done personally, but it's given up the ghost after barely a week.
AssistantAh. Right. Have you got the receipt with you, by any chance?
YouI have, yes — here you go. It was ten days ago, so it's very much still under warranty. The thing is, it worked beautifully for a week and then simply stopped — no water, no lights, nothing.
AssistantThe trouble is, once it's a fault with the machine itself, that's really one for the manufacturer. You'd normally have to ring their helpline and go from there.
YouI do understand that's the usual route — but to be honest, I bought it from you, not from them, and it's failed almost straight out of the box. I'm sure you can appreciate I'd rather not spend an afternoon on hold for something that was faulty when I got it.
AssistantI hear you. It's just that our returns window is fourteen days for a change of mind, and —
YouOh, this isn't a change of mind — I'd love to keep it if it worked. It's faulty, which is rather a different matter, and I'm well within the ten days in any case. I'd hate to have to make a formal thing of it, so is this something we can sort out here today?
AssistantLet me have a proper look… Yeah, that's not right at all, that. Okay — leave it with me a sec. I could offer you a repair, if you like?
YouI appreciate that, but a repair on a ten-day-old machine feels a bit much, doesn't it? What I'd really like is a straight replacement — or a refund, if that's simpler at your end.
AssistantFair enough, honestly. The only snag is we're out of that exact model — sold the last one Friday. I can order you a new one in, but it'd be Thursday at the earliest.
YouHmm. I'd rather not go without my morning coffee till Thursday, if I'm honest. Could we meet halfway — you've the newer model on the shelf there; would you let me have that one instead, and we'll call it even?
Assistant…Go on, then. It's twenty quid dearer, but given the palaver, I'll waive the difference. I'll swap it over and pop a note on the system in case it plays up.
YouThat's genuinely kind of you — thank you. You've turned a rotten morning right around, and I'll happily come back knowing you sort people out when things go wrong.
AssistantNo bother. And if this one so much as hiccups, you come straight back to me — don't be ringing any helpline.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The assistant offers three ways to make the problem go away before finally agreeing — "contact the manufacturer", the fourteen-day window, and a repair. How does the customer take each one apart without ever getting cross?

2. The customer keeps stressing that the machine is "faulty" and not a "change of mind". Why does that one distinction matter so much to how the conversation goes?

3. In the end the customer doesn't get the identical machine back — they get something better. What was the compromise, and which of the customer's moves made the assistant willing to offer it?

1. "Manufacturer" — the customer agrees it's the usual route, then points out they bought it from the shop and it failed almost immediately. The 14-day window — they cheerfully separate "change of mind" (14 days) from "faulty" (a different rule), and note they're inside ten days anyway. The repair — they call it "a bit much" for a ten-day-old machine and steer straight to replacement or refund. Every time: agree first, then move the goalposts back gently.
2. A "change of mind" is a favour the shop doesn't have to grant, and it's tied to the fourteen-day window. A "faulty" item is the shop's responsibility to put right — different rules, and the customer's strongest card. By insisting on "faulty", they move the conversation off the shop's comfortable ground and onto their own.
3. The exact model was out of stock, so the customer asked to "meet halfway" and take the newer model on the shelf instead — and the assistant waived the £20 difference. What earned it: staying friendly, framing it as fair ("call it even"), and having stayed so pleasant that the assistant wanted to help.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that get things put right — firmly, warmly, and without a single raised voice. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚪 Opening the complaint gently

Phrase 1

You've come to complain, but flying in hot only puts the other person on the defensive. Which opener flags a problem while keeping the room friendly?

A. "This machine you sold me is rubbish."
B. "I want to make a complaint."
C. "I'm afraid I've got a bit of a problem with something I bought here."
C — "I'm afraid I've got a bit of a problem with something I bought here."
"I'm afraid…" softens the bad news before it lands, and "a bit of a problem" downplays it just enough to keep things civil — you can turn the pressure up later. A and B put the other person on the back foot before you've even started.
Phrase 2

The person in front of you almost certainly didn't cause your problem. Before you push, you take them off the hook so they feel like your ally, not your target: "I'm sure this ___ nothing ___ ___ done personally, but…" Fill the gap.

"I'm sure this is nothing you've done personally, but…"
Separating the person from the problem is the secret engine of a good complaint. Cousins: "This clearly isn't your fault, but…" and "I know you didn't make it, but…" — all of them let you be tough on the issue and soft on the human.

🔍 Laying out the problem

Phrase 3

Understatement lands harder than outrage. You want to say the product fell badly short — without a single dramatic word. Put the pieces in order:

quitethis isn'twhat I wasled to believe.led to expect
"This isn't quite what I was led to expect."
(Or "…led to believe.") Cool, precise, faintly disappointed — and far more effective than "this is terrible". "Led to expect" also quietly reminds them that they set the expectation you're now measuring against.
Phrase 4

This is the single most important word to keep repeating — it's the one that turns the problem from your bad luck into the shop's legal responsibility. What one adjective (not "broken", not "rubbish") should anchor your whole case?

"It's faulty."
"Faulty" is a claim about the product, not about you — and in most places it triggers the seller's duty to refund, replace or repair. Keep steering back to it: "This isn't a change of mind — it's faulty." Say "faulty", not "I don't like it".

📈 Turning up the pressure (still politely)

Phrase 5

They're stalling. You want to hint — without threatening — that you're prepared to escalate this if you have to. What's the classic velvet-glove warning that means "please don't make me take this higher"?

"I'd hate to have to take this further, but…"
It's a warning shot fired in the friendliest possible tone: you're signalling the manager, the head office, the review — while making it sound like you'd genuinely rather not. Relatives: "I'd rather not make a formal thing of it…" and "Let's not let it come to that…"
Phrase 6

Rather than telling them how annoyed you are, you invite them onto your side — you ask them to imagine your position. Reorder this appeal to their empathy:

you canI'm surethis is.how frustratingappreciate
"I'm sure you can appreciate how frustrating this is."
"I'm sure you can appreciate…" hands them your feelings as a shared, reasonable fact rather than an accusation — it's very hard to argue with. Note "appreciate" here means understand / see, not "be grateful for".

🤝 Getting a fair result

Phrase 7

Vague complaints get vague answers. It's time to name exactly what you want — but there's a polite way and a blunt way. Which lands better?

A. "I want my money back, now."
B. "What I'd really like is a full refund — or a replacement, if that's simpler."
B — "What I'd really like is a full refund — or a replacement, if that's simpler."
"What I'd really like is…" states your goal clearly while sounding reasonable, and offering them a second option ("or a replacement") makes saying yes to something easy. A is a demand; B is a request they can grant with dignity.
Phrase 8

They've half-agreed, but nothing is actually happening yet. You want to pin the vague goodwill down to a concrete action, today, without sounding pushy: "Is this something you'd be able ___ ___ ___ for me today?" Fill the gap.

"Is this something you'd be able to sort out for me today?"
It converts sympathy into a commitment and quietly sets a deadline ("today"). "Sort out" is the perfect everyday verb for it. Variants: "Is that something you could sort for me now?" / "Can we get this sorted today?"
Phrase 9

They can't give you exactly what you asked for — but there's room to deal. You propose a compromise that makes you sound reasonable, not greedy. What's the friendly phrase for "let's both give a little"?

"Could we meet halfway on this?"
It frames the outcome as a fair deal between equals rather than a battle someone has to lose. Pair it with a concrete offer — "…you let me have that model instead, and we'll call it even." "Call it even" seals the compromise warmly.

🌟 Closing — firm but grateful

Phrase 10

They've come through for you. The way you close decides whether they'll go the extra mile for you next time. Which exit line does that?

A. "About time too."
B. "Fine. Bye."
C. "That's genuinely kind of you — you've turned a rotten morning right around."
C — "That's genuinely kind of you — you've turned a rotten morning right around."
You were firm the whole way; now you're warm, and you reward the person for helping. "Genuinely" makes the thanks sound sincere, and praising the outcome ("turned my morning around") leaves them glad they said yes. A punishes them for helping; B wastes the goodwill you earned.
Quick test: run the whole complaint out loud using all ten, in order — gentle opening → lay out the fault → turn up the pressure → name what you want → strike the deal → warm close. When it flows without a single raised voice, you're ready for Your Turn.

Your Turn 🎭

No script this time. You'll have a real conversation with a real person across the counter. They have their own mind, and you have a goal.

1 · Warm up your voice 🎤

Say each step once, out loud, in your own words. No writing, no peeking at the phrases.

  1. Open gently. Flag the problem and take the pressure off the other person.
  2. Say what's wrong. Be exact. Use the word that makes it their responsibility.
  3. Redirect. Agree it's the usual route, then explain why it's not the right one here.
  4. Hold firm, smiling. Turn the pressure up a notch without turning rude.
  5. Name what you want. A replacement or a refund, and make it easy to say yes.
  6. Close warmly. Reward them for helping.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1773, a shipload of tea sat in Boston Harbour, and with it a tax the American colonists had never agreed to. Their complaint was simple and reasonable: no taxation without representation. They had written the letters, argued the case, asked politely — and been ignored.

So one December night, they stopped asking. They boarded the ships and tipped ninety thousand pounds of tea into the sea — history's most expensive returned product, complaint escalated to the very top.

You're standing at a far friendlier counter. But it's the same old game: state your case, stay firm, and make it clear you won't quietly go away. The difference is that you get to win with charm — and keep the tea.

Two bigger questions

  1. The colonists tried polite complaints for years before they threw the tea in the harbour. When does a reasonable complaint stop working — and how do you know it's time to raise your voice, or your stakes?
  2. You get more from a shop by being charming than by being furious. But the Boston protesters got results precisely by not being polite. When does "firm but friendly" work — and when does only a bigger, louder gesture actually change anything?
