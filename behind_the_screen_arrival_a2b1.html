Arrival

The film, in three parts

Denis Villeneuve's Arrival (2016). Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one. (There are spoilers — that is fine, we want to talk about it.)

Part 1 — Twelve ships arrive

One day, twelve big alien spacecraft appear over different parts of the world. Nobody knows why. The army asks Louise, a language expert, to help. She must try to talk to the aliens. A scientist called Ian helps her.

Inside the ship, Louise meets two huge aliens. She calls them "heptapods." The aliens do not talk like humans. They make circle shapes in the air. This is their written language. It is very different from their language. Slowly, Louise learns to read and write it.

As she learns more, Louise starts to see strange pictures in her mind. She sees a young girl she does not know. She thinks these pictures are old memories. But she is wrong.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does the army ask Louise for help?

2. What strange thing starts to happen to Louise?

1. Twelve alien ships have arrived, and they need a language expert to try to talk to the aliens.

2. She starts to see pictures in her mind of a young girl she does not know.

Talk about it:
  • The aliens have not done anything bad, but people are already afraid. Is fear a normal first reaction to something new and strange?

Part 2 — The world moves toward war

Different countries read the alien message in different ways. China thinks the message is about a "weapon." Other countries think it means "tool." Because of this mistake, countries stop working together. China's army gets ready to attack. Some soldiers are scared and put a bomb inside the alien ship.

Louise's strange pictures of the young girl become stronger. She still does not understand them. Time is running out. If the countries do not agree soon, there will be a war.

Louise can now read the aliens' language very well. She asks them a direct question: why are you here, and why now?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why do countries stop trusting each other?

2. What is happening to Louise's strange pictures?

1. China reads the alien message as "weapon," but it may mean "tool." This mistake makes countries stop trusting each other.

2. They are becoming stronger and clearer, though she still does not understand what they mean.

Talk about it:
  • One wrong word almost starts a war. Do you think most fights between countries come from real disagreement, or from misunderstanding?

Part 3 — A future she already knows

The aliens explain why they came: they want to give humans their language as a gift, because in the future, humans will help the aliens. Learning the alien language changes something in Louise's brain. She does not see the past in her strange pictures — she sees her own future.

War is close. Louise uses something she knows from her future — a phone number and some special words — to call a Chinese general. She says his own wife's last words back to him. He is shocked, and he stops the attack. The war does not happen.

Now Louise understands everything. She will marry Ian and have a daughter called Hannah. But Hannah will become very sick and die young. Ian will also leave Louise one day, when he learns that she always knew this would happen. Louise knows all of this — the happy parts and the sad parts — and she chooses to live it anyway.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the aliens really come to Earth?

2. What does Louise finally understand about her own future?

1. They came to give humans their language as a gift, because they will need help from humans in the future.

2. She will have a daughter, Hannah, who will die young; Ian will leave her later. She chooses to live this future anyway.

Talk about it:
  • Louise chooses a future with great joy and great sadness in it. Would you choose to know your future, even the sad parts?

Grammar: going to

This film is full of plans and predictions about the future. English uses going to for plans you can already see coming, and for things you feel sure will happen.

be going to + verb = a plan, or something you can already see is coming: "The army is going to attack the ship." "Louise is going to learn the alien language."
be going to for predictions with proof: "Look at the news — there is going to be a war."
Feel the difference:
• going to = a plan already decided, or something you can already see clearly.
• Compare with will, which is more for a sudden decision or a simple guess about the future.

1. Complete with going to

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence.

1. The army has a plan. Tomorrow, soldiers (enter) the alien ship. (a plan)

2. Look at the two armies — there (be) a war very soon. (you can see it coming)

3. Louise has decided. She (learn) the whole alien language. (a plan)

1. are going to enter (a plan)

2. is going to be (you can see it coming)

3. is going to learn (a plan)

2. study (going to + base verb)

The Real Story — is any of this true?

Part of this film comes from a real idea in science. But the film also goes much further than real scientists would agree with.

🔤 A real idea, with a name

Some scientists think that the language you speak can change, a little, the way you think. This idea is called the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, named after two linguists, Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf. For example, some studies show that people who use different words for colours can notice colours slightly differently. Other studies show that people think about time differently depending on the words their language uses for it.

But the film goes much further

Most scientists agree that language can change small things about how we think — not what we can see, or what colours we notice a little faster. But no real language lets a person see the future. That part of the film is not real science — it is a big, imaginative idea for a story.

The film comes from a book by the writer Ted Chiang, called Story of Your Life. He used the real idea about language and thought, but made it much bigger and stranger, to ask an interesting question: what would it be like to know your whole future, good and bad, before it happens?

Talk about it:
  • Before this lesson, did you think language could change how you think? What is one example from your own life?
  • Does it feel better to know the idea is a little bit real? Or do you prefer the film's bigger, more magical version?
  • Would you want to know your whole future, even the sad parts? Why, or why not?
