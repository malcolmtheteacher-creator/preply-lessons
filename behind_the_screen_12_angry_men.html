12 Angry Men

Eleven jurors are sure the boy is guilty. One isn't — and by simply asking questions, he slowly turns the whole room. The psychology behind him is real.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of 12 Angry Men — twelve strangers locked in a hot room, deciding whether to send a teenage boy to his death — discover what a famous psychology experiment proved about groups exactly like this one, and master how English reports a question someone else asked: not "Did you see him?" but he asked whether she had seen him.
Before we begin — talk it through: Have you ever been the only person in a group who disagreed with everyone else? Did you speak up, or stay quiet? And how sure would you need to be, to decide someone's fate based only on other people's word?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. Before announcing any decision, the committee would shut the doors and deliberate for hours, turning every argument over and testing it from every angle before a single word reached the public.
to deliberate (v) — to think about and discuss something carefully and thoroughly before reaching a decision.
2. The witness gave her testimony in a calm, steady voice, describing in detail exactly what she said she had seen from her window that night.
testimony (n) — a formal statement of what someone has seen, heard or knows, especially given as evidence in court.
3. He decided he didn't like the stranger the moment she walked in, judging her only by her accent and her clothes — a prejudice formed in seconds, long before either of them had said a real word to each other.
prejudice (n) — an unfair opinion about someone or something, formed in advance and not based on real experience or evidence.
4. Her excuse for being an hour late — that her watch had stopped and she'd genuinely lost track of time — sounded entirely plausible, the kind of ordinary mistake that could happen to anyone.
plausible (adj) — reasonable and likely to be true; easy to believe.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. adamant is closest in meaning to: (a) uncertain   (b) firmly refusing to change your mind   (c) easily persuaded
(b) firmly refusing to change your mind — adamant (adj) describes someone completely fixed in an opinion or decision, unwilling to be argued out of it.
6. corroborate is closest in meaning to: (a) confirm with extra evidence   (b) contradict   (c) forget completely
(a) confirm with extra evidence — to corroborate (v) a claim is to support it with independent facts or evidence, making it more believable.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. One small careless mistake in her report was enough to discredit the whole investigation, making people doubt every other finding in it too.
To discredit something is: (a) to prove it completely correct; (b) to make people stop believing or trusting it; (c) to publish it for the first time.
(b) — to discredit (v) — to damage someone's or something's reputation for honesty or reliability, so that people stop trusting it.
8. The closing speech was so emotional that it began to sway even jurors who had walked in feeling completely certain.
To sway someone is: (a) to bore them; (b) to gradually influence or change their opinion; (c) to ignore them completely.
(b) — to sway (v) — to influence someone's opinion or decision, often by argument or emotion, so that they start to change their mind.

D. Infer from context — two bigger ideas

9. In a company where everyone was expected to agree with the boss without question, the one manager who kept voicing open dissent in every meeting was treated as a troublemaker — even on the two occasions when she turned out to be completely right.
dissent (n) — open disagreement with an official decision or a widely-held opinion, especially when expressed inside a group that expects agreement.
10. After weeks of insisting it would never lower its prices, the shop finally capitulated once every single customer had walked out to its cheaper rival across the street.
to capitulate (v) — to stop resisting and give in completely to someone else's demands or to pressure from others. The noun is capitulation.

The film, in three parts

Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men (1957), from Reginald Rose's own script. Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — Twelve strangers, one hot afternoon

On a stifling summer afternoon in a New York courthouse, twelve men file into a bare jury room to decide the fate of a boy they have never met. An eighteen-year-old from the slums stands accused of stabbing his father to death, and if this jury finds him guilty, the law gives the judge no choice: the sentence is death. The film never leaves that one room, and almost never leaves real time either.

A quick show of hands looks like a formality: eleven vote guilty without much thought. One man, known throughout the film only as Juror 8, votes not guilty. He doesn't claim the boy is innocent. He simply isn't sure — and in a case like this, he says, "not sure" should be enough to stop them convicting.

The other jurors are irritated. One has tickets to a baseball game and wants this over fast. Another, sneering about "these people" from the slums, is already certain of the boy's guilt because of where he comes from, not because of the evidence. Reluctantly, the foreman agrees to go around the table: each juror will explain, one by one, exactly why he's sure — and Juror 8 will get the chance to push back.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the initial vote, and what happens to the boy if the jury convicts him?

2. Why does Juror 8 vote not guilty — does he believe the boy is definitely innocent?

1. Eleven vote guilty and one, Juror 8, votes not guilty; if convicted, the sentence is automatically death.

2. No — he isn't convinced the boy is innocent, only that there's enough doubt that he can't in good conscience vote guilty.

Discuss:
  • Would you have the nerve to be the only person in a room of twelve strangers to openly disagree with everyone else, on your very first vote?

Part 2 — Taking the evidence apart, piece by piece

Question by question, Juror 8 starts pulling at the case the prosecution built. The murder weapon was a rare switchblade knife, and the shop owner testified it was "the only one of its kind" he had ever sold in that neighbourhood — meaning, the prosecution argued, it had to be the exact knife the boy admitted buying that night. During a break, Juror 8 quietly walks a few blocks and buys an identical knife at a pawn shop for a couple of dollars, then slams it onto the table next to the "one of a kind" murder weapon. It proves nothing about the boy's guilt or innocence — but it proves the knife wasn't unique, and if the prosecution got that "fact" wrong, what else might it have got wrong?

Next, they turn to the elderly man who lives in the flat below the murder. He testified that he heard the boy shout "I'll kill you," heard a body hit the floor, and then — walking with the limp he'd had since a stroke — made it to his door in time to see the boy running down the stairs, all within fifteen seconds. Using the layout of the flat and a stopwatch, the jurors time out his exact route from bed to door. It takes over forty seconds — nearly three times what he claimed under oath.

And there's a second problem: an elevated train roars past that window at almost exactly that time every night, loud enough, they realise, to make it very unlikely he could really have heard a shout through the wall as clearly as he says.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Juror 8 do with the second knife, and what does it actually prove?

2. What two problems do the jurors find with the old man's testimony about the fifteen seconds?

1. He buys an identical knife nearby, proving the "murder weapon" wasn't unique — which doesn't prove the boy innocent, but shows the prosecution's evidence was less solid than it seemed.

2. Timed out, walking to the door would have taken far longer than fifteen seconds; and a loud passing train would likely have drowned out any shout at that exact moment.

Discuss:
  • Which piece of evidence, once questioned, changed your own sense of the case the most — the knife, the timing, or the train?

Part 3 — Turning the room

As the votes shift one by one, the film reveals why each juror believed what he believed — and it is rarely really about the evidence. Juror 10's contempt for "people like that" finally spills out as raw, ugly prejudice, and one by one the other jurors simply stand up and turn their backs on him until he falls silent, ashamed. The woman across the street who swore she saw the stabbing through the windows of a passing train is undermined too: another juror notices the marks pressed into the sides of her nose — left by glasses she wasn't wearing in court — and asks how clearly she could really have seen anything, half-asleep, at that distance, in the dark, without them.

By the end, only Juror 3 still holds out for guilty, and it becomes clear his fury has nothing to do with this boy at all: he is estranged from his own son and has been punishing the defendant for it all afternoon. Confronted with a photograph of his son, he finally breaks — tears it, sobs, and votes not guilty. The jury informs the court, unanimous at last, that they cannot convict beyond a reasonable doubt.

Outside, on the courthouse steps, two of the twelve — the men we have only ever known as Juror 8 and Juror 9 — pause and, for the first time in the whole film, ask each other their actual names.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does Juror 10's outburst end the way it does, and what makes the jurors doubt the woman witness?

2. Why does Juror 3 finally change his vote, and what is unusual about the very last scene?

1. His open prejudice disgusts the room and the others turn their backs on him; the woman witness is doubted because she wasn't wearing, in court, the glasses that had clearly left marks on her nose, raising doubt she could have seen so clearly and precisely at night, from that distance.

2. He finally admits his anger has been about his own estranged son, not the case, and breaks down; on the steps, two jurors exchange names for the first time — a reminder that for the whole film, we never actually learned who these twelve men were.

Discuss:
  • The jury never decides the boy is innocent — only that there's reasonable doubt. Does that ending feel satisfying, or does it leave things too unresolved for you?

Grammar — reported & indirect questions

No situation needs reported questions more than a jury room, where everyone is constantly asking everyone else what they meant, what they saw, and why they're so sure. English has a clear way to report a question someone else asked, without repeating their exact words — and without a question mark.

The two rules:
• Reported wh- questions: subject + verb, with no do/does/did and no question-word-first inversion. Direct: "Why are you so sure?" → Reported: He asked why she was so sure. Direct: "What did you see?" → Reported: She wanted to know what he had seen.
• Reported yes/no questions: add whether or if, then subject + verb — again, no do/does/did. Direct: "Is the witness reliable?" → Reported: He asked whether the witness was reliable. Direct: "Could she really have seen it from there?" → Reported: He asked whether she could really have seen it from there.
Backshift — move the tense one step into the past:
am/is/are → was/were  ·  do/does + verb → verb-ed (or past form)  ·  did + verb → had + past participle  ·  can → could  ·  will → would  ·  have done → had done. (Modals like could, would, should, might don't shift any further.)

1. Report the question — fill the gap with the whole reported clause

Use a connector (whether/if, or a question word) plus the subject and the backshifted verb.

1. Direct: "Can you really have seen the murder from that distance?" (asked to the woman across the street) — Juror 8 turned to her and asked

2. Direct: "Why do you want to send this boy to the chair so quickly?" (asked to Juror 3) — Juror 9 quietly asked him

3. Direct: "Did the old man really walk to the door in only fifteen seconds?" — The jurors wanted to know

1. whether/if she could really have seen the murder from that distance

2. why he wanted to send the boy to the chair so quickly

3. whether/if the old man had really walked to the door in only fifteen seconds

2. Fix the form

Each sentence reports a question but keeps a mistake — an extra do/does/did, or the words in the wrong order. Write the reported clause correctly.

1. Wrong: "He asked did she remember what she saw." Correct: He asked

2. Wrong: "She asked why was he so certain." Correct: She asked

3. Wrong: "He wanted to know had the boy bought a knife like that." Correct: He wanted to know

1. whether/if she remembered what she had seen

2. why he was so certain

3. whether/if the boy had bought a knife like that

3. Complete the story — mix connectors and backshift

Fill each gap with a full reported clause. Read it aloud when you're done.

"Wait," Juror 8 said, standing up. He asked the room (why / everyone / so sure already) after only one vote. Then he turned to the old man and asked (can / really / hear a shout over a passing train). Before the next vote, he wanted to know (has / anyone in the room / ever / been wrong before).

why everyone was so sure already · whether/if he could really have heard a shout over a passing train · whether/if anyone in the room had ever been wrong before

Notice the pattern: wh- questions keep no do/does/did; yes/no questions add whether or if; and every verb takes one step back into the past.

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

Here's what's unusual about 12 Angry Men: almost none of it was invented purely for drama. Reginald Rose, who wrote the original 1954 teleplay and this 1957 screenplay, based it directly on his own experience of jury duty — a real manslaughter trial in New York that left him fascinated by what actually happens once twelve strangers are locked in a room. The legal standard the film dramatises, and the psychology of the room, are both real — though, as you'll see, the film is kinder to the jury system than reality usually is.

⚖️ "Beyond a reasonable doubt" — and why it takes all twelve

In the American criminal justice system, a defendant cannot be convicted on a judge or jury's mere suspicion, or even on a balance of probabilities. The prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt — a standard so high that, as the jurors in the film keep repeating, if there is a genuine, reasonable doubt left in your mind, the law requires you to acquit, even if you personally suspect the person may be guilty. For serious crimes like the one in the film, American law requires a unanimous verdict: every one of the twelve must agree, or the jury is "hung" and cannot convict. That's the whole engine of the film — while even one juror keeps a reasonable doubt alive, the other eleven cannot simply outvote him.

The psychology of the lone dissenter is real too

Juror 8's ability to shift eleven strangers isn't just good drama — it echoes a famous set of psychology experiments from exactly the years this film was written. In the early 1950s, the psychologist Solomon Asch asked volunteers to judge something absurdly simple: which of three lines matched a reference line in length. The catch was that each real participant sat in a small group of actors who had secretly agreed, on certain rounds, to all give the same wrong answer out loud before the real participant answered. Around a third of participants' answers conformed to the group's wrong answer across those rounds, and roughly three-quarters conformed at least once — even though, questioned afterwards, most of them privately knew the group was wrong.

Asch also tested what happened if just one other person in the room broke ranks and gave the correct answer alongside the real participant. Conformity to the wrong majority collapsed. A single ally — one confident dissenting voice — was often enough to free other people to trust their own eyes again. That is exactly the mechanism the film dramatises for two hours: eleven people conform to a quick, confident-sounding majority, and it takes only one person willing to say "I don't agree" to start unravelling it. Decades later, in 1972, the psychologist Irving Janis coined the word groupthink for a group's tendency to close ranks around a shared decision and stop critically testing it — precisely the trap this jury very nearly falls into before Juror 8 speaks up.

Where the film is kinder to reality than reality usually is

It would be dishonest to pretend real jury rooms usually work like this one. The most influential real study of American juries, Harry Kalven and Hans Zeisel's The American Jury (1966), found that the vote taken on the very first ballot predicts the jury's final verdict in roughly nine cases out of ten — deliberation afterwards rarely changes the outcome. And when a minority does eventually turn the room around, or forces a hung jury, research suggests it is usually a minority of four or five jurors from the very first vote, not a single holdout arguing alone against eleven, the way Juror 8 does. An 11–1 split that ends by fully reversing itself, as in the film, is the kind of thing that can happen — but it is genuinely rare.

It's also worth noting that real jurors are instructed to decide a case only on the evidence presented at trial. Buying a matching knife at a pawn shop, timing a walk with a stopwatch, or examining a witness's nose for glasses marks — the very things that make the film so gripping — would, in a real jury room, likely count as jurors conducting their own outside investigation, against the rules and potentially grounds to overturn a verdict on appeal. Rose dramatised the spirit of careful, sceptical reasoning; a real judge would probably have stopped the reenactments before they got that far.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, did you think the jury's dramatic change of mind was a realistic picture of how justice actually works, or did it feel like a Hollywood ending?
  • Does knowing that Asch's experiment is real change how you see Juror 8 — is he just stubborn, or is he doing something psychologically rare and difficult?
  • The film idealises the jury system, even while criticising the prejudice inside it. Does a story need to be realistic to tell the truth about something?
