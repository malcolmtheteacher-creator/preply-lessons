The Culture That Signed It Off

Two crashes, 346 lives, and one question that outlasted every investigation: who was supposed to say no?

Today: you'll work through the hardest case in this series — where commercial pressure met safety engineering, with the worst possible outcome — and you'll master the precise language of duty: was required to, was supposed to, should never have been able to. This case involves the loss of 346 lives; it is taught here soberly, because its lessons are exactly the ones executives most need.
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • In your industry, what stands between commercial pressure and an unsafe shortcut? Name the actual mechanism, not the value statement.
  • When a regulator relies on the regulated company's own experts, what is being assumed — and when is that assumption reasonable?
  • What is the difference between a company that says "safety first" and one where safety actually has the power to stop a schedule?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Before any new airliner may carry passengers, the aviation authority must examine and approve its design. The aircraft must be certified.
to certify (v) / certification (n) — official approval that something meets required standards. Type certification; to ground / re-certify an aircraft. The process at the centre of this case.
2. Rather than design a new aircraft — a decade and many billions — the company chose to modify its fifty-year-old bestseller yet again: a derivative of the existing type.
a derivative (n/adj) — a design developed from an existing one rather than started fresh. The commercial logic: shared training, shared parts, faster approval. The engineering cost: every old constraint inherited.
3. The regulator had for years delegated much of the detailed certification work to engineers employed by the manufacturer itself — a system of delegated oversight.
oversight (n) — supervisory control (regulatory oversight, congressional oversight). Note the trap: an oversight also means a thing accidentally missed — English's darkest pun, and this case contains both meanings.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: to ground a fleet · a fix · a deferred prosecution agreement. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means to order every aircraft of a type out of the sky?
to ground (v) — the fleet was grounded worldwide. Here: twenty months, the longest grounding of a major airliner in modern history.
2. Which term means the engineering change that corrects a fault?
a fix (n) — a software fix, to roll out a fix. Beware its casual register in safety contexts — official documents say corrective action, and the difference in weight is part of today's lesson.
3. Which term means a deal in which prosecutors suspend criminal charges if the company meets conditions?
a deferred prosecution agreement (DPA) — charges held over the company's head, typically with fines, monitors and compliance conditions. Boeing's 2021 DPA — and what later happened to it — is part three of the story.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. The new software could move the aircraft's controls on the basis of a single point of failure — one sensor, no cross-check. From the Maersk case, this means…
  (a) one component whose failure defeats the whole system   (b) a backup system   (c) a minor fault
(a) — the same engineering concept as Maersk's domain controllers, here with the gravest possible stakes: MCAS took its data from one angle-of-attack sensor.
2. Internal messages later released showed employees mocking the programme and the regulator. The documents were damning. This means…
  (a) strongly suggestive of guilt or failure   (b) legally privileged   (c) forged
(a) — damning (adj) — damning evidence, a damning report. The strongest adjective English offers a document short of calling it proof.

D · Collocation & word building

1. Build the duty family. Complete: a duty ___ care · to ___ a duty (fail it) · to discharge a duty (___ it). And which adjective describes a person bound by duty?
A duty of care · to breach a duty · to discharge a duty = to fulfil it. Adjective: duty-bound. The legal spine of every negligence argument in this case.
2. One verb completes all three: to ___ corners (save time unsafely) · to ___ costs · to ___ training to an iPad course. Same verb, three levels of consequence.
to cut — cut corners, cut costs, cut training. The case's commercial engine was the third: keeping pilot conversion training so minimal that no simulator time would be required.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss. The facts here follow the official investigations; 346 people died in the two crashes, and this lesson treats that as the constant weight behind every business detail.

Part 1 — The aeroplane the schedule designed

In 2011 Boeing faced a competitive ambush. Airbus had fitted new, dramatically more efficient engines to its A320 and was taking orders at a pace that threatened Boeing's hold on the single-aisle market — including, decisively, a possible defection of American Airlines. Boeing had been debating an all-new aircraft, a decade-long project; instead, in a decision reached within weeks, it announced the 737 MAX: a fourth-generation derivative of a design first certified in 1967, with the new engines grafted on. A derivative could be certified faster, built on existing lines — and, crucially, sold to airlines with the promise that existing 737 pilots would need only minimal conversion training. That promise had a contractual edge: one major customer's deal reportedly included compensation per aircraft if simulator training turned out to be required.

The old airframe, however, sat low to the ground, and the new engines were too large to hang where the old ones had been. Moved forward and up, they changed the aircraft's aerodynamics: in certain conditions the nose tended to pitch up more than the previous model's. Rather than alter the airframe — or accept handling differences that might trigger training requirements — engineers added software: MCAS, a system that would automatically push the nose down when a sensor indicated the angle of attack was too high. In its final form, the system could act repeatedly, with substantial authority over the controls, on the reading of a single angle-of-attack sensor — and, consistent with the strategy of sameness, it was left out of the pilot manuals and the training. Most pilots flying the MAX did not know it existed.

Certification proceeded under the delegation system, in which the regulator authorises manufacturer employees to perform much of the detailed safety assessment on its behalf. Later investigations found that the FAA's own review never fully caught up with what MCAS had become as it evolved during development — the safety analysis on file described an earlier, weaker version of the system than the one that flew.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What commercial constraints shaped the MAX's design, and what did MCAS exist to do?

2. What two facts about MCAS's final form matter most?

1. Speed against Airbus, a derivative rather than a new design, and above all the promise of minimal pilot retraining — with contractual penalties if simulator training were required. MCAS existed to make the re-engined aircraft handle like the old one, in software.

2. It acted on a single sensor with repeated, substantial authority over the controls; and pilots were not told it existed. The certification file described a weaker version than the one that flew.

Discuss:
  • "The schedule designed the aeroplane." At what point in this chain — derivative, engine placement, software patch, training promise — should someone have been required to stop and reassess the whole?

Part 2 — Lion Air 610, Ethiopian 302

On 29 October 2018, Lion Air flight 610 took off from Jakarta with a faulty angle-of-attack sensor feeding MCAS. The system repeatedly commanded the nose down; the crew, fighting a system they had never been told about, lost the struggle thirteen minutes after take-off. All 189 people aboard died. Only in the aftermath did Boeing issue a bulletin effectively informing the world's MAX pilots of MCAS's existence, while emphasising that established emergency procedures for runaway trim were sufficient. The aircraft kept flying; a software revision was promised in due course. Within the company, documents later showed, the programme's own test pilots had exchanged messages during development describing the simulator behaviour of MCAS in alarming terms — messages the company would eventually have to hand to prosecutors.

On 10 March 2019, Ethiopian Airlines flight 302 left Addis Ababa. A sensor failed in the same way; MCAS activated in the same way; the crew followed elements of the published procedure and were nonetheless overwhelmed within six minutes. All 157 people aboard died. Two essentially identical accidents, five months apart, on a brand-new aircraft type. Regulators across the world — China first, the FAA last, days later and under visible pressure — grounded the MAX. It stayed grounded for twenty months.

What the investigations then established changed the story from tragedy to case study. Congressional investigators described a culture of concealment and cost pressure; released internal messages showed employees boasting of using "Jedi mind tricks" on regulators and one describing the aircraft, in a phrase that has entered the language of engineering ethics, as designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys. The chief executive, Dennis Muilenburg, initially framed the accidents partly around foreign pilot performance — a framing the evidence dismantled — and was dismissed in December 2019. In January 2021 Boeing entered a deferred prosecution agreement with the US Department of Justice over deception of the FAA's training-evaluation group, paying some 2.5 billion dollars, the bulk of it to airline customers.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened between the two crashes, and why does that interval matter?

2. What did the investigations establish beyond the technical cause?

1. After Lion Air, Boeing disclosed MCAS's existence, pointed to existing procedures, promised a software revision — and the fleet kept flying. Ethiopian 302 then failed the same way five months later. The interval is the case's moral centre: the second accident occurred inside a known risk.

2. A culture problem, documented in the company's own messages — concealment toward the regulator, contempt for oversight, cost pressure on engineering — plus an initial executive framing that shifted focus to pilots until the evidence removed it. The DPA followed in 2021.

Discuss:
  • The hardest question in the record: after Jakarta, who was obliged to do what? Ground the fleet? Disclose everything? Fly on with a bulletin? Assign the obligations, party by party.

Part 3 — The door plug, and the long repair

The deeper post-mortems reached past the MAX to a merger. In 1997 Boeing — historically an engineers' company, run by people who had built aircraft — absorbed McDonnell Douglas, and, in the telling of many insiders and analysts, the acquired company's finance-driven culture won the internal war. Headquarters moved away from the factories; shareholder returns became the scoreboard; outsourcing fragmented engineering knowledge; and the authority of the technical voice — the person entitled to say "not yet" to a schedule — eroded over two decades. On this reading the MAX was not an aberration but an arrival: the moment a long cultural drift finally intersected with physics.

The MAX returned to service from late 2020 with MCAS rebuilt — dual-sensor inputs, limited authority, full documentation and simulator training, the requirement whose avoidance had shaped the original design. Boeing's leadership declared the lessons learned. Then, in January 2024, a door plug blew out of a nearly new 737 MAX 9 in flight; by good fortune, no one was seriously hurt. Investigators found the panel had left the factory without its retaining bolts installed. The failure was different in kind — assembly quality, not design — but it detonated the credibility of the recovery narrative: prosecutors moved to declare the 2021 DPA breached, the chief executive announced his departure, production was capped by the regulator, and in 2024 a new CEO, Kelly Ortberg, was brought in from outside with a mandate summarised in his own early messaging: move leadership back to the factory floor, and restore the engineering voice.

The case stays open — legally, commercially and culturally — and it closes this series' arc for a reason. Nokia showed truth failing to rise; VW showed impossible targets manufacturing deception; Boeing shows what those same mechanisms cost when the product carries passengers. The question it leaves is not rhetorical, and every organisation has a version of it: when your schedule and your engineers disagree, who is required — not encouraged, required — to win?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the merger argument, and what does it claim the MAX represents?

2. What happened in January 2024, and why did it matter beyond the incident itself?

1. That the 1997 McDonnell Douglas merger shifted Boeing from an engineering culture to a finance-led one — headquarters away from factories, shareholder returns as scoreboard, technical authority eroded — making the MAX an arrival, not an accident.

2. A door plug left the factory without bolts and blew out in flight. Nobody died, but the recovery narrative did: DPA declared breached, CEO out, production capped, and an outsider brought in to restore the engineering voice.

Discuss:
  • "Not encouraged — required." What formal power does the safety or quality voice hold in your organisation? Could it stop a launch this quarter, and has it ever?

Grammar — obligation & responsibility, precisely

Investigations, regulations and post-mortems run on the exact strength of duty words. "Should have" and "was required to" describe different failures with different consequences — and this case turned, repeatedly, on which one applied.

The obligation scale, present and past:
• be required / obliged to — binding external duty: "Manufacturers are required to disclose systems that affect handling." Past: was required to — breach is a violation, not a judgement call.
• be supposed to — the expected arrangement, often signalling it failed: "The delegated engineers were supposed to represent the regulator."
• be meant to — design intention: "MCAS was meant to activate rarely."
• should (have) — the speaker's judgement of right conduct: "The fleet should have been grounded after Jakarta."
• needn't have / didn't need to — the necessity that wasn't: "Pilots needn't have been kept in the dark to preserve the training promise — that link was commercial, not technical."
The responsibility formulas:
• bear responsibility for — carries it: "The company bears primary responsibility for the design."
• be accountable to X for Y — answers to someone: "The delegated engineers were accountable to the FAA for their assessments."
• owe a duty of care to — the legal frame: "A manufacturer owes a duty of care to every passenger."
• It falls to X to… / the onus is on X to… — locating the burden: "The onus was on Boeing to volunteer what the filings understated."

Precision test: required = the rulebook says so · supposed to = the arrangement says so · should = I say so. An investigator who confuses them loses the room.

Going deeper

A note on the record: this account follows the congressional, NTSB-adjacent and prosecutorial findings as publicly reported; figures are rounded; legal proceedings continued for years and aspects remain unresolved. 346 people died — their families' advocacy materially shaped the investigations, and any classroom use of this case owes them its seriousness.

What the case actually tests. First, the compounding of individually defensible decisions. A derivative instead of a new aircraft: defensible. Software compensation for handling: standard practice. Minimal training difference: the product's core promise. Each step was locally rational — the Kodak mechanism operating on a safety-critical path — and the sum was an undisclosed system with single-sensor authority. The lesson is structural: on safety-critical paths, somebody must be required to price the accumulated sum, because no individual step will ever look like the place to stop.

Second, regulatory capture by capacity. Delegation existed because the regulator could not staff the expertise it was supervising — a structural dependency, not a bribe. Wherever the referee must borrow the players' eyes — aviation, finance (recall EY at Wirecard), AI safety, pharmaceuticals — the honest question is not "is the regulator independent?" but "independent with what information, generated by whom, checked how?"

Third, the limits of culture-talk. "Safety first" hung on every Boeing wall throughout. The cases in this series keep converging on the same finding under different logos: values are what the organisation pays for under pressure, and the only reliable evidence is institutional — who can stop the line, what happens to their career, and whether the schedule has ever actually lost. Ortberg's move of leadership back to the factory floor is the correct kind of answer precisely because it is structural rather than rhetorical. Whether it is sufficient, the next decade of aircraft will answer.

Talk it through:
  • Design the "accumulated sum" checkpoint: at what moments in a long programme should someone be required to re-price all the compromises together — and what authority must they hold?
  • Delegated oversight exists because expertise is scarce. Propose a better structure for a regulator that cannot match its industry's knowledge — or defend delegation with stronger safeguards.
  • The families of the victims forced disclosures, hearings and the DPA's revisiting. What does their role say about where accountability actually comes from when institutions fail?
  • Series capstone: Nokia (truth couldn't rise), VW (targets manufactured deception), Boeing (both, with lives attached). Write the single structural rule that addresses all three — and name what it would cost your organisation to adopt it.
