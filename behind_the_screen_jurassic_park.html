BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Jurassic Park

A billionaire clones living dinosaurs for a theme park — and discovers, too late, that some experiments should stay in the ground. The real science is stranger than the film.

Today: you'll tell the story of Jurassic Park — a billionaire's dream of a theme park full of cloned dinosaurs that turns into a nightmare — discover how much of its science is real (and how much never could be), and master the way English argues both sides of an idea at once: concession and contrast — although, even though, despite, whereas and however.
Before we begin — talk it through: If scientists could genuinely bring an extinct animal back to life, should they? Is there a difference between something being possible and something being a good idea? Would you visit a zoo of real, living dinosaurs — or does that sound like a terrible idea to you?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. After the chemical leak, engineers built a second wall around the tank, hoping this simple containment would stop the poison from ever reaching the river.
containment (n) — the act of keeping something, especially something dangerous, controlled and prevented from spreading or escaping.
2. Within minutes of the fire alarm, chairs were knocked over, papers scattered everywhere, and the whole office was thrown into total havoc.
havoc (n) — great confusion, disorder or widespread destruction.
3. The bridge had passed every safety test, yet an unforeseen crack, hidden deep inside the metal, brought it down without warning.
unforeseen (adj) — not expected or predicted in advance.
4. He drove through the red light at full speed with his eyes on his phone — a reckless decision that could easily have killed someone.
reckless (adj) — careless of danger or consequences; acting without proper thought.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. resilient is closest in meaning to: (a) easily broken   (b) able to recover quickly from damage   (c) impossible to control
(b) able to recover quickly from damage — resilient (adj) means able to withstand or recover quickly from difficult conditions.
6. lucrative is closest in meaning to: (a) highly profitable   (b) very dangerous   (c) completely legal
(a) highly profitable — lucrative (adj) means producing a great deal of money.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. Someone had clearly tampered with the lock overnight — the mechanism was scratched and no longer closed properly.
To tamper with something is: (a) to repair it carefully; (b) to interfere with it in a way that causes damage or changes it dishonestly; (c) to admire it from a distance.
(b) — to tamper with (v) — to interfere with something, often secretly or dishonestly, in a way that damages or changes it.
8. Opening the cage door by mistake would unleash a hundred angry dogs into the crowded street.
To unleash something is: (a) to lock it away safely; (b) to release it suddenly, often with dangerous force; (c) to slowly repair it.
(b) — to unleash (v) — to suddenly release a force, especially one that is powerful or destructive.

D. Infer from context — two bigger ideas

9. The captain was so certain the ship could never sink that he ignored every warning about the ice ahead — a hubris that history has never forgotten.
hubris (n) — excessive pride or confidence that leads a person to ignore danger or their own limits.
10. The two engineers were so preoccupied with making the new engine faster that neither of them stopped to ask whether it was actually safe.
preoccupied (adj) — so absorbed in one thought or task that you fail to consider other, often more important, things.

The film, in three parts

Steven Spielberg's Jurassic Park (1993), based on Michael Crichton's novel. Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — Every safety system imaginable

On a private island off Costa Rica, the billionaire John Hammond has built something no one has ever seen: a theme park stocked with living, breathing dinosaurs, cloned from genetic material found in prehistoric mosquitoes trapped in amber. When an accident with a raptor spooks his investors, Hammond invites a small group of outside experts to endorse the park before it opens: the paleontologist Dr Alan Grant, the paleobotanist Dr Ellie Sattler, and the mathematician Dr Ian Malcolm, along with the park's lawyer and Hammond's own grandchildren, Lex and Tim.

The tour begins in wonder. A towering Brachiosaurus rears up on its hind legs to reach the treetops, and Grant, a lifelong sceptic of theme parks, is reduced to speechless awe. But the cracks show quickly. A sick Triceratops worries the scientists, and in the laboratory, Grant is horrified to learn that the dinosaurs — engineered to be all female, to make breeding impossible — have somehow found a way around it, using genetic material borrowed from frogs capable of changing sex.

Malcolm, whose entire career is built on chaos theory — the study of how small, unpredictable events can spiral through a complex system — warns Hammond bluntly: no system this complicated can ever really be controlled, whatever the engineers believe. Hammond is charmed by his own creation, and deaf to the warning.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What has Hammond built on the island, and how were the dinosaurs cloned?

2. Why does Malcolm say the park can never really be safe?

1. A theme park of cloned, living dinosaurs, made from DNA taken from ancient mosquitoes preserved in amber, with gaps in the DNA filled using frog DNA.

2. Because it is such an enormously complex system that unpredictable, chaotic events are certain to occur, no matter how well it is engineered.

Discuss:
  • Malcolm argues that some systems are too complex to ever fully control, no matter how much money or technology you throw at them. Can you think of a real system — a power grid, a financial market, an aircraft — where the same idea applies?

Part 2 — A storm, and a stolen truck

That night, a tropical storm rolls in — and so does betrayal. Dennis Nedry, a park programmer secretly paid by a rival company to steal dinosaur embryos, shuts down the island's computer systems to cover his theft, cutting the power to every electric fence at once. The tour vehicles, carrying Grant, Malcolm, the lawyer and the two children, stall right beside the paddock of the park's single most dangerous resident: a full-grown Tyrannosaurus rex.

Without its fence, the rex breaks free. It flips one vehicle, kills the fleeing lawyer, and Malcolm is badly injured shielding the children. Grant manages to get Lex and Tim to safety, but the group is now scattered across the island in the dark, with the power down and no way to call for help.

Meanwhile, Nedry — lost in the storm and hunting for his getaway route — meets a small, almost comical-looking dinosaur, a Dilophosaurus, that turns out to be far more venomous and dangerous than anyone at the park had bothered to warn him about.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why do the park's electric fences fail?

2. What happens to the tour group when the T. rex escapes?

1. Nedry sabotages the security system to cover up his theft of embryos, cutting power to the electric fences during the storm.

2. Their vehicle is stranded beside the T. rex paddock; the rex attacks, kills the lawyer, injures Malcolm, and scatters the group across the island in the dark.

Discuss:
  • Nedry's greed, not the dinosaurs themselves, sets the disaster in motion. Is human dishonesty always the real danger in stories like this — more than the technology itself?

Part 3 — Life finds its own way home

Grant and the children survive the night by improvising: sleeping in trees, wading past a herd of Gallimimus fleeing the rex, and finding proof, in an abandoned nest, that the dinosaurs really are breeding on their own. Back at the visitor centre, Sattler and the park's game warden risk the darkened grounds to restore the power, with help from Lex's genuine talent for computers.

But restoring power reactivates every system at once — including the door to the raptor enclosure. Two Velociraptors, the smartest and most calculating predators on the island, stalk the reunited group through the empty kitchens and corridors of the visitor centre, in the film's tensest sequence.

Just as the raptors are about to corner them all, the escaped T. rex crashes through the wall and finishes the fight instead. The survivors escape the island by helicopter, Hammond's dream in ruins — and Malcolm's warning proven right: the people who built this park were so consumed by whether they could do it that nobody had properly asked whether they should.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How do Grant and the children discover the dinosaurs are breeding?

2. What finally saves the group from the raptors?

1. They find hatched eggshells out in the wild, proof that the frog DNA let the "all-female" dinosaurs reproduce anyway.

2. The escaped T. rex bursts in and attacks the raptors, letting the group escape while the two predators fight each other.

Discuss:
  • By the end, the park's own worst mistake — the escaped rex — is what saves the humans. What do you think the film is really saying about our confidence in controlling nature?

Grammar — concession & contrast

Jurassic Park is a film built on one contrast: what science could do versus what it should do. English has several tools for holding two contrasting facts in a single thought — some soften the contrast as a concession ("yes, but…"), others simply set two facts side by side.

The four connectors:
• although / even though / though + subject + verb (a full clause): "Although the island had every safety system money could buy, one storm brought it all down."
• despite / in spite of + noun or -ing (never a full clause): "Despite its state-of-the-art security, the park fell apart in a single night." "In spite of knowing the risk, Hammond opened the park anyway."
• whereas + clause — sets two different facts side by side, without necessarily any surprise: "Grant trusted the animals, whereas Hammond trusted the machines completely."
• however — links two separate sentences, usually after a full stop or semicolon, with a comma after it: "The engineers had thought of everything. However, they hadn't reckoned with one dishonest employee."
Feel the difference: although and despite both mean "yes, but surprisingly" — the difference between them is only grammar (clause vs noun/-ing), not meaning. Whereas is calmer — it compares, without implying anything went wrong. However is the most formal, and always starts (or restarts) a sentence of its own.

1. Clause or noun? Choose the connector the structure needs

Look at what follows the gap, then choose although / even though / though (+ clause) or despite / in spite of (+ noun/-ing).

1. the raptors' cage was reinforced steel, they still worked out how to open the door.

2. decades of research into the animals' behaviour, nobody at InGen expected the raptors to hunt as a coordinated team.

3. being warned repeatedly by his own mathematician, Hammond refused to delay the park's opening.

1. Although / Even though / Though (followed by a full clause: "the raptors' cage was...")

2. Despite / In spite of (followed by a noun phrase: "decades of research...")

3. Despite / In spite of (followed by an -ing phrase: "being warned...")

2. Fix the mistake

Each sentence uses the wrong connector for its structure. Write the connector that should replace it.

1. Despite Nedry stole the embryos to sell them, he never made it off the island alive. (wrong: "despite" + full clause)

2. Although the chaos inside the park, Malcolm had predicted almost exactly how it would unfold. (wrong: "although" + noun)

3. The T. rex seemed to be the island's greatest danger; despite, the smaller raptors turned out to be far deadlier. (wrong: "despite" joining two sentences)

1. Although / Even though / Though ("Nedry stole" is a full clause)

2. Despite / In spite of ("the chaos" is a noun)

3. However (linking two separate sentences)

3. Complete the story — mix all four connectors

Fill each gap with although, despite, whereas or however as the context needs.

The park's investors were reassured by the electric fences and the motion sensors. , none of that technology mattered once the man in charge of the whole system had already sold their secrets. Grant had never trusted computers, Hammond trusted almost nothing else. And everything that went wrong on the island, Malcolm took no pleasure in being right — he had hoped, more than anyone, that his own theory would fail this time.

However · whereas · despite / in spite of

Notice the pattern: a new sentence needs however; two clauses side by side need whereas; a noun phrase needs despite / in spite of.

Carry these into the Speak tab — the whole film lives in one contrast: although the science was extraordinary, however impressive the park looked, nobody stopped to ask whether it should exist at all.

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

The film's central method for bringing back dinosaurs turns out to be scientifically impossible. But real "de-extinction" science exists, and it's stranger — and more careful — than the film ever imagined.

🧬 Could scientists really clone a dinosaur from a mosquito in amber? No.

DNA breaks down steadily over time, no matter how well it is preserved. Research on DNA decay has found it has a "half-life" of only around 521 years even under ideal conditions, meaning that even in perfectly preserved remains, genetic information becomes too fragmented to read at all within roughly 1.5 million years at the very most — a tiny fraction of the 66 million years since the dinosaurs died out. Some researchers in the early 1990s (right around when Crichton's novel and the film appeared) reported recovering DNA fragments from insects preserved in amber tens of millions of years old, but no other laboratory has ever been able to repeat those results, and most scientists now believe the "ancient DNA" in question was really modern contamination. The amber-mosquito method at the heart of Jurassic Park is today considered scientifically impossible.

But real de-extinction science is advancing — just not this way

A real biotechnology company, Colossal Biosciences, is working to bring back the woolly mammoth — not by finding ancient mammoth DNA and growing it (mammoth DNA, though far better preserved than any dinosaur's since mammoths died out only a few thousand years ago, is still too fragmented to use directly), but by editing the genome of the Asian elephant, the mammoth's closest living relative, inserting mammoth traits such as a shaggy coat and cold tolerance directly into elephant DNA. The company reports producing stem cells that can be propagated indefinitely, and is aiming for mammoth-like embryos as early as 2026 and live calves by 2028.

The same company has already used a related gene-editing approach — not ancient-DNA cloning — to produce wolf pups with dire-wolf-like traits, though its own chief scientist has been candid that the animals are genetically modified grey wolves, not a true resurrection of the extinct dire wolf. Real "de-extinction," in other words, means editing a living relative's genome — not reviving a lost one.

The ethics: chaos theory and "just because we can"

Ian Malcolm's warning is grounded in a genuine field of mathematics called chaos theory, developed from the 1960s onward, which shows that complex systems — weather, ecosystems, financial markets — can be so sensitive to tiny starting differences that their future behaviour becomes fundamentally unpredictable, an effect sometimes nicknamed the "butterfly effect." Real scientists working on de-extinction today face a version of the same question Malcolm raised: even as gene-editing technology keeps improving, reintroducing a long-vanished species could have consequences for today's ecosystems that nobody can fully predict. The live debate isn't really about whether the technology will work — increasingly, it does — but about whether success would even be a good idea.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, did you think the amber-DNA method was real science or pure invention?
  • Does it change how you feel about the film, knowing its central method is impossible — while a very different kind of de-extinction really is happening?
  • Malcolm's warning applies well beyond dinosaurs. Where else in modern science or technology do you think people focus on whether something is possible, without stopping to ask whether it's wise?
