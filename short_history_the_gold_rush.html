The California Gold Rush

A few flakes of metal in a mountain river — and hundreds of thousands of people threw everything away to chase them west.

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Deep in the mountains, a small crew of men worked a sawmill, cutting logs into timber to build the new houses of a growing town.
a sawmill (n) — a place with machinery for cutting logs (whole trees) into planks and boards of wood.
2. She crouched by the river with a shallow metal pan, swirling the water and gravel round and round to see what settled at the bottom.
a pan (n, here) — a shallow, round metal dish used to wash river gravel and separate out heavy gold; to pan for gold = to search for gold this way.
3. Every hopeful newcomer wanted to stake a claim — a marked piece of ground that, by the rough law of the camps, gave them the sole right to dig for gold there.
a claim (n, here) — a plot of land a miner marks out and registers, claiming the exclusive right to mine it; to stake a claim = to mark and register such land.
4. Word of the strike spread like fire, and soon a great stampede of wagons, ships and desperate men was pouring toward the goldfields.
a stampede (n) — a sudden rush of a large crowd (or herd), all moving fast in the same direction, often out of excitement or panic.
5. When a rich vein of gold was found running through the rock, the miners followed its glittering line deeper and deeper into the hillside.
a vein (n, here) — a long, narrow deposit of metal or mineral running through rock, like a line or seam.
6. The general store sold shovels at ten times their old price; a single egg cost a fortune, because in a boomtown, prices obeyed no ordinary rule.
a boomtown (n) — a town that grows huge, rich and chaotic very fast, usually because of a sudden discovery or industry.
7. Most who came struck nothing; the real fortunes were made not by the diggers but by the merchants who sold them boots, flour and picks.
a merchant (n) — a person whose business is buying and selling goods, especially in large amounts; a trader.
8. For those who came with nothing and left with nothing, the goldfields brought ruin — debts, broken health, and years of their lives simply gone.
ruin (n) — the complete loss of one's money, position or hopes; total collapse.
9. The land itself paid a price: whole hillsides were torn open and rivers were diverted from their beds so that men could reach the gold beneath.
to divert (v) — to change the direction or route of something (here, to force a river to flow a different way).
10. For the Native peoples already living there, the rush was a catastrophe — their lands seized, their communities shattered in only a few years.
a catastrophe (n) — a sudden, terrible event that causes great suffering, damage or loss.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A glitter in the tailrace

On the morning of 24 January 1848, a carpenter named James Marshall was inspecting the water channel of a half-built sawmill on the American River, in the foothills of the Sierra Nevada. The mill belonged to John Sutter, a Swiss immigrant who dreamed of building an agricultural empire in the near-empty Mexican territory of California. Something in the ditch caught Marshall's eye — a few bright, heavy flakes glinting in the gravel. He picked them up, and his hand trembled. It was gold.

Sutter, when he heard, was horrified rather than delighted. He understood at once what a discovery like this could do to his careful plans, and he begged his workers to keep it quiet. But a secret of that magnitude cannot be kept. Word leaked to a merchant in San Francisco named Sam Brannan, who saw the real opportunity — not in the gold itself, but in the people who would come for it. He bought up every shovel, pick and pan he could find, then marched through the streets waving a bottle of gold dust and shouting, "Gold! Gold from the American River!"

The effect was instant. San Francisco half-emptied within weeks. Sailors abandoned their ships in the harbour, shopkeepers locked their doors, soldiers deserted their posts. Nobody wanted to be the last to arrive. Sutter's own workers walked off the job to dig, his land was overrun by trespassers, and the empire he had planned quietly collapsed beneath a flood of strangers.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who found the gold, where, and whose land was it on?

2. How did Sam Brannan turn the discovery into a stampede, and why?

1. James Marshall, a carpenter, found gold in the water channel of a sawmill on the American River; the land belonged to John Sutter, a Swiss immigrant.

2. The merchant Sam Brannan first bought up all the mining supplies, then paraded through San Francisco shouting about the gold — so he got rich selling equipment to the crowds he had stirred up.

Discuss:
  • Sutter tried to keep the discovery secret and Brannan tried to broadcast it. Whose instinct do you understand better, and why?

Part 2 — The world comes to California

By 1849 the news had crossed oceans, and the great migration began. They came to be called the "forty-niners," and there were hundreds of thousands of them: farmers from the American Midwest, Chinese labourers, Mexicans, Chileans, Australians, Irish and French. Some travelled thousands of miles overland by wagon across deserts and mountains; others took the long sea voyage around the tip of South America, or risked the fever-ridden jungles of Panama to cut the journey short. A great many of them died on the way.

Those who survived found a world turned upside down. In the early days, the gold lay close to the surface, and a man with hardly any skill could kneel by a river with a pan and, with luck, wash out a little fortune. Camps sprang up overnight with names like Rough and Ready and Hangtown. San Francisco swelled from a sleepy village of a few hundred into a roaring boomtown of tens of thousands in barely two years.

But prices in the goldfields were insane. There was hardly any food and very little shelter, so a loaf of bread might cost what a labourer back east earned in a week. The plain truth, which most miners learned too late, was this: a great deal of gold was taken out of California, but only a lucky few grew rich from digging it. The steadiest fortunes were made by the merchants — the men who sold the shovels, the eggs, the whisky and the boots. One young trader named Levi Strauss arrived to sell supplies, and later made hard-wearing trousers for miners; his name is on jeans to this day.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who were the "forty-niners," and by what routes did they reach California?

2. Why did so few miners actually get rich, and who did make reliable money?

1. The forty-niners were the hundreds of thousands who rushed to California from 1849, coming from all over the world; they travelled overland by wagon, by sea around South America, or across Panama.

2. The easy surface gold soon ran out and prices were ruinously high, so most miners barely broke even; the merchants who sold supplies made the steadiest fortunes.

Discuss:
  • "Sell shovels, not dreams." Why do you think the traders so often did better than the dreamers who came to dig?

Part 3 — What the gold left behind

The rush remade California in a handful of years. In 1850, on the strength of its exploding population, it became the thirty-first state of the Union with astonishing speed. San Francisco grew into a major city; roads, banks and businesses followed the gold. The whole centre of gravity of the young United States tilted westward, and the dream of a nation stretching "from sea to shining sea" began to feel real.

Yet the cost was heavy, and it fell hardest on those who had been there all along. For the Native peoples of California, the rush was a catastrophe: their land was seized, their food sources destroyed, and their communities devastated by violence and disease. In only about twenty years their population collapsed by the great majority — one of the darkest chapters in American history. Chinese and Latin American miners, too, faced special taxes and open hostility as the easy gold disappeared and competition turned ugly.

The land itself was scarred. When the surface gold was gone, companies turned to hydraulic mining, blasting hillsides apart with jets of water and diverting whole rivers, choking the valleys downstream with mud. By the 1850s the wild, hopeful days of the lone miner with his pan were fading, and heavy industry had moved in. The rush had lasted only a few short years — but it had summoned a state into being, reshaped a nation, and left wounds, on people and on the earth, that would take generations to heal.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the Gold Rush do for California and the United States as a whole?

2. Who and what paid the price for the rush?

1. It made California a state in 1850 with remarkable speed, grew San Francisco into a major city, and pulled the whole nation's growth westward.

2. The Native peoples of California suffered a catastrophe (land seized, population collapsing by the great majority); Chinese and Latin American miners faced hostility and special taxes; and the land itself was ravaged by hydraulic mining and diverted rivers.

Discuss:
  • The Gold Rush is often told as a story of adventure and opportunity. How does the story change when you tell it from the point of view of the people who already lived there?

Grammar — quantifiers

The Gold Rush is a story about amounts — a few flakes, thousands of people, hardly any food, a great deal of gold. Quantifiers are the words English puts before a noun to say how much or how many. Choosing the right one depends on one key question: can you count the noun, or not?

Countable vs uncountable — the heart of it:
• Countable nouns have a plural and can take a number: one miner, two ships, thousands of people, a few flakes.
• Uncountable nouns have no plural and cannot take a number directly: gold, water, food, luck, money. (Not "a gold / two golds".)
The quantifier you choose must match the type of noun.
The main quantifiers, sorted:
• With countable nouns: many, a few, hardly any, thousands of, hundreds of, (not) very many. — "There were thousands of hopeful miners." "Only a few struck it rich."
• With uncountable nouns: much, a little, a great deal of, hardly any, (not) very much. — "They dug out a great deal of gold." "There was hardly any food."
• With both: a lot of / lots of, some, any, plenty of, no. — "A lot of people came." / "A lot of money was made."
Two things worth noticing:
• hardly any means "almost none" — it works with both types: "hardly any food" (uncountable), "hardly any nuggets" (countable).
• a few (countable) and a little (uncountable) mean "a small but positive amount"; drop the a — "few / little" — and the meaning turns negative: "few miners got rich" = not many, disappointingly.

1. Countable or uncountable? Choose the right quantifier

Choose from: a great deal of, thousands of, hardly any, a few, many. Use each once.

1. By 1849, people were pouring into California from all over the world.

2. In the early camps there was food, so prices were extremely high.

3. Only of the miners actually became rich from digging.

4. gold was taken out of the ground, but few diggers kept it.

5. How ships were abandoned in San Francisco harbour?

1. thousands of (countable — people)

2. hardly any (uncountable — food; "almost none")

3. a few (countable — miners; "a small number")

4. A great deal of (uncountable — gold)

5. many (countable — ships, in a question)

2. much or many? a little or a few?

Write the correct quantifier for each noun.

1. There wasn't shelter in the camps, and the winters were brutal. (shelter)

2. There weren't doctors within a hundred miles. (doctors)

3. With luck and a good spot, a man might wash out some gold. (luck)

4. He kept nuggets in a leather pouch and spent the rest. (nuggets)

1. much (uncountable — shelter, in a negative)

2. many (countable — doctors, in a negative)

3. a little (uncountable — luck; a small positive amount)

4. a few (countable — nuggets; a small positive amount)

3. Tell the story with the right amounts — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with a suitable quantifier that fits the noun. Read it aloud once you're done.

When the news spread, people rushed west, more than the roads could hold. In the goldfields there was gold in the ground, yet there was food to buy and prices were savage. Only lucky diggers grew rich, while merchants made steady money selling shovels and boots.

Sample answers: thousands of / hundreds of thousands of (countable — people) · a great deal of / a lot of (uncountable — gold) · hardly any / very little (uncountable — food) · a few (countable — diggers) · many / a lot of (countable — merchants).

Notice how the quantifier flips with the noun type: "a great deal of gold" (uncountable) but "a few diggers" (countable).

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The Gold Rush drew people from every continent chasing the same dream. What is it about the promise of sudden riches that makes so many people abandon safe lives to chase it?
  2. Most forty-niners ended up with hardly any gold, yet the myth of the rush is one of opportunity and adventure. Why do the winners' stories survive and the failures' stories vanish?
