Looking ahead: By the 16th century, Epicureanism had been recovered and defended by Valla. Now other humanists began to embrace aspects of Epicurean thinking openly. Erasmus, Thomas More, and other leading Renaissance thinkers found in Epicureanism values they wanted to promote.

Desiderius Erasmus (1466-1536): The "Epicurean" Christian

Erasmus was the most influential Renaissance intellectual. He pioneered "Christian humanism" - combining classical learning with Christian faith. But for Erasmus, this included celebrating Epicurean values.

Who Was Erasmus?

The Prince of Humanists

Born in Rotterdam (probably), Erasmus became the leading intellectual in Europe. He was a scholar, a writer, a religious reformer, and a diplomat. He knew everyone - kings, popes, scholars.

Key insight: Erasmus believed that classical learning and Christian faith could be unified. You didn't have to choose between secular wisdom and religious truth.

He championed studia humanitatis - the study of human culture, literature, languages, and philosophy as paths to wisdom and virtue. This included studying pagan authors seriously.

Erasmus and the "Epicurean Christian"

Erasmus famously said: "I could wish my life resembled that of the ancient philosophers." He was drawn to ancient philosophy because it valued wisdom, friendship, and freedom from fear.

In his Praise of Folly and other works, Erasmus celebrates:

  • Friendship as the highest good: Epicurus taught this; Erasmus embraced it.
  • Simple living: Why accumulate wealth you don't need? Why create anxiety through excess?
  • Intellectual pleasure: The greatest pleasure comes from learning, conversation, reading - not bodily excess.
  • Freedom from superstition: He attacked false belief and empty ritual - even in the Church itself.

Key point: Erasmus didn't call himself an Epicurean, but he lived according to Epicurean principles.

The Clash with Luther

When Martin Luther sparked the Reformation, Erasmus was caught between two camps. Luther's harsh, combative theology clashed with Erasmus's humanistic faith in reason and conciliation.

The issue: Luther believed in free will or predestination; Erasmus believed in human moral agency and the power of education.

Erasmus's position was essentially Epicurean: humans have the capacity and freedom to reason about right action. The good life comes from exercising our rational faculties, not blind obedience to authority (whether papal or prophetic).

"I could wish my life resembled that of the ancient philosophers... Study brings pleasure, and if it brings no other reward, at least it keeps you from vice. The cultivated mind finds happiness in contemplation and wisdom." - Paraphrased from Erasmus

Thomas More's Utopia (1516)

Thomas More was an English lawyer, diplomat, and saint. He wrote Utopia - an imaginary account of a perfect society on an unknown island. The book is shot through with Epicurean values.

What Is Utopia?

The Setting and Society

Utopia describes an ideal commonwealth where:

  • No private property: Resources are shared communally.
  • No extreme wealth or poverty: Everyone has basic security.
  • Everyone works: But work hours are limited (6 hours daily).
  • Time for leisure: The goal is to provide time for reading, conversation, friendship.
  • Rational pleasure: People enjoy simple meals, music, and intellectual pursuits.

Epicurean core: The society is designed to minimize unnecessary labor and anxiety while maximizing friendship, learning, and simple pleasure.

How Utopia Is Epicurean

More's vision embodies Epicurean principles:

  • Freedom from fear: Security through communal provision means no fear of poverty or starvation.
  • Minimizing desires: By eliminating wealth-seeking, greed is eliminated.
  • Valuing friendship: With basic needs met and work reduced, people can spend time with loved ones.
  • Intellectual pleasure: Libraries and schools are central. Learning is encouraged as the highest pleasure.
  • Rejection of excess: Utopians reject luxury, gaudy ornament, and wasteful display - just like Epicurus taught.

Difference from Epicureanism: More adds communal emphasis. Epicurus valued friendship and moderate community. More imagines a whole society built on these principles.

Religious Dimension

Interestingly, Utopia has religious elements. The Utopians believe in God and practice various religions tolerantly (except atheism, which they don't permit).

This parallels Valla's strategy: More incorporates religious faith into a fundamentally Epicurean vision. The society is organized to achieve happiness (Epicurean goal) while respecting belief in divine providence (Christian element).

Reception and Meaning

What was Utopia really? Is it sincere? A satire? A dream? More left it ambiguous. But it showed how powerful the Epicurean vision was: a society built on rational pleasure, friendship, and security rather than wealth, power, and dominance.

Renaissance Reception of Lucretius and Atomism

Beyond ethics and society, Epicureanism influenced Renaissance thought in science and natural philosophy.

The Renaissance Discovery of Lucretius's Physics

What Lucretius Offered

Lucretius presented a complete natural philosophy: atoms and void explain all matter and change. No need for final causes (Aristotle's "purpose" in nature). No need for celestial spheres or divine intervention in nature.

Why this mattered: It offered an alternative to Aristotelian physics, which dominated medieval and Renaissance universities. It suggested that nature operates through mechanical principles, not divine purposes.

This planted seeds for the Scientific Revolution.

Atomism in the 16th-17th Centuries

After Lucretius was recovered, atomism gained slowly. Renaissance natural philosophers debated:

  • Can matter be infinitely divisible, or are there indivisible atoms?
  • Does the void exist, or is space filled with matter or aether?
  • Can mechanical principles explain natural phenomena?

Key figure: Pierre Gassendi (1592-1655) developed an explicit Christian atomism, building directly on Epicurean foundations.

The Bridge to Modern Science

The acceptance of atomism helped open the way for modern science because:

  • Mechanism: If atoms moving in void explain nature, you don't need divine intervention to explain events.
  • Quantification: Atoms are discrete, countable, measurable - making mathematics applicable to nature.
  • Empiricism: To understand nature, you observe how atoms combine and move - not pure reason alone.

Result: Lucretius and Epicureanism provided intellectual resources for empirical, mechanical natural philosophy - the foundation of modern science.

Crucial connection: Epicureanism was not just an ethical philosophy. Its atomism and materialism were scientifically fruitful. The recovery of Epicureanism helped enable the Scientific Revolution.

The Arc of the Story: We've traced five centuries of Epicureanism's journey. Medieval damnation (Lesson 20). Hidden survival (Lesson 21). Dramatic rediscovery (Lesson 22). Careful reconciliation with Christianity (Lesson 23). And finally, open embrace by leading Renaissance minds (Lesson 24).
What Epicureanism Offers: A vision of the good life based on rational pleasure, friendship, freedom from fear and superstition, and the pursuit of wisdom. Over 2,000 years, through persecution and suppression, this vision survived because it speaks to something fundamental in human nature: the desire to live well, freely, thoughtfully.
Continuing Legacy: After the Renaissance, Epicureanism would face new challenges and new advocates. In the 17th century, Gassendi would develop it further. In the Enlightenment, Epicurean ideas would influence debates about ethics, politics, and the proper scope of government. Today, these questions remain urgent: What constitutes the good life? How should we balance pleasure with virtue? Can reason guide our desires?
