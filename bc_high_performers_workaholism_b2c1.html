Setting the Scene

Before the debate, let's prepare. Consider these questions about workaholism:

Why is workaholism on the rise? What pressures exist in modern working cultures that might encourage excessive work?
What are the dangers? Beyond stress, what serious consequences can workaholism have on individuals and organisations?
What can be done? What role should employers, governments, or individuals play in addressing workaholism?
Think about it: Is workaholism really an illness? What evidence would you need to accept it as a medical condition?
Brainstorm: Debate Topics

What topics should a TV debate about workaholism cover? Consider these angles:

Why workaholism is a serious problem — health impacts, productivity paradox, social costs
Why some argue it can be positive — ambition, achievement, career advancement
What can be done about it — company policies, government intervention, individual responsibility
Measures companies or governments should take — limits on working hours, mental health support, penalties for overwork
Advice for someone caught in the workaholic cycle — practical steps to recovery

The Four Roles
1Workplace Counsellor
▼

Your role: Counsellor at a medium-sized American company.

Your perspective: You have extensive research into the disadvantages of workaholism. You're trying to get it medically accepted as an illness. You're currently working with seven employees struggling with overwork.

Your goal: Convince others that workaholism is a serious, treatable condition that requires company and medical support.

Key points to consider: Health impacts, cognitive decline, family breakdown, long-term productivity loss, need for workplace policies.

2HR Manager, Multinational Corporation
▼

Your role: HR Manager at a large multinational company.

Your perspective: You don't see workaholism as a real problem. You regard concerns about it as "fluffy." You're under pressure from senior leadership to ensure the workforce works hard and delivers results.

Your goal: Argue that the business needs dedicated, hardworking employees and that some people simply have high ambition.

Key points to consider: Business competitiveness, employee selection, high performers vs. burnout, individual responsibility.

3Fully-Recovered Former Workaholic
▼

Your role: Someone who was deeply caught in workaholism but has recovered.

Your perspective: Your private life was a disaster — relationships broken, health damaged. You've found peace through therapy and lifestyle change. But now you earn less, and the financial stress creates new anxiety.

Your challenge: Recovery is possible, but it comes with real costs. You need to navigate between wellness and financial survival.

Key points to consider: Personal transformation, therapy effectiveness, the price of recovery, support systems, relapse risk from financial pressure.

4Current Workaholic (Unaware)
▼

Your role: Working in advertising, fully embedded in workaholic culture.

Your perspective: You're completely unaware you have a problem. You manage important client accounts. You have no partner, limited family contact. Work feels like your identity — you love it.

Your goal: Defend your lifestyle; justify why constant work is normal and necessary in your industry.

Key points to consider: Industry norms, client demands, personal satisfaction, lack of awareness, resistance to criticism about lifestyle.

Discussion Prompts

To the Counsellor: What specific evidence do you have that workaholism causes impaired brain and cognitive function? How would you respond to someone who says they simply enjoy working?
To the HR Manager: How do you balance the need for business success with employee well-being? At what point does dedication become dangerous?
To the Recovered Workaholic: How did you first recognise you had a problem? What would you say to your younger self?
To the Current Workaholic: How would you respond if your employer told you to reduce working hours? What would you lose?
All panellists: Were there any points all of you could agree on? Where do your views overlap?
After the debate: Reflect on whether you used the collocations and prepositions from Tab 2. Which arguments were strongest? Which changed your perspective?
