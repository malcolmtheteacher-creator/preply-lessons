Putting the News Last

The passive isn't really about hiding who did something — it's about word order: old information first, new information last.

Spot the pattern

  • "That painting was actually painted by my grandmother — nobody believes me when I tell them."
  • "The whole department's being run by someone who only joined six months ago, apparently."
  • "The planning application was finally rejected by the council last night, after months of arguing."

Before you check: in each sentence, what's already being talked about, and what's the actual news? Which comes first, and which comes last?

Check the pattern CLICK
The already-known thing (the painting, the department, the application) opens the sentence as the subject. The actual news — often who did it — comes last, usually introduced by "by". Say it the other way round ("My grandmother painted that painting" / "Someone who only joined six months ago is running the whole department") and you front-load a name the listener isn't ready for, and bury the thing they actually asked about. That's the real reason the passive is so common in news and gossip: it isn't about hiding the agent, it's about putting old information before new information.
[already-known subject] + be + past participle + by + [the actual news]

From new-first to news-last

"A former MasterChef contestant runs the new restaurant on the corner."
→
"The new restaurant on the corner is run by a former MasterChef contestant."

The restaurant — already the topic — moves to the front. The surprising fact (who actually runs it) becomes the news, saved for the end.

Passing On a Rumour

For claims you can't (or won't) confirm — reputations, rumours, careful official wording.

Spot the pattern

  • "The new manager is said to be extremely difficult to work with — though I've never met her myself."
  • "That painting in the hallway is now thought to have been a fake all along."
  • "It's widely believed that the merger fell through because of a personality clash at the top."

Look closely at "is said to be" and "is thought to have been" — what's different about the time each one points to? And what's different about how the first two sentences are built compared with the third?

Check the pattern CLICK
"To be" points to now, or to something generally true ("she's difficult, right now"). "To have been" points further back — something that happened, or turned out to be true, before now ("it was a fake all along — we only realise that today"). Sentences 1 and 2 make the person or thing the subject ("the manager is said...", "that painting is thought..."); sentence 3 keeps the claim itself as the subject: "It is/was + said/thought/believed + that...". Both patterns report something without naming who told you, or promising it's true — ideal for rumours, reputations, and careful official statements.
It is / was + said / thought / believed / rumoured / reported + that + clause
subject + is / was + said / thought / believed + to be ... (true now, or generally)
subject + is / was + said / thought / believed + to have been ... (true earlier, already finished)
There's technically a continuous version too — "is said to be being repaired" — but native speakers avoid stacking that many words together. In real conversation you'll only hear "to be" and "to have been".

Which one sounds more natural here?

Compare: "It's rumoured that the shop is closing down" and "The shop is rumoured to be closing down." Which one makes the shop feel more like the actual topic of the conversation?

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
The second one — "the shop is rumoured to be closing down" — because the shop is the subject of the whole sentence, not buried inside a that-clause. Use the personal pattern (subject + is said/thought/believed + to...) when that person or thing IS what you're talking about; reach for the impersonal "It is/was said/thought/believed that..." when the whole claim is the news, or there's no single natural subject to put first — which is why official statements often prefer it: "It is understood that talks will continue."

One Thing After Another

For linking two actions by the same person or thing — one finished before the other began.

Spot the pattern

  • "Having missed the last two trains, I just gave up and got a taxi."
  • "Having been warned twice about turning up late, he finally lost the job altogether."
  • "Having already spoken to the landlord that morning, she knew exactly what to say at the meeting."

In each sentence, which action happened first, and which happened second? And who does both actions belong to — is it ever two different people?

Check the pattern CLICK
"Having (been) + past participle" packages the earlier action into one tight opening clause, so the main clause can get straight to what happened next. This is a perfect participle clause — not a gerund, even though "having" looks like one. Crucially, the subject right after the comma has to be the one who did the first action (active: having missed, having spoken) or had it done to them (passive: having been warned). Both actions belong to the same person or thing in every example above.
Having + past participle, subject + verb ... (the subject DID the earlier action)
Having been + past participle, subject + verb ... (the earlier action was done TO the subject)
⚠ Watch out: whoever or whatever comes right after the comma must be the true subject of "having (been)". If it isn't, you get a dangling participle — the clause attaches to the wrong subject, and the sentence can end up meaning something you never intended. "Having been left in the fridge overnight, the smell woke everyone up" technically says the smell was left in the fridge — not the food.

Two clauses become one

"She had already resigned from her post. She felt free to speak her mind at the meeting."
→
"Having already resigned from her post, she felt free to speak her mind at the meeting."

One possible answer: tenses usually move back one step (a "backshift") — "I am tired" becomes "She said (that) she was tired"; "I have finished" becomes "She said she had finished".
