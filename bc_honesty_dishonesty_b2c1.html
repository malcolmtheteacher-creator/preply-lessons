The Art of the Question

Good survey questions follow patterns. Let's look at how professional surveys ask about sensitive topics like dishonesty.

Question Structure 1: Hypothetical Situation with "Would"

Structure: "What do you think the average person in your country would do if...?"

"What do you think the average person in your country would do if they bumped into another car while parking?"

Question Structure 2: Generalisation with "On the Whole"

Structure: "On the whole, do you think people...?"

"On the whole, do you think people pay as much tax as they're supposed to?"

Question Structure 3: Imagine + Past Simple

Structure: "Imagine you [did something]. Do you think they'd...?"

"Imagine you offered a policeman a bribe to forget about a speeding offence. Do you think they'd let you off?"

Question Structure 4: Acceptability

Structure: "Do you think it's acceptable to...?"

"Do you think it's acceptable to earn money privately and not declare it to the financial authorities?"

Question Structure 5: Likelihood with "How Likely"

Structure: "How likely do you think people would be to...?"

"If you left your wallet in the gym changing room, how likely do you think people would be to return it?"

Question Structure 6: Trust & Generalisation

Structure: "To what extent do you feel you can trust...?"

"To what extent do you feel you can trust second-hand car salesmen?"

Question Structure 7: Frequency & Commonness

Structure: "How commonplace do you think it is...?" or "Do you think people tend to...?"

"Do you think people tend to tell the truth about people's appearance?"

"How commonplace do you think it is for people to blame the traffic for not arriving at work on time?"

Grammar Patterns in Survey Questions

Hypothetical "Would"

We use "would" to ask about imaginary or likely situations. It softens the question and makes it less direct.

"What do you think the average person... would do if...?"

"How likely would someone be to...?"

Indirect Questions

We ask "Do you think...?" before the main question. This is more polite and gives space for opinion.

"Do you think it's acceptable to...?"

"Do you think people tend to...?"

Adverbials for Generalisation

These phrases help you talk about general patterns without being too absolute.

"On the whole..." (overall, generally)

"To what extent..." (how much)

"How commonplace..." (how frequent)

"People tend to..." (general tendency)

Pattern Hunt

Breaking Down a Question

Let's deconstruct one question:

"What do you think the average person in your country would do if they bumped into another car while parking?"

  • Indirect question: "What do you think...?" (softens the question)
  • Hypothetical "would": makes it less personal, more about general patterns
  • Generalization: "the average person in your country" (not you personally)
  • Specific scenario: "bumped into another car while parking" (clear, concrete situation)

Design Your Survey

Now you'll create your own complete survey about dishonesty. Choose 3-4 categories and write 5-6 questions.

Tips for Good Survey Questions

  • Use neutral wording — avoid leading questions that suggest an answer
  • Be specific — "stealing" is vague; "taking office supplies home from work" is clear
  • Use the structures we practised: hypothetical "would," indirect questions, generalisations
  • Ask one thing per question — don't ask "Do you think people lie AND cheat?"

Conducting Your Survey

Ask your survey questions. Listen carefully to the responses and take brief notes.

Presentation Frame

"Based on my survey, I found that..."

Example: "Based on my survey, I found that people would probably be honest about returning a lost wallet, but many would underreport income if they had a chance. When I asked about bribes, the response was that it's unacceptable but probably happens in business."
