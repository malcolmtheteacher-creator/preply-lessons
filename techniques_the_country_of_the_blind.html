The Country of the Blind

H. G. Wells's fable of a sighted man who walks into a valley where sight means nothing — and learns that the majority makes the rules.

Need an easier version? Read this story at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll read a classic H. G. Wells story about a man who can see arriving in a valley of people who cannot — and you'll master the language of ability and possibility: what people can do, what they could do, what they couldn't, and what they managed to do against the odds.
Warm up — before the story. Two quick questions to think about and answer out loud. There are no right answers — just talk.
Talk it through:
  • Is there an old saying you know that turns out to be less true than it sounds? Say it, then explain when it fails.
  • Imagine arriving somewhere where everyone does one thing differently from you — a custom, a habit, a way of speaking. Do you change to fit in, or do you stay yourself?

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. High in the mountains lay a hidden valley, a green floor of fields and streams shut in on every side by walls of grey rock.
a valley (n) — a low area of land between hills or mountains, often with a river running through it.
2. For fifteen generations the community had been completely isolated, cut off from the outside world by a landslide that sealed the only pass.
isolated (adj) — far away from other people or places; separated and alone.
3. He had lost his footing on the ice and gone tumbling down the slope, over and over, until the snow finally stopped his fall.
to tumble (v) — to fall suddenly and roll or turn over as you go down.
4. The old proverb, "In the country of the blind the one-eyed man is king," is a well-known saying about power belonging to whoever has the advantage.
a saying (n) — a short, well-known phrase that expresses a common truth or piece of advice; a proverb.
5. The villagers listened to his story with polite patience, but they clearly thought his talk of "seeing" was pure nonsense.
nonsense (n) — words or ideas that have no meaning or make no sense; foolish talk.
6. They were sure the poor man was deluded — that his mind had invented a whole world of "light" and "colour" that simply did not exist.
deluded (adj) — firmly believing something that is not true; mistaken in a way you cannot see for yourself.
7. Far from ruling them, he was treated as an outsider — someone who did not belong, whose strange ideas set him apart from everyone else.
an outsider (n) — a person who does not belong to a group and is not accepted as part of it.
8. Over fifteen generations their other senses had grown remarkably keen — their hearing so sharp that they knew a stranger by the sound of his breath.
keen (adj) — very sharp, sensitive or highly developed (of a sense, mind or ability).
9. Their world was perfectly adapted to darkness: smooth paths, walls they knew by touch, and work that needed no light at all.
adapted (adj / v) — changed or suited to fit particular conditions or surroundings.
10. He grew to resent being told what to do by people he had come to save; the bitterness sat heavily in his chest.
to resent (v) — to feel bitter or angry about something you think is unfair.

Now use them — out loud

Talk it through before we read:
  • Using valley, isolated, adapted and keen, predict: what kind of place do you think this story describes, and how might its people live?
  • Which of these words carry a sense of belonging or power, and which carry a sense of being pushed out? Sort them out loud and say why.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The fall into the valley

High in the wildest peaks of the Andes, cut off from the rest of the world, lay a valley that people had almost forgotten. Long ago, settlers had found their way in and built a happy little community there. But then a great landslide sealed the only pass, and a strange sickness came, and one by one the people lost their sight. Children were born blind, then their children, until after fifteen generations no one in the valley could see at all — and no one even remembered what "seeing" had ever meant. They had, quite simply, adapted. Their houses had no windows; their paths were smooth and known by touch; their hearing and sense of smell had grown so keen that they moved through their world with perfect ease.

Into this hidden place, by pure accident, fell a mountaineer named Nunez. He was climbing a great peak nearby when he slipped on the ice and went tumbling down a vast snow-slope, half-buried, bruised, but alive. When at last he found his feet, he made his way down and discovered — to his astonishment — the isolated valley and its people. And as he looked at their strange, unseeing faces and their eyes that never met his, one old saying rose in his mind, bright and confident: "In the country of the blind, the one-eyed man is king." Here he was, a man who could see, among people who could not. Surely, he thought, he would soon be their master.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was everyone in the valley blind, and how had the community changed because of it?

2. How did Nunez arrive there, and what did he expect his position among the people to be?

1. A landslide sealed the valley and a sickness took the people's sight; over fifteen generations everyone was born blind, so they adapted — no windows, smooth paths, and very keen hearing and smell.

2. He was a mountaineer who slipped, tumbled down a snow-slope, and found the valley by accident. Remembering the saying about the one-eyed man being king, he expected to rule them because he could see.

Discuss:
  • Nunez assumes that being able to see will automatically make him powerful. Why might an advantage in one place be worth nothing in another?

Part 2 — "Seeing" makes no sense here

It did not go as Nunez had planned. When he spoke of the mountains he had seen, of the sky, of light and colour and distant things, the villagers only frowned. To them these were meaningless words — the babble of a man newly made, whose mind was not yet formed. They had a whole explanation for him: he was a wild creature who had come over the rocks, and his talk of "seeing" was simply nonsense, the sign of a poor, deluded brain. In their world, built entirely without sight, the very idea was madness. The more Nunez insisted, the more certain they became that something was wrong with him.

His "gift" turned out to be no help at all. In the pitch dark of their windowless houses he was clumsy and lost, while they moved with total confidence. They knew every path by heart; they could hear him coming from far off; they worked by touch faster than he could by sight. When he boasted of his eyes, they set him simple tasks and watched him fail. Bit by bit, Nunez was forced to see the truth: far from being a king, he was the slowest, the most helpless, the most ignorant person in the valley — an outsider whose one advantage counted for nothing. And slowly he began to resent it, and then to accept it, and to learn their ways as a child learns, hoping only to be allowed to stay.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the villagers explain Nunez and his talk of "seeing"?

2. Why did his sight fail to give him any power over the blind villagers?

1. They decided he was a newly made, wild creature with an unformed, deluded mind; his talk of light and seeing was nonsense to them, and they thought he was ill or mad.

2. Their world had no light and was perfectly built for the blind; they moved with confidence in the dark while he was clumsy and lost, so his sight made him the most helpless person there, not the strongest.

Discuss:
  • The villagers are not foolish — they are simply certain of what everyone around them believes. How does a whole community come to be sure of something that is not true?

Part 3 — The price of sight

In time, Nunez fell in love with a young woman of the valley named Medina-saroté, and she with him. But her family and the elders were troubled. To them he was a strange, defective man, full of odd notions about "seeing," and they could not give her to someone so plainly disordered. At last the wise men of the community found what they believed was the answer. They had studied him and decided that the cause of all his trouble — his restlessness, his strange talk, his failure to settle — lay in the two odd, moving things in his face. If those were simply removed, they said, he would be cured, calmed, made whole, and then he could marry and live among them as a normal man.

They asked him, gently and reasonably, to give up his eyes. And for a while, out of love, Nunez almost agreed. But on the morning fixed for it, he walked out alone and looked — really looked — at the world: the sunrise gold on the rock, the deep blue of the sky, the great white peaks far above the valley walls. He understood then that he could not trade all of that away, not even for her. So instead he began to climb. He climbed up and out of the valley, toward the light and the mountains and the wide world beyond, leaving behind the only place that had ever offered to keep him — if only he would agree to stop seeing.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the elders decide was the "cause" of Nunez's trouble, and what did they propose to do about it?

2. What choice did Nunez make in the end, and why?

1. They decided his eyes were the source of his strangeness and offered to remove them, believing this would cure and calm him so he could marry and live normally among them.

2. He almost agreed out of love, but looking at the sunrise, sky and mountains he could not give up sight. He chose to climb out of the valley toward the wider world instead.

Discuss:
  • The valley offers Nunez love and a home — but only if he gives up how he sees the world. Was he right to refuse? What would you have done?

Grammar — can / could / be able to / managed to

The whole story turns on one question: who can do what? Nunez can see; the villagers cannot. They can move in the dark; he can't. English has a small family of forms for ability and possibility — get them right and you can talk about what people are, and aren't, able to do.

The core forms:
• can / can't — ability or possibility now, in the present: "The villagers can hear a stranger's breathing." "Nunez can't find his way in the dark."
• could / couldn't — general ability or possibility in the past: "For generations no one could see." "He couldn't convince them that light was real."
• be able to — the same meaning, but usable in any tense and after other verbs: "He hopes to be able to stay." "They have never been able to imagine colour."
The key contrast — could vs managed to:
• Use could for a general ability over time: "As a climber, he could read the mountains well."
• For one specific success — a single difficult thing that actually happened — use was/were able to or managed to, NOT could: "After a long climb, he managed to reach the valley." / "he was able to reach the valley." (Not: "he could reach the valley.")
• But for a single thing that failed, couldn't is fine: "He couldn't persuade a single villager."

Going deeper

You've read the story and you have the words — now let's think about what it means. Wells wrote this in 1904, but its questions have not aged a day.

Why this story endures

Everyone expects the proverb to come true: "In the country of the blind, the one-eyed man is king." Wells takes that comfortable idea and turns it inside out. In his valley, sight is not power — it is difference, and difference is treated as a defect. The story asks a question we still argue about today: what counts as "normal" is often just whatever the majority happens to be. The person who is different isn't wrong; they are simply outnumbered.

And notice how the villagers are drawn. They are not helpless or pitiable. They are skilful, capable and completely at home in a world built for them — it is Nunez who is clumsy and lost. Wells is careful: the "advantage" belongs to whoever fits the world they live in.

Questions to open up

Talk these through — take your time on each:
  • The proverb promises that one advantage makes you a ruler. Why does Wells make it come out the opposite way? What is he really saying about power and the majority?
  • The villagers decide that Nunez is "deluded" and even offer to "cure" him by removing his eyes. When have you seen a group treat a difference as something that needs to be fixed rather than understood?
  • Think about the ending. Nunez gives up love and safety rather than give up sight. Is that courage, or stubbornness? Could it be both?
  • Turn it around: is there a way of seeing the world that you have, that the people around you don't share? What does it cost to hold on to it?

Speak

This is the heart of the lesson — do all of it out loud. The timer is there to keep you going: fill the time with detail rather than escaping it.

Useful language: describe ability with today's forms — can / can't for now ("the villagers can hear everything"), could / couldn't for the past ("Nunez couldn't convince them"), and managed to / was able to for a single hard-won success ("he managed to climb out"). For opinions, reach for: "What's striking is…", "You could argue that…", "It's no accident that…", "On one level… but on another…".

To finish — the bigger questions
