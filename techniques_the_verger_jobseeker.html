The Verger

W. Somerset Maugham (1929) — a story for anyone who has been told they are no longer suitable, and is wondering what to do next

Before we read

Your St Peter's, Neville Square

Before we open the story, take 3–4 minutes and tell me about the institution or role you have most recently belonged to.

  • What did you do there?
  • What did you wear, or carry, or hold? (A book? A title? A badge?)
  • What were you respected for?
  • How did your day open and close?

Why we're doing this: the story opens with a man whose identity is fused with his post. To read him with full sympathy, we should be able to see our own version of that fusion first.

Read in three parts

Maugham gives us the whole story in about three thousand words. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The ending arrives in a single sentence — do not skip ahead.

Part 1 — The new vicar

There had been a christening that afternoon at St Peter's, Neville Square, and Albert Edward Foreman still wore his verger's gown. For sixteen years he had been verger of this church, and he discharged his duties with quiet dignity. He was a tall, spare man with dignified features, and his face was clean-shaven. In his long black gown he might have been mistaken for a bishop, or at the very least an archdeacon. He took a pride in his work.

But that afternoon the new vicar had asked him to remain behind. The vicar was a red-faced energetic man in the early forties, and Albert Edward still resented the change. He had served three previous vicars and had buried each one with the dignity due to his cloth. This new one was different.

The vicar entered the vestry with two churchwardens and closed the door.

"Foreman, we have something rather painful to say to you. You have been here a great many years and I think his lordship and the wardens will agree with me that you have fulfilled the duties of your office to the satisfaction of everybody concerned."

Albert Edward's heart began to beat a little quicker. There was clearly something wrong.

"But a most extraordinary thing came to my knowledge the other day. I discovered to my astonishment that you could neither read nor write."

The verger's face betrayed no sign of embarrassment.

"The last vicar knew that, sir," he replied. "He said it didn't make no difference. He always said there was a great deal too much education in the world for 'is taste."

The vicar and the wardens exchanged a glance.

"It is the most amazing thing I ever heard. Do you mean to say that you have been verger of this church for sixteen years and never learned to read or write?"

"I went into service when I was twelve, sir. The cook tried to teach me once, but I didn't seem to 'ave the knack for it. And then what with one thing and another I never seemed to 'ave the time."

The vicar drew himself up.

"I cannot allow it. I must ask you to make up your mind to learn. I will give you three months. If at the end of that time you cannot read and write I am sorry, but I shall have to let you go."

Albert Edward had never liked the new vicar. He had said from the beginning that they had made a mistake when they appointed him. He was not the kind of man one wanted at St Peter's, Neville Square.

"I'm very sorry, sir, I'm afraid it's no good. I'm too old a dog to learn new tricks. I've lived a good many years without knowin' 'ow to read and write, and without wishin' to praise myself, self-praise is no recommendation, I don't think I've done my duty in this position any the worse for it. If I could learn now I don't 'ardly think I would."

"In that case, Foreman, I'm afraid you must go."

"Yes, sir, I quite understand. I shall be 'appy to 'and in my resignation as soon as you 'ave found somebody to take my place."

Pause and discuss:
  • What does the vicar see when he looks at Albert? What does he miss?
  • Albert refuses to learn, without arguing. Is this obstinacy, dignity, or something else?
  • The vicar discovered something that had been true for sixteen years. Why now? What changed — Albert, or the institution?
  • Notice how Albert drops his "h"s ('ave, 'appy, 'ardly). How is Maugham using language to mark power?

Part 2 — The walk home, and the idea

When Albert Edward had locked the church door behind him he walked slowly down the steps with a heavy heart. He did not know what he was going to do. It was true that he had saved a small sum of money, but to live on it he would have to be very careful. He sighed deeply.

Albert Edward was not a smoker. He did not drink beer. But he liked a cigarette with his evening cup of tea, and now he felt the need of one badly. It occurred to him that he had not bought any that morning, and as he had no tobacco in the house he thought he would go out and get some. As he passed shop after shop in search of a tobacconist, he noticed with surprise that there was not one on the whole length of this long street.

"That's strange," said Albert Edward, to put an end to the matter, "I can't be the only man as walks along this street and wants a fag. I shouldn't wonder but what a fellow might do very well with a little shop here. Tobacco and sweets, you know."

He gave a sudden start. The thought struck him like a thunderbolt. He turned, walked home, and had his tea.

"You're very silent this afternoon, Albert," his wife remarked.

"I'm thinkin'," he said.

He considered the matter from every point of view, and next day he went down the street and by good luck found a little shop to let that looked as if it would exactly suit him. Twenty-four hours later he had taken it. A month after that he resigned his post as verger. He set up in business as a tobacconist and news-agent.

The undertaking proved a success. In a year or so he thought he might venture on another shop, and he looked about for a long street that had no tobacconist in it. He started a second shop, then a third. In ten years he had acquired no fewer than ten shops, and he was making money hand over fist. He went round to all of them himself every Monday morning, collected the week's takings, and took them to the bank.

Pause and discuss:
  • Albert sees a gap that nobody else has noticed — a long street with no tobacconist. What kind of seeing is that?
  • The moment of insight comes after the firing — when he is walking, alone, with no plan. Why might insight arrive in that state rather than at his desk?
  • Maugham compresses ten years of business-building into one paragraph. He spends a thousand words on the firing and a hundred on the success. What is he telling us?
  • "I'm thinkin'," he says to his wife. Notice how short the line is — and how much it contains. What is he not saying?

Part 3 — The bank manager

One morning when he was at the bank, the cashier told him that the manager would like to see him. Albert Edward was shown into the office. The manager shook hands with him.

"Mr Foreman, I wanted to have a talk to you about the money you've got on deposit with us. D'you know exactly how much it is? Just over thirty thousand pounds. It's a large sum to have on deposit, and I should have thought you'd do better to invest it."

"I wouldn't want to take no risk, sir. I know it's safe in the bank."

"You needn't have the least anxiety. We'll make you out a list of absolutely gilt-edged securities. They'll bring you in a better rate of interest than we can possibly afford to give you."

A troubled look settled on Mr Foreman's distinguished face.

"I've never 'ad anythin' to do with stocks and shares and I'd 'ave to leave it all in your 'ands," he said.

The manager smiled. "We'll do everything. All you'll have to do next time you come in is just to sign the transfers."

"I could do that all right," said Albert uncertainly. "But 'ow should I know what I was signin'?"

"I suppose you can read," said the manager a trifle sharply.

Mr Foreman gave him a disarming smile.

"Well, sir, that's just it. I can't. I know it sounds funny-like, but there it is. I can't read or write, only me name, an' I only learnt to do that when I went into business."

The manager was so surprised that he jumped up from his chair.

"That's the most extraordinary thing I ever heard."

"You see, it's like this, sir. I never 'ad the opportunity until it was too late, and then somehow I wouldn't. I got obstinate-like."

The manager stared at him as though he were a prehistoric monster.

"And do you mean to say that you've built up this important business and amassed a fortune of thirty thousand pounds without being able to read or write? Good God, man, what would you be now if you had been able to?"

"I can tell you that, sir," said Mr Foreman, a little smile on his still aristocratic features. "I'd be verger of St Peter's, Neville Square."

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • Read the last line again. What did it just do to the whole story?
  • The bank manager assumes one specific qualification is the foundation of success. What was it actually, in Albert's case?
  • Maugham doesn't give us the manager's face after the answer. We don't see his reaction. Why not?
  • What would Albert have said if the manager had asked him a year earlier, when he was still standing on the church steps with a heavy heart? Would the answer have been the same?

Register contrast — how Maugham gives each character a voice

The whole story is built on three voices colliding. Notice how Maugham uses spelling, contractions and word choice to mark each one. This is exactly the skill you need on the job market: knowing which voice to use in which room.

The VicarHigh-Anglican formal"I think his lordship and the wardens will agree…"

Your equivalents: academic-formal · application-letter-formal · interview-professional · networking-warm · home-language. Five registers, one person. Maugham's lesson is that the people with power often do not realise which register the room actually needs.

The grammar this story teaches itself

Three structures Maugham puts on a plate: the third conditional (the ending), reported speech (the firing and the bank scenes), and past perfect (the sixteen years before the story begins). All three are interview gold.

1. Third conditional — the form behind the punchline

The ending hangs on a third conditional. The manager says: "What would you be now if you had been able to read?"

Pattern: If + past perfect, would have + past participle. (Counterfactual past — talking about something that did not happen.)

1. If Albert (learn) to read at twelve, he (stay) a verger.

2. If the new vicar (not be) so concerned about literacy, Albert (never open) a shop.

3. If Albert (find) a tobacconist on that street, he (not have) the idea.

4. The manager thinks: "If only you (know) how to read!"

5. If I (be) the vicar, I (keep) Albert on.

1. had learned / would have stayed

2. had not been / would never have opened

3. had found / would not have had

4. had known (wish / regret — "if only" + past perfect)

5. had been / would have kept

Apply it — interview counterfactuals

You will be asked some version of this question in interviews. Practise three answers aloud, each using a clean third conditional:

  • "If you had stayed in your previous role, where would you be today?"
  • "If your field had not changed so much, what would your research have looked like?"
  • "If you had moved to Switzerland five years earlier, what would have been different?"

The point is not to lament — it is to demonstrate that you can think clearly about paths not taken without being trapped by them.

2. Reported speech — the firing scene without quotation marks

Convert Albert's direct speech to reported speech. Watch the tense shift back one step, and the pronoun and time-reference changes.

Direct: The vicar said, "I will give you three months."
Reported: The vicar said he Albert three months.

Direct: Albert replied, "I cannot learn now."
Reported: Albert replied that he learn then.

Direct: The manager said, "I have never heard anything so extraordinary."
Reported: The manager said he anything so extraordinary.

Direct: Albert said, "I went into service when I was twelve."
Reported: Albert said he into service when he twelve.

Direct: The manager asked, "What would you be now if you had been able to read?"
Reported: The manager asked what Albert be then if he able to read.

1. would give (will → would)

2. could not (cannot → could not)

3. had never heard (present perfect → past perfect)

4. had gone … was

5. would … had been (third conditional stays unchanged in reported speech — already maximally back-shifted)

Apply it — cover letters and meeting summaries

Reported speech is the working grammar of professional writing. A cover letter often says: "The advertisement stated that… The role description indicated that… My previous Dean wrote that…" Try one:

Direct: The job advert says: "The successful applicant will have a doctorate in a relevant humanities discipline and will have published in peer-reviewed journals."
Reported: The advert states that the successful applicant a doctorate in a relevant humanities discipline and in peer-reviewed journals.

Answer: will have / will have published (when reporting general / present-time job descriptions, modal tense often stays — the rule "shift one back" applies to past-tense reporting verbs. Here "states" is present, so no shift needed.)

3. Past perfect — describing the backstory

The story opens with "For sixteen years he had been verger of this church." That past perfect signals: this is the situation that existed before our story begins. Useful when you're writing a personal statement or telling your own story to a new audience.

1. By the time the new vicar arrived, Albert (serve) three previous vicars.

2. Before he opened his first shop, he (save) a small sum of money.

3. When the bank manager called him in, Albert (build) his business up to ten shops.

4. The cook (try) to teach him to read once, but he never picked it up.

1. had served

2. had saved

3. had built

4. had tried

What Maugham does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are the reason this story still works in 2026.

1. Dramatic irony — we know what the vicar discovers

From paragraph one, Maugham lets us see Albert's dignity, his sixteen years of service, the bishop-like presence. Then the vicar discovers he's illiterate. The discovery destabilises the vicar's assumptions — but not ours. We were already in on it.

2. Compression — ten years in one paragraph

Maugham gives us the firing in a thousand words and the next ten years of business growth in about one hundred. He compresses and distils time around the events that matter — the door opening, not the long walk through it.

3. Register contrast — three voices in one room

The vicar speaks in high-Anglican formal English. The bank manager speaks in measured professional English. Albert drops his 'h's and speaks in working-class London. Maugham juxtaposes these registers to do the characterisation for him — we don't need to be told who has the power; we hear it.

4. The closing line — the reframe

The last sentence — "I'd be verger of St Peter's, Neville Square" — reframes the entire story. Up to that point we have read a rags-to-riches tale. After it, we read a story about how a missing credential can be a door, how setbacks contain shapes, how dignity travels with the person rather than the post. The whole text rotates around one sentence.

Three speaking tasks

Task A · Your last role, then leaving it (4 minutes)

Two parts, two minutes each.

Part 1 (2 min): Describe the institution and role you most recently belonged to: what you wore, what you carried, how your day started and ended, what you were respected for. Use past perfect ("For X years I had been…") and concrete detail. Describe one ordinary morning rather than summarising.

Part 2 (2 min): Describe what you did and felt in the period straight after you left — what you noticed once the pressure was off. Be honest — don't force optimism you don't feel. If someone said something to you at that time, report it using reported speech.

Task B · The interview counterfactual (3 minutes)

An interviewer asks you: "If you had stayed in your previous role, where would you be today?"

Answer for three minutes in a way that shows you can think clearly about a path you didn't take without being trapped by it. Use at least two clean third conditionals. End with one sentence that turns the question into something positive about the direction you did take.

Aim to sound clear and settled about your choices, not regretful.

Task C · The gap you see (4 minutes)

Albert noticed a gap no one else had spotted — a busy street with no tobacconist. In your field — academic history, public history, museums, policy, publishing, teaching — or in Switzerland generally, what is a gap that nobody is filling?

Argue for two minutes that the gap is real. Then for two more, describe what a role, service, or resource that filled it might look like: who would use it, and where it would sit.

Use cleft sentences ("What no one is doing is…" / "The role that doesn't yet exist is…").

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. Albert refuses to learn at sixty. Has there been a moment in your career when an institution wanted you to acquire something you refused to? What did your refusal protect, and what did it cost?
  2. The bank manager assumes literacy is the foundation of business success. In your field, what is the equivalent assumption — and is it actually true?
  3. Maugham gives us Albert's ten years of expansion in one paragraph. If you compress your own next ten years into one paragraph, what does the paragraph need to say?
  4. What would Albert have answered if the bank manager had asked the same question a year earlier, when he was still standing on the church steps?
