At the Pharmacy

You feel rotten, you're not sure what it is, and there's a queue behind you. Time to describe it well, ask the right things, and leave knowing exactly what to take.

The Scene 🎬

You've been battling a cough for the best part of a week, and it's winning. Your head is thick, you're not sleeping, and this morning you finally admitted defeat and walked to the chemist on the corner.

Here's the thing: nobody's going to fix this for you. Whether you walk out with the right little bottle depends entirely on how well you can describe what's wrong — and how carefully you listen to the instructions. Vague symptoms get vague help. And there are two questions people almost always forget to ask: are there side effects? and is it safe with what I'm already taking?

Behind the counter: a pharmacist who could do this in their sleep — patient, precise, and genuinely on your side, as long as you give them something to work with.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last feel properly under the weather? Did you go to a pharmacy, a doctor, the internet — or just crawl into bed and hope?
  2. In your language, how do you describe a cough, an ache or a "thick head" to someone? Are some symptoms genuinely hard to put into words?

Who's who

You
The Customer 🤧

Run-down, a bit foggy, and secretly hoping there's a magic bottle. You need to describe the problem clearly, get a remedy, and understand exactly how to take it.

The other voice
The Pharmacist 💊

Calm, careful, has seen every cold going. Will help the moment you give them the details — and will always double-check what's safe before handing anything over.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Listen for how the symptoms get described, and for every little check the customer makes before pocketing the bottle.

PharmacistMorning. What can I do for you?
YouMorning. I wonder if you could help me with something — it's nothing serious, but I think I've come down with something. I've had this cough for the best part of a week now, and it's just not shifting.
PharmacistA week's a fair while. Is it a dry, tickly cough, or more of a chesty one — bringing anything up?
YouChesty, mostly, and it's much worse at night. No real fever to speak of, but my head's been thick as soup and I'm barely sleeping.
PharmacistRight. If it's been going a week and there's no fever, it sounds like it's on its way out rather than coming on — the cough's often the last thing to go.
YouThat's reassuring. Is there anything you'd recommend for it? I'd rather not take anything too strong, if I can help it — I've still got to get through work.
PharmacistThen this is the one you want. It loosens a chesty cough without knocking you sideways — two spoonfuls, three times a day.
YouTwo spoonfuls, three times a day. Just so I've got this right — is that before or after meals?
PharmacistAfter food, ideally — it can be a touch harsh on an empty stomach. And no more than four doses in any twenty-four hours, mind.
YouUnderstood. And are there any side effects I should watch out for? I'll be driving later.
PharmacistThis one's non-drowsy, so you're fine to drive. It's the night-time version that would send you to sleep — save that for bedtime.
YouGood to know. And would it be safe to take alongside the paracetamol I've been having for the headache?
PharmacistPerfectly — just check the total. This syrup has no paracetamol in it, so as long as you don't go over the daily limit on the tablets, you've nothing to worry about.
YouAnd if it still hasn't cleared up in a week or so — should I be concerned?
PharmacistIf it drags on past three weeks, or you start bringing up anything nasty or running a temperature, see your GP. Otherwise: rest, plenty of fluids, and a bit of patience.
YouThat's put my mind at rest. Thanks ever so much for your help.
PharmacistNot at all. Get some sleep tonight if that syrup will let you — and be kind to yourself for a few days.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The pharmacist decides the illness is "on its way out rather than coming on." Which two details led them to that conclusion — and why does it matter for what they recommend?

2. Before pocketing the bottle, the customer runs three separate safety checks. What are they checking each time — and why would forgetting any one of them be a real mistake?

3. "It's the night-time version that would send you to sleep." Why does that detail matter so much today, and what would have gone wrong if the customer hadn't mentioned their plans?

1. It's already lasted a week, and there's no fever. Together those suggest the infection has peaked and is fading, with the cough simply lingering — so a gentle remedy to ease the cough is enough; nothing stronger is needed.
2. First: how and when to take it — before or after meals (after food, or it's harsh on an empty stomach). Second: side effects — will it make them drowsy (no, it's the non-drowsy one, so safe to drive). Third: whether it's safe alongside the paracetamol they're already taking (fine, as long as the daily total stays within the limit). Skip any one and you risk taking it wrongly, dangerously, or on top of a clashing medicine.
3. The customer is driving later, and a drowsy remedy could make that unsafe. Because they mentioned their plans, the pharmacist steered them to the non-drowsy version and warned them off the night-time one — advice they'd never have received if they'd stayed silent.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1348, when the Black Death swept through Europe, people went to the only "pharmacists" they had — and were handed vinegar to sniff, herbs to burn, and posies of flowers to hold against their noses. The advice was confident, detailed, and completely useless. Nobody understood what was making them ill.

You've just done something that would have astonished them: described a symptom to an expert who actually knew what it was, and walked away with something that genuinely works — and a clear warning about what doesn't mix with it.

Seven centuries of trial, error and quiet science stand behind that little bottle. All you had to do was ask the right questions.

Two bigger questions

  1. For most of history, the "cure" for an illness was whatever a confident person told you it was. What makes us trust a remedy today — the science, the person recommending it, or simply that it's on a shelf in a shop?
  2. A pharmacist's most important job is sometimes to say "don't take that" or "go and see a doctor." Why is knowing the limits of a remedy just as valuable as knowing the remedy itself — and where in your own life is "I'm not sure, so I won't" the wiser answer?
