The Brazilian Cat

A broke young man visits his wealthy cousin's private zoo. One locked door turns hospitality into a hunt.

Adapted for B2/C1 from Arthur Conan Doyle's 1898 story

The story in three movements

After each movement, answer from memory before revealing the response. The turn remains hidden until you commit to a prediction.

1 · The generous cousin

Marshall King possessed an old family name and almost nothing else. His creditors were losing patience when an invitation arrived from his wealthy cousin Everard, who had recently returned from Brazil.

At Greylands Court, Everard received him warmly. He spoke of family loyalty, offered excellent food and showed no hurry to discuss money. His greatest pleasure was a private collection of wild animals.

In the animal house, Marshall saw an enormous black cat behind iron bars. It moved without sound, its yellow eyes following the two men.

“A Brazilian cat,” Everard said. “Stronger than a leopard and far more intelligent.”

That evening Everard asked unexpected questions. Had Marshall made a will? Who would inherit if Lord Southerton, their elderly relative, died? Marshall answered carelessly, but later the conversation troubled him. If Southerton died before Marshall, Marshall would inherit the family estate. If Marshall then died unmarried, Everard would receive it.

The order of those deaths had always seemed like dry family law. In Greylands Court it became a chain with money at one end and Marshall's life at the other. He told himself that poverty was making him suspicious of generosity.

The following night his cousin proposed another visit to the cat.

“You have not seen her properly awake.”

They entered the animal house. Everard opened the outer door of the enclosure and gestured towards the narrow viewing space.

Marshall stepped inside. The door closed behind him, and the key turned.

Answer from memory

Which questions turn generosity into possible calculation?

Questions about wills and inheritance suggest Everard is measuring financial consequences rather than simply helping a relative.

2 · Inside the cage

Everard remained safely beyond the bars.

“It will look like an accident,” he said. “A foolish guest entered the cage. By the time anyone comes, there will be little left to explain.”

The black cat rose and stretched. Marshall understood that there would be no appeal. His cousin had measured the inheritance and chosen the simplest route to it.

As Everard left, Marshall climbed the central framework of the cage. He pulled himself onto a narrow beam just before the animal sprang. Its claws struck the wood below his boots.

For hours the cat circled. Whenever his grip weakened, it rose against the beam and reached upwards. Marshall removed his coat and dropped it at the opposite end of the cage. The animal attacked the cloth, giving him seconds to change position.

He tried calling for help, but the animal house stood apart from the servants' rooms. His voice excited the cat, and its answering roar shook the iron bars. Everard had not merely locked a door; he had chosen a place where sound itself worked against the victim.

His arms burned. The air was thick with the animal's breath. Once he slipped and felt a claw tear his trouser leg.

Near dawn he noticed a cord connected to the feeding door. It hung several feet away. He tore strips from his shirt, tied them together and weighted one end with a metal buckle. Again and again he threw the line.

At last it caught. He pulled. A small inner gate lifted, and the smell of meat from the passage distracted the cat long enough for him to reach the service door.

Answer from memory

What immediate options does Marshall have, and what makes each dangerous?

He can remain above the cat until exhausted, attempt the locked door, distract the animal or call for help that may not come.

Stop and predict. Give the most likely ending and the most surprising ending that still fits the evidence.

3 · The animal chooses

Marshall escaped into the passage and locked the gate behind him. He could barely stand. The sensible choice was to leave the estate at once, but footsteps were already approaching.

Everard entered, smiling. He expected to find a body.

When he saw Marshall outside the cage, the smile disappeared. The cousins faced one another across the feeding passage.

“You had better not come closer,” Marshall said.

Everard rushed at him. In the struggle, his hand struck the control lever. The inner gate rose.

The Brazilian cat came through like a black wave.

Marshall threw himself behind the outer door and pulled it shut. Everard had only time to turn. The animal recognised the man who had controlled, starved and displayed it. His death was swift.

Later, the household accepted the obvious story: the owner of a dangerous animal had entered its enclosure and paid for his carelessness. Marshall told no one what had happened during the night.

The official explanation contained almost every fact and none of the truth. Everard had entered the passage; the animal had killed him; the surviving witness had locked the gate. Only intention—the thing that made sense of the facts—remained invisible.

News arrived soon afterwards that Lord Southerton had died. The inheritance Everard had tried to secure passed to Marshall.

The plan had succeeded in one respect. A death in the animal house did look like an accident. Everard had simply chosen the wrong victim.

Answer from memory

Is Everard's death justice, coincidence or another form of cruelty?

The reversal feels like poetic justice, but the animal acts from conditioning and hunger, not moral judgement.

Grammar — had better / would rather / would prefer

had better + base verb

You had better climb now.

Strong advice with an implied negative consequence.

would rather + base verb

I would rather risk the door.

Direct preference; negative: would rather not.

would prefer + to-infinitive

I would prefer to wait.

More neutral or polite preference.

Mixed practice

Complete all six before revealing. Explain the choice, not only the missing words.

You ___ move before the cat notices. (strong advice)

Marshall would rather ___ than remain still. (act)

He would prefer ___ until help arrived. (wait)

You had better not ___ your strength. (waste)

I would rather Everard ___ the truth. (tell; different subject)

Would you prefer ___ the gate or distract the animal? (force)

  1. had better — Had better gives urgent advice and implies danger if it is ignored.
  2. act — Would rather is followed by the base verb when the subject stays the same.
  3. to wait — Would prefer is followed by a to-infinitive.
  4. waste — After had better not, use the base verb without to.
  5. told — With a different subject after would rather, use a past form: Everard told.
  6. to force — Would prefer takes to + verb when choosing an action.

A short analysis

Give your answer first. Then reveal a model and identify one idea you handled differently.

The reversal satisfies because Everard is destroyed by the mechanism he designed. Yet the cat cannot judge guilt; it remains an exploited animal responding to treatment. Doyle therefore delivers poetic justice while preserving discomfort about the violence that produces it.
