The line study: true or false?

In the early 1950s, Solomon Asch brought groups of students into a laboratory for what he called a vision test. Here are six claims about the study. Decide which are true.

1. When just one confederate broke ranks with the group, conformity fell sharply.
True. A single ally made it much easier to disagree, even when the ally's own answer was also wrong.
2. The lines were hard to tell apart, so mistakes were understandable.
False. The right answer was obvious. Alone, people made almost no mistakes.
3. About three-quarters of participants went along with the group's wrong answer at least once.
True. Only around a quarter never did.
4. Across all the critical trials, most answers given were wrong.
False. Roughly one answer in three was wrong. Most answers were still correct.
5. In interviews, everyone who conformed said they truly believed the group was right.
False. Some said they had doubted their own judgement, but others claimed they had known the group was wrong and went along anyway.
6. Each participant believed the other people in the room were also volunteers, but all but one were working for the researcher.
True. They are called confederates.
Talk about it:
  • Why would someone say something they know is wrong in front of strangers?
  • Which of the true claims would you have predicted, and which surprised you?

The evidence, in three steps

Before each part, make a prediction out loud. Then read, and check yourself.

Part 1 — The line study

Before you read: Asch told the students it was a vision test. What do you think was really going on, and who might the other people in the room have been?

In the early 1950s, the psychologist Solomon Asch brought groups of male college students into a laboratory in Pennsylvania for what they were told was a vision test. Each group looked at a card showing a single line and a second card showing three lines of different lengths, and each person had to say which one matched. The answer was obvious. Alone, people got it right over 99% of the time.

But in each group only one person was a genuine participant. The rest were confederates, told to give the same wrong answer on twelve of eighteen trials. The real participant sat second to last, so he heard nearly everyone else answer first. His eyes told him one thing; the unanimous room told him another.

Words from this part — what does each word in bold mean here? Say your idea, then reveal.

“The rest were confederates, told to give the same wrong answer on twelve of eighteen trials.”
confederate (n) — a person who secretly works with a researcher while pretending to be an ordinary participant. Example: "The man who 'fainted' in the street was a confederate, and the study was about who would stop to help."
“His eyes told him one thing; the unanimous room told him another.”
unanimous (adj) — agreed by everyone in a group, with nobody against. Example: "The board's decision was unanimous, so the plan went ahead the same day."

Part 2 — The results

Before you read: How often do you think people went along with the group's wrong answer, and what might make it easier for them to resist?

About three-quarters of participants conformed at least once, and across all the critical trials roughly one answer in three was wrong. Only around a quarter never went along with the group. In interviews afterwards, some admitted that they had doubted their own judgement, while others claimed that they had known the group was wrong but had not wanted to stand out.

Asch also varied the conditions. Conformity rose with the size of the group, up to about three or four confederates, and then levelled off. Most strikingly, if a single confederate broke the consensus, even by giving a different wrong answer, the participant's own errors fell sharply. One ally was enough to make dissent possible.

Words from this part — what does each word in bold mean here? Say your idea, then reveal.

“About three-quarters of participants conformed at least once, and across all the critical trials roughly one answer in three was wrong.”
conform (v) — to behave or think in the same way as the people around you, or as a rule expects. Example: "New employees often conform to the office dress code without anyone telling them to."
“Most strikingly, if a single confederate broke the consensus, even by giving a different wrong answer, the participant's own errors fell sharply.”
consensus (n) — an opinion or decision that most or all members of a group share. Example: "After two hours of discussion, the committee finally reached a consensus."
“One ally was enough to make dissent possible.”
dissent (n) — open disagreement with an opinion that most people in a group hold. Example: "In a healthy team, dissent is welcomed rather than punished."
Your call:
  • Is the study mostly about weakness, or mostly about how people read a situation?

Part 3 — Reading the study carefully

Before you read: Some people gave the group's answer. Do you think they really believed it, or were they just saying it? Why might the study be less simple than it looks?

The study has become shorthand for the danger of the crowd, but it is more complicated than that. Most answers were still correct, and most participants resisted most of the time. It was an artificial task, with strangers, in a particular place and time: critics have pointed out that later studies in other cultures and eras produced different levels of conformity, and some have argued that the effect has varied with the period.

There is also a question about what conformity means. Some participants complied only in public. Others seemed genuinely persuaded. Psychologists distinguish between these two kinds of influence, one that changes what we say and one that changes what we believe. Brain-imaging work from the mid-2000s suggested that going along with a group may sometimes change how the person actually perceives, though that finding remains debated.

Words from this part — what does each word in bold mean here? Say your idea, then reveal.

“The study has become shorthand for the danger of the crowd, but it is more complicated than that.”
shorthand (n) — a short, simple way of referring to something that is really more complicated. Example: "For many people, 'Silicon Valley' has become shorthand for the whole tech industry."
“Some participants complied only in public.”
comply (v) — to do what a person, group or rule requires, even if you privately disagree. Example: "Most drivers comply with the speed limit when they can see a camera."
“Brain-imaging work from the mid-2000s suggested that going along with a group may sometimes change how the person actually perceives, though that finding remains debated.”
perceive (v) — to notice something through the senses, or to understand it in a particular way. Example: "Colours can be perceived differently depending on the light around them."
Your call:
  • Where, in modern life, might we find a unanimous crowd that is not quite what it seems?

Reporting verbs

Reporting verbs let you say what researchers and participants said, found or claimed without quoting them. Each one has its own pattern.

say / find / claim / suggest + that — Asch found that participants conformed in about one third of trials.
admit / deny / recommend + -ing — One participant admitted not wanting to look foolish.
advise / urge / warn + object + to — Critics urge readers to treat the study with care.
Pick the verb for what the speaker meant: claimed suggests doubt, found suggests evidence.

Build it

Make one sentence for each one. Type it, say it, then reveal a model.

1. Asch's results: participants gave the group's wrong answer on about one third of the critical trials. Report this as a finding. → Asch found …
Asch found that participants gave the group's wrong answer on about one third of the critical trials.
2. 'I didn't want to look foolish,' said one participant, a little embarrassed. → One participant admitted …
One participant admitted not wanting to look foolish. (Or: One participant admitted that he hadn't wanted to look foolish.)
3. 'I knew all along that the group was wrong,' said another, but you are not sure you believe him. → Another participant claimed …
Another participant claimed that he had known all along that the group was wrong.
4. 'You should speak up if you disagree,' the manager told her team. → The manager urged …
The manager urged her team to speak up if they disagreed.

Would you speak up?

Five situations where a group agrees and you may not. Choose what you would do, then explain your choice in one sentence with a reporting verb, such as found that, suggested that or claimed that. Then reveal the evidence or one idea.

1. Your team unanimously approves a plan that you think has a serious flaw. Do you object in the meeting? found that
Asch found that a single ally makes dissent far easier: when one confederate broke the consensus, the participant's errors fell sharply. Finding one supporter before the meeting changes the odds.
2. At a dinner, everyone praises a film you disliked. Do you say what you really think?
Idea. The stakes are low. Keeping quiet here may be politeness rather than weakness: a kind of public compliance that costs nothing.
3. A senior colleague states a figure in a meeting that you know is wrong. Do you correct them in front of everyone?
Idea. This is harder than the lines, because status is involved. A question such as 'Could we double-check that figure?' lets you disagree while allowing them to save face.
4. Everyone in a group chat is angry about a news story that you suspect is false. Do you say so?
Idea. When many voices appear to agree, the pressure can feel strong, even if few people have checked the facts. One calm message raising a doubt can act like Asch's ally for others who are unsure.
5. On an interview panel, the other two interviewers both prefer a candidate you think is weaker. Do you argue for your choice? found that
In Asch's variations, conformity rose with the size of the group up to about three or four people and then levelled off. With only two others, the pressure is real but weaker, so it is a good moment to give your reasons.
