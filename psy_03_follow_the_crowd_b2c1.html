The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The line study

In the early 1950s, the psychologist Solomon Asch brought groups of male college students into a laboratory in Pennsylvania for what they were told was a vision test. Each group looked at a card showing a single line and a second card showing three lines of different lengths, and each person had to say which one matched. The answer was obvious. Alone, people got it right over 99% of the time.

But in each group only one person was a genuine participant. The rest were confederates, told to give the same wrong answer on twelve of eighteen trials. The real participant sat second to last, so he heard nearly everyone else answer first. His eyes told him one thing; the unanimous room told him another.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the task?

2. Who were the other people in the room?

1. To say which of three lines matched a standard line; the answer was obvious.

2. Confederates: they had been told to give the same wrong answer on twelve of eighteen trials.

Discuss:
  • Why did Asch make the task so easy? What does that tell you about the point of the study?

Part 2 — The results

About three-quarters of participants conformed at least once, and across all the critical trials roughly one answer in three was wrong. Only around a quarter never went along with the group. In interviews afterwards, some admitted that they had doubted their own judgement, while others claimed that they had known the group was wrong but had not wanted to stand out.

Asch also varied the conditions. Conformity rose with the size of the group, up to about three or four confederates, and then levelled off. Most strikingly, if a single confederate broke the consensus, even by giving a different wrong answer, the participant's own errors fell sharply. One ally was enough to make dissent possible.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many participants conformed at least once?

2. What reduced conformity most?

1. About three-quarters; roughly one answer in three was wrong overall.

2. The presence of just one ally who broke the consensus.

Discuss:
  • Why might a single dissenting voice be so powerful?

Part 3 — Reading the study carefully

The study has become shorthand for the danger of the crowd, but it is more complicated than that. Most answers were still correct, and most participants resisted most of the time. It was an artificial task, with strangers, in a particular place and time: critics have pointed out that later studies in other cultures and eras produced different levels of conformity, and some have argued that the effect has varied with the period.

There is also a question about what conformity means. Some participants complied only in public. Others seemed genuinely persuaded. Psychologists distinguish between these two kinds of influence, one that changes what we say and one that changes what we believe. Brain-imaging work from the mid-2000s suggested that going along with a group may sometimes change how the person actually perceives, though that finding remains debated.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why should we be careful with the findings?

2. What are the two kinds of influence?

1. Most answers were correct, the task was artificial, and later studies found different levels of conformity.

2. Public compliance (changing what we say) and genuine acceptance (changing what we believe).

Discuss:
  • Is public compliance a moral failure, a practical strategy or both?

Grammar — reporting verbs

Different reporting verbs show how someone spoke or what they intended. Choosing the right one lets you describe a source accurately and add your own judgement without saying so.

Four patterns:
• verb + that: admit, claim, insist, suggest, argue, deny: "He admitted that he had doubted himself." "She claimed that she had known."
• verb + -ing: admit, deny, suggest, recommend: "He denied following the group."
• verb + object + to: persuade, urge, warn, encourage: "The group persuaded him to change his answer."
• verb + to: refuse, agree, promise: "Some refused to follow the crowd."
Feel the difference:
• say is neutral. claim suggests you may doubt it. admit suggests the person did not want to say it. insist shows strength of feeling. point out adds a fact.
• The verb you choose carries your attitude to the source.

Conform or resist?

Five situations. Say whether you would go along or speak up, and which principle you are using. Then reveal.

1. Your team unanimously approves a plan that you think has a serious flaw. Do you object?
The ally effect. Asch found that a single supporter makes dissent far easier. Finding one before the meeting changes the odds.
2. At a dinner everyone praises a film you disliked. Do you say so?
Public compliance. Low stakes, and keeping quiet may be sensible politeness, not weakness.
3. In an emergency, a crowd runs in one direction. Do you follow?
Rational conformity. The crowd may know something you don't, so following can be the sensible choice.
4. A senior colleague states something you know is factually wrong. Do you correct them?
Authority and consensus. Harder than the lines. Asking a question can be a way to disagree while saving face.
5. Everyone online is angry about a story that you suspect is false. Do you say it?
Unanimity online. Public pressure can be stronger when many voices appear to agree, even if they have not thought independently.
