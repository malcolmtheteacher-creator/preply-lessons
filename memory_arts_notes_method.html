How to Build a
Memory Palace

The method, step by step, gathered from Roman teachers and Elizabethan guides. Five steps, a few warnings, and a way to scale it up into something much bigger.

1

The big idea

We remember places far better than ideas. So don't try to hold an idea in your head. Put it somewhere. Give it a picture, and set the picture in a room you know.

Where it began

A Greek poet, Simonides, was called out of a banquet just before the roof fell in. The guests were crushed beyond recognition. Simonides could name every one of them, because he remembered where each had been sitting.

Roman teachers took the lesson and built a method from it. Two thousand years later the English were still teaching it.

order + place = recall.
that's the whole trick.

sources: Roman handbook Rhetorica ad Herennium (c. 85 BC), Cicero's De Oratore, and the English guides in Part I of the anthology.

2

Step 1 · Choose your building

Use a place you can walk through in your head with your eyes shut. A real building beats an invented one, because you already know it.

What the old writers say

  • Roman teachers: well-lit, not crowded, spaces of a moderate size.
  • Quintilian: pick real buildings you know. Don't over-engineer it.
  • Plat (1594): a large building, with rooms you know so well each one comes to mind at once.
  • D'Assigny (1697): your own town. Walk in at the gate and use its streets, churches and friends' houses.
  • Willis (1621): build it like a real theatre, down to the measurements, so you can stage it.
a house I know
= free memory.

My quick test

  1. Can I walk it in my head, in order, without getting lost?
  2. Is each spot different enough from the last?
  3. Can I "see" the light there?

If yes to all three, it's a palace.

3

Step 2 · Set your markers

Walk the building in one fixed direction and pick the spots where things will sit. These are your loci (that's Latin for "places").

  • Five per room is a good size: the doorway, a window, the fireplace, the table, the floor.
  • Mark every fifth place with something special, so you can count. The Elizabethan guides used a golden hand. It means you never lose your place.
  • Space them out. Not too close (they blur) and not too far (you forget the route). The old guides said a few feet to a dozen.
  • Always go the same way round, and always start at the door.

4

Step 3 · Make the images

This is the step that matters. A plain picture slips away. A strange one sticks. The old books say the same things again and again:

  1. Make it move. Action beats a still life. (A lion roaring, not a lion.)
  2. Make it feel. Funny, grotesque, tender, terrifying: anything that stirs you.
  3. Use people you know. They're already vivid in your mind.
  4. Add the senses. Smell, sound, texture. A foul smell for a bad thing, perfume for a good one.
  5. Resize it. Make small things huge (a coin becomes a heap) and shrink the huge (a battle becomes a painting on the wall).
  6. Colour-code. Give each room its own colour, and dress every image in it.
  7. Use puns and likeness. A chickpea (cicer) in a garden (hortus) stands for Cicero against Hortensius.

Two tricks I like

Paradise and hell. To fix an image, load it with the most delightful things for all five senses, or the most disgusting. Nothing in between.

Personify it. An idea with a face is unforgettable. A fifth-century writer turned the seven liberal arts into seven women: Grammar with a knife for cutting out mistakes, Rhetoric in a gown covered with figures of speech. Give your ideas a body, a gesture and a story.

sources: Gratarolo (1562), Plat (1594), Willis (1621), Herdson (1651), D'Assigny (1697); Martianus Capella's Marriage of Mercury and Philology.

5

Step 4 · Place, then walk

  1. Read your material three or four times first. You can't picture what you don't understand.
  2. Turn each point into one image. One image for one thing. Never reuse an image for something else.
  3. Put the images in their places, in order, starting at the door.
  4. Walk the route slowly in your head. At each marker, let the image tell you what it stands for.
  5. Then walk it backwards. If you can do that, it's really there.
things (res) are easy to picture.
words (verba) are harder: use puns, sounds, or just picture the thing the word means.

Notes into places

  • A sermon or speech: one part per room.
  • A story: split it into people (Genesis into Adam, Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Joseph), one per spot.
  • A list of names: Gratarolo's example — a friend you know well doing something ridiculous to another friend.
  • A sentence: one image per key word, in a line along the wall.
6

Step 5 · Keep it alive

A palace you never visit falls down. Forgetting is the default. So you rehearse.

WhenWhat I do
The same dayWalk it once, start to end.
Last thing at nightOne more walk. The old guides say the mind is quiet and the images are still fresh when you wake.
+3, +7, +14 daysWalk it again, and say what each image stands for before you check.
LaterReuse the rooms for new material, but only once the old images have faded or been written down.

Don't throw the pen away

Willis, who invented a shorthand, said writing is the best aid to memory, and that the art of places is the closest thing to writing. So keep a small notebook of striking sentences under headings. The notebook and the palace are partners.

7

Numbers · the PAO trick

Dates and numbers have no pictures. So we give them some. PAO means Person, Action, Object.

  1. Split a year into two pairs: 1066 → 10 and 66.
  2. Every two-digit number has its own person, action and object, from a table you build once.
  3. Combine: the person from 10 does the action from 66 with the object from 66.
  4. Place that scene in the room for the event.
three dates per room is plenty.
20 rooms = 60 dates.

A portable palace: the hand

In 1671 Richard Saunders put ten letters on the palm and the fingers and used them as places for ideas, with a set of questions (who? why? how much? when? where? how?) hanging from each. A palace you carry everywhere.

8

Scaling up · a palace of meaning

A shopping list is one room. But a palace can hold a whole subject. Giordano Bruno (1582) thought the trained mind should become a mirror of the universe: not just storing facts, but arranging ideas the way the world is arranged.

Ten, ten, ten

One way to build it: take the ten classes of the Dewey library system, and turn each into a building.

10 buildings× 10 rooms each× 10 markers each= 1,000 places

Use real buildings you know: the strongest palaces are made from places you've actually lived and worked in. Put one interesting idea at each marker. When you meet anyone, you know which building to walk into.

choose carefully what to remember.
memory can be a portal if it's well used,
a prison if it's not.
9

Warnings from the old books

  • Don't make it too complicated. Quintilian and D'Assigny both say the elaborate systems cost more effort than they save.
  • One image, one meaning. Reuse an image and your memory will mix them up.
  • Not too humble, not too grand. An image that's too dull won't stick. One that's too far above you won't feel real.
  • It isn't magic. Plat found it good for ten to forty things, and said the teacher who promised miracles had delivered a good tool, no more.
  • It's not for speeches word for word. It's best for the shape of things: names, headings, the order of an argument.
  • Be wary of anyone who sells it. The first English teachers charged a fortune. A few used it to cheat at cards.
10

The short version

one page, five steps

  1. Building. A place you know, lit and uncluttered.
  2. Markers. Five per room, one direction, a gold mark on every fifth.
  3. Images. Moving, odd, emotional, with the senses switched on.
  4. Place and walk. In order, from the door, forwards and then backwards.
  5. Rehearse. Tonight, +3, +7, +14 days. Keep a notebook too.
