Warm-up

Out loud, before anything else — don't worry about mistakes, just talk.

SPEAK

Imagine a friend you haven't seen for three months calls you tonight. What are the first three questions they'll ask you? Say them out loud.

Listen in on this catch-up

Sam: Hey! Long time. What have you been up to?

Alex: Honestly? I've been working non-stop. And you won't believe it — the whole square by my flat is being dug up. Months of drilling.

Sam: Nightmare. Any plans to escape it?

Alex: Actually yes — I'm visiting my cousins next month. And after that, who knows. Something'll turn up.

Look at what Alex does in four short lines. Three completely different verb structures, all in one everyday chat. Can you spot all three? Say them before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "I've been working non-stop" — have been + -ing, for what's filled your recent life.
2. "the square is being dug up" — the passive, for what's happening around you.
3. "I'm visiting my cousins next month" — a future form, for what's coming.

This is why these structures matter: they ARE the catch-up conversation. Master these and you can answer "how's life?" like a native speaker.
Today: the three engines of everyday conversation — have been doing, is being done / got done, and the future forms — so you can talk about your life, your news, and your plans without stopping to think.

What Have You Been Up To?

The tense for what's been filling your days — still going, or only just stopped.

Spot the pattern

  • "I've been going to bed far too late recently."
  • "She's been learning to drive since the spring."
  • "What have you been watching lately?"

What's the shared machinery? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
have / has + been + -ing. Use it when the activity itself is the news — it's been going on and it colours your life right now. Compare: "I've painted the hallway" (the result — it's done) vs "I've been painting the hallway" (the activity — that's why I smell of paint).
have / has + been + verb-ing  (+ lately / recently / for / since / all week)
One trap: feelings and states stay simple — "I've known her for years", never "I've been knowing".

Quick check

1. Your friend started a pottery course in March. It's still going. Tell someone about it — use the continuous form of do:

"She a pottery course since March."

2. The couple upstairs started arguing about their kitchen renovation weeks ago — and it's still not over. Use the continuous form of argue:

"They about that kitchen for weeks."

Guess What's Happened

The passive isn't formal-letter grammar — it's how you share news when the doer doesn't matter.

Spot the pattern

  • "Our street's being resurfaced — the noise is unbelievable."
  • "The corner shop has been taken over — new owners, same prices, thankfully."
  • "My umbrella got stolen. Who steals an umbrella?"

Three different flavours of the same idea. What do they share, and what's different? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
All three put the thing first because the doer is unknown or unimportant.
is/are being + participle — happening right now, ongoing.
has/have been + participle — recent news, result matters now.
got + participle — the spoken favourite for accidents and misfortunes: got stolen, got cancelled, got soaked. In conversation, "got" often sounds more natural than "was".
is / are being + past participle  (happening now)
has / have been + past participle  (recent news)
got + past participle  (spoken, often bad luck)

Quick check

1. Workers are repainting the gym right now — you can smell it from the street. Start with "The gym":

"The gym at the moment — hold your nose."

2. Bad luck: someone cancelled your friend's flight this morning. Give her the news using get:

"Your flight — I'm so sorry."

So What's the Plan?

English has several futures — and the one you pick tells your listener how real the plan is.

Spot the difference

  • "I'm having dinner at my sister's on Friday." (it's in the diary — arranged)
  • "We're going to repaint the bedroom this autumn." (decided, not yet arranged)
  • "You know what — I'll come with you." (decided this second)
  • "I'll be passing the pharmacy anyway — want anything?" (happening in the natural course of things)

Four futures, four levels of "how fixed is this?". Which would change meaning if you swapped it? Talk it through, then check.

Check the idea CLICK
-ing = arranged (people and times involved). going to = intended (decided before now). will = decided right now, or an offer/promise. will be -ing = it'll happen anyway — great for casual offers and polite questions ("Will you be using the car?").

Swap test: "I'll have dinner at my sister's on Friday" sounds like you just decided this second — the diary feeling disappears. The forms aren't interchangeable; they're information.

Quick check

1. Your neighbour is struggling with heavy shopping bags. Offer to carry them — this is a this-second decision, with carry:

"Here — I those for you."

2. Which fits? "I can't do Tuesday evening — I ___ my cousin at the airport. It's been booked for weeks."
