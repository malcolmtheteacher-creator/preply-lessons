Memory and Truth

A memoir says: this really happened. But memory edits, and a drawn childhood is always a reconstruction. How far can a true story be trusted — and what kind of truth does it offer?

← Between Two Worlds  ·  The whole series

Today: you'll understand the difference between event, feeling and meaning in a personal story, and use a three-level ladder to judge what a memoir can and cannot prove, so you can write about truth, memory and testimony with precision.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — The promise of a memoir

A memoir makes a promise to the reader: this happened, and I was there. That promise is its power. Readers of Persepolis know they are not reading an invention — they are reading what a real woman says she lived through, in a country that most of them have never seen from the inside.

But the promise is harder to keep than it sounds. The author must recall events that happened decades earlier, and memory does not work like a camera. It forgets, repairs and rearranges. The adult must also reconstruct conversations, and decide what to draw: how a room looked, what people wore, exactly what was said.

There is another problem. In a graphic memoir the author draws herself: a child, a teenager, then a young woman. She is both the person telling the story and the person the story is about. The "I" of the book is a character that she has chosen how to draw.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What does a memoir promise the reader?

2. Why is it hard to keep that promise?

3. What is special about the "I" in a graphic memoir?

1. That the events really happened and the author was there.

2. Because memory forgets, repairs and rearranges, and the author must reconstruct what was said and how things looked.

3. It is both the person telling the story and a character the author has chosen how to draw.

Discuss:
  • If a writer changes the order of events or invents the exact words of a conversation, is the memoir still true?

Part 2 — Story-truth and happening-truth

The American writer Tim O'Brien, who fought in Vietnam, wrote a book called The Things They Carried (1990) that sits uneasily between memoir and fiction. The narrator is called Tim, like the author, and the stories feel like true war stories, yet the book admits that some of what we read is invented.

O'Brien's narrator makes a bold argument. There is "happening-truth", what actually occurred, and there is "story-truth", the truth of how it felt and what it meant. Sometimes, he says, the second is more honest than the first. A factual list of what a soldier carried says little about what it was like to carry it.

Persepolis takes a different path. It claims to be true, and it is told in a simple, controlled voice. But it faces the same question: when a person tells a painful story, what matters most — the exact words that were spoken, or the feeling that she can make a reader share?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What kind of book is The Things They Carried?

2. What is the difference between happening-truth and story-truth?

3. How does Persepolis face the same question?

1. A book between memoir and fiction: stories that feel true, with a narrator named after the author, and some invented material.

2. Happening-truth is what actually occurred; story-truth is the truth of how it felt and what it meant.

3. It claims to be true, but it must still decide whether exact facts or shared feeling matters more.

Discuss:
  • Can a story that contains something invented still be truthful? Give an example.

Part 3 — Reading a true story with care

A personal account is testimony: one witness saying what she saw. That is its value. It lets readers feel the weight of history through one life instead of through statistics. But it also has limits. One person's view is always subjective, and no memoir can speak for a whole country.

For that reason, careful readers do two things at once. They give the author credible trust, because she has a reason to be honest and the details fit together. And they notice bias: the choices she makes about what to include, and the way her own feelings colour the story.

A memoir therefore works best when readers treat it as a window, not a mirror of the whole truth: a real view from a real place, to be set beside other voices. Its authenticity does not come from being complete. It comes from being honest about what one person knew.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What makes a memoir valuable as testimony?

2. What are its limits?

3. How should a careful reader read a memoir?

1. It lets readers feel history through one real life instead of statistics.

2. It is one subjective view and cannot speak for a whole country.

3. With trust in the author's honesty, but also noticing her choices and bias, and setting her voice beside others.

Discuss:
  • Whose voice should we hear first if we want to understand a war: a historian, a soldier, or a child? Why?

How the tool works

When someone tells you a true story, three different kinds of truth are mixed together. The Truth Ladder separates them, so you can say what a story proves and what it only shows.

Using it, step by step

  1. Event. What actually happened? This is the base of the ladder. It can sometimes be checked against dates, records and other witnesses.
  2. Feeling. What was it like for the person who lived it? This cannot be checked, but it can be shown, and it is what makes a story move us.
  3. Meaning. What does the story say it was for — what does the author want us to understand? This is an argument, and it should be judged as one.
  4. Effect. Say which level carries the weight: The memoir is most reliable on …, most powerful on …, and most open to question on …

Try it yourself

Each sentence comes from an imagined personal account. Which level of the ladder is it — or is it something that cannot be checked?

1. The school became single-sex in the early 1980s.
2. I felt as if everyone in the street was watching me.
3. A government that controls what you wear is trying to control what you think.
4. She remembers her grandmother's exact words from thirty years ago.
5. A war began in September 1980.
6. It was like being a stranger in my own house.
7. Silence can be a way of surviving, and a way of giving in.
8. The conversation took place inside her own head, and no one else heard it.

1. Event — a fact about what happened that records could confirm.

2. Feeling — it reports an inner experience.

3. Meaning — it is an argument about what the event means.

4. Cannot be checked — no record exists, and memory over that time is unreliable.

5. Event — a checkable historical fact.

6. Feeling — it describes how something felt.

7. Meaning — it draws a conclusion about what silence means.

8. Cannot be checked — nobody else could confirm it.

Where the tool breaks

The levels overlap. A single sentence can mix event, feeling and meaning. Pull them apart before you judge.

Checkable does not mean important. The most powerful part of a memoir may be the part that cannot be checked.

Unchecked is not false. Many true things cannot be verified. The right response is care, not dismissal.

Talk it through:
  • Which level matters most to you when you read a memoir? Is it the same level as for a history book?
  • Can a memoir be trusted on feeling and meaning if it is weak on events?

Language — Reporting verbs and attribution

When you write about a memoir you constantly report what someone says, claims or remembers. The choice of verb tells the reader how far you trust it. Each verb also has its own pattern.

1 · Verb + that + clause. argues, claims, maintains, suggests, insists, admits: “The narrator admits that parts of the story are invented.”
2 · Verb + -ing. recalls, denies, admits (to), suggests: “She recalls walking to school the day the rule changed.” “He denies being afraid.”
3 · Verb + to + infinitive. claims, promises, aims: “The author claims to remember every detail.”
4 · Verb + on + -ing. “She insists on drawing herself as a child.”
5 · Strength of the verb. suggests is cautious, argues is firm, claims can sound doubtful, insists sounds forceful. Choose the verb that matches how far you accept the idea.
6 · The traps. She explained me the rule → “She explained the rule to me.” He suggested to draw → “He suggested drawing / that she draw.” She said me → “She told me.”
