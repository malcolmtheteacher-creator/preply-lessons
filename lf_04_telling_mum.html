Telling Mum

A Sunday, your mother's kitchen, the potatoes on. Your marriage ended months ago and she still doesn't know. Today you tell her — and then you sit there while she takes it.

The Scene 🎬

You've driven over on a Sunday, the way you always do. The hall smells of roast potatoes, the radio's on in the kitchen, and there on the wall — where it has been for eleven years — is the photograph of you and Sam on a beach, both squinting. Your mother has never taken it down and has never had any reason to.

You and Sam separated. Not last week: months ago, after a couple of years of the slow, undramatic version where nobody shouts and nothing gets thrown. You didn't tell her at Easter because the kids were there. You didn't tell her in May because she'd had her knee done. And in the meantime she has carried on texting you about lunch — when are you all coming, tell Sam there's a beer in for him — and every message has made the next conversation slightly harder to have.

So it's today. The news is yours, the grief is yours, and you're the one who has to say the words out loud — to somebody who will be upset, who loves him, and who will want to help in exactly the ways you can't cope with.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Is there anything you've delayed telling your family — not because it's shameful, but because you couldn't face their reaction? What made it harder to say the longer you left it?
  2. When you're the one with the bad news, do you want your family to be practical, sympathetic, or just ordinary with you? Do they know that about you?

Who's who

You
The One With the News 🚪

Steadier than you were six months ago, but nowhere near fine. You want to say it plainly, once, without a speech — and get out of the house before you cry in front of her.

The other voice
Mum 🍽️

She adores Sam. His photo's in the hall, she's been asking when you're all coming for lunch, and she'll be hurt twice over — once for you, and once because you didn't tell her sooner.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's not a smooth conversation, and it isn't meant to be. Listen for the plain sentences underneath the mess.

MumThere you are. Kettle's just gone on — d'you want tea, or shall I — I've done far too many potatoes again. I always do it. I don't know why I do it.
YouMum. Sit down a minute — nothing's happened to the kids.
Mum…Well now I'm going to worry, aren't I. Why would you say that. What. What is it.
YouI've got something to tell you and there's no gentle way into it. So — right. Sam and I have separated. It's done — it's not a wobble.
MumSeparated. What do you mean, sep— since when? You were both here at Easter. You were fine at Easter, the pair of you, you were sat right—
YouWe weren't, though. That's the thing. It's been coming for about two years, to be honest. We just — with the kids there, we weren't going to do it over the roast, were we.
MumTwo years. Two years, and I've been texting you about lunch like a — I've been asking when you're all coming.
YouI know. I didn't tell you because I knew you'd worry, and here we are.
MumMm. Well, I'd rather have worried, love, than found out on a Sunday.
YouYeah. That's fair. And look, I know it's not what you wanted for me. There's a picture of him in your hall, I'm not — sorry, that came out wrong, I'm not having a go at you about the hall. I just mean I know it's a loss for you as well, sort of.
MumOh, the photo. Don't be daft. Look at me a minute. Are you alright? Actually alright, not — are you?
YouI'm alright. Not brilliant, but alright.
MumBecause I never said anything, but I did always think he was a bit—
YouMum. Please don't say anything about him — I'll only end up defending him, and then we'll fall out, and I'll drive home cross with you, and none of that's any use to anybody.
MumRight. Alright. I'll say nothing. It's not easy, mind.
YouI know. One other thing, and then we can — could you not ring Auntie Jean about it yet? Just for a bit. I'd rather people heard it from me.
Mum…She'll ask, though. She always asks. But no. No, alright, love. What do you want me to do, then? Because I don't know what — I don't know what I'm meant to say to you here.
YouYou don't have to say anything clever. Just be normal with me. Do the potatoes. Tell me about next door's fence.
MumThe fence. Right. Well, it's still up, more's the pity, and he's still — no, hang on. Come here a minute first.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The very first thing she says is not the news. Why does she start where she starts, and what would her mum have been thinking if she hadn't?

2. Her mum begins to give an opinion about Sam and gets stopped. What does the daughter say will happen if her mum carries on — and why is that a reason her mum can accept?

3. Her mum ends up asking what she's supposed to say. What does the daughter actually ask her for — and what does that tell you about what she came round for?

1. She tells her mum to sit down, which by itself sounds like a death, so she cancels the worst fear in the same breath — nothing's happened to the kids. Without that, her mum would have spent the next thirty seconds somewhere far worse than the truth, and wouldn't have heard a word of it.
2. That she'll end up defending him, they'll fall out, and she'll drive home cross with her mum. It works because it isn't a telling-off — it's a warning about what the criticism would cost the two of them, so her mum can stop without feeling she's been put in the wrong.
3. Nothing clever: do the potatoes, be normal, talk about the fence. She didn't come for advice or sympathy, which she can get anywhere — she came to be somewhere ordinary, with someone who knows, and stop performing for an afternoon.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for handing someone news they don't want, when the news is about you. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Bad news is carried by the pace, not the words. Say it slowly, in short sentences, and then stop talking, because the silence after it is where the other person catches up. The pull is to fill that silence — to explain, soften, add a bit more — and every extra sentence makes the news sound less certain than it is. Flat and gentle beats gentle and endless.

🚪 Getting the words out

Phrase 1

You ask her to sit down, and her face changes. Whatever you say next, she is already imagining the worst thing that could have happened. Which line stops that in one move?

A. "Don't panic. It's nothing serious."
B. "Sit down. There's something serious I need to talk to you about."
C. "Sit down a minute — nothing's happened to the kids."
C — "Sit down a minute — nothing's happened to the kids."
The moment a grown-up child says "sit down", a parent goes straight to the worst thing they can think of, which is almost always the grandchildren, so you name that fear and close it before you open anything else. A doesn't work because it's not true — this is serious — and B leaves her sitting there imagining it for as long as you take to get to the point.
Phrase 2

There's no clever run-up that will make this land softly, and pretending there is only drags it out. Say so, and then say it. Put the pieces in order:

and there's noI've got somethinginto it.gentle wayto tell you
"I've got something to tell you and there's no gentle way into it."
A "way into" something is the route you take to start it, so saying there's no gentle one admits that you've looked for a kind opening and there isn't one. It buys you about four seconds of warning, which is all she needs, and it stops you doing the long tactful build-up that would leave her hanging.
Phrase 3

Now the news itself. She will reach, immediately, for the version where this blows over by Christmas. Which one leaves her nowhere to put that hope?

A. "Sam and I are having a few problems at the moment."
B. "Sam and I have separated. It's done — it's not a wobble."
C. "Me and Sam might be splitting up. We'll see how it goes."
B — "Sam and I have separated. It's done — it's not a wobble."
A "wobble" is a bad patch that passes, so naming it and denying it takes away the one comfort she was about to reach for, which sounds hard but saves her from months of hoping. A and C are both softer to say and much crueller in the end, because she'd carry on believing it might come right and you'd have to break it to her all over again.

🕰️ The questions underneath

Phrase 4

Her first question won't be a question — it'll be "but you were fine at Easter". Answer the real thing she's asking: how long has this been true while I've known nothing? Put the pieces in order:

for about two years,It's been comingto be honest.
"It's been coming for about two years, to be honest."
If something has "been coming", it's been on its way for a while and it wasn't a surprise to the people in it, which tells her this wasn't a row that got out of hand. "About" and "to be honest" keep it human rather than a statement you've prepared — although you have, of course, been preparing it for months.
Phrase 5

Here's the one that stings her: you knew for two years and she found out over the potatoes. She's hurt, and she's got a point. Don't defend it and don't grovel — admit the reason, and admit that the reason didn't work. Say it out loud in your own words first.

"I didn't tell you because I knew you'd worry, and here we are."
The first half gives her the true reason, which is kinder than it sounds because it's about protecting her rather than hiding from her. Then "and here we are" quietly admits the whole plan failed — she's worried anyway, and now she's hurt as well — so you've owned it without a long apology that would only make her comfort you.

💛 Letting her be your mum

Phrase 6

She's lost something too: the son-in-law, the photo in the hall, the picture she had of your life. Name it, so she doesn't have to pretend she hasn't. Complete it — one word: "I know it's not what you wanted ___ me."

"I know it's not what you wanted for me."
"Wanted for me" means the life she hoped I'd have, which is hers to grieve; "wanted from me" would mean something she was owed, and that's a row. One small word decides whether you're telling her you understand her disappointment or accusing her of it, so it's worth getting right.
Phrase 7

"Are you alright?" — and she means it, and she'll know instantly if you lie. Which answer keeps her calm without pretending?

A. "I'm fine, honestly. Don't worry about me."
B. "No. I'm a mess. I don't know what I'm doing."
C. "I'm alright. Not brilliant, but alright."
C — "I'm alright. Not brilliant, but alright."
It's true and it's finished, which is the trick: it tells her you're standing up without inviting her to fix anything. A is the lie she won't believe and will keep picking at, while B is honest but hands her a job she can't do, and then you'll spend the afternoon looking after her instead.

🚧 Setting the terms

Phrase 8

She's building up to an opinion about him — the one she's been sitting on for years. Stop her, but give her a reason that isn't a telling-off. Put the pieces in order:

anything about him —defending him.Please don't sayI'll only end up
"Please don't say anything about him — I'll only end up defending him."
To "end up" doing something is to do it without meaning to, so you're not accusing her of anything — you're warning her what you'll turn into, which is much easier for her to accept than "don't be horrible about him". It also happens to be true, and she probably knows it.
Phrase 9

By tonight the whole family could know, and you'd rather that didn't happen while you're still driving home. Ask her to hold off — softly enough that it isn't an order. Complete it — one word: "Could you ___ ring Auntie Jean about it yet?"

"Could you not ring Auntie Jean about it yet?"
"Could you not…?" asks someone to hold off doing something without ever saying they were going to, which lets her agree while keeping her dignity — nobody's been accused of gossiping. "Yet" does the rest of the work, because you're asking for a delay rather than silence, and a delay is something a person can actually promise.

🤝 Saying what you need

Phrase 10

She's floundering — "I don't know what I'm meant to say to you" — and she's about to reach for advice, or worse, a speech. Let her off. Tell her, in your own words, that she doesn't need the right words, and give her something ordinary to do instead. Say it out loud first.

"You don't have to say anything clever. Just be normal with me."
Most people go quiet around bad news because they're hunting for a wise thing to say and can't find one, so telling them there isn't one is a genuine relief. Then "just be normal with me" gives her something she can definitely manage — the potatoes, the fence, an ordinary afternoon, which is what you came for.

🔑 Make them yours

These aren't only for the worst news. They work for anything you've been carrying around for weeks and haven't said — the job, the diagnosis, the money, the plan they won't like. Pin three of them to your own life so you'll have them when you need them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I've got something to tell you and there's no gentle way into it." — something you've been putting off saying to someone. What is it, and who to?

"I'm alright. Not brilliant, but alright." — when did you last say you were fine when you weren't? What would the honest version have been?

"You don't have to say anything clever. Just be normal with me." — what do you actually want from people when things are bad? Have you ever told them?

The Twist

📞 She picks up her phone

One more round — no script. You've said your bit, you've asked her not to ring Jean, and she's agreed. Then she reaches for her phone off the worktop and says, quite gently, "I'm going to ring him. Not to interfere. He'll listen to me — he always did." She isn't being spiteful; she's lost him too and she thinks she can help. Start the timer. You've now got to keep her out of it without telling your own mother who she's allowed to love, and you have to do it before she scrolls to his name. Decide, out loud, in real time: does she ring him, does she keep him, does she never speak to him again — and can you say that plainly without a row and without crying at your mother's kitchen table? One rule: "do what you like" doesn't count. Say what you actually want.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I stopped her fearing something worse before I said anything else.
  • I said the news once, plainly, and left no room for "it might come right".
  • I answered why she didn't know sooner, without a long apology.
  • I stopped the talk about him with a reason she could accept.
  • I asked for what I actually wanted from her, instead of hoping she'd guess.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and get it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Telling your parents is rarely the hard part because of the words. It's hard because for a few minutes you have to be the strong one in a room where you were the child, and because their disappointment arrives on top of your own and you're expected to carry both.

Most of us delay it for a decent reason — they'll worry, they'll take it badly, they'll ring everyone. The delay is usually kind and almost always makes it worse, because the longer they don't know, the more of your life they're being kept out of, and the more the eventual telling feels like a second thing you've done to them.

And the thing people ask for, when they finally ask, is nearly always smaller than the thing we try to give them. Not advice. Not a plan. Somebody doing the potatoes, talking about the fence, behaving as if you're still the same person — because you are.

Two bigger questions

  1. Is there such a thing as a kind lie by omission? You've certainly been on both ends of one — protecting someone from news, and finding out late that you were protected. Did the protection ever work?
  2. When a marriage or a long friendship ends, the people around it lose someone too, and nobody thinks their loss counts. Should they get to keep him? Who decides that — and what happens to a family that gets it wrong?
