Four digits and a locked door

You are standing outside a rented flat at eleven at night, and the door has a keypad. The owner wrote the code on a card, and you have just watched the card blow out of your hand into the canal. The owner's phone is switched off. You are quite sure the code was four digits. It might have begun with an eight.

You can see the card in your mind: black ink, a very ordinary number. But you did not turn it into anything. It was just four digits, and four digits are very easy to let go.

Why can't you remember the door code?
You never turned it into a picture or word. It stayed as four meaningless digits.
Talk about it. What numbers do you have in your head right now: phone numbers, codes, dates? Which ones do you remember, and why?

The idea that fixes it is old. In 1634 a French mathematician, Pierre Hérigone, published a huge textbook in Paris and included in it a code that let letters stand for digits, so that a number could be read as a word. Over the next two centuries others improved it. In the nineteenth century a Polish army officer living in Britain, Major Beniowski, lectured on it; some writers connect his rank with the system's name.

By the twentieth century the code had become the standard way of turning numbers into words. People call it the Major System, and it is the base for most of the number methods you will meet in the rest of this wing.

What is the Major System?
A code in which each digit stands for a consonant sound, so that numbers can be turned into words.
Talk about it. Why do you think this idea has survived for four hundred years?

The key idea is surprisingly small. Do not give each digit a picture; give each digit a sound. Ten digits, ten sounds. Then any number becomes a string of sounds, and you fill the gaps with vowels, which are free, to make a real word.

The word tie contains one consonant sound, t, so it means 1. Moon has m and n, so it means 32. Once you have a word, you can see it, put it in a scene and keep it. When you need the number back, you read the consonants. The door code that blew away would be a few seconds' work.

What does 'vowels are free' mean?
Vowels (and w, h, y) carry no number, so you can add them to make real words out of consonant sounds.
Talk about it. Try it: how would you turn the number 32 into a word? What picture does it make?

Ten digits, ten sounds

Each digit stands for one consonant sound. Learn the ten and you can say any number as a word.

DigitSoundWhy it fits
0s, zZero begins with a z sound.
1t, d, thA printed t has one downstroke.
2nAn n has two downstrokes.
3mAn m has three downstrokes.
4rThe word four ends with r.
5lL is the Roman numeral for 50, and a hand held up with the thumb out makes an L.
6j, sh, ch, soft gA reversed 6 looks like a j.
7k, hard g, qTwo sevens make a k.

The rules

  • Sounds, not spelling. Knee is n (2). Phone is f-n (82). Judge is j-j (66). Cake is k-k (77).
  • Vowels, w, h and y are free. They carry no number. Use them to build words.
  • A doubled letter counts once when you hear one sound. Hammer is h-m-r: 34 (m once).
  • Soft and hard letters follow the sound. City starts with s (0); cat starts with k (7).
