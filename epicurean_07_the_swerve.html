How atoms deviate and create the possibility of freedom

Today: We're solving the central problem of atomic physics: If atoms simply fall straight down by gravity, how does anything happen? Epicurus's answer — the spontaneous swerve — is one of history's most brilliant physics ideas, and it's directly about freedom.

Setting Up the Problem

In the previous lesson, we learned that atoms have weight and naturally fall downward. But here's the puzzle: if every atom falls straight down infinitely through the void, they never collide. They never combine. Nothing ever forms. The universe is just a rain of atoms falling separately forever.

Yet we see a world of objects — plants, animals, rocks, stars. How did these form if atoms never meet?

Democritus didn't have a fully satisfying answer. Epicurus's solution changed philosophy forever.

The Problem: Determinism and Collision

Democritus's Physics

Democritus, Epicurus's intellectual predecessor, established atomic theory but hit a logical wall. If atoms have only three properties (shape, size, weight) and all fall straight down by gravity, then:

  • Every event is determined: Given the initial state of all atoms, everything that follows is mathematically inevitable.
  • Atoms never collide: If all atoms fall at the same rate in the same direction, faster atoms don't catch up to slower ones. They maintain their relative positions forever.
  • Nothing can form: Without collisions, atoms never combine. No objects, no worlds, no life.
⚠️
The Logical Problem:
Democritus's atomism leads to a contradiction: atoms must collide to form things, but pure mechanical fall in a void makes collisions impossible. Either atoms don't exist (contradiction) or something is missing from the theory.

The Philosophical Stakes

This isn't just a physics problem. It's a problem about freedom. If everything is determined — if every atom's motion is fixed from eternity — then human choices are not truly free. You didn't choose to be born or to exist. Your thoughts and actions are just the inevitable consequence of prior atomic states.

Epicurus saw this clearly. A deterministic universe means no real responsibility, no real choice, no real ethics. It means fatalism. And fatalism is one of the great sources of human suffering.

If a man believes that nothing is in our power, he will not believe that anything is shameful or praiseworthy. Rather, he will think all things are fated and bound by necessity.

— Lucretius, based on Epicurus

Pause: In a purely determined universe (where everything follows from prior causes), can you meaningfully choose anything? Can you be morally responsible? Can you be happy?

The freedom problem:

If every event is caused by prior events infinitely backward, then your choices are not truly yours. You didn't cause your choices; they were caused by factors outside your control (prior atoms, prior events, genetic inheritance). You're a spectator of your own life, not an agent.

Ethical consequences:

  • Blame becomes meaningless (you couldn't have chosen differently)
  • Virtue is pointless (you can't improve yourself)
  • Hope is irrational (the future is already fixed)

Epicurus rejected this conclusion. He believed freedom was real, and his physics had to make space for it.

Epicurus's Solution: The Swerve (Parenklisis)

The Spontaneous Deviation

Epicurus proposed something radical: atoms don't always fall straight. At unpredictable moments, for no reason, an atom spontaneously swerves from its straight path — just a tiny deviation, but enough to change everything.

In Greek, he called this parenklisis (παρέγκλισις). In Latin, Lucretius called it clinamen (the swerve). The swerve is:

  • Spontaneous: Not caused by anything. Just happens.
  • Uncaused: No prior event forces the swerve. It's genuinely random.
  • Infinitesimal: The deviation is tiny, but cumulative effects of many swerves create visible change.
  • Unpredictable: You can't know when or where it will happen.

When the atoms are moving straight down through the void by their own weight, at quite indeterminate times and places, they swerve a little from their course. So small is this swerve that we can scarcely speak of it.

— Lucretius, De Rerum Natura (Book II, lines 217-220)

How the Swerve Solves the Problem

Now atoms can collide:

  1. Atom A falls straight down
  2. Atom B falls straight down, but swerves slightly
  3. This swerve brings Atom B into Atom A's path
  4. The two collide, bounce, stick together
  5. A compound forms. Objects begin to exist.

The swerve is the key to everything: without it, there's no motion, no collision, no formation of objects, no life, no change. The entire universe depends on the swerve.

Atoms Without vs. With the Swerve
Without Swerve:

All atoms fall straight | No collisions | Nothing forms | Static universe

With Swerve:

Some atoms swerve slightly | Collisions occur | Compounds form | Dynamic universe | Life possible

Critical Question: Epicurus says the swerve is "uncaused" — it just happens. But is an uncaused event the same as a random event? And how does introducing randomness solve the freedom problem?

Uncaused vs. Random:

For Epicurus, an uncaused event means: not determined by prior atomic states. It's not "random" in the modern sense (where we have probability distributions). It's simply indeterminate — not caused, not determined, just happening.

How this enables freedom:

If every event is determined by prior causes, then "you" are just the inevitable result of prior atoms. Your choices follow necessarily from prior states. You're not really choosing.

But if some events are indeterminate (the swerve), then there's a break in the chain of necessity. Your choices don't follow necessarily from prior conditions. You have genuine options. You can act, and your action isn't predetermined.

The swerve makes freedom possible. Without it, fatalism rules.

The Swerve and Human Freedom

Breaking the Chain of Necessity

Epicurus's swerve is an indeterministic physics. It breaks the causal chain that stretches back to infinity. Here's the crucial move:

If determinism is true: Every event, including every human choice, is determined by prior events. You didn't choose your genetics, your environment, your past. Those prior conditions determine what you choose now. Are you really free?

If indeterminism is true (via the swerve): Some events are not determined by prior causes. They just happen. The future is not fully determined by the past. You can make choices that are genuinely open — not forced by prior conditions.

🎯
Epicurus's Insight:
For human agency and responsibility to be real, the future cannot be entirely determined by the past. We need indeterminacy — not chaos or randomness, but genuine openness. The swerve provides this.

How Free Will Arises from the Swerve

Epicurus doesn't say your choices are random. Rather:

  1. Your mind (psyche) is made of very fine atoms
  2. These atoms can swerve — they're not perfectly determined
  3. Through the swerve, you can deliberate and choose
  4. Your choices are genuinely yours — not predetermined by outside forces
  5. You're responsible for your choices because they're not determined

There is also something we must understand: that the soul has the greatest share in bringing about our safety and happiness. Understand that our soul, being made of very fine atoms, can swerve and affect our choices and actions.

— Epicurus (paraphrased by later Epicureans)

A Brief Note: How This Differs from Modern Physics

Modern quantum physics also discovered indeterminacy — the idea that the universe is not perfectly deterministic. Particles behave probabilistically, not predictably. This might seem to vindicate Epicurus.

But there's a difference. In quantum mechanics, indeterminacy is probabilistic — governed by probability waves and statistical laws. Epicurus's swerve is wholly uncaused — not governed by any law, not probabilistic, just spontaneous deviation.

Still, both break the deterministic chain. Both suggest that the future is genuinely open. In that sense, modern physics vindicated Epicurus's intuition: the universe is not a clockwork of fixed causes and effects.

Deeper question: Can you have freedom and responsibility if your choices are uncaused? If I choose to help a friend, but that choice "just happens" (via the swerve), am I really responsible? Or is responsibility only possible if choices are caused by my character, values, and deliberation?

The deep tension:

This question haunts philosophy even today. Modern compatibilists argue that freedom doesn't require indeterminism — you're free if you act on your own desires, values, and deliberation (even if those are ultimately determined). Others argue you need indeterminism (libertarian free will) — the swerve allowing genuine openness.

Epicurus's answer: The swerve is not "just happening" at the conscious level. Your mind deliberates, considers options, chooses. That deliberation is what it means to be free. The swerve at the atomic level enables this deliberation without it being predetermined. Your choice is genuinely yours because it's not forced by prior conditions.

This is subtle. The swerve doesn't make every choice random. It makes genuine choice possible. Once choice is possible, you're responsible for the choices you make.

The problem without the swerve:

  • All atoms fall straight down by gravity
  • Atoms at the same weight fall at the same speed
  • Therefore atoms never collide (no relative acceleration)
  • Therefore nothing forms, no objects, no world, no life
  • Also: everything would be determined — no freedom, no choice

Three characteristics of the swerve:

  • Spontaneous — happens without external cause
  • Uncaused — not determined by prior atomic states
  • Infinitesimal — tiny deviation, but cumulative effect is significant
  • Unpredictable — you can't know when or where it occurs

Swerve and human freedom:

  • Indeterminism breaks the chain of necessity
  • Without determination, genuine choice becomes possible
  • Your mind's atoms can swerve, enabling deliberation and choice
  • You're responsible because your choices are genuinely yours, not forced
  • This solves fatalism — the future is not fixed

Connection to ethics:

  • If you're free, you can choose virtue, friendship, ataraxia
  • If you're free, you're not trapped by fate or divine will
  • Freedom from fatalism = freedom from irrational fear
  • This supports Epicureanism: you can control your own happiness
