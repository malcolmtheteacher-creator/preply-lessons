Warm-up

Out loud, before anything else — don't worry about mistakes, just talk.

SPEAK

Think about the last time you had to own up to something small — being late, forgetting something, eating the last biscuit. What did you actually say? Say the excuse and the admission out loud.

Listen in on this catch-up

Jess: Did you end up talking to Priya about the flat?

Tom: I did. She was fuming at first, but she admitted staying out that late without texting was rough on me too.

Jess: Good. And the money you lent her?

Tom: I offered to let it go, actually. But she insisted — she persuaded me to let her pay it back in two parts instead of one.

Jess: Did you finally apologise properly, though?

Tom: I did. I promised to text next time I'm out late. She warned me not to make her wait up again.

Look at what Tom does in this short exchange — four different reporting verbs, four different shapes. Can you name the pattern each one uses? Say it, then check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "admitted staying out" — verb + -ing, for the fact itself, no "to" involved.
2. "offered to let" and "promised to text" — verb + to-infinitive, for what Tom himself will (or won't) do.
3. "persuaded me to let" — verb + object + to-infinitive, for getting someone else to act.
4. "warned me not to make" — the same shape, negative: verb + object + not + to-infinitive.

This is why it matters: one verb carries the whole attitude — no need for "said" plus three extra sentences to explain what really happened.
Today: four reporting-verb shapes — verb + -ing, verb + to-infinitive, verb + object + to-infinitive, and verb + that — so you can retell an argument, an apology, or a broken promise using one strong verb instead of plain "said".

Just Admit It

Admit, deny, suggest and recommend go straight into -ing — no "to", no person in between.

Spot the pattern

  • "He admitted forgetting the tickets."
  • "She denied touching the thermostat."
  • "I suggested splitting the bill instead."

What's the shared shape? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
verb + -ing, directly. No "to", and no object sitting between the verb and the -ing form. Use this family when you're reporting the fact or the idea itself — recommend works the same way: "I'd recommend booking early."
admit / deny / suggest / recommend + verb-ing
The classic trap: these verbs never take a person as an object the way "want" or "tell" do. "She suggested me to book early" doesn't exist in English — it's suggested booking early, or suggested that I book early.

Quick check

1. Your brother swears he didn't eat the last slice of cake, but there's chocolate on his shirt. When you press him, he finally cracks. Complete with admit + -ing (eat):

"He finally the last slice."

2. You and a friend are deciding what to do about dinner — neither of you wants to cook. Complete with suggest + -ing (order):

"I takeaway instead."

3. Your flatmate insists she never touched your charger, even though you saw it in her bag. Complete with deny + -ing (take):

"She it."

Now say it

SPEAK

Out loud, use admit, deny, suggest and recommend — one each — about something from your own week: something you admitted, something you denied, something you suggested doing, and something you'd recommend.

Get Them To Do It

Persuade, warn, remind and encourage always take a person before "to".

Spot the pattern

  • "My cousin persuaded me to try the spicy version."
  • "The pharmacist warned her not to mix the tablets with alcohol."
  • "He reminded them to bring their passports."

What sits between the verb and "to" every time? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
verb + object (the person) + (not) + to-infinitive. The object always sits right there between the verb and "to" — that's what makes this shape different from admit/suggest, where no person appears at all.
persuade / warn / remind / encourage + object + (not) + to-infinitive
Word-order trap: the negative goes right before "to", not before the main verb — warned him not to forget, never "warned to not forget". And don't drop "not" by accident: "warned him to forget" says the opposite of what you mean.

Quick check

1. Your friend was terrified of the roller coaster and refused to go near it. By the end of the day, you'd completely changed her mind. Complete with persuade + to-infinitive (go), finishing with "on it":

"I finally ."

2. The trainer could see the new guy about to lift the bar with his back, not his knees. Complete with warn + not + to-infinitive (lift), finishing with "like that":

"The trainer ."

3. Your little sister wanted to quit piano after one hard lesson, but you didn't let her give up. Complete with encourage + to-infinitive (keep going):

"I for one more week."

Now say it

SPEAK

Think of someone you've persuaded, warned, reminded or encouraged to do something this year. Tell the story out loud, using at least two of these verbs with their object + to-infinitive shape.

I'll Do It — Or I Won't

Offer, promise, refuse, threaten and agree take a plain to-infinitive — no object, no -ing.

Spot the pattern

  • "He offered to drive us to the station."
  • "She promised to call as soon as she landed."
  • "They refused to lower the price."

Who's doing the driving, calling, refusing? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
verb + to-infinitive — no object at all. These verbs are about what the subject itself will or won't do. Compare with Tab 3: "persuaded HIM to drive" needs an object; "offered to drive" doesn't.
offer / promise / refuse / threaten / agree + to-infinitive
Trap: these five never take -ing. "Offered driving", "promised going", "agreed helping" are all the same mistake — swap in the plain to-infinitive: offered to drive, promised to go, agreed to help.

Quick check

1. Your neighbour borrowed your ladder in June — it's now October. Complete with promise + to-infinitive (return), naming the ladder:

"He within the week."

2. The airline lost your bag, and when you asked for a refund for what you had to buy, they said no — flatly. Complete with refuse + to-infinitive (cover), naming the cost:

"They ."

Same news, more formal

Many of today's verbs also work with that + a full clause — useful when you want to keep more of the detail or the other person's exact tense: "She promised that she'd call." "He admitted that he'd forgotten." "The waiter warned us that the kitchen was closing."

admit / deny / suggest / recommend / warn / promise + that + subject + verb

3. A friend texts you: "Careful — the lift's broken again." Report this using warn + that (the lift / be broken):

"She again."

What Do You Remember?

Before today's grammar — a quick basic check

1. What's the difference between "she said she was tired" and "she told me she was tired"? Say the rule, then check.

Check your answer CLICK
"Say" never takes a person directly as its object — not "say me". "Tell" always needs one — tell me, told him. Both can introduce reported statements, but only "tell" points straight at the listener.

2. Your flatmate keeps overstaying at parties you host. Complete with want + object + to-infinitive (leave) — the basic pattern behind today's verb + object + to-infinitive family:

"I by midnight next time."

Say first, then check — fresh situations, no looking back

3. A friend's been putting off telling her boss she's leaving. Complete with suggest + -ing (write):

"I a resignation letter this weekend, before you lose your nerve."

4. Your gran keeps forgetting to take her tablets. Complete with remind + to-infinitive (take):

"Can you her medication before you leave?"

5. Your landlord kept saying no to fixing the boiler — this time, though. Complete with agree + to-infinitive (fix), finishing with "it":

"He finally by Friday."
