Why Keynotes Are Different

A keynote is not a paper. It's not data-heavy. It's an intellectual argument delivered with rhetorical force. You're paid to move an audience, to redirect scholarly attention, to say something memorable that people will cite for years. The standards are higher—but so is the impact.

Move 1: The Opening Hook

1 First 60 seconds: grab intellectual attention +
Open with a paradox, an unexpected observation, or a reframed question—not with thanks or a definition. You have 60 seconds to signal: this is not a standard talk.
Opening Hook

"We've spent twenty years optimizing X. And we're getting better results. But I want to suggest we're optimizing the wrong thing."

Move 2: Building Narrative Arc

2 Structuring 45 minutes as a journey, not a list +
Arc: Here's what we assumed (past). Here's what we've discovered (present). Here's what that means (future). Not: Point 1. Point 2. Point 3. Each section builds toward the thesis, not away from it.
Arc Language

"For decades, we believed Y. But recent work shows Z. This forces us to reconsider our entire approach to..."

Move 3: Signposting for Large Audiences

3 Making your structure audible (people can't see your outline) +
In a 400-person auditorium, people get lost. You must narrate where you are constantly. "I've just shown you why the old model fails. Now I want to show you what replaces it." Repeat your thesis. Restate your argument.
Signposting Phrases

"This brings me to my second claim..." "What I'm about to show you challenges..." "This is crucial because..." "Return to the opening: I said we were optimizing the wrong thing. Here's why."

Move 4: The Memorable Thesis

4 Deliver your big idea in language people will remember +
Your thesis should be quotable—clear enough that people can tweet it, cite it, teach it. Not a complex sentence. A claim so well-phrased that it sticks.
Memorable vs. Forgettable

Forgettable: "The implications for policy are considerable if we take into account various stakeholders." Memorable: "We're solving for efficiency when we should be solving for justice."

Move 5: Managing Expert Q&A

5 Handling hostile specialists without losing credibility +
Someone will challenge you. They may know your field better than you know parts of it. Concede gracefully where you're wrong. Stand firm where you're right. Never get defensive. Say: "That's a fair push-back. Here's what I'd say..." or "You're right that X. But that actually supports my point about Y."
The Move

"That's a legitimate concern. In fact, I'd say it's part of the problem I'm describing."

Move 6: Closing with Impact

6 Last 5 minutes: deliver your thesis with force +
Don't end by listing what you said. Don't end with questions (you'll have a Q&A). End by restating your core claim and articulating what it means for the field. This is your final argument.
Strong Close

"So here's where I want to leave you: [restate thesis]. It means we need to [implication]. And it means this field will either [consequence if adopted] or [consequence if not]. The choice is ours."

Scaffold: Keynote Structure

  • Opening: Paradox or reframe (2 min)
  • Why this matters: Context and stakes (5 min)
  • What we got wrong: The old model (8 min)
  • What I'm proposing: The new direction (15 min)
  • Implications: What this means for the field (10 min)
  • Closing: Thesis + call to action (5 min)

The Q&A: Difficult Questions

After your keynote, expert challenges. You have 60 seconds to respond to each. Stay intellectual. Don't defend your ego—defend your argument.
