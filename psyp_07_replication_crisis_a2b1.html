Can We Repeat the Result?

One hundred psychology studies were done again. How many gave the same result?

Today: you'll explain the replication crisis in psychology and use the passive to describe how studies are done, checked and reported.
Researchers repeated 100 published psychology studies. How many gave the same result again? Choose one.

Repeating studies: real or made up?

In 2015, a large group of scientists tried to repeat psychology studies. Here are six things about it. Some are real and some are made up for this game. Which are real?

1. The scientists tried to repeat 100 psychology studies.
Real. They are called replications.
2. They tried to repeat 1,000 studies.
Made up. It was 100 studies from three journals.
3. Journals print results that find nothing as often as results that find something.
Made up. Journals print positive results more often.
4. In 2011, a well-known study said that people can feel the future.
Real. Most scientists did not believe it, but it followed the normal rules.
5. Almost all of the first studies, 97 out of 100, had a significant result.
Real. That is what the journals printed.
6. Almost all of the repeats gave the same result again.
Made up. Fewer than half did.
Talk about it:
  • Are you surprised that fewer than half of the repeats gave the same result?
  • Why would a journal prefer a surprising result?

The evidence, in three steps

Before each part, make a prediction out loud. Then read, and check yourself.

Part 1 — The warnings

Before you read: A scientist says people can feel the future. Most scientists do not believe it, but the study followed the normal rules. What does this tell us about the rules?

From 2011, psychologists became worried. A well-known researcher published a study that said people can feel the future. Most scientists did not believe it. But the study followed the normal rules, and it was published in a top journal. This made people ask: what is wrong with the rules?

In the same year, three researchers showed something even stranger. They used normal methods and found that listening to one song made students younger by a year and a half. This was not true, of course. They wanted to show that it is easy to find a 'significant' result when you make many small choices.

The result was significant: it was probably not an accident.

Part 2 — One hundred studies

Before you read: Scientists repeat 100 studies. What do you think happens? Say a number.

In 2015, a big group of researchers, called the Open Science Collaboration, repeated 100 studies from three psychology journals. Nearly all of the original studies (97%) had a significant result. In the repeats, only about 36% of studies had a significant result.

The effects were also smaller. On average, the repeated studies found an effect about half as big as the original. This was a shock for the field. Other fields, for example medicine, also found similar problems.

New words in this part — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

To replicate a study means to do it again in the same way.
replicate (v) — to repeat a study to see if you get the same result. The noun is replication.
The sample was small: only twenty students took part.
sample (n) — the group of people in a study.
Your call:
  • Does a repeat that fails prove that the first study was wrong? Why or why not?

Part 3 — What changed

Before you read: How can scientists make research better?

A failed repeat does not always mean the first study was wrong. The people, the place or the time can be different. But the researchers agreed that things must change.

Now many studies are preregistered. Big samples are used more often. Data and materials are shared. Some journals accept a paper before the results are known, so they cannot choose only exciting results. Many researchers say psychology is stronger today.

New words in this part — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

Which word means 'to write your plan and share it publicly before you start the study'? (p__________)
preregister (v) — to write your plan and put it online before you start. Example: "We preregistered the study."
Which word means 'when a journal prints only exciting results'? (b___)
bias (n) — publication bias: journals publish positive results more often than negative ones.
Your call:
  • What is one rule that would make research fairer?

The passive

Use the passive when the action is more important than who did it. It is common in science writing.

The form: be + past participle.
• Present: The data are shared online.
• Past: The study was repeated in 2015.
• Present perfect: Many studies have been preregistered.
• Can / must / should: Results must be checked.
Active or passive?
• Active: The researchers repeated 100 studies. (who did it is important)
• Passive: 100 studies were repeated. (what happened is important)
Use by only when the person is important: The study was published by a famous researcher.

Would you trust it?

Five situations about research. Choose whether you would trust the result, then say why in one sentence. If you can, use the passive: was published, were repeated. Then check an idea.

1. A study has 20 people and a result that is just significant. Do you trust it?
Idea. Not much. A small sample can give an unusual result by chance.
2. A journal prints only positive results. What is the problem?
Idea. We never see the studies that found nothing, so the literature looks stronger than it is.
3. A researcher tries three different analyses and reports one. Is this a problem?
Idea. Yes, if he does not say it. More tests give more chances for a false result.
4. A repeat found no effect. Does this prove the first study was false?
Idea. No. But it is a reason for care. Another repeat can help.
5. A study was preregistered. Does that make it true?
Idea. No, but it makes the plan clear, so it is harder to change the question after seeing the data.
