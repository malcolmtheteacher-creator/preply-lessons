The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A Scottish prince in England

David was the youngest son of Malcolm III and Margaret. After 1093 he grew up mostly at the English court of Henry I, who married his sister Matilda. Through marriage David became Earl of Huntingdon, a great English lord. He learned Norman ways of government.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did David grow up?

2. What did he learn there?

1. Mostly at the English court of Henry I.

2. Norman ways of government.

Discuss:
  • How does where we grow up shape how we lead?

Part 2 — The revolution

David became king in 1124. Towns called burghs were founded, with rights to trade. Scotland's first royal coins were struck. Sheriffs were appointed, and land was granted to Norman and French knights by charter.

Great abbeys were founded or supported — Holyrood, Melrose, Kelso, Jedburgh and Dryburgh among them.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were burghs?

2. Name two abbeys linked to David.

1. Towns with special trading rights.

2. Any two of Holyrood, Melrose, Kelso, Jedburgh, Dryburgh.

Discuss:
  • Why would coins and towns make a king stronger?

Part 3 — The "sore saint"

In 1138 David invaded England during the civil war between Stephen and Matilda, his niece. He was defeated at the Battle of the Standard, but he gained Northumbria and Cumbria. He died at Carlisle in 1153.

Nearly three centuries later King James I reportedly called him "a sore saint for the crown", because he gave so much land to the church. He was never officially made a saint.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened in 1138?

2. Why "sore saint"?

1. He was defeated at the Battle of the Standard, but still gained northern lands.

2. He gave so much land to the church that it cost the crown.

Discuss:
  • Can generosity be a weakness?

Grammar — The Passive Voice

Historians often care more about what was done than who did it — or they simply do not know who did it. That is the job of the passive.

How it works:
• Form: be + past participle: "The king was crowned at Westminster."
• Add by only when the doer matters: "The castle was built by Norman masons."
• Reporting passives hedge a legend: "He is said to have…", "It was believed that…"

1. Make it passive

Complete each sentence about David I the Saint. Use the word in brackets.

1. Burghs (found) across Scotland.

2. The first royal coins (strike).

3. Melrose Abbey (found) in 1136.

1. were founded

2. were struck

3. was founded

2. Choose the right passive form

1. Sheriffs (appoint) by the king.

2. Land (grant) by charter.

3. David (defeat) at the Battle of the Standard.

1. were appointed

2. was granted

3. was defeated

Going deeper

Historians call David's reign the "Davidian Revolution". Scotland gained towns, money and a new kind of government — built from ideas David learned abroad.

Worth noticing: David is often called "the Saint" in popular histories, but the church never canonised him — unlike his mother Margaret.
Learning from the past — for today: Steal good ideas. David brought back what worked elsewhere and adapted it at home. In your work, look outside your field for systems that could improve yours.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Was David right to bring in foreign knights?
  • What idea would you borrow from another country?
  • Which institution lasts longest — towns, coins or churches?
