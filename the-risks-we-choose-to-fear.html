Why Do We Fear the Wrong Things?

What scares you more: flying in an aeroplane or driving to the airport?

Most people feel more nervous about flying - yet statistically, the drive to the airport is far more dangerous. This gap between actual risk and perceived risk shapes policy, media coverage, and our daily decisions in fascinating (and frustrating) ways.

Why the Mismatch?

Psychologists have identified several factors that make us overestimate certain risks:

  • Dread factor: Invisible, uncontrollable threats (radiation) feel worse than familiar ones (cars)
  • Media coverage: Rare events get more headlines than common killers
  • Control illusion: We feel safer when we're "in control" (driving) even when we're not
  • Availability bias: If we can picture it vividly (plane crash), we think it's common

Dr. David Ropeik, Harvard Risk Analyst: "We have a risk perception system that evolved to keep us alive on the savannah, not to evaluate nuclear statistics. Our brains are magnificent - but they're not calculators."

Ingrid, 61, former safety inspector: "I spent 30 years explaining that coal plants release more radiation into the environment than nuclear plants. People would look at me like I was lying. The word 'nuclear' just triggers something deeper than logic."

If you've ever tried to explain real vs perceived risk to someone, you know how difficult it is. Why don't facts change minds?

Vocabulary: Talking About Risk and Probability

Click each card to reveal the meaning and practice.

1. to overestimate / underestimate (a risk)

Click to reveal
Meaning
To think something is more/less likely or dangerous than it really is
Form
overestimate / underestimate + noun (the risk, the danger, the probability)
Note
Often used with "tend to" - "People tend to overestimate..."

Examples:

"The public consistently overestimates the risk of nuclear accidents."

"We massively underestimate everyday dangers like poor diet."

NOW YOU TRY: What's a risk that people in your experience tend to overestimate? And one they underestimate?

2. statistically speaking

Click to reveal
Meaning
According to the numbers/data (often contrasts with how things feel)
Form
Sentence adverb - usually at the start: "Statistically speaking, ..."
Note
Often introduces a surprising or counterintuitive fact

Examples:

"Statistically speaking, you're safer in a nuclear plant than in your own bathroom."

"Statistically speaking, the most dangerous part of flying is the taxi ride."

NOW YOU TRY: Complete this: "Statistically speaking, _____________ is much safer/more dangerous than people think."

3. perceived risk vs actual risk

Click to reveal
Meaning
How dangerous something feels vs how dangerous it really is
Form
Often used with "gap between" - "the gap between perceived and actual risk"
Note
Key concept in risk communication - technical but widely understood

Examples:

"There's an enormous gap between perceived risk and actual risk when it comes to flying."

"My job was essentially bridging the gap between perceived and actual risk."

NOW YOU TRY: In your professional experience, where was the gap between perceived and actual risk most frustrating to deal with?

4. to put something into perspective

Click to reveal
Meaning
To compare something with other things so people understand its true scale/importance
Form
put + something + into perspective / "To put this into perspective, ..."
Note
Very useful for presentations, explanations, calming fears

Examples:

"To put this into perspective, more people die from falling out of bed than from nuclear accidents."

"Let me put the numbers into perspective for you."

NOW YOU TRY: Choose a risk people worry about and put it into perspective by comparing it to something else.

5. a knee-jerk reaction

Click to reveal
Meaning
An immediate, emotional response without thinking carefully - often leading to bad decisions
Form
a knee-jerk reaction (to something) - often negative connotation
Note
From the medical reflex test - your knee "jerks" automatically when tapped

Examples:

"Shutting down all nuclear plants after Fukushima was a knee-jerk reaction."

"We need policy based on evidence, not knee-jerk reactions to headlines."

NOW YOU TRY: Describe a knee-jerk reaction (by governments, companies, or individuals) that you think made things worse, not better.
