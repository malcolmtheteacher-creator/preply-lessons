Now — the words for today's story

1. Stretching from one edge of the bay to the other, the armada blocked out the sea itself — rank upon rank of warships, more than any port had ever seen gathered in one place.
an armada (n) — a large fleet of warships, especially one sent to attack an enemy.
2. By dawn the whole fleet was under sail — over a hundred ships moving together in a single great column, their captains taking orders from one flagship at the front.
a fleet (n) — a group of ships sailing or operating together under one command.
3. The king's plan was an invasion: land an army on the enemy's shore, march on the capital, and force the queen from her throne.
an invasion (n) — an act of entering a country by force, usually with an army, to take control of it.
4. The galleon sat high and heavy on the water, four decks tall, its sides bristling with cannon and its hold crammed with soldiers ready to board the enemy.
a galleon (n) — a large, heavy sailing warship of the 1500s and 1600s, used in battle and for carrying treasure.
5. The defenders lined the walls and swore that, whatever it cost them, they would repel the attackers and not let a single one over the gate.
to repel (v) — to drive back or force away an attack or an enemy.
6. On the highest hill stood a beacon — a great heap of dry wood ready to be lit the instant danger appeared, so that the next hill could light its own and pass the warning on.
a beacon (n) — a signal fire or light set high up to warn people or pass a message across a distance.
7. Under cover of darkness they filled eight old boats with tar and powder, set them alight, and let the fireships drift down the wind straight into the crowded enemy anchorage.
fireships (n) — boats deliberately set on fire and steered or floated into an enemy fleet to burn and scatter it.
8. The calm did not last. Within an hour a gale had risen, ripping at the sails, throwing spray over the decks and driving the ships helplessly toward the rocks.
a gale (n) — a very strong, dangerous wind; a storm at sea.
9. The first explosion broke their order completely: ships cut their ropes and ran in every direction, and within minutes the tight formation had scattered across miles of open water.
to scatter (v) — to break apart and spread out in many directions, especially in panic or confusion.
10. As long as they kept the wind behind them and stayed beyond the enemy's reach, the smaller, faster ships held the upper hand and could strike whenever they chose.

The story, in four parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A king's holy mission

By the 1580s, two powers eyed each other across the sea with open hostility. On one side stood Philip II of Spain, ruler of the richest empire the world had ever known, a devout Catholic who believed God himself wanted England returned to the old faith. On the other stood his rival, Elizabeth I of England — Protestant, unmarried, and, in Philip's eyes, a heretic who had no right to her throne. English sailors like Francis Drake were plundering Spanish treasure ships in the Americas, and England was helping Dutch rebels fight Spanish rule. To Philip, the answer was simple: remove Elizabeth, and the problem ended.

So he planned the grandest invasion of the age. A vast fleet — an armada of around 130 ships — would sail from Spain up the English Channel to the coast of Flanders. There it would meet the Duke of Parma and his battle-hardened army, ferry those soldiers across the narrow sea, and land them on English soil to march on London. Philip was so certain of God's favour that he called it "the Enterprise of England". Unless something went badly wrong, he believed, nothing on earth could stop it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Philip II want to remove Elizabeth I from her throne?

2. What were the two halves of Philip's plan — what was the fleet supposed to do once it reached Flanders?

1. She was Protestant where he was Catholic; he saw her as a heretic, English sailors were robbing his treasure ships, and England was helping Dutch rebels against him.

2. The fleet would sail to Flanders, meet the Duke of Parma's army, carry those soldiers across the Channel, and land them in England to march on London.

Discuss:
  • Philip believed God was on his side and victory was certain. How does that kind of total confidence change the way a leader plans — for better or worse?

Part 2 — The watch on the coast

England knew it was coming. All along the southern coast, chains of beacons had been built on the hilltops, heaps of dry wood ready to be lit the moment the Armada was sighted, so that warning could leap from hill to hill to London in a single night. The English navy, smaller than Spain's but quicker and lower in the water, gathered at Plymouth under Lord Howard of Effingham, with the famous Francis Drake as his second-in-command. On land, Queen Elizabeth rode to her troops at Tilbury and told them that, though she had "the body of a weak and feeble woman", she had "the heart and stomach of a king" — and that she would live or die among them.

In July 1588 the Armada appeared off the south-western tip of England, sailing in a tight, disciplined crescent that the English ships could not break. For more than a week the two fleets fought running battles up the Channel. The Spanish galleons were huge, packed with soldiers, and deadly if they could grapple an enemy ship and board it. But the English refused to come close. Provided they kept their distance and used their faster ships and longer-range guns, they could sting the Armada again and again without ever being caught. Neither side could land a knockout blow — and the Spanish crescent sailed on toward Flanders, still unbroken.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were the beacons for, and why were they placed on hilltops?

2. Why did the English ships refuse to come close to the Spanish galleons?

1. They were warning fires; lit on hilltops, each could be seen from the next, so the alarm could pass quickly across the country to London.

2. The galleons were huge and full of soldiers and lethal if they could board an English ship; staying at a distance let the faster English ships use their longer-range guns safely.

Discuss:
  • The English were outgunned but faster and harder to catch. When is being smaller and quicker an advantage rather than a weakness?

Part 3 — The night of fire

The Armada anchored off Calais, near the French coast, to wait for the Duke of Parma's army to come out and join it. But Parma was not ready — his boats were not loaded, and the shallow waters were blockaded by Dutch ships. The great fleet sat at anchor in a tight, crowded mass, exactly where the English wanted it. Unless Parma appeared quickly, the Armada was dangerously exposed.

On the night of 7 August, the English struck. They filled eight old ships with tar, pitch and gunpowder, set them ablaze, and let the fireships drift on the tide straight into the packed Spanish anchorage. The Spanish captains panicked. Terrified that the burning hulks might be floating bombs, most cut their anchor cables and fled into the darkness — and in doing so, the perfect crescent formation that had protected them for a week finally scattered. By morning the Armada was spread out and broken, and the English fell on it off Gravelines. In a long, fierce battle the smaller English ships now held the upper hand, sinking and crippling Spanish galleons that could no longer protect one another.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the Armada in such a vulnerable position while it waited off Calais?

2. What did the fireships actually achieve — what did they cause the Spanish to do?

1. Parma's army wasn't ready and Dutch ships blocked the shallow water, so the fleet sat crowded at anchor with nowhere to go, exposed to attack.

2. Fearing the burning ships were bombs, the Spanish cut their anchor ropes and scattered in panic, breaking the tight crescent that had kept them safe — letting the English attack them off Gravelines.

Discuss:
  • The fireships did little direct damage — but the fear they caused did enormous damage. Why can the threat of something be more powerful than the thing itself?

Part 4 — The Protestant Wind

Battered but mostly afloat, the Armada now faced an impossible choice. The wind had turned, pushing the ships north into the North Sea, and the English fleet sat behind them, blocking any return down the Channel. Medina Sidonia — the duke Philip had put in command, a brave man but no sailor — made the only decision left to him. The fleet would flee the long way home: north around the top of Scotland, then down the wild Atlantic coast of Ireland, and back to Spain. As long as the weather held, some of them might make it.

The weather did not hold. Fierce autumn gales — later remembered in England as the "Protestant Wind", as though Heaven itself had taken Elizabeth's side — caught the exhausted, leaking fleet on those unfamiliar coasts. Ship after ship was driven onto the rocks of Scotland and Ireland and smashed to pieces; thousands of men drowned or were killed as they struggled ashore. Of the roughly 130 ships that had sailed so proudly from Spain, only about 65 limped home, many of the survivors sick and starving. The invasion had failed completely. Philip's "Enterprise of England" was over, and England — against all the odds — had survived. The story has echoed down the centuries ever since, as the moment a small island navy turned back the most powerful empire on earth.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the Armada try to escape by sailing all the way around Scotland and Ireland?

2. What was the "Protestant Wind", and what did it do to the fleet?

1. The wind had pushed them north and the English fleet blocked the way back down the Channel, so the only route home was the long way round, north of Scotland and west of Ireland.

2. The name England gave the violent autumn gales that wrecked many Spanish ships on the coasts of Scotland and Ireland; it destroyed much of the fleet, so only about half returned to Spain.

Discuss:
  • The English said the storm proved God was on their side. When something lucky goes our way, why are people so quick to believe it was meant to be?

Grammar — Conditional Linkers (unless / as long as / provided that)

This whole story hangs on conditions: if the wind turned, if Parma was ready, if the English kept their distance. English has a set of precise little linkers that let you say exactly which condition you mean — and getting the right one changes the whole meaning. These are the words that decide whether the plan floats or sinks.

The five linkers, in one line each:
• unless = if … not — the one thing that would stop it: "Unless Parma came quickly, the Armada was trapped." (= if Parma did not come…)
• as long as / provided (that) / on condition that = only if — the requirement that must be met: "England was safe as long as the navy held the Channel." / "They could win provided that they kept their distance." / "Philip would help the rebels on condition that they accepted his terms."
• in case = a precaution, done in advance in preparation for a possible problem: "The beacons were ready in case the Spanish landed." (= prepared beforehand, just to be safe)
• supposing (that) = imagine if — used to raise a hypothetical: "Supposing the wind had not turned, would Spain have won?"
Two traps to avoid:
• unless already contains the "not" — don't add another: say "Unless the wind changed…", not "Unless the wind didn't change…".
• in case ≠ if. "Take an umbrella in case it rains" means take it now, as a precaution. "Take an umbrella if it rains" makes no sense — you can't decide once you're already wet.

1. Which linker fits the meaning?

Choose unless, as long as, provided that, in case or supposing. (More than one may be possible — be ready to say why yours works.)

1. The Armada was trapped the wind changed in its favour.

2. The English could keep stinging the fleet they stayed out of boarding range.

3. The beacons were kept ready on the hilltops the Spanish appeared in the night.

4. Parma had been ready on time, the invasion might have succeeded.

1. unless (= if the wind did not change)

2. as long as / provided that (= only if they stayed out of range)

3. in case (a precaution, prepared in advance)

4. Supposing (raising a hypothetical: imagine if…)

2. Rewrite with unless

Replace "if … not" with a single clean unless. Remember: unless already means "if not", so the verb stays positive.

1. If the fireships did not scatter the fleet, the English could not win. →

2. If the weather did not stay calm, the ships would be wrecked. →

3. If Spain did not control the Channel, no army could cross. →

1. Unless the fireships scattered the fleet, the English could not win.

2. Unless the weather stayed calm, the ships would be wrecked.

3. Unless Spain controlled the Channel, no army could cross.

3. Put it together — narrate with conditions

Fill each gap with the linker the meaning needs. Read the finished paragraph aloud.

Philip believed nothing could stop him God turned against him. His captains knew the plan would work Parma's army was waiting and ready. England, for its part, lit beacons along the coast the enemy came by night, and the navy could hold the Channel it kept its faster ships beyond Spanish reach. the great storm had never risen, the outcome might have been very different.

unless (= if God did not turn against him) · provided that / as long as (= only if Parma was ready) · in case (a precaution against a night attack) · as long as / provided that (= only if it kept its distance) · Supposing (raising the hypothetical)

Notice: "unless" carries the "not"; "provided that / as long as" set a requirement; "in case" is a precaution taken in advance; "supposing" opens an imagined possibility.

Hold on to these linkers — you'll need them in the Speak tab, where so much of the story comes down to "this would have happened, but only if…".

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Philip was certain God was on his side. Did that certainty help him plan well, or blind him to what could go wrong?
  2. The English called the storm the "Protestant Wind", as if Heaven had chosen them. Is it healthy for a nation to believe its luck is a sign of being right?
  3. The Armada is remembered as a glorious English victory — but much of it was wind and weather. Should we celebrate a win that owed so much to chance?
