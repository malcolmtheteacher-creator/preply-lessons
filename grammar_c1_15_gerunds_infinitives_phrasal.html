Look closely at what Tom says. He says "I saw him leaving the office" — not "I saw him leave the office." Why might that tiny difference matter? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
"Leaving" (-ing) suggests Tom only glimpsed part of the action — Marcus mid-way through walking out, at five, "like normal." That's exactly why it didn't strike Tom as unusual: a glimpse in progress doesn't tell you the whole story. If Tom had watched Marcus clear his desk, hand in his keys and walk out for good, he'd say "I saw him leave" — the complete action, start to finish. That tiny choice — one word ending in -ing or not — carries real information, and native speakers make it instinctively.

Placing It Earlier: "seems to have..." / "admits having..."

For talking about something that happened before another point in time — before now, or before you found out.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday remarks:

  • "He seems to have forgotten our meeting completely."
  • "She admits having made a mistake with the invoice."
  • "They claim to have already paid the deposit."

Before you check: what do all three bold phrases have in common, and what time are they really pointing to?

Check the pattern CLICK
Each one has two parts: have + past participle, either after "to" (perfect infinitive) or after "-ing" (perfect gerund — technically, when "having" combines with a past participle like this, it's a perfect participle functioning as a gerund). Both point to something that happened earlier than the main verb — before he seemed it, before she admitted it, before they claimed it.
seem / appear / claim / pretend + to have + past participle
admit / deny / remember / regret + having + past participle

From two separate moments to one sentence

"He lost his job. Now he looks stressed."
→
"He seems to have lost his job."

The losing happened first; the "seeming" is now. "To have lost" places the action clearly before the moment you're speaking.

What's Done to You, and What You Catch Someone Doing

Two more choices that native speakers make automatically — passive non-finite forms, and how much of an action you actually witnessed.

Spot the pattern: being done / to be done

Three everyday complaints and updates:

  • "I can't stand being told what to do."
  • "The parcel's supposed to be delivered before noon."
  • "Nobody likes being kept waiting."

What's the structure this time — and who's doing the action?

Check the pattern CLICK
being + past participle (passive gerund), or to be + past participle (passive infinitive). Notice nobody says who tells you, delivers the parcel, or keeps you waiting — the focus is entirely on what happens to the subject.
being + past participle
to be + past participle
"Someone kept us waiting for an hour. We hated it."
→
"We hated being kept waiting for an hour."

Spot the pattern: seeing the whole thing, or catching a moment

Three things people noticed:

  • "I watched her get into the car and drive off."
  • "As I walked past, I noticed him crossing the road."
  • "They caught him copying his neighbour's answers during the test."

Two of these use the bare verb, one always uses -ing. What's the difference in meaning?

Check the pattern CLICK
After see / watch / hear / notice + object, the bare infinitive ("get," "drive") reports the whole action, start to finish. The -ing form ("crossing") reports only part of it — you caught a moment in progress. After catch or find + object, it's always -ing — you're describing discovering someone in the act.
see / watch / hear / notice + object + infinitive (no "to") → the whole action
see / watch / hear / notice + object + verb-ing → part of it, in progress
catch / find + object + verb-ing → discovered someone in the act

Phrases That Do a Lot of Work

Four phrasal verbs that sound completely natural in conversation — and would sound stiff if you replaced them with their one-word equivalents.

Spot the pattern

Three things you might say to a friend:

  • "Honestly, I don't know how she puts up with him — he's always late."
  • "In the end, it'll all come down to the flight prices."
  • "He's always saying he'll fix the shelf, but he never actually gets around to it."

Try saying each one with a single formal verb instead — tolerate, depend on, finally do. Does it still sound like something you'd say to a friend?

Check the pattern CLICK
Probably not — "she tolerates him" or "it'll depend on the flight prices" are correct, but they sound like a report, not a conversation. These fixed, multi-word verbs are what a native speaker actually reaches for. And notice: after get around to, "to" behaves like a preposition, not an infinitive marker — so it's followed by a gerund: get around to fixing, never "get around to fix."
put up with + noun / -ing = tolerate
come down to + noun / -ing = the real deciding factor is
do away with + noun = get rid of, abolish
get around to + -ing = finally find time for, after a delay
Register note: in speech, all four sound completely natural. In a formal report you might switch to tolerate, essentially depend on, abolish/eliminate, or simply finally do — but in a real conversation, using the formal version usually sounds odd, not impressive.

Compare with the answer CLICK
"I stopped to smoke" means I paused what I was doing in order to have a cigarette (stop + to-infinitive = purpose). "I stopped smoking" means I quit the habit (stop + -ing = the activity that ended). Same two words, opposite kind of meaning — a classic trap.
