The Stories We Agree On

A river that is a person. A company that cannot be touched. A ten-euro note that is only paper. How humans built a world out of things that exist only because we agree they do.

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. In the eyes of the law, a company is a separate entity: it can own buildings, sign contracts and be taken to court, even though nobody can see it, hear it or shake its hand.
entity (n) — something that exists as a separate unit, especially an organisation treated as one body by the law. Example: "The charity is a legal entity in its own right."
2. A river cannot speak, attend a meeting or sign a paper, so the law appoints a guardian to act and speak on its behalf, just as it would for a young child.
guardian (n) — a person with the responsibility to protect someone or something and to speak for them. Example: "The court named her uncle as the child's guardian."
3. After more than a century of petitions, courtrooms and negotiations, parliament finally granted the river the same legal status as a person.
grant (v) — to give or allow something officially, especially a right or permission. Example: "The government granted the town the right to hold a market."
4. Because the company is a separate person, its owners are not liable for its debts: the law will not force them to pay out of their own pockets.
liable (adj) — legally responsible for something, especially for paying for damage or debts. Example: "Drivers are liable for any damage they cause."
5. When the boot company collapsed, its creditors — the shops, suppliers and lenders who were still owed money — rushed to claim whatever was left.
creditor (n) — a person or organisation that someone owes money to. Example: "The creditors met to decide what to do about the unpaid bills."
6. A mountain is tangible: you can climb it, touch it and photograph it. A company is not: it exists only in documents, names and agreements.
tangible (adj) — real enough to be touched; physical and clearly present. Example: "The tangible results of the project were three new schools."
7. Lawyers call a company a legal fiction: something the law treats as real, even though everybody knows it was invented.
fiction (n) — something invented or imagined rather than real. Example: "The idea that the company 'wants' anything is a useful fiction."
8. Money works only because of consensus: millions of strangers quietly agree that these small pieces of paper have value, and nobody has to force them.
consensus (n) — general agreement among a group of people. Example: "After a long meeting, the committee reached a consensus."
9. Banks, courts and parliaments are institutions: lasting organisations with rules that continue to work long after any one person in them has gone.
institution (n) — a large, established organisation or system with its own rules, such as a bank, a court or a university. Example: "The university is one of the oldest institutions in the country."
10. Most of us take it for granted that a ten-euro note will buy a sandwich; we almost never stop to wonder why a stranger would accept it.
take for granted (phr v) — to expect something to always be there, without thinking about it or valuing it. Example: "She took her good health for granted until she became ill."

The story, in three parts

Part 1 — A river with rights

In March 2017, the parliament of New Zealand passed a law that sounds like a joke until you read it slowly: the Whanganui River, one of the longest in the country, had become a legal person. For the Māori people of the region, the Whanganui iwi, the river is not a resource but an ancestor. Their saying is "I am the river, and the river is me". For more than a century they had argued, in petitions, in courtrooms and at negotiating tables, that the law should treat it that way.

The new law gave the river the rights, powers and duties of a person, which immediately created a practical problem: a river cannot attend a meeting, sign a document or instruct a lawyer. So the law created two guardians, one chosen by the iwi and one by the government, whose task is to speak and act for the river and to protect its health. In effect, those two people are the river's voice.

The idea was less strange than it first appeared. Courts and parliaments had been inventing persons for centuries, and the river was only the newest in a long line. In the same year, a court in India made a similar ruling about the Ganges, although a higher court later put the decision on hold.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the law need to create guardians for the river?

2. What did the Whanganui iwi mean when they said the river was an ancestor?

1. A river cannot speak, sign papers or hire a lawyer, so two guardians, one chosen by the iwi and one by the government, speak and act for it.

2. That the river is part of the family and has a life of its own, not just a resource to be used. They say: "I am the river, and the river is me."

Discuss:
  • Should a river, a forest or a mountain have legal rights? If so, who should speak for it — and who decides?

Part 2 — A bootmaker and his company

The oldest and most powerful of these invented persons is the company. In the 1890s a London leather merchant called Aron Salomon made boots for a living. In 1892 he did what many businessmen were beginning to do: he turned his business into a limited company. The company bought the business from him, and Salomon and members of his family became its shareholders. He also lent the company money and, in return, became one of its most important creditors.

Within a few years the business was in trouble, and in 1893 the company collapsed, owing money to many people. The official in charge of the collapse, known as the liquidator, argued that the company was nothing but Salomon in disguise, and that Salomon should therefore pay its debts himself. The lower courts accepted much of this argument. But in 1897 the House of Lords, at that time Britain's highest court, ruled unanimously the other way. Once a company has been properly created, the judges said, it is a person in its own right, separate from the men and women who own it, and its debts are its own.

That decision shaped the modern world. A company can own property, borrow money, sign contracts and be sued. It can live for centuries, long after every founder has died. And, crucially, when it fails, the owners usually lose only what they put in. This protection, called limited liability, is why strangers are willing to hand their savings to businesses they will never visit. Yet a company has no body, no face and no hands. It is a fiction — one that millions of people rely on every day.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the House of Lords decide in 1897, and who benefited?

2. Why does limited liability make people more willing to invest in a company?

1. It decided that a company is a legal person, separate from its owners, so its debts belong to the company, not to Salomon. Owners of companies benefited, because they were no longer personally responsible for the debts.

2. If the business fails, they usually lose only the money they put in, not everything they own, so the risk is limited and strangers are more willing to take part.

Discuss:
  • A company is a legal person. Should it have some of the same rights as a human being, such as the right to speak freely? Where would you draw the line?

Part 3 — The number 150

Why do human societies need so many invented persons and rules? One well-known answer begins with the British anthropologist Robin Dunbar, who in the 1990s compared the brain sizes of primates with the sizes of their social groups. He suggested that humans can keep track of roughly 150 relationships at a time, about the size of a small village or a hunting band. In a group that small, gossip is enough to keep order: everyone knows who is honest, who is lazy and who cannot be trusted.

A modern country has millions of citizens, and you must cooperate with almost all of them without ever meeting them. The Israeli historian Yuval Noah Harari argues, in his bestselling book Sapiens, that we manage this because we believe the same stories, about money, companies, laws, nations and human rights. None of these exists in the physical world; you will not find a nation by digging in the ground. They exist because enough people agree that they exist, and behave as if they were real.

A ten-euro note shows how it works. It is a small piece of coloured paper that you cannot eat or burn for warmth. Yet a stranger will give you a sandwich for it, because she knows that the baker will accept it, and the baker knows that the farmer will. If everyone stopped believing, the paper would stay exactly the same, and its value would vanish. A shared story lasts only as long as the sharing does.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is Dunbar's number, and why does it matter for this story?

2. Why is a ten-euro note a good example of a shared story?

1. About 150: the number of people we can know well. In bigger groups gossip is not enough, so societies need shared stories, rules and institutions that let strangers trust each other.

2. It is only paper, but it has value because everyone believes that everyone else will accept it. If the belief disappeared, the value would disappear with it.

Discuss:
  • Think of a shared story that is changing in your lifetime — a kind of money, a brand, a rule, a tradition. Why are people starting to believe in it, or to stop?

The grammar this story runs on: as if and as though

A river is treated as a person, a company as a body that can own things, a piece of paper as a sandwich. English has a pair of expressions for exactly this: treating or describing something in a way that does not match reality. They are as if and as though (identical in meaning; as though is slightly more formal).

1 · Unreal: use a past tense, even when you mean now
• as if + past simple → unreal about now: "The law treats the river as if it were a person." ("it is not really a person"; were is the formal form after as if, although was is common in speech.)
• as if + past perfect → unreal about the past: "Salomon's creditors spoke as though he had borrowed the money himself." ("but he hadn't; the company had")
2 · Real: use a normal present tense when it may well be true
• "It looks as if it is going to rain." (the clouds are real evidence) · "You look as if you haven't slept." (probably true)
• Compare: "He talks as if he owned the place." (he doesn't) and "He talks as if he owns the place." (maybe he does).
So the tense tells the listener whether you believe it: past form = not true; present form = possibly true.

1. Put the verb in the correct form

Each sentence describes something that is not true. Choose the right unreal form.

1. The law treats the river as if it (be) a person.

2. A ten-euro note is only paper, but people behave as though it (have) value in itself.

3. The creditors argued as though Salomon himself (borrow) the money, although it was the company that had done so.

4. He has never worked in a bank, but he explains the crash as if he (be) there.

1. were (or was) (unreal present: past form)

2. had (unreal present: past form)

3. had borrowed (unreal past: past perfect)

4. had been (unreal past: he wasn't there)

2. Real or unreal? Choose a, b or c

Is the speaker describing something that could be true, or something that is not?

1. The sky is black and the wind is rising. It looks as if it ___ soon.
  (a) rained   (b) is going to rain   (c) had rained
(b) — the clouds are real evidence, so the speaker thinks it may be true: present form.
2. He has never been to Paris, but he talks as though he ___ there for years.
  (a) had lived   (b) lives   (c) will live
(a) — he hasn't, so it is unreal about the past: past perfect.
3. "Does a company have a body?" "No, but the law treats it ___ it were a person."
  (a) like if   (b) as if   (c) as
(b) — as if introduces the unreal comparison. Like if is wrong, and plain as does not work before it were.
4. This ending feels familiar. I feel as if I ___ this film before.
  (a) saw   (b) would see   (c) have seen
(c) — the speaker thinks it may well be true, so a normal present perfect.

3. Say it with as if — write your answer first

Rewrite each idea in one sentence with as if or as though.

1. A ten-euro note is only paper, but people treat it as something valuable in itself.

2. A river is not a person, but the law gives it the rights of one.

3. Salomon and his company were different persons, but the creditors argued they were one.

1. People treat a ten-euro note as if it were valuable in itself.

2. The law gives the river the rights of a person as though it were one. / The law treats the river as if it were a person and gives it a person's rights.

3. The creditors argued as though Salomon and the company were one and the same person.

Check: after as if / as though, did you use were (or another past form) for something that is not true now?
