Strategic Storytelling

One Story, Three Rooms

The story doesn't change. The telling does.

Think and write

Think of something you need to communicate to three different groups — your team, your board, your clients. How would you tell the same story differently for each? What changes?

Today: We're learning how to adapt the same core story for different audiences without losing authenticity.

Here's the core tension: you want to be consistent. You want to tell the truth. And you also want every audience to care about the truth the way you do. But they don't. They bring different knowledge, different fears, different needs to the conversation.

A company turnaround story told to investors is about ROI and timeline. Told to employees, it's about job security and purpose. Told to customers, it's about continuity and trust. Same core facts. Three completely different tellings.

The question isn't whether to adapt — you have to. The question is whether you can adapt without deceiving. And that requires understanding what each audience knows, fears, wants, and needs to do.

The Audience Empathy Map

Four questions that shape how you tell the same story.

For any audience, ask yourself four things:

What do they KNOW?

Prior knowledge. What can I assume they already understand? Where do I need to start?

What do they FEAR?

Hidden concerns. What's the unstated worry in the room? What are they anxious about?

What do they WANT?

Desired outcome. What's their measure of success? What would make them say "I'm satisfied"?

What will they DO?

Action required. What do you need from them? What decision or commitment are you asking for?

The same story, three different tellings

Scenario: A company went through a major turnaround — restructured, cut costs, focused on high-margin products. The turnaround worked. Now the CEO needs to tell this story to: investors, employees, and customers. Same facts. Different story.

For Investors

What they KNOW: We were losing money. They know restructuring happened.

What they FEAR: That the changes are temporary. That we're just cutting our way out.

What they WANT: Clear ROI. Evidence that we're profitable and will stay profitable.

What they'll DO: Decide whether to increase their investment or cut losses.

The story: "We were burning cash. We made hard choices. The data shows we're now profitable on a smaller revenue base, with higher margins. This proves the business model works. Here's the ROI timeline."

For Employees

What they KNOW: Some people lost their jobs. Things changed.

What they FEAR: More cuts are coming. Their jobs aren't safe. The company might fail anyway.

What they WANT: Job security. A sense that the hard part is over. Purpose.

What they'll DO: Decide whether to stay and rebuild, or leave for a safer ship.

The story: "We made hard cuts that were painful and necessary. That part is behind us. Now we're focusing on deepening what we do best. We're stable. We're hiring in growth areas. We're here for the long term, and we need you to help us rebuild it."

For Customers

What they KNOW: Something changed. Maybe they noticed service shifts.

What they FEAR: We're going out of business. Our service will degrade. We don't care about them anymore.

What they WANT: Continuity. That we're the same reliable company they chose.

What they'll DO: Decide whether to stay or explore alternatives.

The story: "We've refined what we do. We eliminated the parts that didn't matter to you and doubled down on what does. Your experience is better, not different. We're stronger and more focused. Your partnership matters more than ever."

The key: The CORE doesn't change (the company restructured and is now profitable). The FRAMING changes based on what each audience knows, fears, and needs. All three versions are true. But they lead with different truths.

The Authenticity Test

The difference between adapting and deceiving.

The critical question

If audience A and audience B compared notes after your presentations, would they feel you told them different things, or the same thing differently?

If the answer is "the same thing differently" — you've adapted. If the answer is "different things" — you've started deceiving.

Adapting: Same core facts, different framing. Investors hear "we're profitable," employees hear "we're stable," customers hear "we're focused." All true. All emphasized differently based on what they care about.

Deceiving: Telling investors "we're growing" while telling employees "we're cutting costs" — those are contradictory stories, not adapted ones.

The teach-back

Explain the difference between adapting and deceiving — in about 60 seconds. Then connect it to Lesson 8's ethics framework: are you adding a perspective the listener needs, or removing one they deserve?

3. Which quadrant of the empathy map is most often forgotten when people prepare presentations?

1. What do they KNOW? / What do they FEAR? / What do they WANT? / What will they DO?
