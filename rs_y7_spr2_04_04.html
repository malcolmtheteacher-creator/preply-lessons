Today's Big Idea

Imagine worship that uses ALL FIVE senses at once — sight, sound, smell, taste, touch. What would that feel like? What might it help you experience? That's what Hindu puja is. It's not just words or thoughts — it's a full sensory experience of connecting with the divine.

Puja: Hindu Worship

What is Puja?

Puja is worship in Hinduism. For most Hindu families, puja happens at home — not in temples. Many Hindu homes have a shrine with murtis (sacred statues or images) of deities they have chosen to worship. Every day, family members perform puja.

Home Puja: Step by Step

1. Ring the Bell

The worshipper rings a bell to alert the deity that someone is coming to worship. It also focuses the mind on the present moment.

2. Light the Diya

A diya (oil lamp) is lit before the murti. The flame represents knowledge and the removal of darkness.

3. Make Offerings

Flowers, fruit, and food are offered to the murti. These show respect and gratitude to the deity.

4. Wave Incense

Incense is lit and waved before the murti. The smoke carries prayers upward and fills the space with fragrance.

5. Chant Mantras

Sacred words (mantras) are chanted or sung. Bhajans (devotional songs) may also be sung to express love for the deity.

6. Apply Tilak

A sacred mark is applied to the forehead using ash, sandalwood paste, or coloured powder. This shows the worshipper has been blessed.

Temple Puja (Mandir)

In a temple (mandir), puja is more elaborate and is usually led by a priest called a pujari. The murti in a temple is usually larger and more ornate. The priest washes and dresses the murti, offers food, and leads worship.

Darshan: Seeing and Being Seen

Click to reveal: What is darshan?

In Hindu theology, darshan means "seeing" — but it's a two-way thing. When you have darshan, you see the deity (through the murti), and the deity sees you. It's a moment of connection and blessing. Many Hindus visit temples specifically to have darshan. The arti ceremony (waving flames) is part of darshan — you see the light and feel the heat of the flames, connecting physically and spiritually with the divine.

Arti: Waving Flames

Arti is a key moment in temple worship. A priest (or devotee) waves a tray of burning oil lamps (or flames) in front of the murti while singing. Worshippers then pass their hands through the smoke and touch their eyes and forehead — this is seen as receiving the deity's blessing.

Prasad: The Blessed Food

Click to reveal: What happens after puja?

After puja, the food that has been offered to the murti is distributed as prasad (blessed food). By eating prasad, worshippers are believed to receive the grace of the deity. Everyone — regardless of caste or background — can eat prasad together. This is one reason puja creates community.

Murtis: Not Idols, but Windows

Common Misconception

Some people think Hindus "worship idols," as if the statue itself is a god. This misses the point. In Hindu theology, a murti is not the god itself — it's a focus for worship, like a window into the divine. Hindus believe that the divine presence can inhabit the murti, making it a point of connection. The murti is treated with respect: it's washed, dressed in fine clothes, and offered food — like welcoming an honoured guest into your home.

Puja and the Five Senses

Sight

The murti, the decorated shrine, the flames of the diya and arti, the colours of the offerings.

Sound

The bell ringing, mantras being chanted, bhajans (devotional songs) being sung.

Smell

Incense, flowers, sandalwood paste — fragrances fill the prayer space.

Taste

Prasad (blessed food) — usually fruit, sweets, or rice. Eating it is part of receiving blessing.

Touch

The tilak applied to the forehead, the warmth of the arti flames, the texture of the murti or shrine.

Key Terms

  • Puja: Hindu worship, usually performed at home or in a temple.
  • Murti: A sacred image or statue of a deity, used as a focus for worship.
  • Mandir: A Hindu temple.
  • Shrine: A sacred space or altar where worship happens.
  • Arti: A ceremony of waving flames before a murti.
  • Prasad: Food blessed by being offered to a deity, then distributed to worshippers.
  • Darshan: The experience of seeing (and being seen by) the divine through the murti.
  • Bhajan: A devotional song sung in worship.
  • Tilak: A sacred mark on the forehead, applied during or after puja.
  • Diya: An oil lamp, lit during puja.

Activity 2: Idol Worship or Divine Window?

There's often confusion about whether Hindu puja is "idol worship." Read these statements and decide: Would a Hindu agree or disagree?

Statement 1

"The murti IS the god, so we worship it as a god."

Hindu view: Disagree. The murti is a focus for worship, a way to connect with the divine, not the divine itself.

Statement 2

"The murti helps us see the divine and feel its presence in our home."

Hindu view: Agree. This is the purpose of the murti.

Statement 3

"If we break the murti, the deity is hurt or offended."

Hindu view: Not quite. The deity is not trapped in the stone or metal. But we would be disrespectful to treat a sacred object carelessly.

Evaluating the Statement

Discuss Both Sides

"Using images and statues in worship helps people connect with God."

✓ FOR (Agree)

A visual focus helps people concentrate. Many people find it easier to pray when they have something to look at. Hindu puja and Catholic worship both use images. The senses can draw us deeper into spirituality. A murti or crucifix is like a bridge between the physical and spiritual worlds.

✗ AGAINST (Disagree)

God (or the divine) is everywhere and invisible — you don't need a statue to connect. Images can distract from true spiritual practice. Muslims argue that creating images goes against God's will. Quakers and some Protestants find their deepest connection through silence and simplicity, not objects. Focusing on an image might make people worship the image instead of the divine.
