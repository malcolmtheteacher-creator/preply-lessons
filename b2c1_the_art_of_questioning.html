The Art of Asking Better Questions

B2/C1 Level · A small skill that quietly shapes whole conversations

The skill almost no one is taught

Most of us were trained, all the way through school, to be good at giving answers. Almost nobody was trained, with the same care, to ask good questions. And yet anyone who has ever run a meeting, held a real conversation, or tried to draw out someone shy knows perfectly well that the quality of any exchange is set, almost entirely, by the questions inside it. Good questioning is not a mysterious talent. It is a small, recognisable set of moves that can be learnt — and that, once learnt, quietly improves every conversation you ever have.

Speak · first reactions

Answer each in two or three full sentences. Don't rush.

  1. Who is the best question-asker you've ever met — a teacher, a colleague, a journalist, an old friend? What did they do that other people don't?
  2. In your own working day, when do you find yourself asking questions, and what's your default style — short and probing, long and open, or somewhere in between?
  3. Some people seem to ask questions because they genuinely want to learn; others to demonstrate intelligence. How can you tell, in real time, which is happening?
  4. There's a school of thought that the best question is the one nobody in the room has dared to ask. Do you find that true, or romantic?
Speak · pick a position

Pick the claim you most want to defend, and defend it for two minutes. Then pick the one you find most uncomfortable and shore it up anyway.

  • (a) "A really good question reorganises someone's thinking. A poor one just confirms what they already believe."
  • (b) "Most arguments fail not because anyone was wrong, but because the right clarifying question came too late."
  • (c) "If you can't get someone to open up, you are asking the wrong question. The skill is theirs, but the responsibility is yours."
  • (d) "The fastest way to test whether someone has understood something is to ask them a question they didn't expect."

Eight chunks to put to work

Read each card; close it; move on. The point isn't to learn them on the card — it's to use them, out loud, in the speaking work that follows.

a probing question One that pushes past the first comfortable answer and asks the person to go further.
to clarify (a point / a position) To make exact what was vague — usually before you start agreeing or disagreeing.
a leading question One that has quietly buried its preferred answer inside itself. Easy to ask by accident; expensive to leave unchallenged.
a hypothetical A question that imagines a different state of affairs — "What if…?", "Suppose for a moment that…"
a follow-up question One built on what the other person just said — rather than the next item on your own list.
to play devil's advocate To argue against a position, gently and on purpose, in order to test it. Not to attack it.
to interrogate (an idea) To examine an idea carefully and from several angles. Crucially: the idea, not the person who happens to be holding it.
wait time The deliberate silence after a question — three to five seconds — that gives the other person room to actually think.
Speak · rapid-fire drill

Your teacher reads each prompt aloud. Respond in one full sentence using at least one of the chunks. Don't write. If you stall, move on.

  1. "Describe the last time someone asked you a really good probing question. What made it good?"
  2. "When did you last catch yourself asking a leading question without meaning to?"
  3. "Tell me about a moment when wait time would have helped, but you filled it with another question instead."
  4. "Defend, in one sentence, why playing devil's advocate is worth the social cost."
  5. "Give me an example of a hypothetical you've used — or wish you'd used — in a real conversation."

One paragraph. Then we talk.

Read this aloud, slowly. It's here to give us shared ground — not to be analysed to death.

Most of our schooling trains us to give answers. Very little of it trains us to ask the right questions. And yet, as anyone who has ever sat in a serious meeting, run a discussion, or conducted an interview knows, the quality of any conversation is set, almost entirely, by the questions inside it. The good news is that good questioning is not a mysterious talent. Good questioners deploy a small, recognisable kit of moves: they clarify a vague position before they start arguing with it; they ask probing questions that push past the first comfortable answer; they use follow-up questions to build on what someone has just said instead of moving on; they offer a hypothetical to test what the other person really thinks; they play devil's advocate, gently, to see whether a position holds; and when they hear a leading question — one that has quietly buried its answer inside itself — they notice it and refuse to be steered. The discipline of all this is to interrogate the idea, not the person who happens to be holding it, and to allow enough wait time after a question that the other person actually has room to think. Practise these moves; you become someone other people want to think with — and, more importantly, someone whose own understanding deepens every time you open your mouth.

Phrasing · read aloud, pausing at every dash and semicolon. The sentence is long; chunk it.

The six-move toolkit

1. Clarify

"What exactly do you mean by X?"

"Could you give me a concrete example?"

2. Probe

"What's the evidence for that?"

"Push past the obvious answer — what's underneath?"

3. Follow up

"And what about …?"

"You said X a moment ago — say more."

4. Hypothetical

"Suppose Y weren't true — what then?"

"What would change if …?"

5. Devil's advocate

"Couldn't someone reasonably argue the opposite?"

"Steel-man the position you disagree with."

6. Spot the leading question

"That question seems to assume X — is that fair?"

"Let me strip the assumption out and ask it cleaner."

Two quick orientations before we open it out:

1. What does the writer say is the single discipline running through all six moves?

Show Answer
Interrogate the idea, not the person holding it. Every move — clarifying, probing, hypothetical, devil's advocate — works only if it is aimed at the position rather than the person. Take that discipline away and the same moves become cross-examination instead.

2. Why does the writer say that good questioning deepens YOUR understanding, not just the other person's?

Show Answer
Because the act of formulating a real question forces you to notice what you don't yet understand — what's vague, what's assumed, what's missing. The other person may answer or may not; either way, the question itself has already done useful work on the asker. Good questioners learn faster than good answerers.
Speak · press on it
  1. Of the six moves above, which do you do well already? Which do you tend to skip?
  2. The writer says you should interrogate the idea, not the person. Where, in your own life, do you see those two things get tangled together? What do you do when they do?
  3. "Practise these moves; you become someone other people want to think with." Defend or complicate that claim — is being thought-with always a good thing for the one doing the thinking?
  4. There's a real difference between questioning to UNDERSTAND and questioning to TEACH. Talk about how those differ in the way you ask, the way you wait, and the way you respond.

Indirect / embedded questions

The questions in the toolkit above are deliberately direct — that's their job. But in real conversation, especially with someone you don't yet trust or respect deeply, you want the same content delivered in a slightly softer wrapper. That's what indirect, or embedded, questions are for. Same probe; different temperature.

Politeness openers

Wrap the question inside a polite phrase.

Could you tell me what you mean by X?

I was wondering whether …

Help me understand …

I'd be curious to know …

Word order — the trap

After the polite opener, switch to statement word order. No "do / does / did".

Direct: What does this mean?

Indirect: Could you tell me what this means?  (not "what does this mean")

Yes/no questions

Use whether (more formal) or if (more conversational).

I was wondering whether there's any evidence for that.

Do you know if anyone has tested this?

Speak · oral transformation drill

Your teacher reads a direct question. You reformulate it on the spot, aloud, using the polite opener indicated. Listen for the word order. Don't write. Models hidden below each item.

1. Direct: "What does this actually mean?"   →   use Could you tell me…

Show Answer
Could you tell me what this actually means? (No "does"; statement word order: this means.)

2. Direct: "Is there any evidence for that?"   →   use I was wondering…

Show Answer
I was wondering whether there is any evidence for that.

3. Direct: "Why did you change your mind?"   →   use I'd be curious to know…

Show Answer
I'd be curious to know why you changed your mind. (No "did"; statement word order: you changed.)

4. Direct: "Where did this idea come from?"   →   use Could you explain…

Show Answer
Could you explain where this idea came from?

5. Direct: "Has anyone disagreed with this?"   →   use I'd love to know…

Show Answer
I'd love to know whether anyone has disagreed with this.

6. Direct: "How did you arrive at that?"   →   use Help me understand…

Show Answer
Help me understand how you arrived at that.
Speak · live micro-task

Think of one position you've heard recently that you didn't quite agree with. In sixty seconds, ask three indirect questions about it — using three different politeness openers. The goal: probe the position carefully without sounding hostile.

Three speaking tasks

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · the six-move drill (4 minutes)

Pick any topic you've been thinking about recently — a book, an idea, a current question in your working life. Tell your teacher what it is in one sentence. Then, working through the six question types in any order, ask one genuine question of each type about that topic. Your teacher will play someone who knows almost nothing — your job is to deepen the conversation through your questions, not to give answers yourself.

Use at least three chunks from Tab 2 and at least three indirect-question forms from Tab 4.

Task B · demolish the cliche (3 minutes)

A friend says to you, over a long lunch:

"All this 'art of questioning' stuff is overdone. Smart people just ask whatever comes into their head. Trying to apply a framework to a conversation kills the conversation."

Demolish, for three minutes, with concrete examples. Use at least one indirect question and at least one piece of devil's-advocate move embedded in your own argument.

Task C · reverse engineering (4 minutes)

Your teacher will ask you five questions in a row about something they don't yet know much about — your work, an idea you've been chewing on, a recent experience. Answer naturally. Afterwards, talk through:

  1. Which of the six moves your teacher was making at each turn.
  2. Which question made you think hardest, and why.
  3. Whether any of them were quietly leading questions — and how you handled it.
  4. Which one question you'd have asked differently in their place.

Final open discussion

  1. Where in your own work do you most need to be a better questioner? Be specific — one situation, one type of question you keep skipping.
  2. If you had to install just one questioning move in a younger colleague, which would you choose, and why that one?
  3. Some cultures and workplaces actively discourage probing questions, especially from juniors. What does that cost the organisation? What, occasionally, does it protect?
  4. Could a question be too good — sharp enough to embarrass, or to redirect a conversation against someone's will? Where, in your view, is the line between probing and pressuring?
