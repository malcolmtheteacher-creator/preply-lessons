Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. When the plague reached the port, it moved faster than any army: within weeks the markets were empty, the bells rang for the dead, and a third of the town was gone.
a plague (n) — a deadly infectious disease that spreads quickly and kills very large numbers of people.
2. The holds of the ships were crawling with vermin — you heard them gnawing in the dark, felt them run across the sacks of grain, and no amount of poison ever cleared them out.
vermin (n) — small wild animals and insects, such as rats, mice or fleas, that spread disease and are hard to get rid of.
3. The first symptom was a headache; then came the fever and the aching joints, and only later did the doctor understand which illness all of these signs pointed to.
a symptom (n) — a sign of an illness that you feel or notice in the body, such as a fever or a cough.
4. By midsummer the sickness was rampant: it raged through one quarter of the city after another, and no street was left where the bells had not already rung for the dead.
rampant (adj) — (especially of a disease or something harmful) spreading fast and freely, completely out of control.
5. Anyone arriving by ship was held apart from the town for forty days of quarantine, kept on a separate island until the officials were sure they carried no disease.
quarantine (n) — a period when people who may carry a disease are kept apart from everyone else to stop it spreading.
6. Beneath the skin of his neck rose a hard, blackened bubo, the size of an egg, hot to the touch and growing larger by the hour — the sign every household had come to dread.
a bubo (n) — a swollen, inflamed lymph gland, especially in the neck, armpit or groin, and the classic sign of bubonic plague.
7. There were too many to bury one by one, so the gravediggers dug a single wide pit and lowered each corpse into it together, layer upon layer.
a corpse (n) — a dead body, especially a human one.
8. The doctors warned everyone to stay away from the sick man, because whatever he had was deeply contagious and could pass to anyone who came near.
contagious (adj) — (of a disease) able to be passed easily from one person to another, often by touch or by air.
9. As soon as they heard the sickness was near, half the village packed what they could carry and fled into the hills, hoping distance alone might save them.
to flee (v) — to run away quickly from danger (past tense: fled).
10. Desperate for any remedy, people tried everything — strange herbs, sweet smells, prayers — searching for the one cure that might finally stop the suffering.
a remedy (n) — a treatment or cure that is meant to stop an illness or solve a problem.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A road for the disease

The catastrophe began far from Europe, somewhere in the dry grasslands of Central Asia. There, a bacterium called Yersinia pestis lived quietly in the blood of wild rodents and in the fleas that fed on them. On its own it could go nowhere fast. But the fourteenth-century world was already a connected one, stitched together by long trade routes that carried silk, spices and grain from Asia toward the markets of the Mediterranean. The same routes carried rats, and the rats carried fleas, and the fleas carried death.

By 1347 the disease had ridden these routes all the way to the Black Sea. When Genoese trading ships sailed home to Italy that autumn, the sickness sailed with them, hidden among the rats in the holds. Sailors who had seemed healthy at sea staggered ashore with fevers and dark swellings, and within days they were dead. The disease was extraordinarily contagious. It spread from the ports inland along the rivers and roads, faster than any warning could travel ahead of it. People died of an illness no one understood, and no remedy the doctors tried made the slightest difference.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did a disease that began in Central Asia reach the cities of Europe?

2. What role did rats and fleas play in carrying it?

1. It travelled west along the trade routes that connected Asia to the Mediterranean, then arrived in Italy on Genoese ships sailing home from the Black Sea in 1347.

2. The bacterium lived in fleas, the fleas lived on rats, and the rats travelled with the caravans and in the holds of trading ships — so trade carried the disease everywhere it went.

Discuss:
  • The very thing that made Europe rich — trade — was also what carried the disease. Can you think of a modern example where the same connection brings both benefit and danger?

Part 2 — The horror, and the search for someone to blame

The illness worked on the body with terrifying speed. The first symptoms were fever and exhaustion; then came the dark, painful swellings in the neck, the armpits and the groin — the "buboes" that gave the bubonic plague its name. Many who suffered from these swellings were dead within three or four days. So many died, so quickly, that the living could not keep up. Bodies lay in the streets; priests refused to come; and in city after city the dead were no longer buried one by one but tipped together into great pits, corpse upon corpse.

Across Europe, perhaps a third of all people died — modern estimates of the wider pandemic run from 75 to 200 million dead. Whole villages emptied. Those who could afford to fled to the countryside; those who could not, stayed and prayed. And in their terror, people reached for explanations. Some blamed the planets, some blamed bad air, some blamed the sins of mankind. Worse, many blamed their neighbours: communities accused outsiders of poisoning the wells, and across parts of Europe Jewish communities in particular were blamed for the outbreak and attacked, in a wave of violence built on nothing but fear. Fear led to panic, and panic resulted in cruelty.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were the "buboes," and why did the disease come to be called the bubonic plague?

2. When people could not explain the disaster, who did some of them choose to blame?

1. Buboes were the dark, painful swellings that appeared in the neck, armpits and groin; the disease was named after them.

2. Some blamed the planets, bad air, or human sin — but many blamed outsiders and minorities, and Jewish communities in particular were accused and violently attacked.

Discuss:
  • Frightened people looked for someone to blame rather than something to understand. Why do you think fear so often turns into the search for a scapegoat?

Part 3 — Forty days, and a changed world

Nobody in the 1340s knew about bacteria or fleas, yet some cities slowly learned, by hard experience, that keeping people apart could slow the dying. The Italian-speaking port of Ragusa — today Dubrovnik, in Croatia — ordered that arriving ships and travellers be held in isolation before they could enter. The waiting period was set at forty days. In Italian, forty is quaranta — and from that word comes our own word quarantine. It is one of the oldest public-health measures we still use, and we owe both the practice and the name to the plague years.

The great wave passed by about 1351, but the disease never truly disappeared; it returned again and again for centuries. And the world it left behind was not the same. So many had died that workers who survived were suddenly scarce and could demand better pay, while the old order that had bound peasant to lord began to crumble. Faith in the Church, which had been powerless against the sickness, was shaken. Out of catastrophe came change — in wages, in religion, in art, in how ordinary people saw their place in the world. Centuries later, scientists finally identified the culprit, Yersinia pestis, in the teeth of the medieval dead. The Black Death had annihilated a generation; but the society that recovered from it was already becoming something new.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where does the word "quarantine" come from, and what does it have to do with the number forty?

2. Name two ways the world changed because so many people had died.

1. The port of Ragusa (now Dubrovnik) held arrivals in isolation for forty days; "forty" in Italian is quaranta, which gives us the word "quarantine."

2. Any two of: surviving workers became scarce and could demand higher wages; the old peasant-and-lord order began to break down; faith in the Church was shaken; there were lasting changes in art, religion and society.

Discuss:
  • The disaster ended up giving ordinary workers more power and better pay. Is it true that great catastrophes can sometimes push a society forward — or is that too comforting a story to tell?

Grammar — Dependent Prepositions (die of, suffer from, result in)

Some verbs and adjectives in English come with a small word fixed to them — a preposition you simply have to learn as part of the phrase. You don't choose it by logic; you choose it because that's the partner English has settled on. This story is full of them, because telling the tale of a disease means saying what people died of, what they suffered from, what fear led to, and who got blamed for it all.

The core pairs, straight from this story:
• die of / die from — "Millions died of a disease no one understood."
• suffer from — "Those who suffered from the swellings were often dead within days."
• result in — "Panic resulted in cruelty."
• lead to — "Fear led to panic."
• blame (someone) for — "Communities blamed outsiders for the outbreak."
• responsible for — "They believed outsiders were responsible for poisoning the wells."
• immune to — "No one was immune to it — rich and poor alike fell ill."
• recover from — "The society that recovered from the plague was already changing."
• spread to — "The sickness spread to every corner of Europe."
Two traps worth flagging:
• blame takes two shapes — you blame somebody FOR something, or you blame something ON somebody ("they blamed the outbreak on outsiders"). Same idea, different preposition.
• die usually takes of or from (both are accepted for illness), but never "die by a disease."

1. Add the missing preposition

Fill each gap with the one small word the verb or adjective demands.

1. Millions of people died the plague within just a few years.

2. Anyone who suffered the swellings feared the worst.

3. The panic quickly resulted violence against innocent people.

4. Frightened communities blamed outsiders the outbreak.

5. No one, rich or poor, was immune the disease.

1. of (or from) — both are accepted with illness.

2. from — we suffer from an illness or symptom.

3. in — an action results in a consequence.

4. for — you blame somebody for something.

5. to — you are immune to a disease.

2. Choose the right partner

These verbs are easy to mix up. Choose the preposition that belongs with each one.

1. Fear led panic, and panic led cruelty.

2. The disease spread every corner of the continent.

3. The few who recovered the illness were lucky indeed.

4. People believed the planets were responsible the catastrophe.

1. to … to — something leads to a result.

2. to — a disease spreads to a place.

3. from — you recover from an illness.

4. for — you are responsible for something.

3. Put them together — narrate a paragraph

Fill each gap with the preposition the phrase needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

The plague spread Europe along the trade routes. Millions died it, and almost no one was immune the disease. Those who suffered the swellings rarely recovered them. In their fear, people blamed outsiders the outbreak, and that blame resulted terrible violence.

to (spread to) · of/from (died of) · to (immune to) · from (suffered from) · from (recovered from) · for (blamed … for) · in (resulted in)

Notice that each preposition belongs to its verb or adjective — change the partner and the phrase stops sounding like English.
