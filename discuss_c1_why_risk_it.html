Why Risk It?

Why do people choose hard, dangerous, apparently pointless things, and what do the philosophers make of it?

← All the Big Questions

Today: you'll explain why people take on dangerous, difficult challenges that seem to achieve nothing, and you'll master the structures that let you grant an opponent's point before you answer it.

The ideas, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The puzzle

Picture someone lying in cold mud, pushing through a gap narrower than a car tyre, headlamp flickering, a hundred metres of rock above. Hours later they will reach a chamber that no human being has seen. There is no gold in it. There is no audience. There is, quite possibly, a cold wait for a rescue team. Similar scenes happen on icy ridges, on open water, on race courses that last for days. Anyone watching from outside asks the same question: why?

In 1923 a journalist asked the climber George Mallory why he wanted to climb Everest. His reply, "Because it's there", has become one of the most quoted lines in the history of adventure, and one of the most frustrating, because it sounds like a refusal to answer. Mallory disappeared high on the mountain the following year, and people still argue about whether he reached the summit.

Philosophers take the reply seriously. They ask whether "because it's there" is really an evasion, or whether it is a short and honest answer to a question that has no more useful answer: some things are worth doing for their own sake, and a reason beyond them would only spoil them.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the opening scene suggest is missing from the explorer's situation?

2. Why do some philosophers take Mallory's answer seriously?

1. No treasure, no audience: nothing external to explain the effort, and a real chance of needing rescue.

2. They think it may be an honest answer: the activity is valuable in itself, and a further reason would add nothing, or even spoil it.

Discuss:
  • "Because it's there" can sound lazy or deep. Which is it for you, and what would change your mind?

Part 2 — Three serious answers

The Canadian philosopher Bernard Suits, in his 1978 book The Grasshopper, offered a definition that sounds like a joke and is not one: playing a game is "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles". Think about what that means. The shortest route up the mountain is a road. The shortest route into the cave is no route at all, if you simply stay outside. Climbers and cavers choose the harder way, accept rules that make the task difficult, and the difficulty is the point. Remove the obstacle and you have removed the game.

The French writer Albert Camus, in The Myth of Sisyphus (1942), took a different starting point. Sisyphus, in Greek myth, is condemned to push a boulder up a hill for ever, and each time it rolls back down. The task is futile. Yet Camus ends with the line "one must imagine Sisyphus happy": meaning, he argued, is found in the struggle itself, not in any final result.

The psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi gave us a third answer, in his 1990 book on what he called flow. When the challenge in front of us is just hard enough to match our skill, attention narrows, worry disappears, and hours pass like minutes. Dangerous sports, he suggested, are among the most reliable ways to reach that state, because the cost of a mistake forces total focus.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Suits define playing a game, and why does the cave example fit?

2. What do Camus and Csikszentmihalyi each say a person gains from a hard, apparently pointless task?

1. "The voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles": the cave could be left alone, so entering it is chosen difficulty, and the difficulty is the point.

2. Camus: meaning is found in the struggle itself, not the result. Csikszentmihalyi: flow, a state of total focus where challenge matches skill and worry disappears.

Discuss:
  • Suits says the obstacle is the point. Is there a hard thing in your own life that would be worthless if someone made it easy?

Part 3 — The price

None of this settles whether risk-taking is right. Aristotle, over two thousand years ago, described courage as a middle point between two vices: cowardice, which fears too much, and rashness, which fears too little. By that standard, the brave climber and the foolhardy one can look identical from a distance. The difference lies in judgement: how well they understand the danger they have chosen.

The nineteenth-century philosopher John Stuart Mill gave the strongest defence of the right to take risks. In On Liberty (1859) he argued that society may interfere with an individual against their will only to prevent harm to others. Your own life, he said, is yours to risk. But that is exactly where the difficulty begins, because in many countries mountain and cave rescue depend largely on volunteers, who may put themselves in danger to retrieve someone who chose theirs. Families, too, carry the cost of a loss.

So the question divides into two. Is a life with some risk in it a better life? Many people say yes. And who should carry the cost when risk goes wrong? That is a harder question, and an honest answer has to hold both thoughts at once.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. According to Aristotle, how can a brave person and a foolhardy one look alike?

2. Why does Mill's principle not settle the question of rescue?

1. Both do something dangerous; the difference is in judgement and understanding of the danger, between fearing too much and too little.

2. Mill allows risk to yourself, but rescuers and families may be harmed by your choice, so the risk is not purely your own.

Discuss:
  • Should people who take on extreme risks pay for their own rescue? Where would you draw the line?

Grammar — conceding a point before you answer it

In any argument about risk, the strongest speaker is the one who admits the other side's best point and still wins. English has several neat structures for that move. Used well, they make you sound fair and in control at the same moment.

Three structures:
• Granted, … / Admittedly, … — you accept a point, then answer it: "Granted, the view from the top is spectacular, but that hardly justifies the danger."
• Much as + subject + verb, … — "although I very much …": "Much as I enjoy a long hike, I would never sleep on a cliff face."
• However / No matter how + adjective (or much/many) + clause, … — "However steep the slope, the skier never hesitated." "However much you train, a cave can still surprise you."
Feel the difference:
• Granted concedes a fact, then pushes back.
• Much as concedes a feeling or attitude (I admire, I enjoy, I respect…) and is followed by a full clause.
• However puts the adjective straight after it: "however dangerous it is", never "however it is dangerous". The same order works with as / though: "Dangerous as it is, people still do it."

Whose idea is it?

Five people describe their experience. Say whose idea fits each one (Suits, Camus, Csikszentmihalyi, Aristotle or Mill) and why, then reveal.

1. "The squeeze, the cold and the dark are not in the way of the experience. They are the experience. Take them away and nothing is left."
Suits. The unnecessary obstacle is the whole point of the game.
2. "A town council asks whether a volunteer rescue service should be paid for by the people whose risks it covers, or by everyone."
Mill. Freedom to risk your own life is limited where others are harmed or put at risk.
3. "After the third hour on a hard route I no longer notice the time, my worries, or even my own name."
Csikszentmihalyi. The description of flow: total absorption when challenge and skill match.
4. "The race achieves nothing and the finish line is only a line. That is exactly why it feels like a free act."
Camus. Meaning comes from the struggle itself, not from any result.
5. "Two people attempt the same dangerous descent. One has prepared for years and respects the danger. The other has not. Only one of them is brave."
Aristotle. Courage is judgement between fearing too much and too little; the other person is rash.

A thought experiment

Imagine this:
  • A helicopter offers to drop you in the deepest chamber of a cave, or on the top of a mountain, with exactly the same view as if you had earned it. Would you take it? If not, what does your refusal show about what you were really after?
  • Now imagine a pill that gives you the feeling of having completed any challenge, with no effort. Would a life of pills be a good life? Which philosopher would you want on your side?
