What "Guided" Means

Each passage comes with a guiding question to focus your analysis. You don't have to answer it directly, but it should shape your response.

"How does the writer use language and structure to convey their perspective on [topic]?"

The Real Challenge

The 15-Minute Annotation System

A reliable process for any unseen text:

1

First Read (3 min)

Read the whole text. Don't annotate yet. Just absorb. Ask: What is this about? What's the tone? What's the purpose?

2

Identify the Basics (2 min)

Text type: Speech? Article? Extract from novel? Poem?
Purpose: Persuade? Inform? Entertain? Reflect?
Audience: Who is this written for?

3

Hunt for Patterns (7 min)

Second read with pen. Mark:
- Repeated words/images (motifs)
- Interesting word choices
- Structural features (paragraphing, shifts)
- Tone changes

4

Find Your 3 Points (3 min)

Group your annotations into 3 main observations. These become your body paragraphs. Each should connect to the guiding question.

The Analysis Paragraph Structure

Use this for each body paragraph:

P - Point (your argument)
E - Evidence (short quote)
A - Analysis (technique + effect)
L - Link (connect to guiding question/thesis)

Example

Point: The writer creates a sense of claustrophobia to reflect the character's mental state.

Evidence: The repeated references to "walls closing in" and "shrinking room"

Analysis: This spatial imagery creates a visceral sense of entrapment, suggesting that the character's anxiety is not just emotional but physically experienced.

Link: This technique reinforces the text's exploration of how trauma manifests in bodily sensation.

Your Practice Text

Guiding Question: How does the writer use language to present the experience of returning to a childhood place?

Text A

Literary Extract

The house had shrunk. That was my first thought as I stood at the gate, key in hand, staring at what had once been a castle. The garden that had taken an eternity to cross now measured perhaps fifteen steps. The oak tree that had touched the sky barely cleared the roof.

I had prepared myself for decay. Peeling paint, cracked windows, the slow surrender of abandoned places. But this was worse. The house looked exactly as I remembered it — the same blue door, the same brass knocker shaped like a lion's head — only smaller. Diminished. As if my memories had been lying to me all along.

The door opened with the same reluctant creak it had always made, a sound that bypassed my brain entirely and landed somewhere in my chest. For a moment I was seven years old again, sneaking in past bedtime, holding my breath on the third stair that always groaned.

But the spell broke quickly. The hallway smelled of dust and absence. The wallpaper — had it always been that shade of yellow? — seemed to watch me with faded indifference. I was a stranger here now, a tourist in my own history.

In the kitchen, I found a single cup in the sink. My mother's cup, the one with the chipped handle she refused to throw away. Someone had washed it and left it there, waiting. For what? For whom? The question sat in my throat like something I couldn't swallow.

I had come back to say goodbye. To close a door, literally and figuratively. But standing in that kitchen, with the afternoon light falling through the window exactly as it always had, I understood that some doors don't close. They just get smaller. And we get too big to fit through them anymore.

From "The Returning" by Eleanor Vance, 2019

The Shrinking Motif

The repeated imagery of things becoming smaller ("shrunk," "diminished," "get smaller") creates a central metaphor for how childhood memories distort scale. This isn't just physical — it represents the emotional "shrinking" of significance as we grow up. The final line "we get too big to fit through them anymore" makes this explicit.

Sensory Memory

The writer uses sound ("reluctant creak," "third stair that always groaned") and smell ("dust and absence") to show how sensory experience triggers memory more powerfully than sight. The creak "bypassed my brain entirely and landed somewhere in my chest" — this somatic language suggests memory is bodily, not just mental.

The Cup as Symbol

The mother's cup — "chipped" but kept — symbolizes both presence and absence. It's been "washed and left there, waiting" — personification that suggests the past still expects something from us. The unanswered questions ("For what? For whom?") that sit "like something I couldn't swallow" use physical imagery for emotional difficulty.

Structural Shift

The piece moves from external observation (the house, the garden) to internal reflection (memory, emotion). The short final paragraph delivers the thematic conclusion. The metaphor of doors "that don't close" subverts the narrator's stated intention — they came to "close a door" but realize this is impossible. Structural irony.
