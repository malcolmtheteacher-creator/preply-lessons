Literature Review Architecture

PhD Academic English · Synthesis · Finding the Gap

The single biggest mistake in PhD literature reviews

Literature reviews fail when they are organised by author rather than by argument. Reading them feels like reading a list: Smith says X. Jones says Y. Brown says Z. The reader can't see why these sources sit together, what they argue about, or where the writer's contribution will land.

A PhD-level literature review is organised by theme of argument — and within each theme, the sources are made to converse with each other. The author's job is not to summarise sources individually but to map the conversation they are having and locate the unfilled position.

The architectural rule

Each section of your literature review should be organised around a claim about the field, not around an author. The claim then becomes a thesis statement that the section's sources are marshalled to support.

❌ Author-organised (undergraduate move)

Bourdieu (1986) argues that cultural capital functions as a marker of distinction. Lareau (2003) builds on this by showing how middle-class parents transmit cultural capital to their children. Reay (2017) extends the analysis to higher education access...

Problem: reads like a chronological list. No argument, no synthesis, no position.

✅ Argument-organised (PhD move)

Cultural capital is widely treated as a stable inheritance, transmitted intergenerationally and reproduced through educational institutions (Bourdieu, 1986; Lareau, 2003; Reay, 2017). What this consensus underplays, however, is how cultural capital is increasingly contested in digital contexts...

Why it works: opens with a claim about the field, cites the consensus in one move, then signals where it falls short — opening the gap.

The "claim → consensus → tension" pattern

The most reusable lit-review paragraph structure for PhD work:

  1. State a claim about the field — what does the literature broadly say about X?
  2. Cite the consensus in one breath — three or four key sources after a single claim, not three sentences each summarising a source.
  3. Mark the tension — However, what this consensus underplays is... / Yet this position rests on an assumption that... / Less attention has been given to...
  4. Cite the dissenters or the gap — sources that complicate or that don't yet exist.

The synthesis matrix — how to actually organise 50 sources

Before you write a literature review, you need to see your sources arranged by argument, not by author. The synthesis matrix is the working tool. Each row is a source. Each column is a thematic argument. Each cell is your one-line note on what that source says about that theme.

Once the matrix is full, the architecture of the review writes itself: each column becomes a section, organised around what that group of sources collectively argues — and where they disagree.

The language of synthesis

A PhD-level literature review uses a specific repertoire of moves to make sources converse. These are the building blocks. Memorise them; they will appear in every chapter you write.

MOVE 1

Aggregating consensus

Several sources, one claim about the field. Names go in parentheses, not in the prose.

It is widely argued that... (Smith, 2018; Jones, 2020; Brown, 2022).

A growing body of research suggests... (e.g., Lareau, 2003; Reay, 2017; Davies, 2018).

Recent work has converged on the view that... (Smith et al., 2019; Park, 2020).

MOVE 2

Marking divergence

Two sources disagree — name the disagreement explicitly.

While Smith (2018) argues that X, Jones (2020) takes the opposite view, contending that...

This consensus has been challenged by...

A second strand of the literature, however, complicates this picture...

MOVE 3

Marking what is underplayed

The literature broadly says one thing, but something is missing or thin. This is where your gap lives.

What this consensus underplays is...

Yet this position rests on an assumption that has been little examined: namely that...

Less attention has been given to...

Surprisingly little work has addressed...

MOVE 4

Building on rather than just citing

Show what each source does, not just what it says. Verbs matter.

Smith (2018) extends Bourdieu's framework by...

Jones (2020) challenges this view, drawing on...

Brown (2022) complicates the picture by introducing...

Park (2021) departs from earlier accounts in arguing that...

MOVE 5

The "bridge to your work" sentence

Every section of the review should end (or build toward) a sentence that signals how this argument connects to your contribution.

This study addresses that gap by...

The present research takes up this question by examining...

Building on this, the current chapter argues that...

Articulating the gap

The "gap" is where your PhD justifies itself. It is not enough to say nobody has written about this. The gap must be a theoretically meaningful absence — one that, when filled, advances understanding of something the field already cares about.

Four types of gap a PhD can claim

  1. Empirical gap — a context, population, or case has not been studied. Weakest type on its own; only convincing when paired with a theoretical reason it matters.
  2. Theoretical gap — an existing concept has not been extended to a new domain, or two literatures have not been brought into conversation.
  3. Methodological gap — the question has been asked but with methods that miss something a different method would catch.
  4. Contradiction gap — two well-established findings sit in tension, and resolving the tension matters for theory or practice.

Although the cultural capital framework has been productively applied to higher education access (Reay, 2017) and to early-childhood parenting practices (Lareau, 2003), far less attention has been paid to how digital platforms reconfigure the very forms cultural capital takes. This study addresses that gap by asking whether platform-specific competencies — managing one's online presence, navigating algorithmic visibility — now operate as a distinct and increasingly consequential form of capital. Doing so not only extends Bourdieu's framework but also tests whether the original tripartite typology remains adequate in digital contexts.

Why this paragraph works

  • Concedes the strength of existing work first — credibility move
  • Names the absence precisely — not "nobody has done this" but a specific underdeveloped area
  • Positions the contribution — addresses the gap explicitly
  • Articulates theoretical pay-off — not just "fills a gap" but "tests whether the framework is still adequate"

The "so what?" test

For every gap claim you make, ask: if I successfully fill this gap, what does the field gain? If the answer is "we'd know one more thing," the gap is too thin. The strong answer is "we'd revise our theoretical framework / resolve a long-standing tension / open a new research direction."

Architectural audit

Rewrite one paragraph

Pick the weakest paragraph in your section — the one that reads most like a list. Rewrite it using the claim → consensus → tension pattern. Don't add new sources; just reorganise.
