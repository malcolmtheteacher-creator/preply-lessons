The replication crisis: true or false?

Around 2011, psychology began to question its own results. Here are six claims about what happened. Decide which are true.

1. A failed replication always proves that the original study was wrong.
False. The sample, context or power may differ, although repeated failures should lower trust.
2. In 2015 about 270 researchers tried to replicate 100 studies. Ninety-seven of the originals had reported significant results.
True. The project was called the Open Science Collaboration.
3. Journals publish significant and non-significant results about equally often.
False. Significant results are published much more often. That is called publication bias.
4. Most of the replications also reached significance.
False. Only around a third did.
5. On average, the effect sizes in the replications were around half those of the originals.
True. Even the effects that held up were usually weaker.
6. In 2011 a leading journal published experiments that claimed people could sense future events. Few colleagues believed them, yet the studies followed the usual methods.
True. That is what alarmed many researchers.
Talk about it:
  • What does it say about a field that 97 per cent of its published studies are significant?
  • Why might researchers, journals and newspapers all prefer exciting results?

The evidence, in three steps

Before each part, make a prediction out loud. Then read, and check yourself.

Part 1 — The warnings

Before you read: If a study can follow all the normal rules and still produce a false result, what is wrong: the researcher, or the rules?

In 2011, two events shook social psychology. One was the publication, in a leading journal, of a series of experiments claiming that people could sense future events. Few colleagues believed it, but the studies had followed the standard rules. The question that followed was uncomfortable: if the field's methods can publish this, what else do they publish?

The second was a paper by Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn showing that flexible analysis can make almost anything look significant. By making a handful of apparently reasonable choices about sample size, exclusions and outcome measures, they 'demonstrated' that listening to a particular song made participants younger by about eighteen months. Later surveys of researchers suggested that some of these questionable practices were common, and many were barely seen as wrong.

Part 2 — The big test

Before you read: A large group repeats 100 studies. What would you expect, and how would you explain the difference?

In 2015, the Open Science Collaboration, a group of around 270 researchers, attempted to replicate 100 studies published in 2008 in three leading journals. Although 97 of the originals had reported significant results, only about 36 of the replications did, and the average effect size was roughly half of the original. The results varied by sub-field: studies in cognitive psychology replicated more often than those in social psychology.

The interpretation has been contested. Some argued that the replications differed from the originals in ways that mattered, or that a single failure is weak evidence. Others pointed out that the pattern fits what publication bias and low statistical power would produce. The most careful reading is that the field's published effects were, on average, overestimated, and that some of them are probably not real.

The original finding did not ______ in larger, preregistered samples. (h___ u_)
hold up (v) — to remain valid when tested again. Also: stand up to scrutiny.

Part 3 — Reform

Before you read: What practical changes could make published results more trustworthy?

The response has been unusually practical. Researchers now preregister hypotheses and analysis plans, so a confirmatory test is distinguished from an exploratory one. Registered reports commit a journal to publish a study based on its question and method, regardless of the result. Large multi-lab projects test effects across many samples at once, and journals increasingly encourage or require open data and code.

None of this is free. Larger samples are costly, and early-career researchers who report null results may find them harder to publish. Yet there is cautious evidence of improvement: preregistered studies report fewer significant results and smaller effects, which is exactly what we would expect if earlier results were inflated. Many researchers argue the crisis has become, in effect, a revolution in credibility.

New words in this part — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

A registered report is peer-reviewed and accepted in principle before the data are collected, so the result cannot affect publication.
registered report (n) — a journal format that judges the question and method before the results are known.
Which word means "a statistical test that you decide in advance"? (c______________)
confirmatory (adj) — a confirmatory analysis tests a pre-specified hypothesis; exploratory analyses generate new ones.

Nominalisation

In academic writing, actions become nouns so that a whole idea can be the subject of the next sentence. It makes your writing denser and more formal.

verb → noun: replicate → replication, analyse → analysis, fail → failure, prefer → preference, share → sharing.
Two sentences → one: Researchers replicated the studies. This showed that many effects were smaller. → The replication of the studies showed that many effects were smaller.
Use it to start a sentence with the idea, not the person.

3. Scientists failed to share their data. Because of this, other teams could not check the work. Combine, starting The failure to… failure

Take a side

Five claims people make about the crisis. Choose I agree, I disagree or It depends, then defend your choice in one sentence. If you can, use a noun phrase from the verb in brackets. Check the evidence last.

1. "Psychology has been shown to be unreliable."(fail)
Too broad. Replicability varies by sub-field and method; some effects are very robust. The accurate claim is that many published effects were overestimated.
2. "A failed replication means the original was wrong."(replicate)
Not necessarily. The replication could differ in sample, context or power; but repeated failures should lower confidence.
3. "Preregistration solves the problem."(register)
Helps, does not solve. It separates exploratory from confirmatory work, but it cannot fix poor measurement or weak theory.
4. "The crisis shows the scientific method is working."(correct)
Partly. Science corrects itself, but slowly and with costs; correction depends on incentives that reward checking.
5. "Larger samples are always better."(measure)
An oversimplification. Large samples reduce error but not bias; a large, badly measured study can still mislead.

Say the word

publication bias

reproducible
