Can We Repeat the Result?

When a field tries to reproduce its own findings and fails more often than it expected, what should it conclude?

Read the sentence aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the context, then reveal.

1. P-hacking is the practice of trying different analyses until a result crosses the threshold for significance.
p-hacking (n) — adjusting analyses, exclusions or outcomes until p < .05 is reached. Related: researcher degrees of freedom.
2. Publication bias means that journals favour positive findings, so the literature overstates the effects.
publication bias (n) — the tendency for significant results to be published more than null ones. Also the file-drawer problem.
3. A registered report is peer-reviewed and accepted in principle before the data are collected, so the result cannot affect publication.
registered report (n) — a journal format that judges the question and method before the results are known.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The warnings

In 2011, two events shook social psychology. One was the publication, in a leading journal, of a series of experiments claiming that people could sense future events. Few colleagues believed it, but the studies had followed the standard rules. The question that followed was uncomfortable: if the field's methods can publish this, what else do they publish?

The second was a paper by Joseph Simmons, Leif Nelson and Uri Simonsohn showing that flexible analysis can make almost anything look significant. By making a handful of apparently reasonable choices about sample size, exclusions and outcome measures, they 'demonstrated' that listening to a particular song made participants younger by about eighteen months. Later surveys of researchers suggested that some of these questionable practices were common, and many were barely seen as wrong.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the 2011 experiments cause concern?

2. What did Simmons and colleagues demonstrate?

1. Because methods that followed the standard rules had published an implausible claim.

2. That flexible analysis can produce a significant result for something absurd.

Discuss:
  • Where is the line between legitimate flexibility in analysis and p-hacking?

Part 2 — The big test

In 2015, the Open Science Collaboration, a group of around 270 researchers, attempted to replicate 100 studies published in 2008 in three leading journals. Although 97 of the originals had reported significant results, only about 36 of the replications did, and the average effect size was roughly half of the original. The results varied by sub-field: studies in cognitive psychology replicated more often than those in social psychology.

The interpretation has been contested. Some argued that the replications differed from the originals in ways that mattered, or that a single failure is weak evidence. Others pointed out that the pattern fits what publication bias and low statistical power would produce. The most careful reading is that the field's published effects were, on average, overestimated, and that some of them are probably not real.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the project find?

2. Why is the interpretation contested?

1. Only about 36 of 100 replications were significant, and effect sizes were about half the originals'.

2. Because replications may differ in important ways, and one failure is weak evidence; but the pattern also fits publication bias and low power.

Discuss:
  • How should a single failed replication change your confidence in an original finding?

Part 3 — Reform

The response has been unusually practical. Researchers now preregister hypotheses and analysis plans, so a confirmatory test is distinguished from an exploratory one. Registered reports commit a journal to publish a study based on its question and method, regardless of the result. Large multi-lab projects test effects across many samples at once, and journals increasingly encourage or require open data and code.

None of this is free. Larger samples are costly, and early-career researchers who report null results may find them harder to publish. Yet there is cautious evidence of improvement: preregistered studies report fewer significant results and smaller effects, which is exactly what we would expect if earlier results were inflated. Many researchers argue the crisis has become, in effect, a revolution in credibility.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does a registered report do?

2. What cost does reform bring?

1. It commits the journal to publish a study based on its question and method, before the results are known.

2. Larger samples cost more, and null results may still be harder to publish, especially for early-career researchers.

Discuss:
  • Which incentives would you change first: journals, funders or universities?

Grammar — nominalisation

Nominalisation turns verbs and adjectives into nouns. It is the engine of academic and professional writing: it lets you pack a process or a quality into a short noun phrase and make it the subject of a sentence.

Common transformations:
• fail → failure: "The study failed to replicate" → "The failure to replicate raised concerns."
• analyse → analysis, publish → publication, select → selection, reliable → reliability, flexible → flexibility
• researchers changed their methods → a change in methods / a shift in practice
Why use it?
• To avoid long clauses: The fact that researchers selected outcomes selectively caused distortion → Selective reporting caused distortion.
• To link ideas: The flexibility of analysis leads to inflated effects.
• Be careful: too many nominalisations make writing heavy. Mix them with clear verbs.

Type the noun form of the word in brackets.

1. The (fail) to replicate the study raised concerns.

2. Greater (transparent) has improved trust in research.

3. The (select) of outcomes after seeing the data is a problem.

How much would you believe it?

Five claims. Say how far you would trust each and what you would want to see. Then reveal.

1. "Psychology has been shown to be unreliable."
Too broad. Replicability varies by sub-field and method; some effects are very robust. The accurate claim is that many published effects were overestimated.
2. "A failed replication means the original was wrong."
Not necessarily. The replication could differ in sample, context or power; but repeated failures should lower confidence.
3. "Preregistration solves the problem."
Helps, does not solve. It separates exploratory from confirmatory work, but it cannot fix poor measurement or weak theory.
4. "The crisis shows the scientific method is working."
Partly. Science corrects itself, but slowly and with costs; correction depends on incentives that reward checking.
5. "Larger samples are always better."
An oversimplification. Large samples reduce error but not bias; a large, badly measured study can still mislead.
