IELTS Writing Task 2

Time Management & Planning  ·  Module 1 · Lesson 4

You have 40 minutes. That is less than you think.

Most students treat IELTS Writing Task 2 as a 40-minute writing task. It isn't. It's a 5-minute planning task, a 30-minute writing task, and a 5-minute checking task — and the planning and checking are not optional. Skip either of them and your score drops.

How to spend the 40 minutes

Stage Minutes What you actually do
Read & decode 2 min Read the question twice. Underline the topic, the task verb, and any "both" or "and" that signals two parts.
Plan 3 min Decide your position. Brainstorm two reasons. Note one example for each. Sketch the 4-paragraph outline.
Introduction 6 min Three sentences: general → paraphrase → thesis with two reasons signposted.
Body Paragraph 1 11 min Five sentences: topic → explain → example → comment → link. Around 90–120 words.
Body Paragraph 2 11 min Same five-element shape. The reason you signposted second.
Conclusion 3 min Restate position, summarise the two reasons. Two or three sentences. No new information.
Check 4 min Read the whole essay. Fix the obvious slips: "I think", "very good", missing articles, comma splices, word-count.

Why the planning matters more than the writing

Students who skip planning spend body paragraph 1 thinking up their argument as they write. The result is a paragraph that wanders, repeats itself, and doesn't connect to body paragraph 2 because they hadn't yet decided what body paragraph 2 would say. Five minutes of planning prevents thirty minutes of bad writing.

Discuss:

What usually goes wrong when you write under time pressure? Do you tend to over-plan and run out of writing time, or rush in and run out of ideas?

What goes into the five-minute plan

Five minutes feels like nothing — but if you know exactly what to do with it, it is enough. The plan has five steps and they happen in order.

1

Decode the question (60 seconds)

Underline three things: the topic (what is it about?), the task verb (agree? discuss? evaluate? what causes / what solutions?), and any "both" or "and" that signals two parts.

2

Pick a position (30 seconds)

Don't agonise. Pick the side you can defend more easily, even if it isn't your real-life view. The examiner doesn't care what you actually think — only how clearly you argue it.

3

Brainstorm two reasons (90 seconds)

Write down 3 or 4 reasons that support your position. Then pick the two strongest — the ones with the clearest examples behind them.

4

Note one example per reason (60 seconds)

For each of your two reasons, jot down one specific example: a country, a study, a person, a real situation. Without this step, your body paragraphs will be all opinion and no evidence.

5

Sketch the 4-paragraph outline (30 seconds)

Write four short labels in the margin: INTRO (position + reason 1 + reason 2) · BP1 (reason 1, example) · BP2 (reason 2, example) · CONCLUSION (restate). Now you have a map.

What the plan looks like on the page

The plan does NOT need to be neat. It is for you, not the examiner. Two or three lines per paragraph, in note form, is plenty.

Q: Tourism — more harm than good. Agree/disagree. Position: mostly disagree (more good than harm) Reason 1: economic benefit — jobs, foreign currency Example: Thailand — 12% GDP, millions of jobs Reason 2: cultural exchange — both ways Example: Japanese tourists in Europe → Japanese cafes in London Outline: INTRO: paraphrase + position (mostly disagree, two reasons) BP1: economic benefit + Thailand BP2: cultural exchange + London cafes CONC: restate + main reasons

Discuss:

Why is it a mistake to brainstorm five or six reasons during the plan? What is the trade-off between depth and breadth in a 250-word essay?

How to come up with reasons under pressure

Most students freeze when they read the question. The cure is having a few mental "angles" you can run through quickly. These angles work for almost any IELTS topic and produce more reasons than you can use.

Six angles for generating reasons

For any topic, ask yourself: who is affected, what does it cost, what does it gain, what does history show, what would happen if we did nothing, and what is the trade-off?

The six-angle prompts — flip each card to see the angle and a worked example

Who is affected? Angle: Different groups feel the impact differently.
Mobile phones in school: students (concentration), teachers (discipline), parents (safety), schools (policy enforcement).
What does it cost? Angle: Real costs — money, time, attention, freedom, social cost.
Free university: cost to taxpayers, opportunity cost vs other public spending, increased uptake without commitment.
What does it gain? Angle: Concrete benefits — economic, social, personal, environmental.
Public transport investment: reduced congestion, lower emissions, better access for the elderly, urban regeneration.
What does history show? Angle: Real-world cases where this has been tried.
Smoking bans: countries that introduced them saw rapid drops in heart disease admissions.
What if we did nothing? Angle: The cost of inaction — often the strongest argument.
Climate policy: the cost of doing nothing is far greater than the cost of acting now.
What's the trade-off? Angle: Acknowledge the real cost of your position — this is what makes an argument sound mature.
Higher fuel prices: would reduce car use, but would hit rural and low-income drivers hardest.

Practice run

Below are three IELTS-style questions. For each, name two reasons you could use — using two different angles from the cards above. Then check the model.

Question: Some people believe that children should not be given homework. To what extent do you agree?
→ Pick two angles. Name two reasons.
Angle 1 — Who is affected? Younger children need free time for play and physical development; structured homework cuts into this.
Angle 2 — What does it gain? Older children genuinely benefit from independent practice; consolidation of learning happens after the lesson, not during it.
Position: partial agreement — no homework for younger children, modest amounts for older ones.
Question: Many people think governments should ban single-use plastics. Discuss both views and give your opinion.
→ Pick two angles. Name two reasons.
Angle 1 — What does it gain? A ban would directly reduce ocean plastic pollution; alternative materials already exist for most uses.
Angle 2 — What's the trade-off? A ban would hit small businesses hardest (packaging costs); poorer countries with weaker recycling infrastructure would carry a disproportionate burden.
Position: support a phased ban with subsidies for affected businesses.
Question: In many countries, the elderly are increasingly cared for in retirement homes rather than by their families. Is this a positive or negative trend?
→ Pick two angles. Name two reasons.
Angle 1 — Who is affected? Working adults can maintain full-time employment without becoming exhausted carers; the elderly receive professional medical attention they couldn't get at home.
Angle 2 — What's the trade-off? The cost is the loss of intergenerational connection; grandchildren grow up with less contact with grandparents.
Position: mostly positive, but with concern about the social cost.

Discuss:

Which of the six angles do you find easiest? Which would help you most for the topics you usually struggle with?

The whole journey — question, plan, essay

Below is a complete walkthrough: the question, the five-minute plan, and the introduction and one body paragraph that grew out of that plan. Notice how every sentence in the essay traces back to a line in the plan.

The question:

Some people think that the best way to reduce traffic congestion is to raise the cost of fuel. Others believe there are better solutions. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

Stage 1 — the plan (5 minutes)

Topic: traffic congestion — fuel prices vs other solutions Task: discuss both views + give opinion (so: 1 view, other view, my view) Position: fuel prices help a bit, but better solutions are public transport investment View 1 — fuel prices: reason: makes driving expensive → people drive less problem: hits low-income/rural drivers hardest (trade-off angle) View 2 — better solutions: reason: public transport tackles the cause, not the symptom example: Vienna — €1/day annual pass → car ownership FELL Conclusion: fuel prices alone are blunt; public transport investment is the real answer Outline: INTRO → paraphrase + position BP1 → fuel prices view (with trade-off) BP2 → public transport view (Vienna) CONC → restate + main reasons

Stage 2 — the essay grows from the plan

Watch how each line of the plan turns into one or two sentences in the essay.

INTRODUCTION (from the "position" line):

Urban congestion has become one of the defining problems of modern city life. While some commentators argue that the most effective response is to raise the price of petrol, others believe that more substantial investment in public transport offers a better long-term solution. This essay will discuss both positions before concluding that, although fuel pricing has a role to play, public transport investment is the more durable answer, both because it tackles the underlying cause of congestion and because it offers genuine alternatives to private cars.

BODY PARAGRAPH 2 (from the "View 2" lines, including the Vienna example):

The most compelling alternative to fuel pricing is sustained investment in public transport. Building more roads, after all, simply attracts more cars onto them, a phenomenon traffic engineers refer to as induced demand; high-quality public transport, by contrast, removes vehicles from the network entirely. A clear illustration of this is the city of Vienna, where decades of investment in trams, buses and metro lines have given residents an annual travel pass for around one euro per day, with the result that car ownership in the city has actually fallen. What this demonstrates is that, when the alternative is genuinely convenient and affordable, drivers will choose it without needing to be punished by higher prices. For this reason, public transport investment offers a more durable solution to congestion than any fuel-pricing policy ever could.

Plan line → Essay sentence:

"Topic: traffic congestion" → opening sentence "Urban congestion has become one of the defining problems..."
"discuss both views + give opinion" → "This essay will discuss both positions before concluding..."
"Position: fuel prices help a bit, but..." → the thesis sentence with two reasons signposted
"View 2 reason: tackles the cause, not the symptom" → topic sentence + explanation in BP2 (induced demand)
"Vienna €1/day → car ownership FELL" → the example sentence in BP2
"real answer" → comment + link sentences in BP2

Notice how nothing in the essay was made up on the spot. Every move was decided in the plan. The writing time was just turning the plan into sentences.

Discuss:

Could you write the essay if you had only the plan in front of you (and not the worked essay above)? What would be the hardest part?
