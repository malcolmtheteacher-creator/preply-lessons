The Gold That Came Home

For fifty years Europe kept its gold in New York, where it would be safe. Now it is quietly bringing it back.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll follow gold across the Atlantic and back — why Europe sent it to New York when it was frightened, and why it is bringing it home now that it is frightened again — and you'll master the formal phrases English uses to give a reason without a full clause: in view of, in light of, for fear of, with a view to.
Warm up — talk before you read:
  • Where do you keep the things you could not replace — documents, jewellery, savings? At home, or somewhere you consider safer? What made you choose?
  • If you had to leave your country in a hurry, what would you want to be able to reach from abroad?
  • Countries still keep thousands of tonnes of gold, even though nobody pays for anything in gold any more. Why do you think that is? First instinct — we'll come back to it.

The words for today's story

Ten words, four short challenges. Say your answer out loud before each reveal.

1 · Work it out from the sentence

The documents were kept in a vault beneath the building — a room with metre-thick walls, a steel door weighing several tonnes, and no windows at all.
a vault (n) — a strongly built, secure room for storing valuables, usually underground. Also used figuratively: "locked away in the vaults of memory."
The country's reserves had fallen dangerously low: it no longer held enough foreign currency to pay for the food and fuel it needed to import.
reserves (n, usually plural) — the money, gold or supplies a country or organisation keeps in hand for when it needs them. "foreign-currency reserves" · "oil reserves" · "she spoke with reserves of patience I didn't have."
The gallery does not own the paintings; it is merely their custodian, holding them safely on behalf of the family that lent them.
a custodian (n) — a person or institution that looks after something belonging to someone else. From Latin custos, guard. Note the responsibility in the word: a custodian keeps, but does not own.

2 · I give you the meaning — you find the word

Which verb (starting with r) means to bring something — or someone — back to its own country?
to repatriate (v) — to return something or somebody to their home country. "to repatriate profits" · "to repatriate refugees". The noun is repatriation. Patria is Latin for homeland — the same root as patriot.
Which noun (starting with u) describes a state of political trouble and disorder, short of open war?
unrest (n, uncountable) — political or social disturbance: protests, instability, tension. "civil unrest" · "growing unrest among workers". Literally the absence of rest.
Which noun (starting with h) names a place of safety that people run to when things elsewhere become dangerous?
a haven (n) — a safe place, a refuge. In finance, a safe-haven asset is one investors buy when they are frightened of everything else. Originally a harbour — somewhere a ship could shelter from the storm.

3 · Complete the chunk

The metal is not stored as coins or jewellery but as ______: plain bars, stamped with their weight and purity, stacked on wooden pallets.
bullion (n, uncountable) — gold or silver in bulk form, valued by weight rather than as coins or objects. "a bullion market" · "gold bullion". You cannot say "a bullion" or "two bullions".
Farmers often buy land as a ______ against inflation: if money loses its value, the fields will still be worth something.
a hedge (against something) (n) — an investment held to protect you if something else goes wrong. Also a verb: "to hedge against a fall in prices", and "to hedge your bets" — to avoid committing to one outcome.

4 · Choose the meaning

Bankers call an asset liquid when… (a) it loses value quickly, (b) it can be sold for cash quickly and easily, or (c) it is stored as a fluid?
(b) — liquid (adj): easily turned into cash. A house is not liquid; money in a current account is. The noun is liquidity. The metaphor is water: a liquid asset flows, a frozen one cannot.
To diversify is to… (a) spread what you hold across different places or types, (b) sell everything and hold cash, or (c) disagree with expert advice?
(a) — to diversify (v): to spread risk by not keeping everything in one form or one place. "The company diversified into food." The noun is diversification; the proverb behind it is about eggs and one basket.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Why the gold went west

There is a strange fact about national gold: for most of the last century, a great deal of Europe's did not sit in Europe. West Germany's was in New York, London and Paris. The Netherlands kept much of its across the Atlantic. Country after country had built up reserves it could not actually see.

Two reasons put it there, and only one of them was fear. The first was the Cold War. A divided Germany sat directly on the line where the Red Army would arrive if the Cold War ever turned hot, and a nation's gold is precisely the thing an invading army takes. Held in New York, it was beyond reach of any tank. The logic was brutally simple: if the worst happened and the government had to buy weapons, food or friends from abroad, the money had to be somewhere it could still be spent.

The second reason was duller and, in peacetime, more useful. Gold sitting in a vault is only worth what you can do with it, and you cannot do much with it in a hurry unless it is in a place where gold is traded. New York and London are those places. Reserves stored in a major trading centre are liquid: they can be sold, lent or used as security in hours rather than weeks. So the gold went abroad for two quite different motives — to escape a feared invasion, and to sit close to a market — and for decades nobody saw much reason to move it back.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were the two reasons European gold ended up outside Europe, and how do they differ?

2. Why did the Cold War make New York look like the safest place for German gold in particular?

1. Fear of invasion — gold abroad could not be seized by an advancing army and could still be spent from exile; and market access — gold held in a major trading centre can be sold or borrowed against quickly, which gold in a home vault cannot. One motive is about catastrophe, the other about everyday usefulness.

2. A divided Germany lay on the front line where a Soviet advance would begin, so reserves kept at home were the most exposed of anyone's; across the Atlantic they were out of reach of any invading force.

Discuss:
  • Keeping your treasure in someone else's building requires trusting them completely. What has to be true about a country before you would store your national wealth there?

Part 2 — The homecoming

The first big reversal was German. From 2013, after years of public pressure at home, the Bundesbank began moving gold back to Frankfurt: 674 tonnes in all, roughly 300 from New York and 374 from Paris. It finished in 2017, three years ahead of schedule, and at the time the question looked settled for a generation.

It was not. Earlier this year France pulled reserves back from the United States. Then, this week, the Dutch central bank confirmed that it had moved 86 tonnes — out of some 313 held in the US and Canada — to London, between March and August. Its stated reason was carefully worded: the move was made in view of increasing geopolitical unrest, so that the metal would be "readily available for use in a crisis situation". The bank's governor, Olaf Sleijpen, put it more plainly still: "We expect that we will never need to use them, but we do need to strengthen our resilience and preparedness."

Note where the Dutch gold went: not home to Amsterdam, but to London. If the story were purely about distrust, you would expect it to come all the way back. London was chosen because it is where gold is bought and sold — the Bank of England is one of the largest custodians on earth, with roughly 400,000 bars worth more than £200 billion beneath a three-hundred-year-old building in the middle of the City. If you need to sell fast in a crisis, that is where you want to be standing.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the Bundesbank do, over what period, and how did the timing compare with the plan?

2. Why is the destination of the Dutch gold interesting — what would you have expected if the motive were simply distrust?

1. It brought 674 tonnes back to Frankfurt — about 300 from New York and 374 from Paris — starting in 2013 and finishing in 2017, three years early.

2. The gold went to London, not to Amsterdam. Pure distrust would have brought it home; choosing London suggests the aim was to sit beside a major trading market where the metal could be sold quickly if needed.

Discuss:
  • "We expect that we will never need to use them, but we do need to strengthen our resilience." Is preparing for a disaster you don't expect wisdom, or a waste of money? Where does your own life fall on that line?

Part 3 — The lorries that never left

Picture the operation and you probably picture armoured convoys, guards with weapons, a ship crossing the Atlantic under guard. The reality is quieter and much stranger. Of the Dutch gold, around 59 tonnes never travelled at all: the bank simply sold that quantity in New York and bought the same quantity in London — same day, same price, no journey. Only about 27 tonnes was physically moved. A few specialist firms, Brink's among them, do handle the real thing when it has to travel, and they report rising demand from central banks; but the modern way to move a mountain of metal across an ocean is, very often, not to move it.

It is worth being careful about why all this is happening, because the obvious explanation may not be the whole one. The industry's own people play down the drama. Joseph Cavatoni of the World Gold Council allowed that wars and trade tensions "played into" the decisions but said they did not top the list, adding: "I don't get a sense that there's an impending doom." On his account this is mostly a profession getting better at managing its assets. Others point at plainer arithmetic: storing gold at home means paying for security, audits and insurance, costs that fall hardest on smaller countries. And central banks have simply been buying much more of it — around a thousand tonnes a year lately, roughly double the average of the previous decade — which forces the question of where to put it.

What is not in doubt is what gold is for. Nobody buys bread with it. Its entire value lies in being the one asset that is nobody's promise: not a currency a government can print more of, not a bond that depends on somebody paying you back. That is why it climbs when the world feels unstable, and it has climbed hard — to a record high early this year before falling back, though still far above where it stood a decade ago. The Cold War sent Europe's gold west because Europe was afraid. It is drifting back east now for reasons its owners describe as preparedness rather than fear. Whether those are truly different things is the interesting question.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How was most of the Dutch gold "moved", and how much actually travelled?

2. Give two explanations offered for the trend that are not about geopolitical fear.

1. About 59 tonnes was sold in New York and an equal quantity bought in London on the same day, so it never travelled; only around 27 tonnes was physically transported.

2. Any two of: the cost of storing gold at home (security, audits, insurance), which weighs most on smaller central banks; the sheer increase in how much gold central banks have been buying, which forces decisions about storage; and the industry's own view that this is simply more professional management of reserves.

Discuss:
  • The gold's owners say this is "preparedness", not fear. Is that a real distinction, or a polite way of saying the same thing? Does it matter which word a central bank uses?

Grammar — in view of / in light of / for fear of / with a view to

Central banks do not say "because people are worried". They say "in view of increasing geopolitical unrest". This is the register of official statements, serious journalism and formal writing — and it rests on one grammatical trick.

The trick: these are prepositional phrases, so what follows is a noun phrase or an -ing form — never a full clause with its own subject and verb.
• ✓ In view of the crisis, they acted.  ·  ✓ In view of losing access, they acted.
• ✗ In view of the crisis was worsening, they acted. ← this is where learners come unstuck
Compare the clause connectors, which need a full sentence after them: because / since / as the crisis was worsening…
The four, and what separates them:
• in view of / in light of + noun — given this fact, considering it. Neutral reason. ("In light of the new figures…")
• in the face of + noun — confronted by something difficult, and acting anyway. Carries defiance. ("In the face of fierce opposition…")
• for fear of + noun/-ing — to avoid a bad thing you are afraid of. ("They said nothing for fear of alarming the markets.")
• with a view to + -ing — with the intention of. Purpose, not reason. ("They bought land with a view to building on it.") Note: to here is a preposition, so it takes -ing, never the infinitive.

1. Which phrase?

One of the four fits each sentence. Read to the end before you choose — the meaning decides it, not the grammar.

1. the rising cost of insurance and security, several smaller countries have decided to leave their reserves abroad.

2. The transfers were carried out quietly, alarming the markets.

3. The bank sold metal in one city and bought it in another avoiding a dangerous journey altogether.

4. loud criticism at home, the governor defended the decision for years.

1. In view of (or In light of) — a neutral reason: given the cost.

2. for fear of — the quietness exists to avoid a feared outcome.

3. with a view to — this is the intention behind the action, not a reason behind it.

4. In the face of — he was confronted by criticism and continued anyway.

2. Right or wrong? Two of these break the rule

Two sentences are correct and two are not. Find the broken ones and say out loud how you would repair them.

a. In light of the price of gold had risen sharply, the bank reviewed everything it held.

b. For fear of losing quick access to the market, they chose London rather than Amsterdam.

c. With a view to they could sell the metal at short notice, they chose a major trading centre.

d. In the face of growing uncertainty, several governments reviewed where their reserves were held.

a. Broken — a full clause follows. Repair by turning it into a noun phrase: In light of the sharp rise in the price of gold, … (or switch connector: Because the price of gold had risen sharply, …)

b. Correct — for fear of + -ing.

c. Broken — with a view to takes -ing, not a clause: With a view to selling the metal at short notice, …

d. Correct — in the face of + noun phrase.

The repair for a) is the real skill: compressing a clause into a noun phrase. "prices rose sharply" → "the sharp rise in prices".

3. Compress the clause

Rewrite each underlined clause as a noun phrase so the sentence works. Say the whole sentence aloud.

1. The world was becoming less stable. → In view of instability, the bank acted.

2. Costs were rising. → In light of costs, smaller countries stayed abroad.

3. They were afraid they would lose access. → For fear of access, they moved closer to the market.

1. growing / increasing — "In view of growing instability".

2. rising — "In light of rising costs".

3. losing — "For fear of losing access".

Notice the pattern: the verb of the clause becomes an -ing adjective or an -ing noun. This one move is most of what makes writing sound official.

Keep these ready — in the Speak tab your job is to explain decisions made under uncertainty, and this is the language those decisions are announced in.

Task C · What would you keep, and where? (4 minutes)

A country's gold is its answer to the question "what if everything else fails?" Talk about the same question at human scale: what should a person, a family or an organisation hold in reserve, where should it be, and how do you decide how much preparation is enough?

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Something your family keeps "just in case" that has never once been used.

  • Emergency plans that governments ask citizens to make: sensible, or alarming?
  • If you moved abroad tomorrow, what would you want to leave behind in your home country, and what would you take?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Gold's whole value lies in being "nobody's promise" — it depends on no government and no bank. Is that a strength worth thousands of tonnes of useless metal, or a very expensive superstition?
  2. Storing your national wealth in another country is one of the strongest statements of trust one state can make. What does it mean when that arrangement quietly ends — and should it be announced loudly or done discreetly?
  3. Most of the gold never moved: it was sold in one city and bought in another. Does an ownership change on a screen give a country the same security as metal it can walk down and touch? Why might a government want the metal itself?
