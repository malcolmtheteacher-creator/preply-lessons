A contrast to hold

Aristotle to Kant: 2,100 years. Aristotle said morality is about flourishing — eudaimonia, happiness. You cultivate virtues (courage, generosity, wisdom) until being good becomes second nature. Kant turns this upside down. Morality is about duty, not happiness. An action is moral only if you do it from duty, not from love or because it makes you feel good. The highest morality is the most austere. A person who gives to the poor because it makes them happy is not truly moral. A person who gives from duty, grinding against their own inclinations, is. This is Kant’s austere revolution.

II. The Story — The Fortress of Duty

Kant published the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals in 1785, the Critique of Practical Reason in 1788, and the Metaphysics of Morals in 1797. These three works form one of the most systematic, rigorous, and austere moral philosophies ever created.

The Foundation: The Good Will

Kant begins with a startling claim:

Nothing in the world—indeed nothing even beyond the world—can possibly be conceived which could be called good without qualification except a good will. — Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785)

Not intelligence, not courage, not health, not happiness. Only the good will. A good will is not motivated by consequences, not aimed at happiness, not the product of inclination. It is motivated by duty alone. It recognizes and obeys the moral law.

What makes a will good? Duty. What is duty? The recognition that your action is commanded by the moral law — not because it will make you happy, but because reason itself demands it.

The Categorical Imperative

Kant proposes a test for moral actions. Before you act, ask: could I will that everyone act as I am about to act? This is the categorical imperative — not hypothetical (“if you want X, do Y”) but categorical (“do Y, period, because reason demands it”).

Act only according to that maxim whereby you can, at the same time, will that it should become a universal law. — Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals

Consider lying. You think about lying to escape a difficult situation. But could you will that everyone lie when convenient? No. Lying depends on people generally being truthful. If everyone lied when convenient, the whole practice of promising and truthfulness would collapse. Therefore, the maxim of lying cannot become a universal law. Therefore, lying is immoral.

Kant also offers a second formulation:

Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end. — Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals

Persons are not tools. They have dignity, not price. Every person is an end in themselves. The greatest evil is to treat people as mere instruments for your purposes. This principle underlies human rights.

Duty vs. Inclination

Here is where Kant becomes strange. He says an action has moral worth only if done from duty, not from inclination. A shopkeeper who deals honestly with customers to build good reputation has no moral worth. A person who loves their child and cares for them from love has no moral worth. The moral action is done against the grain of your desires.

Why this austerity? Kant’s logic: if you act morally because it makes you happy, you are acting for your own happiness. That is not truly moral. True morality requires acting from duty, even when it costs you. The person who gives to the poor while desiring their gratitude acts for happiness, not duty. The person who gives from duty, indifferent to gratitude, acts truly morally. This seems to destroy love and compassion. But Kant would say: love and compassion are beautiful, but they are not morality. Morality is stricter.

The Starry Heavens Above and the Moral Law Within

In the final passage of the Critique of Practical Reason, Kant writes:

Two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration and awe, the oftener and more steadily we reflect on them: the starry heavens above me and the moral law within me. I do not seek or presume to know them as much as to flatter myself with the thought of their connection. But I see at once, when I turn my gaze from the innumerable multitude of worlds to the universal constitution of the faculty of reason, I begin from the least and rise to the highest; from the conditional to the conditions. — Kant, Critique of Practical Reason, Conclusion (1788)

The starry heavens are the world as it appears to the theoretical mind. The moral law is the voice of practical reason within. Together they reveal the dignity of the human person: you are a rational being, capable of perceiving the universal moral law and acting from it.

The Problem: Where Is Grace?

Kant’s ethics create a crisis. The moral law judges but does not heal. It commands duty but offers no forgiveness. If you fail — and everyone fails — where is the way back? Kant could say: act from duty going forward. But he cannot say: you are forgiven. The law knows no forgiveness.

Kant himself was aware of the problem. He wrote about “radical evil” — the fact that even when we know the moral law, we persistently choose our inclinations over duty. We are guilty. But guilt has no exit in Kant’s system. There is no grace, no mercy, no redemption. Only duty, always duty, forever.

III. The Ideas — Kant’s Moral Revolution

Kant’s ethics rest on three revolutionary ideas. Together, they create the most austere moral philosophy in Western thought.

Idea 1: The Categorical Imperative — Morality is Rational, Not Emotional

Morality is not about making you happy. It is about obeying a rational principle that binds all rational beings.

Before Kant, moral philosophers often started with happiness or virtue or God’s commands. Kant starts differently. He asks: what principle could all rational beings follow? His answer: the categorical imperative. Can you universalize your action? Would you will that everyone act as you are about to act? If not, it is immoral, regardless of consequences.

This divorces morality from emotion, consequences, and individual happiness. It makes morality rational, universal, and binding. It also makes morality dry and austere. There is no warmth, no love, no grace in the categorical imperative.

Idea 2: Duty vs. Inclination — The Moral Worth of Joylessness

An action is moral only if done from duty, not from love, compassion, or the desire for happiness.

This is Kant’s most counterintuitive move. A mother who loves her child does not act morally when she cares for it — she acts from inclination. A person who helps the poor because it makes them feel good acts from inclination. Moral actions are done from duty, despite your inclinations.

Why? Because inclination is contingent. You feel like being generous today; tomorrow you might not. Duty is universal and necessary. It does not waver. A person motivated by duty will do the right thing even when they would rather not, even when nobody is watching, even when it brings them pain.

Idea 3: Persons as Ends — The Sanctity of Rational Being

Every person has dignity, not price. Every rational being is an end in themselves.

This principle flows from the second formulation of the categorical imperative. Because every person is rational, every person can understand and obey the moral law. Because they can, they must be treated as ends, not merely as means. You cannot manipulate, trick, or coerce another person into serving your purposes. To do so is to deny their rationality and dignity.

This principle underwrites human rights. It explains why slavery is not merely inefficient but evil. It explains why lying and manipulation are wrong. They treat persons as things to be used.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Thesis Seed: The Austere Perfection and the Missing Mercy

Kant’s ethics are majestic and austere. They are built on reason, not feeling. They insist on the dignity of every person. They create the foundation for human rights and universal moral law. But they leave a devastating gap. The moral law judges but does not heal. It demands but does not forgive. If you fail — and the moral law, properly understood, makes everyone fail — there is no way back. Only duty, forever, with no grace.

Compare with Jesus. Jesus does not give a law. He gives himself. “I am the way.” Not: follow this principle. But: follow me. Not: obey the law. But: I forgive you. The law tells you what to do. Grace tells you who you are when you fail. Kant offers the law in its purest form. But he cannot offer grace. No philosopher can. Grace is not rational. It cannot be deduced from first principles. It can only be received.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 questions to reflect on. Think deeply before writing.

Is duty without love truly moral?

Kant says an action has moral worth only if done from duty, not love. But is a morality that excludes love and compassion really moral? Or has Kant made morality inhuman?

Can you be good without being happy?

Aristotle thought being good makes you happy. Kant says morality has nothing to do with happiness. Which is right? Is a person who follows duty but is miserable truly good?

Universalizability

Is the categorical imperative—“could you universalize your action?”—really the test of morality? Or are there moral truths that cannot be universalized?

What happens when duty and compassion conflict?

Imagine duty says one thing, but compassion says another. Which should win? Can Kant’s system account for that tension?

Does morality need God?

Kant says we can know the moral law by reason alone, without God. But if God doesn’t exist, why should we obey the moral law? Why can’t I just decide to be immoral?

The gap in Kant

Kant’s moral law judges but does not forgive. It demands but does not redeem. If you fail—and everyone fails—where is the way back? Is his system broken?

Duty or grace?

The lesson contrasts Kant’s duty with Jesus’s grace. Which is more powerful? Which is more human? Can they coexist?
