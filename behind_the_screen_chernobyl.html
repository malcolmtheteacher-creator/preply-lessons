BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Chernobyl

A safety test goes wrong, a reactor tears itself apart, and a government chooses silence. Almost everything you're about to hear really happened.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Chernobyl — a nuclear disaster and the lies that made it worse — discover exactly how much of the drama is documented fact, and master a structure English reserves for its most serious moments: inversion for emphasis, where a negative or limiting word thrown to the front of the sentence flips the normal word order, exactly as a grave subject deserves.
Before we begin — talk it through: Have you ever been told a comforting lie instead of an uncomfortable truth? What happens when an organisation values its own reputation more than the safety of the people it's meant to protect? And how much do you already know about the Chernobyl disaster?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. The flood that followed the dam's collapse was catastrophic, drowning three villages within an hour and leaving thousands of families with nothing.
catastrophic (adj) — causing sudden, extreme damage or suffering; disastrous on a huge scale.
2. For hours the firefighters could do nothing more than contain the blaze, stopping it spreading to the next building, long before anyone could hope to put it out completely.
to contain (v) — to keep something such as a fire, a disease or a problem within limits, preventing it from spreading further.
3. Standing so close to the leaking machine would expose a person to a dose of radiation the body was never built to absorb.
to expose someone to something (v) — to put them in a situation where they are unprotected from something harmful.
4. The inquiry found plain negligence at the heart of it: corners cut, warnings ignored, checks skipped, until a survivable fault became a fatal one.
negligence (n) — a failure to take proper care, causing harm that could and should have been prevented.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. unprecedented is closest in meaning to: (a) ordinary and expected   (b) never seen before   (c) fully understood
(b) never seen before — unprecedented (adj) means having no earlier example; outside all previous experience.
6. lethal is closest in meaning to: (a) harmless   (b) temporary   (c) deadly
(c) deadly — lethal (adj) means capable of causing death.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. Even those who said nothing, who simply looked away and let the false story stand, were complicit in what followed.
To be complicit in something is: (a) completely unaware of it; (b) involved in wrongdoing by allowing it to continue; (c) the single person responsible for it.
(b) — complicit (adj) — sharing responsibility for a wrongful act, especially by knowing about it and doing nothing to stop it.
8. When the investigation ended, it wasn't the men at the top who were punished — a handful of junior engineers were quietly made the scapegoats instead.
A scapegoat is: (a) the person who is truly responsible; (b) someone unfairly blamed for others' mistakes; (c) a reward given for good work.
(b) — a scapegoat (n) — a person unfairly blamed for something that was largely not their fault, often protecting those who were really at fault.

D. Infer from context — two bigger ideas

9. Every cover-up meets its reckoning eventually: years later, an old document surfaces, a witness finally talks, and the people who signed off on the lie are called back to explain themselves.
a reckoning (n) — a moment when past actions or mistakes are finally judged, confronted or paid for.
10. The survivors' harrowing accounts went on for hours, and by the end even those who had done this work for twenty years walked out pale and silent, unable to speak for several minutes.
harrowing (adj) — extremely distressing or disturbing, especially describing an experience or account.

The series, in three parts

Craig Mazin's Chernobyl (HBO/Sky, 2019). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. This is real, grave history — we tell it plainly, without spectacle. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — The night of 26 April 1986

At the Chernobyl nuclear power plant in Soviet Ukraine, engineers are running a late-night safety test on Reactor 4, checking whether the turbines could keep the cooling pumps running for a few seconds during a power cut. The test is delayed for hours, run by an exhausted night shift, and pushed through despite the reactor behaving strangely. Just after 1:23 a.m., an operator presses the emergency shutdown button expecting it to make the reactor safe. Instead, within seconds, the power surges to many times its normal output. Two explosions tear the reactor apart, blow its thousand-tonne roof into the sky, and expose the glowing, burning reactor core directly to the open air.

Plant staff and the deputy chief engineer insist, against all the evidence in front of them, that the reactor itself cannot have exploded — Soviet reactors, they believe, are not built that way. Firefighters are called to what they are told is an ordinary electrical fire. They climb onto rubble that is lethally radioactive, with no protective equipment and no idea of the danger, to fight flames that a garden hose cannot put out.

By morning, several of those firefighters and plant workers are already gravely ill, though no one has yet told them why. A few kilometres away, in the town of Pripyat built to house the plant's workers, ordinary life continues. Families go to work, children play outside, and nobody is warned.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was being tested on Reactor 4, and what happened when the emergency shutdown button was pressed?

2. Why were the firefighters in such terrible danger?

1. A test of whether the turbines could power the cooling pumps during a power cut; pressing the shutdown button caused a power surge instead of a safe shutdown, and the reactor exploded.

2. They were told it was a normal fire and sent in with no protective gear, unaware the rubble and smoke around them were intensely radioactive.

Discuss:
  • The plant's own senior staff refused to believe the reactor had exploded, even as the evidence lay in front of them. Why do people sometimes cling to a comfortable belief rather than an unbearable truth?

Part 2 — The silence spreads further than the radiation

Rather than warn the public, local and national officials try to manage the story. Roads are quietly blocked, but Pripyat itself is not evacuated for 36 hours — residents are told nothing, and some even walk to a nearby railway bridge to watch the strange, glowing fire in the distance. When the evacuation finally comes, residents are told they will be back within three days and should pack lightly. They never return to live in their homes again.

A scientist, Valery Legasov, and a senior party official, Boris Shcherbina, are sent to take charge, alongside Ulana Khomyuk, a nuclear physicist who begins independently piecing together what actually caused the explosion. As the scale of the disaster becomes impossible to hide, an extraordinary mobilisation begins: miners are sent to dig a tunnel beneath the reactor to prevent a second, larger explosion; and when robots fail in the radiation, human "liquidators" are sent onto the reactor roof itself, for barely more than a minute at a time, to clear radioactive graphite by hand.

Meanwhile, the world beyond the Soviet Union starts to notice. It is not a Soviet announcement that reveals the disaster, but radiation alarms going off at a nuclear plant in Sweden, hundreds of kilometres away.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How long did it take to evacuate Pripyat, and what were residents told?

2. How did the outside world first learn something had gone wrong?

1. About 36 hours; they were told to pack lightly, as they would return within three days — they never did.

2. Radiation detectors at a nuclear plant in Sweden picked up abnormal levels, alerting the world before the Soviet Union admitted anything.

Discuss:
  • Ordinary miners and soldiers were sent to do a scientist's most dangerous work, sometimes without being told the true risk. Is it ever acceptable to ask people to take on a mortal danger without their full, informed consent?

Part 3 — The trial and the cost of the truth

At the trial of the plant's senior operators, the official version of events blames the men in the control room alone: human error, and nothing more. Legasov is expected to support that story. Instead, under pressure from Khomyuk and his own conscience, he stands up in court and explains the uncomfortable engineering truth: a flaw hidden inside the reactor's own design meant that, for a few fatal seconds, pressing the "safe" emergency button had made the explosion more likely, not less. The operators made mistakes that night — but the reactor itself had been built with a fault the state had known about and never disclosed.

Telling this truth ends Legasov's career and isolates him from his colleagues and his country. Two years to the day after the disaster, having recorded his own account of what really happened in secret, he takes his own life. His analysis of the reactor's flaw survives him, is later confirmed, and helps ensure the same design fault can never cause a second Chernobyl.

The series closes on the number of official deaths its central lie was supposed to protect, and the human cost — measured in radiation sickness, evacuated towns and a poisoned landscape — that followed from a system unwilling to admit it had been wrong.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Legasov reveal at the trial that the state had wanted kept hidden?

2. What happened to Legasov two years after the disaster?

1. That the reactor itself had a hidden design flaw, so the operators' errors were not the only cause of the explosion.

2. Having recorded the true account of events, he took his own life, isolated and stripped of his standing for having told the truth.

Discuss:
  • Legasov pays an enormous personal price for telling the truth, while those who lied largely keep their positions. Does the story feel like justice, or like a warning?

Grammar — inversion for emphasis

Some events are too grave for ordinary word order. When we open a sentence with certain negative or limiting expressions — never before, not only, only when, at no point, no sooner… than, rarely — English flips the normal subject–verb order, putting the auxiliary (or do/did) before the subject, exactly as in a question. The effect is dramatic emphasis: the sentence itself sounds as serious as what it describes.

The pattern:
• Never before had + subject + past participle: "Never before had the operators seen the reactor behave like this."
• Not only + auxiliary + subject + verb … but (also): "Not only did the explosion destroy the reactor core, but it also released radioactive material for days."
• Only when + clause, + auxiliary + subject + verb: "Only when the graphite was found on the roof did investigators understand the scale of it."
• At no point + auxiliary + subject + verb: "At no point did officials warn the public of the danger."
• No sooner had + subject + past participle … than: "No sooner had the alarm sounded than the control room lost all its readings."
• Rarely + auxiliary + subject + verb: "Rarely does a single design flaw cause a catastrophe on this scale."
Feel the register: this structure is formal and literary — you'll hear it in serious journalism, official reports and courtroom speeches, exactly the register this story's own gravity demands. It would sound strange in casual conversation ("Rarely do I eat breakfast" is technically correct but oddly formal) — save it for moments that deserve weight.

1. Trigger the inversion — complete each sentence

2. Not only (the fire / burn) for hours, but it also threw graphite and fuel fragments across the site. (not only... but also)

3. Only when the full readings finally came in (the commission / grasp) the true scale of the release. (only when + inversion)

1. had the safety team faced (never before + past perfect inversion)

2. did the fire burn (not only + auxiliary + subject)

Each sentence has the right idea but the wrong form. Write the corrected inverted verb phrase.

1. At no point (the town / tell) that the air itself had become dangerous. (at no point + inversion)

2. No sooner (the reactor / stabilise) than a second danger appeared beneath it — the risk of a far larger explosion. (no sooner had... than)

3. Complete the testimony — mix the patterns

Fill each gap with the correct inverted form. Read it aloud when you're done, as if giving evidence.

"Never before (a nuclear accident / require) evacuation and containment on this scale. Not only (the design / have) a fatal flaw, but the operators had also never been told about it. Only now, facing this tribunal, (the truth / start) to come out."

had a nuclear accident required · did the design have · does the truth start

Notice the pattern: a past-focused adverbial (never before, no sooner) pulls in had + past participle; a present or general-truth adverbial (not only in this context, only now, rarely) pulls in do/does/did + base verb.

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

Chernobyl is one of the most historically careful dramas ever made about a real disaster — creator Craig Mazin spent years researching it, and the series is widely praised by scientists and historians for its accuracy. But it is still drama, built from real events with a small number of deliberate simplifications.

☢️ The design flaw was real — and it really did make the "safe" button dangerous

The RBMK reactor design had two hidden weaknesses. First, it had what engineers call a positive void coefficient: under certain conditions, as water coolant turned to steam, the reaction became more intense rather than less. Second, the emergency shutdown rods — designed to stop the reaction — had graphite tips. For a few fatal seconds after the button was pressed, those graphite tips entered the core first and briefly increased the reaction before the rods could finally shut it down. Together, at low power and under the conditions of that test, this combination turned the emergency shutdown into the trigger for the power surge that destroyed the reactor. This was a genuine design fault, known to some Soviet nuclear specialists before 1986 and kept secret from plant operators.

The secrecy, the evacuation, and the true human cost

The 36-hour delay before evacuating Pripyat, and the announcement of danger by Swedish radiation monitors rather than Soviet officials, are both accurate. The international scientific consensus (from bodies including the UN's UNSCEAR and the World Health Organization) attributes 31 deaths directly to the accident and its immediate radiation exposure: two workers killed in the explosion itself, and 28 more — mostly firefighters and plant staff — who died of acute radiation sickness in the following weeks. About 200,000 "liquidators" took part in the clean-up in 1986–87, receiving significant radiation doses.

The long-term death toll from cancers linked to the radiation released is genuinely and seriously disputed: some assessments estimate roughly 4,000 eventual deaths among the most exposed groups; other researchers, using different methods and wider populations, argue the true figure could run into the tens of thousands or more. Both the reactor design and the immediate cover-up are documented fact; the exact long-term toll is one of the few things about Chernobyl that honest scientists still argue over.

What the series compressed or invented

Ulana Khomyuk, the physicist who investigates alongside Legasov, never existed. Mazin has explained she is a deliberate composite, standing in for the many real Soviet scientists who took real risks to establish the truth — condensing them into one character the audience could follow. The scene of townspeople watching the fire from a railway bridge, all later dying of radiation sickness — the so-called "bridge of death" — is also dramatised well beyond what the evidence supports: it draws on real rumours about the bridge, but there is no reliable record that everyone who watched from it died, and at least one known bystander from that night lived a long, healthy life afterwards.

Valery Legasov and Boris Shcherbina were real people, and Legasov's death by suicide, two years to the day after the disaster, is real. Mazin's stated theme for the whole series is simple: "the cost of lies" — the idea that a system built on comforting falsehoods eventually forces someone to pay, in full, for the truth it refused to hear.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, did you know the emergency shutdown button was part of what caused the explosion — or did you assume it was simply human error?
  • Does compressing many real scientists into one invented character (Khomyuk) make a true story more honest, by making it easier to follow — or less honest, because a "fact" the audience remembers never happened?
  • Whose cost matters more in this story: the 31 confirmed deaths, or the uncertainty hanging over everyone else who was exposed?
