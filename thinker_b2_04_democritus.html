If everything, including you, is just tiny pieces moving in empty space, does life have less meaning?

Your favourite song is, in the end, just vibrations in the air. A sunset is light hitting the back of your eyes. A hug is atoms pressing against atoms. Does knowing this spoil the magic, or make it even more amazing? Nearly 2,500 years ago, a laughing man from northern Greece said that everything is made of tiny pieces too small to see.

Today: You'll meet Democritus and his idea that everything is made of atoms, so you can describe what things are made of and discuss whether a world of tiny pieces can still have meaning.

Meet Democritus

Read one part at a time. After each part, close your eyes and answer the question before you check.

I

The laughing philosopher

Democritus was born around 460 BC in Abdera, a town in northern Greece. He travelled widely, perhaps as far as Egypt and Persia, and he is said to have written more than 70 books. All of them are lost; only short fragments survive. People called him the laughing philosopher, because he laughed at human foolishness. Heraclitus, by contrast, was remembered as the weeping philosopher.

Why was Democritus called the laughing philosopher?

Because he is said to have laughed at human foolishness, instead of weeping about it like Heraclitus.
II

Pieces that cannot be cut

Building on the ideas of an older thinker, Leucippus, Democritus developed a bold theory. Imagine cutting a stone in half, then in half again, and again. You can't go on for ever, he said. In the end you reach tiny pieces that cannot be cut. He called them atoms, from the Greek word atomos, which means 'uncuttable'. Atoms are far too small to see, they never break, and they come in many different shapes and sizes.

What does the word 'atom' originally mean?

'Uncuttable'. It comes from the Greek word atomos.
III

Atoms and the void

Parmenides had said that empty space cannot exist, so nothing can really move. Democritus disagreed. There must be empty space, the void, or atoms would have nowhere to move. Everything we see, from rivers to people, is atoms joining together, separating and joining again. Even sweet and bitter, hot and cold, are not in the atoms themselves; they are how atoms affect us. Scientists only proved that atoms exist more than 2,000 years later.

Why did Democritus think the void, empty space, must exist?

Because without empty space, atoms would have nowhere to move, and nothing could ever change.

See it in action

Here is the idea in action. Think of a sandcastle. From far away, it looks like one solid thing with towers and walls. Come closer and you see it consists of millions of grains of sand. When the sea washes it away, nothing is destroyed: the grains simply separate and become part of the beach again. For Democritus, the whole world works like this. Things don't really appear from nothing or disappear into nothing. The same atoms just change places.

Your turn

Now you: explain Democritus' idea to a friend who has never heard of him. Speak for about a minute. Use the starters if they help.

Democritus believed that everything is made up of...The word 'atom' means...Between the atoms there is...So when something seems to disappear, ...
Democritus believed that everything is made up of tiny pieces called atoms. The word 'atom' means 'uncuttable', because these pieces are too small to divide any further. Between the atoms there is empty space, the void, so they can move around. So when something seems to disappear, like a burning log, the atoms are not destroyed; they just separate and form new things. It's amazing that he imagined this more than 2,000 years before scientists proved it.

Connect

Compare

Parmenides and Democritus: Parmenides said that empty space cannot exist, so nothing really moves. Democritus said empty space must exist, so that atoms can move. How did Democritus keep part of Parmenides' idea but still allow change?
Democritus agreed that real things cannot come from nothing or be destroyed: his atoms never appear or disappear. But he added the void, so atoms can move and combine. The atoms themselves never change, but the things they make are always changing.
Thales and Democritus: Thales thought everything is made of one thing, water. Democritus thought everything is made of countless tiny atoms. Which idea is closer to modern science, and why?
Democritus is closer, because modern science also says everything is made of tiny particles. But both were asking the same question that Thales started: what is the world made of?

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From Parmenides

What did Parmenides argue about change, and why?

That change is impossible, because nothing can come from nothing or turn into nothing. So reality is one unchanging whole, and the change we see is an illusion.
From Thales

What new question did Thales ask about the world?

Not who made the world, but what it is made of. He looked for a natural answer instead of a story about the gods, and his answer was water.
