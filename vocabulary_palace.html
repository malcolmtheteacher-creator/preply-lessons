How the palace works

A vocabulary list is ten separate things to carry, and they slide off one by one. A story is one thing — each part pulls the next along behind it. So instead of holding ten words, you hold one story with ten words inside it.

Furnishing a room

Pick a story you have already read. Walk its ten stations in order and, at each one, look at the scene before you say the word. When you can walk the whole room without the page, it is furnished.

Why the scene comes first

Anyone can peg a word to a picture of a shoe on a staircase. The trouble is that a picture gives you back a bare word — not how it behaves in a sentence, not what it sits next to, not whether it is formal or ordinary. Here the word is already inside a sentence that works, so when you go back for it you come away holding all of that too.

Coming back

Rooms fade unless you visit them. Walk gathers whatever is due today from every room you have furnished, and asks for it by its scene. Get it right and it waits longer before it asks again — a day, then three, then a week, then a month. Get it wrong and it comes back tomorrow.

Always reach before you check. The effort of pulling a word out of the dark is what fixes it. Reading the list again feels productive and does almost nothing.

Three rules for leaving a word somewhere

Put it where it actually happened — find the real moment in the story, don't invent a picture. Make it move — still images fade, actions don't. Make it strange, or make it yours — a word hooked to your grandmother's kitchen has an unfair advantage over one hooked to nobody.
