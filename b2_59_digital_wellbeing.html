Digital Wellbeing & Screen Time

B2 Level · Health & Technology

Your Digital Life

Technology is woven into our daily existence. We wake to notifications, work on screens, communicate digitally, and fall asleep scrolling. Most of us couldn't imagine life without our devices. But at what cost?

Screen Time Reality

Global average daily screen time has grown from 3-4 hours in 2012 to over 7 hours currently. Young people often exceed 8-10 hours daily. Some of this is work or education—legitimate use. But much involves social media, streaming, and casual browsing that feels necessary but isn't.

Honestly: How much time do you spend daily on screens? What's your breakdown—work, communication, entertainment, social media? How does this feel to you?

The Habit Loop

Apps are designed to be addictive. They use notifications, streaks, variable rewards, and social validation to keep you engaged. Dopamine—the pleasure chemical—is released when you see likes, comments, or new messages. This creates a habit loop: you feel bored or anxious, so you check your phone; brief pleasure follows; the cycle repeats. Your brain develops neural pathways that make the behaviour feel almost involuntary.

This isn't a character flaw or weakness. It's deliberate design. Companies employ teams of engineers to maximise "engagement"—a metric that means you spending more time in their app. Your attention is the product being sold to advertisers.

Reflection

Do you feel in control of your screen time, or does it feel habitual? Can you go an hour without checking your phone comfortably?

The Invisible Costs

Excessive screen time correlates with decreased sleep quality, anxiety, depression, reduced attention span, and neck/eye strain. It reduces face-to-face interaction, which humans need for psychological wellbeing. It displaces other activities—reading, exercise, outdoor time, deep work. The cognitive load of constant notifications fragmentary attention, making deep focus difficult.

Yet we're ambivalent. Technology connects us to distant loved ones, provides information, enables work flexibility, and offers entertainment. The issue isn't technology itself—it's balance and intention.

Think: What do you gain from technology? What are you losing? Are these balanced?

Wellbeing Language

Vocabulary for discussing digital wellbeing:

Digital Detox A period of abstinence from electronic devices to reduce stress and improve wellbeing
Screen Addiction Compulsive use of screens that interferes with normal functioning and wellbeing
Doomscrolling Compulsively consuming negative news or social media, often causing anxiety
Notification Fatigue Mental exhaustion from constant alerts and digital interruptions
Blue Light Short-wavelength light from screens that disrupts sleep cycles when viewed before bed
Sleep Hygiene Habits and practices that promote good sleep quality and duration
Dopamine Hit A brief rush of pleasure from an activity, reward, or stimulation
Digital Minimalism Intentional use of technology, selecting tools that add real value to life

Reading: A Journalist's 30-Day Digital Detox

Going Offline: One Reporter's Journey Back to Balance

Journalist Sarah Chen spent an average of 12 hours daily on screens—her job required email and research, but personal social media consumption had spiralled. She'd wake checking Twitter, scroll between meetings, doomscroll before sleep. Anxiety and insomnia plagued her. Her relationships suffered; she'd be physically present but mentally elsewhere.

At 35, Sarah decided on a radical experiment: 30 days without voluntary screen time. Work emails during business hours only. Phone in another room. No social media, streaming, or casual browsing. She'd read physical books, take walks, call friends instead of texting.

The first week was brutal. Anxiety spiked. She felt phantom vibrations in her pocket. She craved the dopamine hits. Her mind wandered without distraction to fill the void. But gradually, something shifted. By week two, she read an entire novel—something she'd claimed "no time" for. She slept better. Her anxiety decreased. She noticed beauty: trees, architecture, people's faces.

Most surprisingly: she didn't miss much. The digital world continued without her engagement. She caught up on important news through occasional check-ins. Most social media drama was irrelevant. She'd wasted thousands of hours consuming content that didn't enhance her life.

After 30 days, Sarah didn't fully return to old habits. She established boundaries: no phones at dinner, no screens after 9 PM, 90 minutes screen-free each morning. She's selective about notifications. She deliberately chooses when to engage rather than reactively responding to prompts.

She admits it's hard. The compulsion lingers. But she's reclaimed time and mental space. Her anxiety decreased by 40%. She completed three major writing projects. She rekindles friendships. She doesn't regret it for a moment.

Key Phrases

  • "To reduce screen time" — to spend less time on devices
  • "To set boundaries" — to establish limits on behaviour or time
  • "To feel compelled to..." — to feel an urge to do something
  • "To be mindful of..." — to be aware and intentional about something
  • "To reclaim..." — to take back possession of something
  • "To disengage from..." — to disconnect from or stop participating in something
  • "To prioritise..." — to consider something as most important
  • "To find balance" — to achieve equilibrium between different aspects

Technology and Children

Children's brains are still developing. Excessive screen time during critical periods impacts attention, impulse control, and emotional regulation. Yet preventing children from technology entirely isn't realistic—it's a key tool for learning and connection. The balance is incredibly delicate, and parents face enormous pressure.

Perspective: If you're a parent or work with children, how do you navigate screen time? If you're not, what do you think is a healthy approach for young people?

Workplace Screen Time

Many jobs demand constant digital presence. Emails require responses; meetings happen on video; communication is Slack-based. Productivity tools monitor activity. Working from home blurs boundaries, making "logging off" psychologically difficult. Yet burnout and mental health issues spike with excessive work screen time.

Reflection

How does your work require screen time? What boundaries help you maintain balance? Is "balance" realistically possible in your field?

The Future of Human-Technology Relationship

Technology isn't going away. If anything, immersive technologies like virtual reality will intensify digital engagement. Rather than seeking elimination, we need cultural shifts: normalising "off" time, designing apps for wellbeing rather than engagement maximisation, teaching digital literacy and intentional use, and creating spaces and norms that support real-world connection.

Some organisations are pioneering this. Tech companies are implementing "no-email weekends" and banning notifications after hours. Some nations are exploring regulations limiting app features designed for addiction. Schools are teaching digital citizenship. Change is slow, but it's happening.

Vision: Imagine a society with healthier technology habits. What would be different? What changes would help most?

Your Commitment

Digital wellbeing isn't a one-time fix; it's ongoing practice. Start small. Choose one boundary and commit to it for two weeks. Notice how you feel. Build from there. Remember: you're not trying to eliminate technology—you're reclaiming agency over how it shapes your life.

Final Reflection
