Everything Everywhere All at Once

A tired mother discovers she is the only version of herself who can save every universe — if she can first reach the one person closest to her: her own daughter.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Everything Everywhere All at Once — an exhausted laundromat owner who discovers she can tap into the skills of infinite parallel versions of herself to save the multiverse and reach her estranged daughter — uncover the real physics and philosophy hiding underneath the chaos, and master two conditionals that mix time frames: one that reaches from a past choice into how things stand today, and one that reaches from a present truth back into a moment in the past.
Before we begin — talk it through: Do you ever wonder what your life would look like if you'd made one different choice years ago? Is regret about the past, or about the life you're not living right now? And if you could meet a version of yourself who made completely different decisions, would you actually want to?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. After years of the same commute, the same complaints, and the same paperwork, his job had become utterly mundane — one grey day dissolving into the next.
mundane (adj) — ordinary, dull and repetitive; lacking anything unusual or exciting.
2. She hadn't spoken to her estranged brother in over a decade; an old argument had split the family, and neither side had ever tried to make peace.
estranged (adj) — no longer close to or in contact with someone, usually because of a serious falling-out.
3. The small business owner dreaded the letter marked URGENT: the tax office was demanding a full audit of every receipt from the past three years.
an audit (n) — an official, detailed examination of financial accounts to check that everything is correct.
4. Caring for three young children while working two jobs had become a burden she carried alone, exhausting and impossible to put down.
a burden (n) — a heavy load; more often, a duty, responsibility or worry that weighs on someone.

6. chaotic is closest in meaning to: (a) calm and ordered   (b) wild and disorganised   (c) quiet and slow
(b) wild and disorganised — chaotic (adj) means in a state of complete confusion and disorder.

The film, in three parts

Daniel Kwan & Daniel Scheinert's Everything Everywhere All at Once (2022). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A mundane life buried in paperwork

Evelyn Wang runs a struggling laundromat with her gentle, endlessly patient husband Waymond, who has just decided — quietly, almost apologetically — to file for divorce, though he can never quite find the moment to tell her. Evelyn is too busy: her ageing father, Gong Gong, has arrived from China expecting to see a successful daughter and a respectable family; her own daughter Joy is trying, again, to introduce her girlfriend Becky properly; and above it all hangs a summons from the tax office. Tonight, the family has an audit.

In the lift on the way up to face the auditor, Waymond suddenly changes: his eyes widen, his voice steadies, and a version of him from another universe takes over his body just long enough to tell Evelyn something impossible. There are infinite parallel universes, one for every choice she never made — and she, this exhausted, disappointing, laundromat-owning Evelyn, is the only version capable of stopping a catastrophe unravelling across all of them.

To fight, she must "verse-jump": performing strange, statistically near-impossible actions to borrow the skills and memories of other Evelyns — a movie star, a kung-fu master, a chef. Something, or someone, is destroying the agents sent to protect the multiverse, and it is heading straight for her audit.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is happening in Evelyn's ordinary life at the start, and what does the alternate Waymond tell her in the lift?

2. How does Evelyn access the skills of other versions of herself?

1. Her marriage is quietly failing, her father expects a successful daughter, her own daughter wants acceptance, and the family faces a tax audit; in the lift, an alternate Waymond reveals the multiverse and tells her she alone can stop a growing threat.

2. By "verse-jumping" — performing strange, statistically improbable actions that let her briefly borrow another universe's Evelyn's skills and memories.

Discuss:
  • Alpha-Waymond calls this exact Evelyn — tired, unaccomplished, still running a small laundromat — the one who can save everything, precisely because her life went "wrong." Do failures and disappointments ever turn out to be a kind of hidden strength?

Part 2 — The daughter who has felt everything

The enemy has a name, Jobu Tupaki, and a shocking identity: she is Joy, Evelyn's own daughter, from a universe where an experiment pushed her across so many lives, so fast, that her sense of a single self shattered. Having lived every possible experience at once, Jobu has arrived at a conclusion that feels, to her, like simple mathematics: if you have done everything, nothing can matter, and nothing is worth wanting.

To prove it, she has built something monstrous out of the most mundane object imaginable — an everything bagel, piled with every topping, every regret, every possibility, folded in on itself until it becomes a black hole that can swallow all existence. Alpha-verse agents want Evelyn to hunt Jobu down and stop her before she steps into it and takes everything with her.

The fight spills across universes both ridiculous and dazzling — a world where fingers are hot dogs, a world where humans never evolved beyond googly-eyed rocks, a world where Evelyn became the film star she once dreamed of being, with a devoted, elegant Waymond at her side. In each one, chaos and comedy sit beside something achingly sad: a mother chasing a daughter she has never really seen.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is Jobu Tupaki really, and how did she come to believe nothing matters?

2. What has she built, and what is Evelyn asked to do about it?

1. She is Joy, Evelyn's daughter, pushed by an experiment across so many lives at once that everything blurred into meaninglessness for her.

2. An "everything bagel" that can destroy all universes at once; Evelyn is asked to find and stop her, by force if necessary.

Discuss:
  • Jobu's despair comes from having tried everything and felt nothing hold together. Do you think experiencing more of life makes it feel more meaningful — or could having every option actually make meaning harder to find?

Part 3 — Two rocks, and a choice

In one quiet, near-wordless universe, Evelyn and Joy exist only as two rocks side by side on a hillside, their thoughts appearing only as subtitles. Stripped of chaos and bodies, Evelyn feels, for a moment, the pull of her daughter's nihilism: perhaps nothing does matter, and the easiest thing would be to stop trying.

But Evelyn chooses otherwise. Facing enemies across a dozen universes — the tax auditor, cruel alternate selves, and finally Jobu herself — she stops fighting and starts being kind instead, echoing something Waymond told her at the very start: that in a universe of chaos, being kind, especially when you don't have to be, might be the only thing that makes any sense.

She finds Jobu, and instead of a weapon, she offers acceptance — of Joy, of Becky, of a daughter she never fully saw. It doesn't erase the multiverse's chaos or fix the family instantly, but it is enough. The film ends back where it started: in the same small, mundane laundromat, the same unresolved tax bill on the counter — except that everything between them now feels, quietly, different.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What almost happens to Evelyn in the "two rocks" universe, and what stops her?

2. How does Evelyn finally reach Jobu Tupaki — and where does the story end?

1. She almost gives in to nihilism, feeling that nothing matters; she pulls back and chooses kindness instead, following Waymond's philosophy.

2. Through acceptance and love rather than violence; the film ends back in the ordinary laundromat, the family's problems unresolved but their relationships changed.

Discuss:
  • Waymond argues that in a universe of chaos and confusion, kindness is a conscious, almost strategic choice — not weakness. Do you agree that kindness can be the strongest response to meaninglessness, or is that too easy an answer?

Grammar — Mixed Conditionals

No film needs mixed conditionals more than a story about a woman confronting every version of the life she might have had. Regular conditionals keep one time frame; mixed conditionals deliberately cross two — a past cause explaining a present state, or a present truth explaining a past outcome.

The two mixed structures:
• past condition → present result: if + had + past participle, would/wouldn't + base verb — a past choice or fact explains a state that is still true now: "If Evelyn had followed a completely different path all those years ago, she wouldn't be standing behind this counter today." "If Joy hadn't been pushed across so many lives at once, she wouldn't see the world as meaningless now."
• present condition → past result: if + past simple, would/wouldn't have + past participle — an ongoing truth about someone explains why something did or didn't happen in the past: "If Evelyn weren't so afraid of her father's disappointment, she would have stood up for Joy years ago." "If the multiverse agents didn't believe violence solves everything, they would have tried kindness long before now."
Feel the difference:
• the if-clause with had done always points back to a finished past event; the if-clause with the past simple describes an ongoing truth.
• the result clause tells you when the effect lands: would + base = the effect is still true now; would have + past participle = the effect landed in the past and is already finished.

1. Past choice, present result — complete with would(n't) + base verb

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence.

1. If Joy had never been pushed across every possible version of herself, she (not / see) reality as one big meaningless joke. (true now)

2. If Waymond had stopped being patient with Evelyn decades ago, they (not / still / be) married today. (true now)

3. If Gong Gong had approved of Waymond from the very beginning, Evelyn's relationship with her father (be) far less strained right now. (true now)

1. wouldn't see (past condition, effect still true now)

2. wouldn't still be (past condition, effect still true now)

3. would be (past condition, effect still true now)

2. Present truth, past result — complete with would(n't) have + past participle

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence.

1. If Evelyn weren't so afraid of disappointing her father, she (introduce) Joy's girlfriend properly years ago. (past result)

2. If Jobu Tupaki didn't feel completely alone in everything she had experienced, she (not / build) the everything bagel in the first place. (past result)

3. If the agents weren't so certain that force was the only answer, they (try) kindness long before Evelyn arrived. (past result)

2. wouldn't have built (present truth, past result)

3. Complete the passage — mix both patterns

Fill each gap with the correct mixed-conditional form. Read it aloud when you're done.

If Evelyn (not / spend) her whole life saying yes to everyone else, she might actually feel free by now. And if she weren't so used to running from her problems, she (face) her marriage and her daughter's pain long before either of them exploded across the multiverse. hadn't spent · would have faced · had been

Notice the pattern: a past cause that still shapes today uses if + had done, would + base; a present truth that shaped the past uses if + past simple, would have + done.

Carry both patterns into the Speak tab: the film's whole tragedy and its whole hope live in these two directions — what a past choice explains about today, and what a present truth explains about the past.

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

Underneath the hot-dog fingers and the googly-eyed rocks, Everything Everywhere All at Once is built on three real things: a genuine, if speculative, idea in physics; a serious philosophical debate; and an entirely true story about immigrant family life.

🌌 The multiverse is a real idea in physics — but it isn't proven

The film's central gimmick — countless parallel universes branching off from every choice — echoes a genuine, serious idea in physics: the many-worlds interpretation of quantum mechanics, proposed by the American physicist Hugh Everett in 1957. Everett argued that when a quantum event has several possible outcomes, reality doesn't collapse into just one of them — instead, the universe "splits," and every outcome happens, each in its own separate branch. Separately, some cosmologists have proposed a "multiverse" arising from theories like eternal inflation, in which our own Big Bang was only one of countless "bubble" universes. Both ideas are taken seriously by some physicists and philosophers of science — but neither is proven, neither can currently be tested, and neither is accepted by all scientists. It's real, published physics; it is not established fact, and it may never be.

The "everything bagel" has a name: nihilism

Jobu Tupaki's despair dramatizes a real philosophical position: nihilism, the view that life has no inherent meaning, value or purpose. The film sets nihilism against a different, more hopeful tradition: absurdism, most closely associated with the French-Algerian philosopher Albert Camus. In his 1942 essay The Myth of Sisyphus, Camus argued that the universe truly is indifferent and meaningless — but that giving up, or pretending otherwise, are both mistakes. Instead, he said, we should recognise the absence of meaning and choose, anyway, to act, to care, and to find our own reasons to keep going.

Evelyn's answer — meeting her daughter's void with deliberate kindness rather than violence or despair — is a fairly precise dramatization of Camus's "revolt": not solving meaninglessness, but responding to it with a chosen act of love. The film doesn't claim the universe has a purpose. It claims that you get to choose what matters to you inside it, even if — especially if — nothing was ever guaranteed to matter at all.

The real story underneath — and a historic night at the Oscars

Beneath the multiverse, the film tells an ordinary, true-to-life story: a Chinese immigrant family running a small, exhausting business, caught between a homeland's expectations and a new country's demands, an ageing parent's disapproval, a marriage worn thin by decades of grinding work, and a daughter whose identity her mother struggles fully to accept. Directors Dan Kwan and Daniel Scheinert (credited together as "the Daniels") built the multiverse chaos around this very grounded, very common story of generational and cultural strain.

The film's impact was historic, and entirely real: at the 2023 Academy Awards, Everything Everywhere All at Once won seven Oscars, including Best Picture, Best Director and Best Original Screenplay. Michelle Yeoh became the first Asian actress to win Best Actress; Ke Huy Quan (who plays Waymond) won Best Supporting Actor decades after leaving acting behind; and Jamie Lee Curtis won Best Supporting Actress. The laundromat and the audit are invented; the ache of an immigrant family trying to hold itself together is not.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, did you know the multiverse idea comes from real, published physics — or did you assume it was pure science fiction?
  • Does it change how you read the ending, knowing the "everything bagel" dramatizes a real philosophical position rather than being invented from nothing?
  • Camus said the right response to a meaningless universe is neither despair nor denial, but action anyway. Do you find that convincing, or does it dodge the problem?
