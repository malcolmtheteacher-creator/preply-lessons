Neutral, Tidy, Perfect? The Swiss Reality

Switzerland is imagined as neutral, tidy and perfectly organised — yet it has four languages, 26 governments and a vote every few months. What is the country really like?

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Switzerland stayed neutral in both world wars.
neutral (adj) — not supporting either side in a conflict. Example: "A neutral country does not join other countries' wars."
2. Each canton has its own government and its own laws.
canton (n) — one of the 26 self-governing regions of Switzerland. Example: "Zurich is both a city and a canton."
3. Voters decide big questions in a referendum.
referendum (n) — a direct public vote on one single question. Example: "The country held a referendum on the new tax law."
4. Swiss federal government shares power with the regions.
federal (adj) — describing a system where power is divided between a central government and regional ones. Example: "Germany and the United States are also federal countries."
5. In Zurich people speak a Swiss German dialect.
dialect (n) — a local form of a language, with its own words and sounds. Example: "Her dialect is hard for outsiders to follow."
6. Swiss watches are famous for their precision.
precision (n) — exact accuracy, with no small mistakes. Example: "The surgeon worked with great precision."
7. Decisions are usually made by consensus.
consensus (n) — a general agreement reached by the whole group. Example: "After a long meeting, the team reached a consensus."
8. Switzerland is more diverse than its image suggests.
diverse (adj) — made up of many different kinds of people or things. Example: "London is one of the most diverse cities in Europe."

The stereotype: "Switzerland is neutral, rich and perfectly organised — all banks, chocolate and watches." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The tidy picture

Ask people what they know about Switzerland and the same things come up: chocolate, watches, banks, mountains, neutrality — and trains that run on time. It is an image of a small, rich, orderly country where everything works. Even the famous cuckoo clock is not really Swiss: it comes from the Black Forest in Germany. Much of the Swiss image was built over a long time by advertising, tourism and the success of a few industries.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name three things in the Swiss image.

2. Where does the cuckoo clock really come from?

1. Chocolate, watches, banks, mountains, neutrality or punctual trains — any three.

2. The Black Forest in Germany.

Discuss:
  • Which country has the strongest image in your mind — and where did you get it?

Part 2 — Many Switzerlands

Switzerland has four national languages — German, French, Italian and Romansh — and 26 cantons, each with its own government, schools and laws. Roughly six in ten people speak German as their main language, but in everyday life most use a Swiss German dialect that visitors from Germany often struggle to follow. The cultural border between the German-speaking and French-speaking parts is jokingly called the Röstigraben, "the rösti ditch", after a potato dish. Around a quarter of the people who live in Switzerland do not hold a Swiss passport.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many cantons are there?

2. What is the Röstigraben?

1. 26.

2. The jokey name for the cultural border between the German- and French-speaking parts.

Discuss:
  • Is a country with four languages really one nation?

Part 3 — Voting and staying out

Swiss citizens vote on national questions several times a year. A group that collects 100,000 signatures can force a national vote on a change to the constitution. The system is slow by design: power is shared, the seven-member government includes the major parties, and big decisions usually need broad consensus. Women only got the national vote in 1971. Neutrality, traditionally dated from 1815, is not the same as doing nothing: Switzerland has an army, and in 2022 it adopted sanctions against Russia, which started a fierce debate about what neutrality means today.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many signatures can force a national vote on the constitution?

2. Does neutrality mean having no army?

1. 100,000.

2. No — Switzerland has an army; neutrality means not taking sides in wars.

Discuss:
  • Is it fair that a small group can force a whole country to vote?

Grammar — Second & Third Conditionals

When we imagine how things could be or could have been different, we use conditionals.

How it works:
• Second (imagined present/future): If + past simple, would + infinitive: "If the Swiss voted less often, decisions would be faster."
• Third (imagined past): If + past perfect, would have + past participle: "If women had voted earlier, politics would have changed."
• Formal inversion: "Had they known, they would have acted." / "Were it not for the mountains, …"

1. Complete the conditional

Use the verb in brackets in the correct form.

1. If the Swiss (vote) less often, decisions (be) faster.

2. If women (get) the national vote earlier, Swiss politics (look) different.

3. If I (live) in Zurich, I (need) to understand Swiss German.

1. voted … would be

2. had got (had been given) … would have looked

3. lived … would need

2. Choose the right form

1. (I / know) about the Röstigraben, I wouldn't have been confused.

2. (it / not / be) for the mountains, the regions might have mixed more.

3. I would learn Romansh if there (be) more people to speak it with.

1. Had I known

2. Had it not been

3. were

Going deeper

The tidy Swiss image grows from what is exported: watches, chocolate and banking sell an idea of order and precision. Neutrality looks like standing apart, so it is easy to imagine a country that simply keeps out of everything. But the reality behind the order is a constant, slow negotiation between languages, regions and parties. What looks like calm from outside is a system built to avoid one group overruling another.

Worth noticing: Conditionals let you test a stereotype by imagining it differently — "If Switzerland weren't so small, would it still work this way?"
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Don't assume "Swiss" means German-speaking: ask which language someone prefers. Prepare well and be exact — details matter. Expect decisions to take time because many people are consulted, and don't read a slow process as a lack of interest.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Is slow, shared decision-making a strength or a weakness?
  • What would change in your country if citizens voted on big questions every few months?
  • Can a country be neutral and still take a moral stand?
