When science fiction grew up

By 1960, SF had rockets, robots and a devoted readership — but many writers felt it had a ceiling. The next sixty years would see the genre turn inward and outward at once: inward, to psychology, style and society; outward, to voices and futures the Golden Age had never imagined. This lesson follows that expansion — and looks closely at how SF is actually written.

The New Wave — Inner Space

In the 1960s, around Michael Moorcock's magazine New Worlds, a generation rebelled against the tidy optimism of hard SF. They swapped “outer space” for inner space — psychology, sex, drugs, entropy, style. SF became literary, experimental and often pessimistic. It stopped asking “how does the machine work?” and started asking “what is this machine doing to the human mind?”

Golden Age → New Wave: what changed?

In your own words, contrast the Golden Age (Part 1) with the New Wave. Think about subject, tone and style. Say it, then reveal.

Reveal
The Golden Age was outward, optimistic and plot-driven — competent heroes solving technical problems with plausible science. The New Wave turned inward, pessimistic and style-driven — interested in psychology, society and language, willing to break form and to doubt progress. Estrangement now pointed at the self, not just the cosmos.

New Voices, New Futures

The same decades opened the genre to writers it had long side-lined — and to the question SF too rarely asked: whose future is being imagined? Feminist SF, Afrofuturism and a wave of global writers turned the genre's estranging mirror on gender, race and power.

1937–2011

The Female Man (1975).
Feminist SF; four versions of one woman across four worlds.

1947–2006

b. 1939

Cyberpunk & the Digital Future

In 1984 — the year of Orwell's prophecy — William Gibson's Neuromancer gave SF a new nightmare and a new word: cyberspace. Cyberpunk's future was not clean and chrome but neon, rain-soaked and corporate: “high tech and low life.” Power lay not with governments but with multinationals; the hero was a hacker on the margins. It was the New Wave's pessimism plugged into the coming digital age — and it proved startlingly prescient.

Power

Mood

“High tech, low life.” Alienation, cool, paranoia.

The Writer's Toolkit — How SF Is Written

Beyond the big ideas of Part 1, SF has a set of prose-level techniques — the craft that builds a convincing world on the page. Learn to name these and you can analyse style, not just content. Tap the concept cards, then study the devices.

neologismAn invented word that signals a new world and forces the reader to infer its meaning — “cyberspace,” “ansible,” “grok.”
incluingRevealing a world by scattering concrete clues and letting the reader piece it together — the opposite of explaining.
the info-dumpA block of undramatised explanation (“As you know…”); the clumsy exposition skilled SF avoids.
the “used future”Making the future grubby, worn and ordinary so it feels lived-in and real, not a gleaming showroom.
Spot it live

Think of the last film or series you watched. Did it explain its world to you (an info-dump), or make you work it out from clues (incluing)? Which did you enjoy more — and why?

1. Neologism & coined vocabulary

Invent a word and the reader must reconstruct the world that would need it. The gap between the word and your understanding is the estrangement.

Gibson never fully explains “cyberspace”; we assemble it from use — and feel we've entered a real place.

2. Incluing vs the info-dump

Weak SF stops the story to explain the world (“As you know, Bob, the war ended in 2071…”). Strong SF inclues — drops you in and trusts you to infer from detail.

A character paying with “new dollars” tells you, without a lecture, that the old economy collapsed.

3. Defamiliarising description

Describe the ordinary as if newly seen, or literalise a metaphor, so the reader's attention snags. SF makes the invisible visible.

Describing a phone as a “slab of glass we speak to and obey” estranges an everyday object.

4. Tone as argument

Wonder, dread, irony — the emotional register carries the meaning. Dystopia leans on irony and dramatic foreboding; space opera on awe.

Atwood's flat, controlled narration makes The Handmaid's Tale more chilling than any shouting could.

Close Analysis — Tracing a Theme Across Time

The richest comparative essays trace one idea as it changes across eras. Take the genre's oldest anxiety — the fear of our own creations turning on us — and follow it.

1818 Frankenstein — the created being rebels against a careless maker. Anxiety: scientific hubris.
1920s “Robot” (from Čapek's play) & Asimov's Laws — can we rule what we build? Anxiety: control.
1968 Do Androids Dream… — if the machine feels, what makes us human? Anxiety: identity.
1984 Neuromancer — the AI wants to be free of us. Anxiety: autonomous intelligence.
Now Contemporary AI fiction — the fear made daily news. Anxiety: we already live inside the premise.

Build a comparative thesis

Draft one arguable sentence that compares how two of these treat the same fear — e.g. Shelley's Frankenstein and Dick's androids. It must make a claim, not just note a difference. Use a frame, then say yours aloud.

Thesis frames

  • “Where Shelley locates the danger in the maker's neglect, Dick relocates it to… — a shift that reflects…”
  • “Across a century and a half, the ‘creation turns on us’ story moves from… to…, revealing how our fear of technology has…”

The Big Debate

Choose two and argue them fully — position, evidence from today's writers, and a concession to the other side. The last is what turns a good argument into an excellent one.

Dystopia now feels more believable to many readers than utopia. What does that shift say about our own moment — and is a genre that struggles to imagine better futures failing at its job?

“Whose future gets imagined?” Why does it matter that Afrofuturist and global writers have reshaped SF? Is representation a literary question or a political one — or both?

Has science fiction become the most serious literature of our age — the only fiction really grappling with technology, climate and what comes next?

Cyberpunk predicted the shape of the internet age. Does SF shape the future by teaching us what to imagine, or only reflect anxieties we already have?

Turn it into a global issue

Pick one debate above and frame it as a global issue — a question that reaches beyond any single book, of real significance in the world (e.g. “who controls the technologies that will define our future?”). State the issue in one sentence, then argue how science fiction helps us think about it. Speak for two to three minutes.
