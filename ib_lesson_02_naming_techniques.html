Naming the Technique

Build the vocabulary examiners expect — fifteen authorial choices you will spot again and again

The fifteen techniques that do most of the work

You do not need to memorise a hundred techniques. Examiners see the same fifteen appearing across almost every IB text. Learn these — their definition and their typical effect on the reader — and you will have the vocabulary for 90% of what you need to say.

🎨

Imagery & comparison

1. Diction

▾

What: The author's deliberate word choice — formal vs informal, elevated vs plain, archaic vs modern.

✨ Typical effect: Sets tone, reveals character, signals the author's attitude toward the subject.

Orwell's "our Leader, Comrade Napoleon" — elevated, ritualised diction that exposes the regime's self-flattery.

2. Metaphor

▾

What: A direct comparison — A is B — without "like" or "as".

✨ Typical effect: Makes abstract ideas concrete; layers meaning; creates imagery.

"Hope is the thing with feathers" — makes the abstract emotion tangible.

3. Simile

▾

What: A comparison using "like" or "as".

✨ Typical effect: Helps the reader visualise; creates unexpected connections between unlike things.

"Her smile was cold as a server room" — makes an abstract feeling suddenly physical.

4. Personification

▾

What: Giving human qualities to non-human things.

✨ Typical effect: Creates emotional connection; makes the non-human feel threatening or comforting.

"The wind whispered" — makes nature feel intimate, almost conspiratorial.

5. Symbolism

▾

What: An object, colour, or image standing for something larger than itself.

✨ Typical effect: Adds depth; unifies themes; rewards the attentive reader.

The windmill in Animal Farm — symbolises the promise of the revolution, and later, its exploitation.

6. Allegory

▾

What: An entire story whose characters and events represent something else — politics, morality, history.

✨ Typical effect: Allows the author to comment on real-world power structures without naming them; invites the reader to decode.

Orwell's Animal Farm — the farm is the USSR; the pigs are the ruling class.

🎭

Irony & contrast

7. Irony (dramatic)

▾

What: The reader knows something a character does not.

✨ Typical effect: Creates tension, pity, or dark humour; positions the reader to judge.

We know the pigs have rewritten the commandments; Boxer still believes "Napoleon is always right". We watch with dread.

8. Irony (verbal)

▾

What: Saying the opposite of what is meant.

✨ Typical effect: Mocks; reveals the speaker's true attitude; creates distance.

The pigs calling themselves "Comrades" while eating at the farmer's table — the revolutionary word becomes a costume.

9. Juxtaposition

▾

What: Placing two contrasting images, ideas, or words side by side.

✨ Typical effect: Highlights difference; forces the reader to compare; intensifies both elements.

Orwell juxtaposes the animals' hunger with the pigs' milk and apples — the reader cannot unsee the contrast.

10. Hyperbole

▾

What: Deliberate exaggeration for effect.

✨ Typical effect: Intensifies emotion; overwhelms the reader; can amplify humour or horror.

"I have told you a thousand times" — everyday hyperbole; in literature, used sparingly for emphasis or mockery.

🗣️

Voice & structure

11. Narrative voice

▾

What: Who is telling the story — first person, third person, omniscient, limited.

✨ Typical effect: Controls what we know and when; creates sympathy or distance.

Orwell's detached third-person narrator lets Napoleon condemn himself through his own decrees — more damning than narration would be.

12. Tone

▾

What: The author's attitude toward the subject — ironic, reverent, mocking, warm, cold.

✨ Typical effect: Shapes how the reader feels about what they are reading, often before we notice.

Orwell's tone shifts from revolutionary idealism ("all animals are equal") to bureaucratic menace ("more equal than others"), pulling the reader from hope to disillusion.

13. Pacing

▾

What: The speed at which events unfold — short sentences for action, long for reflection.

✨ Typical effect: Controls urgency; creates tension or calm; manipulates the reader's heart rate.

Orwell uses staccato short sentences during the purges — each confession and execution landing with its own full stop — to numb the reader into compliance.

14. Repetition

▾

What: Repeating a word, phrase, or structure deliberately.

✨ Typical effect: Emphasises; builds rhythm; creates an echo the reader cannot ignore.

Orwell's Seven Commandments are repeated (and revised) so we notice each change.

15. Allusion

▾

What: A reference to another text, myth, historical event, or cultural figure.

✨ Typical effect: Pulls meaning from the referenced text into the new one; flatters the reader who catches it.

Butler's Kindred alludes to the slave narrative genre throughout — reframing a 20th-century woman's experience against 19th-century testimony.

Spotting game

Extract 1

"The wind picked at the banners all afternoon, tugging each slogan loose from its pole and letting it fall back to rest again, as if considering the words before agreeing to hold them up."
— Sample unseen extract (built for practice)

What is the dominant technique here?

Hyperbole Personification Simile Allegory
Answer: Personification

The wind is given human-like judgement — it "picks", "tugs", "considers", and "agrees". This personification unsettles the reader by making even the air seem to be weighing the regime's slogans, as if nature itself is a reluctant witness.

Extract 2

"All animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others."
— Orwell, Animal Farm

What is the dominant technique here?

Simile Verbal irony Paradox (a kind of juxtaposition) Allusion
Answer: Paradox (a form of juxtaposition)

The sentence holds two contradictory ideas side by side — "equal" and "more equal" — and dares the reader to reconcile them. It is the logical impossibility itself that makes the satire devastating. You could also defensibly answer "verbal irony" since the pigs do not mean what they say.

Extract 3

"…our Leader, our ever-watchful Comrade Napoleon, under whose wise guidance we shall thrive, prosper and be free."
— Sample speech in the style of Orwell's Squealer

What is the dominant technique here?

Metaphor Diction / elevated language Allegory Pacing
Answer: Diction / elevated language

The speaker's choice of ritualised, ceremonial words — "our Leader", "ever-watchful", "wise guidance", "thrive, prosper and be free" — elevates Napoleon's rule to mock-heroic heights. The vocabulary itself is the technique. The effect is that the regime incriminates itself through its own grandeur. You could also defend "dramatic irony" here because we readers see what the animals cannot — that the words are hollow, and the "freedom" is a cage.

Extract 4

"The farm was, in a sense, the USSR. The pigs were the ruling class. Every event echoed a real one."
— description of Orwell's method

What is Orwell using across his entire novel?

Metaphor Allegory Hyperbole Personification
Answer: Allegory

A whole story where every character and event systematically stands for something else is allegory, not just metaphor. (A metaphor is local — a single comparison. An allegory runs through the entire work.) This distinction matters: examiners reward students who know the difference.

One passage, all the tools — what is Orwell doing here?

The moment the pigs begin walking on two legs. Read it carefully, then identify every technique you can find. This is the kind of close-work Criterion A rewards.

"It was a pig walking on his hind legs. Yes, it was Squealer. A little awkwardly, as though not quite used to supporting his considerable bulk in that position, but with perfect balance, he was strolling across the yard. And a moment later, out from the door of the farmhouse came a long file of pigs, all walking on their hind legs."

What Orwell is doing here

Click each technique to see how it operates in this passage.

Pacing (slow-motion reveal)

▾

Where: "It was a pig walking on his hind legs. Yes, it was Squealer."

✨ Effect: The two short sentences force the reader to register the transgression in slow motion — a beat for recognition, a beat for confirmation. The confirming "Yes" refuses to let the reader disbelieve what they are seeing.

Parenthetical qualifier

▾

Where: "A little awkwardly, as though not quite used to supporting his considerable bulk in that position…"

✨ Effect: The embedded acknowledgement of awkwardness humanises the gesture. Orwell refuses to let us pretend the pigs have simply "become" human; we have to watch them practise it, which is far more disturbing than a clean transformation.

Repetition / accumulation

▾

Where: "a long file of pigs, all walking on their hind legs."

✨ Effect: The phrase "hind legs" is deliberately repeated. The reader is forced to witness the transgression twice, and the plural "all" turns a single violation into a procession. The taboo has become the new routine.

Tonal irony

▾

Where: "with perfect balance, he was strolling across the yard."

✨ Effect: "Strolling" is a leisure verb — casual, entitled, at ease. It clashes violently with the taboo being broken. The narrator's tone endorses Squealer's composure even as the reader recoils. The ironic gap between narrator's ease and reader's horror is the technique doing the real work.

The examiner's reward: a student who names four techniques in a paragraph this short, and explains the effect of each, is operating at Band 7. Most students find one. Your job is to train your eye to see them all.

Your turn — annotate a passage

Read the Orwell extract below. In the box, list every authorial technique you can identify. Then, next to each technique, write one sentence about its effect on the reader. Aim for four techniques minimum.

"The creatures outside looked from pig to man, and from man to pig, and from pig to man again; but already it was impossible to say which was which."
— Orwell, Animal Farm, final sentence

Your annotations

Four techniques in Orwell's closing line

1. Repetition / anaphora: "from pig to man, and from man to pig, and from pig to man again" — the repeated grammatical structure performs the very confusion it describes. The reader's eye swings back and forth.

2. Pacing: the long, breathless single sentence, broken only by semicolons, forces the reader to read it as one exhausted unbroken gaze.

3. Juxtaposition: "pig" and "man" are placed in endless alternation until they dissolve. Orwell puts the two species side by side so many times that the reader cannot tell them apart either.

4. Closing irony: the novel opened with animals rebelling against the cruelty of men; it ends with animals indistinguishable from men. The irony, placed in the last line, positions the reader to feel defeat.

Notice: every technique here is about creating the same effect — the collapse of difference. When an author uses multiple techniques to point at one meaning, that is what examiners call unity of effect, and it is the hallmark of sophisticated writing (and sophisticated analysis).
