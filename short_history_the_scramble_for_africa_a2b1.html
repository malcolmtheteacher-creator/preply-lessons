The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — Europe takes Africa

For a long time, the countries of Europe knew only the edges of Africa, near the sea. They came to buy and sell things like gold and ivory (the white tusks of elephants). But the big lands inside Africa were new to them.

Then, in only about thirty years (from around 1880 to 1914), this changed very fast. A few strong countries in Europe — like Britain, France, Germany, Belgium and Italy — took almost all of Africa. By 1914, only two countries in Africa were still free: Ethiopia and Liberia.

Why did they do it? They wanted the land, the gold, and other things from Africa for their factories at home. They also wanted to be bigger and stronger than the other European countries. New guns and new machines made it easy for small European armies to take big areas of land.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. In about which years did Europe take almost all of Africa? Which two countries stayed free?

2. Why did the European countries want Africa?

1. From about 1880 to 1914. Ethiopia and Liberia stayed free.

2. They wanted the land, the gold and other things for their factories. They also wanted to be bigger and stronger than the other European countries.

Talk about it:
  • The European countries wanted both money and power. Which one do you think was more important to them? Why?

Part 2 — A map and no Africans

The European countries were taking more and more land in Africa. They started to worry: maybe they would fight each other over it. So in the winter of 1884 and 1885, they had a big meeting in Berlin, in Germany. This is called the Berlin Conference.

At this meeting, the European countries made rules. The rules were not for the people of Africa — they were for the Europeans. The rules said how each country could take a part of Africa without starting a war with the other European countries.

There is one very important fact about this meeting: there were no Africans there. Not one. The Europeans looked at a map and drew lines on it. These lines became the borders of new colonies. They drew the lines straight across rivers, deserts and homes, and they did not think about the people who lived there. Sometimes one line cut a family or a group into two different countries. At this same meeting, they gave a huge area near the Congo River to one man — King Leopold II of Belgium — to keep as his own.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the European countries meet in Berlin? Who was not there?

2. How did they decide the borders of the new colonies?

1. They met (in 1884–85) to make rules so they would not fight each other over Africa. No Africans were there.

2. They drew lines on a map, straight across rivers, deserts and homes, without thinking about the people who lived there.

Talk about it:
  • The Europeans drew borders without asking the people who lived there. Do you think that was fair? Why or why not?

Part 3 — Cruelty, and fighting back

Taking Africa was often very cruel. The worst place was the land of King Leopold II, near the Congo River. The king never went there, but he made the people work to collect rubber for him. If they did not collect enough, they were punished in terrible ways. Many, many people suffered and died. Later, even other Europeans were shocked when they learned the truth. Leopold became very rich.

But this is not only a story of taking and cruelty. The people of Africa also fought back. The Africans were neither asked nor invited to the meeting in Berlin — so many of them said no with their actions. When a European army came, an African leader could either agree or fight, and many chose to fight. They defended their land for years.

The most famous example is Ethiopia. In 1896, Italy tried to take Ethiopia. But the Ethiopian leader, Emperor Menelik II, had a big, strong army. At the Battle of Adwa, the Ethiopians won. They beat a European country in a real battle. Because of this, Ethiopia stayed free. Adwa became a symbol all over Africa: it showed that Europe could be beaten.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal. (One question asks you to put events in order.)

1. What happened in the land of King Leopold II?

2. Put these three events in the right order: (a) the Battle of Adwa; (b) the Berlin Conference; (c) by 1914 Europe has taken almost all of Africa.

1. The people were made to collect rubber for the king. If they did not collect enough, they were punished in terrible ways. Many people died, and Leopold became rich.

2. b (Berlin Conference, 1884–85) → a (Battle of Adwa, 1896) → c (by 1914 almost all of Africa is taken).

Talk about it:
  • At Adwa, a smaller country beat a stronger one. Why do you think this victory was so important for other people in Africa?

The lines that stayed

By 1914, almost all of Africa belonged to Europe. Many years later, after the Second World War, the countries of Africa became free again, one by one. But here is the strange part: most of them kept the same borders — the same lines the Europeans had drawn on the map in Berlin.

So many of the borders on the map of Africa today come from those old European lines. The men who drew them died long ago, but their lines are still there, and they still touch the lives of many millions of people.

Talk about it:
  • Many borders in Africa today come from lines that strangers drew over a hundred years ago. Do you think these borders should change now, or is it too late?

Grammar: both…and / either…or / neither…nor

This story is full of pairs — two countries, two choices, two free lands. English has three nice ways to join two things in one sentence.

both … and = two things together (this one AND that one).
"Both Britain and France took land in Africa." (Britain took land. France took land. → both.)
either … or = two choices (this one OR that one).
"The people could either agree or fight." (They had two choices: agree, or fight.)
neither … nor = NOT this one and NOT that one (two negatives).
"The Africans were neither asked nor invited." (They were not asked. They were not invited. → neither … nor.)
Note: with neither … nor we do not use "not" again — the words neither and nor already make it negative.

1. Choose the right pair

Put both…and, either…or or neither…nor in each gap so the meaning is right.

1. Britain France took land in Africa.

2. The Africans were asked invited to the meeting.

3. When the army came, a leader could agree fight.

1. both … and (two things together — both countries took land)

2. neither … nor (not asked and not invited — two negatives)

3. either … or (two choices — agree or fight)

2. Finish the sentence

2. nor (neither … nor)

3. or (either … or)
