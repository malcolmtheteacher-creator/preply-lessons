BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

The Last Emperor

A boy of two is carried into a palace and told he is a god. For the next fifty years, other people decide what he is to do next.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of The Last Emperor — a child raised as a living god who ends his life digging flowerbeds — discover how much of it is documented history and where the film softens the record, and master the structure English uses for a future somebody else has arranged for you: be to + infinitive, with be about to and be due to beside it.
Before we begin — talk it through: How much of your life so far was decided by other people — where you were born, what you studied, the work you do? If someone had arranged every single thing for you from birth, would you still be responsible for how your life turned out? And is there anything you would refuse to do, whatever the pressure?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. The pressure had been building for months. In the end the king abdicated: he signed the papers one grey morning, walked out through the gates, and was never head of state again.
to abdicate (v) — to give up a throne or high office formally; to stop being king, queen or emperor.
2. After the scandal she lived in complete seclusion — no visitors, no telephone, no letters answered, and years in which almost nobody saw her face.
seclusion (n) — the state of being kept away from other people, alone and out of sight. Adjective: secluded.
3. The new chairman turned out to be a figurehead. He sat at the head of the table and signed whatever was put in front of him, while the three men behind him decided everything.
a figurehead (n) — a leader in name and appearance only, with no real power over what happens.
4. The court spent weeks on the question of his complicity: had he merely been in the building that night, or had he known what was planned and quietly helped it happen?
complicity (n) — involvement in something wrong or criminal done by others; a share in the guilt. Adjective: complicit.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. to renounce is closest in meaning to: (a) to inherit   (b) to formally give up   (c) to repeat
(b) to formally give up — to renounce (v) means to state publicly that you are abandoning a claim, a belief, a title or a former loyalty.
6. contrition is closest in meaning to: (a) deep regret for what you have done   (b) pride in what you have built   (c) confusion about what happened
(a) deep regret for what you have done — contrition (n) is sincere sorrow and shame for one's own wrongdoing. Adjective: contrite.

C. Which meaning fits here? (choose a, b or c)

7. During the occupation a number of local officials collaborated, and when the war ended they were put on trial for it.
Here, to collaborate means: (a) to work together with colleagues on a project; (b) to help an enemy army that is occupying your country; (c) to refuse an order and accept the punishment.
(b) — to collaborate (v) has two lives. In ordinary use it simply means to work jointly with someone. In a war or occupation it means to cooperate with the enemy — and the noun for the person is a collaborator.
8. He believed he was running the negotiations. In truth he was a pawn, moved from city to city by people whose names he never learned.
Here, a pawn is: (a) the most powerful piece in a game; (b) a person used by others for their own purposes; (c) a payment made in advance.
(b) — a pawn (n) is the smallest piece in chess, and so, figuratively, someone who is used and moved about by more powerful people.

D. Collocation and odd-one-out

9. Nothing at that court happened by accident: every bow, every greeting, the order people entered a room, even the number of dishes at dinner, was fixed by centuries-old protocol.
Which is the natural collocation? (a) to observe protocol   (b) to hold protocol   (c) to do protocol
(a) to observe protocol — protocol (n) is the official system of rules governing correct behaviour on formal occasions. We observe or follow protocol; we breach or break it.
10. Odd one out. Three of these describe something settled in advance, by someone or something other than you. One is the opposite. Which is the odd one?
preordained   ·   inevitable   ·   chosen   ·   destined
chosen is the odd one — it implies you decided. preordained (adj) — decided or fixed before it happens, so that nothing can alter it; inevitable — impossible to avoid; destined — certain to end up a particular way.

The film, in three parts

Bernardo Bertolucci's The Last Emperor (1987). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — The god in the walled city

Winter, 1908. Soldiers come at night for a child of two. His mother is not allowed to go with him. He is carried through gate after gate into the Forbidden City, the vast walled palace at the centre of Beijing, and set on a throne in a hall so large he cannot see the end of it. Thousands of officials kneel. The boy, Puyi, climbs down and runs towards a curtain moving in the wind. He is now the Son of Heaven, and he is frightened.

Four years later the empire ends. Outside, China becomes a republic; inside, almost nothing changes. Puyi keeps his title, his eunuchs, his court, his protocol — but only within the walls. He grows up ruler of a country that consists of a few courtyards, waited on by hundreds of people who must obey him in everything except the one thing he wants, which is to go out. His little brother tells him, cheerfully, that he is not emperor of anything any more.

In 1919 a Scottish tutor arrives: Reginald Johnston, who brings spectacles, a bicycle, the English name Henry, and news of a world with taxis and telephones in it. Puyi cycles round the courtyards, expels the eunuchs after a fire, marries a bride chosen from photographs, and dreams of studying at Oxford. Then, in 1924, soldiers arrive at the gate and give the household a few hours. The doors close behind him. He is eighteen, and he has never chosen anything.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happens to Puyi's status in 1912, and why does his daily life barely change?

2. What does the arrival of Johnston open up for him?

1. The empire is abolished and China becomes a republic, but he keeps the title and the whole court inside the palace walls — so he goes on being treated as emperor of a place a few hundred metres across.

2. A window onto the outside world: Western learning, a bicycle, glasses, an English name, and the idea that he might one day leave and study abroad.

Discuss:
  • Everyone in the palace obeys the boy, and the boy can never leave. Is that power, or is it the most elaborate prison you have ever heard of?

Part 2 — The offer he could not refuse, and did not want to

Expelled, Puyi lands in the foreign quarter of Tianjin and reinvents himself: dinner jackets, jazz, tennis, a wife and a secondary consort, photographs in the newspapers. But he wants his throne back, and the only people who take that ambition seriously are Japanese officers, who visit, flatter him and wait. His wife Wanrong begs him to see what they are. He does not listen.

Japan seizes Manchuria in the north, and in the early 1930s Puyi goes to them. He is installed as head of a new state called Manchukuo, and in 1934 he is crowned emperor again in a ceremony that copies the old rituals and means nothing. Very quickly he discovers the shape of his new job: documents arrive already decided; his ministers answer to Japanese officials; his own guards report on him. He signs. Outside his palace, the occupation does what occupations do.

Wanrong collapses into opium. When she bears a child that is not his, the baby is taken away and she is taken away too, and the last he sees of her is a car pulling out of a courtyard. In 1945 Japan surrenders. Puyi, in a small airport waiting room, is about to fly to safety when Soviet soldiers walk in. The film handles all of this quietly: no battles, just doors opening and closing on a man who has never once been in the room where things are decided.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does Puyi accept the Japanese offer, and what does he actually get?

2. How does his time as emperor of Manchukuo end?

1. He wants his throne back and believes he is being restored. What he gets is a crown with no authority — a figurehead in a state run by the Japanese military, signing decisions taken without him.

2. Japan surrenders in 1945; as he waits to be flown out, Soviet troops arrive and take him prisoner.

Discuss:
  • He is used by the Japanese — and he walked towards them with his eyes open, because they offered him what he wanted. Does being used excuse you from what your name was used for?

Part 3 — From emperor to gardener

The film's frame is a prison. In 1950 Puyi is handed back to China and taken to a re-education centre, where he is one number among many. He cannot dress himself; he has never tied his own shoes. Over ten years, in interrogations and in the confession he is required to write, his whole life is taken apart and read back to him by a patient, unyielding governor who wants him to say plainly what he was part of.

The confession is the heart of the film. Puyi wants to be a victim — a child put on a throne, a man moved by others — and much of that is true. But he is also asked about the years when his signature sat on the papers of an occupation, and about the people who suffered under a state that carried his name. What the film is really examining is whether contrition is possible for someone who was never taught that his choices had weight.

He is released in 1959. The last emperor of China becomes a gardener in Beijing, in a cotton jacket, buying his own tram tickets, tending flowerbeds. Years later, an old man pays to enter the Forbidden City as an ordinary visitor, steps over the rope, and reaches behind the throne where he once sat. It is Bertolucci's most beautiful invention, and it says the thing the whole film has been circling: the palace is now a museum, and the god is a man like anyone else.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is Puyi required to do during his years in the re-education centre?

2. What has become of him by the end of the film?

1. Live as an ordinary prisoner, learn to look after himself, submit to years of interrogation, and write a full confession of his own life — including the Manchukuo years.

2. An ordinary Beijing citizen working as a gardener, who at the end returns to his former palace as a paying visitor.

Discuss:
  • The state that imprisoned him also decided what his confession should contain. Can a confession produced under those conditions still be honest? Can it still be useful?

Grammar — be to + infinitive (the arranged future)

Some futures you choose. Others are handed to you already decided — by a court, a government, an army, a family. English has a structure for exactly that: be to + infinitive. It is the language of announcements, of orders, and — in the past — of fate. No life needs it more than this one.

1. The official, arranged future — am / is / are to + infinitive
Formal, and very common in news and announcements: "The ceremony is to take place at dawn." "The delegation is to arrive on Friday." "The palace is to be closed to the public." Notice the passive: is to be + past participle.
It also carries instructions and orders, usually strict ones: "No one is to enter the inner courtyard." "The boy is to be addressed as Your Majesty."
2. The past of fate — was / were to + infinitive
This is the one that makes storytellers reach for it. Standing in the past, looking forward to something the person could not yet know: "The three-year-old on the throne was to lose his country three times over." "They parted at the station, and they were never to meet again." Note the position: was never to see.
And for an arrangement that didn't happen: was to have + past participle — "He was to have studied at Oxford, but the soldiers came first."
3. Two neighbours you need alongside it
• be about to + infinitive = on the very edge of happening, seconds away: "He was about to board the plane when the door opened."
• be due to + infinitive = scheduled, expected at a set time: "The train is due to leave at six." "She is due to give evidence tomorrow."
Be to = decided by an authority. Be due to = on the timetable. Be about to = imminent.

The Real Story — the man, and the question of choice

Almost every improbable turn in this film is documented. Puyi was a real man with a real, verifiable life, and the film draws on the autobiography he produced himself. What the record does not settle is the question the film is built around: how much choice does a person raised as a god ever really have — and how much of the blame is still his?

🐉 A life arranged by other people, in dates

Aisin-Gioro Puyi was born on 7 February 1906 and enthroned in December 1908, at two years and ten months old. He was forced to abdicate on 12 February 1912, ending 268 years of Qing rule and China's imperial system, which had run for more than two thousand years. Under the Articles of Favourable Treatment he kept the imperial title and the northern part of the Forbidden City — so a six-year-old went on being addressed as emperor inside a palace that no longer ruled anything. In July 1917 a warlord briefly restored him; that restoration lasted about twelve days. He was expelled from the palace for good on 5 November 1924 by the warlord Feng Yuxiang, and moved to the Japanese concession in Tianjin in February 1925.

Reginald Johnston was real — and so was the book

Sir Reginald Fleming Johnston was born in Edinburgh and arrived in March 1919 as tutor to the thirteen-year-old Puyi, brought in for his knowledge of Chinese history and culture. He introduced Western subjects and, by most accounts, encouraged both his pupil's monarchism and his interest in Japan. He left in 1924, later became Professor of Chinese at the School of Oriental and African Studies in London, retired to a Scottish island, and died in Edinburgh in 1938. His memoir Twilight in the Forbidden City was published in 1934 and remains one of the very few eyewitness accounts of the court's last years — written, of course, by a man who loved his pupil. In the film he is played by Peter O'Toole.

Manchukuo: the part where "victim" stops being the whole answer

Japan's Kwantung Army seized Manchuria after the Mukden Incident of September 1931. On 1 March 1932 the puppet state of Manchukuo was proclaimed with Puyi as Chief Executive; on 1 March 1934 he was crowned its emperor under the reign name Kangde. Real power never came near him: the Kwantung Army commander could veto his decisions, and each Manchukuo minister worked under a Japanese vice-minister who set the actual policy. The League of Nations found that Japan had acted in breach of international law, and only a handful of states ever recognised the regime.

And that regime's record is not a background detail. Millions of Chinese civilians were put to forced labour in its mines and factories; farmland was confiscated; the state ran an opium monopoly; and it was on Manchukuo territory that Japan's Unit 731 carried out lethal human experimentation. Puyi did not order these things and could not have stopped them. But he was not dragged north in chains either: he wanted his throne back, the Japanese were the only people offering one, and he put his name and the prestige of the Qing house at the service of an occupation for thirteen years. That makes him a collaborator as well as a victim, and the film is gentler about this than the record is.

Capture, testimony, prison — and a very unusual old age

Soviet forces took Puyi prisoner in August 1945. In 1946 he appeared as a witness at the International Military Tribunal for the Far East in Tokyo, testifying across eight days — the longest appearance by any witness there. Observers found him evasive: he presented himself as a wholly controlled front for the Japanese, was vague about dates and documents, and at one point asked not to be questioned about dates at all. His own later autobiography concedes more knowledge, and more willingness, than he admitted on the stand.

He was handed to China in 1950 and held at the Fushun War Criminals Management Centre, where Manchukuo, Kuomintang and Japanese prisoners underwent years of intensive "thought reform". He received a special pardon on 4 December 1959, among the first group of thirty-one prisoners released. He then worked at the Beijing Botanical Garden — first in the mechanical repair shop, then as an assistant gardener — married a nurse, Li Shuxian, in 1962, and later worked as a researcher on historical texts. He died in Beijing on 17 October 1967, aged 61. His wife the empress Wanrong, who is in the film, did not survive the fall: she died on 20 June 1946 at Yanji in Jilin, aged 39, of malnutrition and opium withdrawal, and her burial place is unknown.

Where the film leaves the record

The screenplay is adapted from From Emperor to Citizen, the autobiography published under Puyi's name in 1964 and worked into shape by the editor Li Wenda. That is exactly why historians handle it carefully: it was produced by a pardoned prisoner, inside the political conditions of the state that had re-educated him, and roughly 160,000 words were cut from the manuscript before publication — a fuller version only appeared in 2007. A confession is not a neutral source, and this film's foundations are a confession.

Specific departures worth naming: the film considerably softens Puyi's cruelty as a young man in the palace; it moves his real interrogation, which took place before the Tokyo tribunal, into the hands of Chinese questioners; and its final image — an old man buying a ticket into his own former palace and finding a cricket still alive beneath the throne — is invention, and lovely, and not something that happened. What is entirely true is the film's own place in history: The Last Emperor was the first Western feature permitted by the People's Republic of China to shoot inside the Forbidden City, and it won all nine Academy Awards for which it was nominated at the 1988 ceremony, including Best Picture and Best Director.

  • China's official verdict was that a man can be remade: emperor to war criminal to gardener to citizen. Do you believe in that story, or is it too tidy?
  • Should a film be allowed to build a portrait of a real collaborator on that person's own supervised memoir?

Task B · Victim or collaborator? (4 minutes)

Build the case. He was taken from his mother at two, never allowed out, never taught that his choices had consequences — and he then lent his name for thirteen years to an occupying power whose regime used mass forced labour and ran human experiments. Argue one side properly: is he principally a man who was used, or a man who chose badly and should be judged for it? Then argue the other side as well as you can. Bring in comparisons if you know any — other figureheads, other collaborators, other people tried after a war.

Task C · How much of a life is chosen? (4 minutes)

Step back from the film. How much of any life is actually decided by the person living it — and where do you personally draw the line between explanation and excuse? Make your case.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Whether children of famous or powerful families are freer or less free than everyone else.
  • A historical figure you think has been judged too harshly, and why.
  • Whether a country should try to re-educate its enemies rather than punish them.
  • How much of a person's career is decided by where and when they happened to be born.
  • Whether a museum should tell the story of the people who lived in it, or only show the building.
  • A book or film that made you change your mind about someone you had already judged.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. He spent fifty years being told what he was to do next, and then spent his last eight years choosing what to plant. Which of those two lives would you call the more successful?
  2. The film's kindest idea is that a person can be completely remade. The uncomfortable version is that a state decided what he should become, twice — once as a god and once as a gardener. Was he ever free?
  3. The whole film rests on a memoir written under the eye of the government that imprisoned him. Does that make it a lie, a document, or something in between?
