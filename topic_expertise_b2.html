The Knowledge

London's taxi drivers spend years memorising 25,000 streets — and it changes their brains. This is the true story of how experts are made, and of the famous 10,000-hour rule that turned out to be only part of the story.

Part 1 — The Knowledge

If you want to drive one of London's famous black cabs, you must first pass one of the hardest memory tests in the world. It is called the Knowledge. Trainees must learn the layout of about 25,000 streets within six miles of Charing Cross station in central London — plus the locations of thousands of places: hotels, hospitals, theatres, restaurants. It usually takes three to four years, and ends with a series of tough exams, called "appearances".

Scientists at University College London, led by the neuroscientist Eleanor Maguire, became interested in the drivers' brains. In earlier studies, they had found that experienced London taxi drivers had more grey matter in the back part of the hippocampus — an area of the brain that is important for memory and finding your way — than other people. But there was a problem. Maybe people with that kind of brain simply became taxi drivers. Was it nature, or training?

So Maguire and her colleague Katherine Woollett followed 79 men who were just starting the Knowledge, plus 31 men of similar age who weren't, as a comparison group. They scanned their brains and tested their memories at the start, and again three to four years later. At the start, there were no differences between the groups.

By the end, only 39 of the trainees had qualified — a normal result, since only about half of trainees ever do. In the men who qualified, the back part of the hippocampus had grown. In the trainees who had failed or given up, and in the comparison group, it hadn't changed. The brain had been changed by the training. But there was a price: the qualified drivers were better at London-based memory tests, but worse than the others at remembering certain kinds of new visual information.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What do trainees have to learn for the Knowledge, and how long does it take?

2. How did the study show that the training changed the brain, rather than the brain choosing the job?

1. About 25,000 streets within six miles of Charing Cross, and thousands of places. It usually takes three to four years.

2. The researchers tested the trainees before and after training. At the start there were no differences. Only the men who qualified showed growth in the hippocampus.

Part 2 — The violinists of Berlin

Years before the taxi study, another piece of research had made practice famous. In a paper published in 1993, the psychologist Anders Ericsson and two colleagues, Ralf Krampe and Clemens Tesch-Römer, described their study of young violinists at the Music Academy of West Berlin. The professors there helped to choose three groups of ten: the best violinists, who had the potential for careers as international soloists; good violinists; and students who were training to become music teachers.

The researchers asked the musicians to estimate how much they had practised alone at every age since they had started. The results were very clear. By the age of 18, the best violinists had practised on average about 7,400 hours. The good violinists had practised about 5,300 hours, and the future teachers about 3,400. By 20, the best group had passed 10,000 hours.

Ericsson concluded that the key was not just practice, but deliberate practice: hard, focused work designed to overcome your weaknesses, with careful attention to feedback. It requires effort, they wrote, and it is not enjoyable in itself. By 2014, the paper had been cited more than 4,200 times.

Then, in 2008, the writer Malcolm Gladwell discussed this research in his popular book Outliers, and gave the idea a catchy name: the 10,000-hour rule — the idea that it takes 10,000 hours of practice to become an expert. The phrase became famous far beyond psychology.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the three groups of violinists differ in practice by the age of 18?

2. What is "deliberate practice"?

1. The best had practised about 7,400 hours, the good about 5,300, and the future teachers about 3,400.

2. Hard, focused work designed to overcome your weaknesses, with attention to feedback — not just repeating what you can already do or playing for fun.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think the "10,000-hour rule" became so popular? What is attractive about it?

Part 3 — What practice can't explain

If practice is the main reason some people are better than others, scientists should be able to see it in the numbers. In 2014, three psychologists — Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald — checked. They combined the results of 88 studies with more than 11,000 people, from chess players to musicians, athletes, students and professionals.

Practice mattered — but much less than the rule suggests. Deliberate practice explained about 26 percent of the differences in performance in games like chess, 21 percent in music, and 18 percent in sports. In education it explained just 4 percent. And in professions — the studies included computer programmers, military pilots, football referees and insurance salespeople — it explained less than 1 percent. In other words, most of the difference between people came from other things.

What other things? The researchers suggested several possibilities, such as the age at which people start and general abilities like intelligence and working memory — the ability to hold information in your mind while you use it. They also found that practice mattered more for very predictable activities, like running, than for unpredictable ones, like dealing with an emergency in a plane.

And in 2019, Macnamara and a colleague, Megha Maitra, repeated the Berlin violin study itself. Practice still mattered — the effect was substantial — but it was considerably smaller than in the original, and the amount of practice did not line up neatly with each skill level as it had in 1993. The message of both studies: practice is important. It just isn't the whole story.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How much of the difference in performance did practice explain in games, music, sports, education and professions?

2. What did the 2019 repeat of the violin study find?

1. About 26% in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and less than 1% in professions.

2. Practice still mattered, but the effect was considerably smaller than in the original, and the amount of practice did not line up neatly with each skill level.

Grammar — talking about ability: can, could, be able to, manage to

The story is all about ability — what the drivers could remember, what the violinists were able to do, what practice can and can't explain.

Present and future
• can / can't for present ability: "She can play the violin."
• will be able to for future ability (there is no future of can): "After the Knowledge, you'll be able to find any street." ✗ will can
• be able to after other verbs and in perfect tenses: "I'd like to be able to speak Japanese." · "I haven't been able to finish it."
Past — general ability vs one success
• could = a general ability in the past: "At six, she could already read music."
• was able to / managed to = success on one particular occasion, often after difficulty: "After four years, he was able to / managed to pass the final exam." ✗ he could pass the final exam
• In the negative, couldn't is fine for both: "He couldn't pass the exam."

Don't mix it up: manage to suggests it was difficult: "I managed to finish the report" = it was hard, but I did it.

Going deeper

The studies in this story are real, and the numbers come from the researchers who did them. Together, they give a more complicated — and more honest — answer to the question how are experts made?

Three big ideas from the story.

1. Training really does change us — even as adults. The taxi study is one of the clearest examples that years of hard learning can physically change the adult brain. The trainees started with the same brains and memories as the comparison group. Only the ones who completed the work showed the change. Interestingly, when the researchers asked trainees why they stopped, very few said the memory work was too difficult; most mentioned time, money or family.

2. Expertise has a cost. The qualified drivers became better at one kind of memory and worse at another. Ten thousand hours on one thing is ten thousand hours not spent on something else. Every expert makes a trade.

3. A good rule is not the same as a true rule. "10,000 hours" is easy to remember and gives hope: anyone can be great if they work hard enough. The evidence says practice matters a lot in some areas and very little in others — and that other things, such as starting age and general abilities, also play a part. That is less inspiring, but more useful when you are choosing where to spend your time.
Talk it through:
  • If practice explains less than 1% of the difference between professionals, what does make a great salesperson, manager or engineer?
  • Should companies hire for talent, or for willingness to learn? Can you test either?
  • Is it unfair to tell people that anyone can become an expert with enough practice? Who could that message hurt?
  • The Knowledge takes years; GPS takes seconds. Which human skills do you think are worth keeping, even when a machine can do the job?
