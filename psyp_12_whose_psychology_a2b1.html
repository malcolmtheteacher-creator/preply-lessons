Whose Psychology Is It?

Most of what we know about the mind comes from a small part of the world. Does it matter?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — Who takes part?

In 2010, three researchers, Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan, looked at many psychology papers from top journals. They asked: who were the participants in these studies?

The answer was surprising. About 96% of the participants came from Western countries, such as the USA and Europe. These countries have only about 12% of the people in the world. And 68% were from the USA alone. Also, about two thirds of the American samples were psychology students.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What percentage of participants came from Western countries?

2. What percentage of the world lives in these countries?

1. About 96%.

2. About 12%.

Discuss:
  • Are you surprised? Does it change how you read a psychology book?

Part 2 — WEIRD people

The researchers made a joke with the letters. They said that most participants are WEIRD: Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich and Democratic. People like this are only a small part of humanity.

Is this a problem? Yes, if WEIRD people think in a different way from other people. And the researchers found that sometimes they do. For example, in an old test of an optical illusion with two lines, people in some small societies saw the lines in a very different way from American students. In games about money and fairness, people from different societies made very different choices.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does WEIRD mean?

2. Give one example of a difference.

1. Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich and Democratic.

2. In the optical illusion or in the fairness games, people in different societies gave different answers.

Discuss:
  • What do you think a WEIRD sample cannot tell us?

Part 3 — What can we do?

Some psychologists answered: many results are probably true in many places, such as basic vision or memory. But for other areas, for example ideas about the self, morality and family, culture is very important. We must check, not guess.

Today more researchers work together across many countries. Some journals ask authors to describe their sample clearly and say who the results are for. In clinical work, this means: take care with tests and treatments that were made in one country, and ask about the culture and the history of the client.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which results are probably true in many places?

2. What can clinicians do?

1. Basic things such as vision or memory.

2. Take care with tests and treatments from another culture, and ask about the client's culture and history.

Discuss:
  • What is the most useful question to ask a client about their culture?

Grammar — numbers, percentages and comparisons

Use these structures to describe numbers and to compare groups.

Numbers and percentages:
• 12% = twelve per cent; 68% = sixty-eight per cent
• about / around / nearly / over / more than / less than: About 96% of participants were Western.
• half, a third, two thirds, a quarter: Two thirds of the samples were students.
• X out of Y: Sixty out of a hundred.
Comparisons:
• more … than, less … than, fewer … than: There are fewer people in the sample than in the world.
• as many … as, as much … as: There are as many men as women.
• the most / the least: The USA has the most participants.
Use fewer with countable nouns and less with uncountable nouns.

Does it apply?

Five situations. Say whether the result will probably be the same in other cultures. Then reveal an idea.

1. A test of memory for numbers made in the USA. Will it work well in other countries?
Idea. Probably, with a good translation. Basic memory is quite similar. But the way people learn numbers may differ.
2. A test about feeling 'proud of yourself'. Will it work well everywhere?
Idea. Maybe not. In some cultures people think more about the group than about themselves.
3. A treatment that asks the client to say what she wants, directly. Will it work in all families?
Idea. It depends on the culture and on the family. Some clients prefer a more indirect way.
4. A description of 'normal' child sleep from one country. Does it apply everywhere?
Idea. No. Habits are different. In some places, children sleep with their parents.
5. A questionnaire is translated word by word. Is it the same test?
Idea. Not always. Words can have different meanings, so researchers need to check it.
