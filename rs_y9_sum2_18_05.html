Recall: GCSE Command Words

Terminology review (5 minutes)
5:00

GCSE examiners use specific command words. Understanding them is half the battle.

What does "Give" mean on a GCSE exam?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Give = state one fact or point briefly (usually 1 mark). No explanation needed; just the answer. Example: "Give one teaching about forgiveness." Answer: "Jesus taught to forgive seventy times seven."

What does "Describe" mean?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Describe = explain what something is or provide details (usually 1–2 marks). Include relevant characteristics or aspects. Example: "Describe what Muslims believe about the Qur'an." Include: revealed to Muhammad, literal word of Allah, primary source of Islamic law.

What does "Explain" mean?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Explain = give reasons why something is true or important (usually 4 marks). Use "because" and link belief to practice or consequence. Example: "Explain why Muslims fast during Ramadan." Include: remembrance, discipline, empathy for the poor.

What's the difference between "Explain" and "Evaluate"?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Explain = reasons why [belief/practice] matters. Evaluate = judge the strength of ideas, consider multiple perspectives, make a reasoned judgment about whether something is true/right/good.

GCSE Command Words Explained

Give (1 mark)

What it asks: State one point or fact.

How to answer: One sentence, no explanation needed.

Example Q: "Give one Christian belief about God."
Example A: "God is omnipotent (all-powerful)."

Describe (1 mark)

What it asks: Explain what something is; provide relevant details or characteristics.

How to answer: 2–3 sentences with specific information.

Example Q: "Describe what the Trinity is."
Example A: "The Trinity is the Christian belief that God is three persons in one God: the Father (creator), the Son (Jesus), and the Holy Spirit (God's presence in the world). Christians believe these three are eternally and equally God, despite being distinct persons."

Explain (4 marks)

What it asks: Give reasons why something is true, believed, or important. Show understanding of connections.

How to answer: 2–3 PEEL paragraphs. Start with a point, provide evidence/example, explain why, and link to the question.

Example Q: "Explain why the concept of incarnation is important in Christianity."
Example A: "Incarnation (God becoming human in Jesus) is important because it shows God's love for humanity. Jesus came to show people who God is and to die for human sin. The incarnation also bridges the gap between the infinite divine and finite human — God became human to restore the relationship broken by sin. Without incarnation, there would be no redemption or salvation."

Evaluate (6 marks)

What it asks: Make a judgment about whether an idea is true, right, fair, or important. Consider multiple perspectives.

How to answer: Use "Some... However... Therefore" structure. Argue for a position, acknowledge a counter-argument, and give a reasoned conclusion.

Example Q: "Evaluate whether belief in God is necessary for living a moral life."
Example A (outline): Some argue God provides absolute moral standards through scripture and divine command. However, secular philosophers argue morality comes from reason and empathy, and many non-believers live ethical lives. Therefore, belief in God strengthens moral conviction but is not strictly necessary for morality.

Evaluate (12 marks — extended)

What it asks: Make a sophisticated, multi-sided judgment with extended reasoning.

How to answer: Intro (clarify the issue) → Argument 1 with evidence → Counter-argument → Argument 2 → Counter-argument → Conclusion with judgment.

Mark scale: 0–3 (unsupported opinion), 4–6 (some reasoning, limited perspective), 7–9 (good reasoning, multiple perspectives), 10–12 (sophisticated, nuanced judgment).

Understanding Mark Scales

1-mark questions: "Give one..." One correct point = 1 mark. No mark for incomplete or wrong answers.

4-mark "Explain" questions — Mark scale:

  • 0: No relevant answer
  • 1: Weak explanation; limited detail; few reasons given
  • 2: Some explanation; partial reasoning; could be more detailed
  • 3: Good explanation; clear reasoning; uses examples or evidence
  • 4: Excellent explanation; sophisticated reasoning; well-detailed with evidence

6-mark "Evaluate" questions — Mark scale:

  • 0: No relevant answer
  • 1–2: Weak evaluation; personal opinion without reasoning; limited perspective
  • 3–4: Some evaluation; reasons given but limited depth; may miss counter-argument
  • 5–6: Good evaluation; clear judgment with reasoning; considers alternative views; specific evidence

12-mark extended evaluation — Mark scale:

  • 0–3: Unsupported opinions; little or no evidence
  • 4–6: Basic reasoning; limited perspective; some evidence but could be stronger
  • 7–9: Good reasoning; multiple perspectives clearly presented; sophisticated analysis
  • 10–12: Excellent; nuanced judgment; multiple perspectives thoroughly explored; specific evidence; sophisticated language

Key insight: To get top marks, you must show you've considered multiple perspectives and made a reasoned judgment, not just stated opinions.

Time Management in the Exam

Paper 1 (Religion): 1 hour 45 minutes

  • 2 × 1-mark questions = 2 minutes
  • 2 × 4-mark "Explain" questions = 20 minutes (5 min each)
  • 2 × 6-mark "Evaluate" questions = 24 minutes (12 min each)
  • Total: 16 marks per section (Christianity, Islam) = 32 marks
  • Strategy: Don't spend 15 minutes on a 4-mark question. Time is tight.

Paper 2 (Thematic Studies): 1 hour 45 minutes

  • 1 + 1 + 4 + 6 + 12 mark questions = 24 marks per theme
  • You'll choose ONE theme to answer. 1 hour 45 minutes for one theme.
  • Time allocation: 1 min + 1 min + 6 min + 9 min + 15 min = ~32 minutes with 10 minutes buffer

Common time-management mistakes:

  • Spending too long on low-mark questions (1–2 marks) and rushing the 12-mark
  • Writing essay-length answers to short questions
  • Not reading the question carefully (e.g., missing "evaluate" vs "explain")
  • No time to check or revise

Pro tips:

  • Read the question twice; underline the command word (Give, Describe, Explain, Evaluate)
  • Plan your 6-mark and 12-mark answers before writing (30 seconds outline)
  • For 12-mark: aim for 3–4 paragraphs; each paragraph = one idea with evidence
  • Leave 2–3 minutes at the end to scan for errors

Using the Mark Scheme to Improve

How to self-assess your practice answers:

  1. Write your answer to a practice question
  2. Read the official AQA mark scheme for that question
  3. Check: Did I include the key points the scheme mentions? Did I use specific evidence? Did I address all parts of the question?
  4. Score yourself against the scale, not as a grade but as feedback: "I got 3/6 because I explained one perspective but missed the counter-argument."
  5. Rewrite the answer, addressing the gaps

What top-mark answers have (that mid-mark answers don't):

  • Specific evidence (scripture, quotes, named scholars/traditions)
  • Explanation of why the evidence matters (not just stating it)
  • Multiple perspectives (for evaluation questions)
  • Sophisticated vocabulary and clear structure (PEEL or "Some... However... Therefore")
  • Direct focus on the question (not generic essays)

Common Exam Mistakes to Avoid

  • ❌ Writing general essays instead of answering the question: A question asks "Explain why Muslims fast during Ramadan." You write about Ramadan history. ✅ Answer the specific question with specific reasons.
  • ❌ Confusing "explain" with "describe": Describe = what something is. Explain = why it matters/happens. Check the command word.
  • ❌ Not giving specific evidence: "Christians believe forgiveness is important." ✅ "Christians believe forgiveness is important because Jesus taught it (Matthew 18:21–22) and because unforgiveness separates people from God's grace."
  • ❌ Evaluation without counter-argument: For 6+ mark evaluation questions, you must consider more than one perspective.
  • ❌ Writing too much on 1–2 mark questions: Three sentences max. Time is money.
  • ❌ Forgetting the link in PEEL: Always end with how your point answers the question.
