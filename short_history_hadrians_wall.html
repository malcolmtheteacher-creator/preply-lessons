Hadrian's Wall

The stone line an empire drew across a whole country — and the soldiers who spent their lives on the wrong side of the weather.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of the great wall an emperor built across the top of Britain — and why the men who guarded it came from every corner of the empire — and you'll master the prepositions English uses to move a listener along, across, through and beyond a place, so your descriptions actually go somewhere.

  • What do you imagine daily life is like for a soldier stationed far from home, in a cold place, for years at a time?

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The northern edge of the Roman world was its frontier — the wild, watched line where the empire ended and unconquered land began.
a frontier (n) — the far border of a country or empire, especially a wild or contested one.
2. The whole structure was designed as a bulwark — solid enough to hold back anyone who tried to force the line without permission.
a bulwark (n) — a strong defensive wall or rampart; figuratively, a defence that protects against a threat.
3. The wall was not held by Roman citizens but by auxiliaries — soldiers recruited from conquered peoples across the empire.
auxiliaries (n, plural) — non-citizen soldiers in the Roman army, drawn from the empire's many peoples to support the legions.
4. Each fort held a garrison — the permanent group of troops who lived there, guarded the gates and patrolled the line.
a garrison (n) — the body of soldiers stationed in a fort or town to defend it.
5. Along the top of the wall, sentries kept up a constant patrol, walking their stretch and watching the ground to the north.
a patrol (n/v) — a small group moving regularly over an area to guard it; to guard an area in this way.
6. Tribes to the north sometimes launched a swift incursion across the line, seizing cattle and goods before slipping back into the hills.
an incursion (n) — a sudden, brief raid into another people's territory to attack or plunder before withdrawing.
7. At regular points, a stone portal let approved traders and travellers pass through the wall under the watchful eyes of the guards.
a portal (n) — a large or important doorway or gateway, especially a grand or closely guarded one.
8. The wall was less a solid seal than a cordon: the guards could see who was moving, tax the goods, and turn back anyone unwelcome.
a cordon (n) — a line of guards or barriers set along or around a place to control who and what may pass.
9. A soldier posted here for years would feel like an exile — cut off from home, family and the warm south he had left behind.
an exile (n) — a person living far from their homeland, often feeling shut out from it.
10. Fourteen hundred years of weather, farming and stone-robbing have worn much of the wall down to low, grassy vestiges.
vestiges (n, plural) — small traces or remains of something that has largely disappeared.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — An emperor draws a line

By the year 122 AD, Rome ruled a vast empire, and it had grown tired of growing. For decades its legions had pushed north through Britain, but the far north — the cold, mountainous country of the tribes the Romans called Caledonians — was never truly conquered. The wars there were expensive, the raids across the border were constant, and the emperor Hadrian had seen enough. He was a soldier and a builder, and he preferred a fixed, defensible frontier to endless bloody conquest. When he visited Britain that year, his order was blunt: build a wall to separate the Romans from the barbarians.

What his army built was staggering. A stone wall ran roughly 73 miles across the whole neck of northern Britain, from the River Tyne in the east to the Solway Firth in the west — sea to sea. It was up to three metres thick and perhaps four or five metres high. A deep ditch was dug along the northern side; a military road ran along the south. Along its length stood a small fort at every Roman mile — a "milecastle" — with two watchtowers between each one, and larger forts holding hundreds of troops spaced out behind. It was the most heavily fortified line in the entire Roman world.

And yet it was never simply a barrier to hide behind. Gateways through the milecastles let approved people pass, north and south, under guard. The wall was a statement of power carved across the land — this far, and no further — but it worked, day to day, as an enormous checkpoint: a way to watch movement, tax the trade, and decide who crossed.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Hadrian choose to build a wall instead of continuing to conquer the north?

2. Describe the scale of the wall — where it ran and what stood along it.

1. The northern wars were costly and the frontier suffered constant raids; Hadrian preferred a fixed, defensible line to endless, expensive conquest.

2. Roughly 73 miles from the River Tyne to the Solway Firth, sea to sea; a thick, high stone wall with a ditch to the north, a road to the south, milecastles every Roman mile, watchtowers between them, and larger forts behind.

Discuss:
  • Hadrian's message was "this far, and no further." What does it say about an empire when it decides to stop expanding and defend a line instead?

Part 2 — The men on the wall

Here is the surprise at the heart of the story: the wall was built mainly by Roman legionaries, but it was not manned by Romans at all. The day-to-day garrison — some fifteen thousand soldiers strung out along the line — were auxiliaries, troops recruited from the many conquered peoples of the empire. On this one cold frontier you would find archers from Syria, cavalry from what is now Spain and Belgium, and infantry raised on the banks of the Rhine and the Danube. An empire built its northern edge with soldiers who had themselves been swallowed by it, and rewarded them, after twenty-five hard years, with Roman citizenship.

Life on the frontier was not the grim, empty siege we might imagine. Behind the wall, at forts such as Vindolanda, whole communities grew up: workshops, bath-houses, temples, and outside the walls a civilian town of families, traders and taverns. Thin wooden writing-tablets found preserved in the mud there have handed us the actual voices of these people. One is a letter about a parcel sent from home — socks, sandals and underpants, against the British cold. Another, written around the year 100, is an invitation from one officer's wife to another to come to her birthday party — the earliest surviving handwriting by a woman anywhere in the Roman world.

Between the boredom and the cold there was real danger too. The tribes beyond the wall did not go away, and patrols moved out through the gateways into hostile country. Every few years the northern peoples would raid across the line, and now and then the frontier erupted into open war. For the soldier from a warm province, posted for a quarter of a century to this grey, rain-soaked edge of the world, the wall could feel less like a posting than an exile.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who actually guarded the wall day to day, and where did they come from?

2. What do the Vindolanda writing-tablets tell us about ordinary life on the frontier?

1. Not Roman citizens but auxiliaries — around 15,000 soldiers recruited from conquered peoples across the empire (Syria, Spain, the Rhine, the Danube, and more), who earned citizenship after about 25 years' service.

2. That it was a living community — letters about parcels of socks and underpants against the cold, a woman's birthday-party invitation — showing families, trade and everyday human life, not just soldiering.

Discuss:
  • The empire defended its border using soldiers from the very peoples it had conquered. Why might a state do that — and would those soldiers have felt Roman?

Part 3 — What the line left behind

The wall stood as a working frontier for the best part of three hundred years. It was manned, repaired, briefly abandoned for a wall built further north, and then reoccupied. But an empire cannot hold a line forever. By the early fifth century, Rome — pressed on every side and shrinking back towards Italy — pulled its army out of Britain for good. The garrisons drifted away, the pay stopped coming, and the great line across the north was simply left.

What followed was slower than any invasion. For fourteen centuries the wall became a quarry. Farmers, churches, villages and even a later road took its shaped stone away, until in many places the mightiest barrier in the Roman world had sunk to a low green ridge running across the moors. Yet it never vanished. It ran, and still runs, straight through the middle of Britain's imagination — a physical memory of the empire that once ruled here.

Today Hadrian's Wall is a World Heritage Site, and people walk its whole length coast to coast, tracing a line laid down nearly nineteen centuries ago. Beyond the surviving ruins, the deeper thing it left behind is a question the modern world has never stopped asking: where does a country draw its edge, who gets to cross it, and what does it cost to guard a line?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How and why did the wall stop being a working Roman frontier?

2. What happened to the physical wall over the centuries after Rome left, and what does it mean today?

1. It worked for roughly three centuries, but by the early 400s a shrinking, hard-pressed Rome withdrew its army from Britain; the pay and garrisons went, and the line was abandoned.

2. Its stone was robbed for farms, churches and roads until much of it became a low green ridge, but it survives as a World Heritage Site people walk coast to coast — and as a lasting symbol of borders and who may cross them.

Discuss:
  • Nineteen centuries on, people still walk the whole wall. Why do old borders and frontiers hold such a grip on us long after they stop mattering militarily?

Grammar — prepositions of place & movement

To describe Hadrian's Wall well, you have to move a listener through space: along its length, across the country, through its gateways, out beyond its edge. These prepositions are the difference between a flat description and one your listener can actually picture and travel through.

The core set, and what each one really does:
• along — following the length of something: "Sentries patrolled along the top of the wall."
• across — from one side to the other: "The wall runs across northern Britain, sea to sea."
• through — in one side and out the other, from inside something: "Traders passed through the gateways."
• beyond — on the far side of, further than: "The tribes lived beyond the wall, in unconquered land."
• between — in the space separating two things: "Two watchtowers stood between each pair of milecastles."
• on either side of — on both sides of a line or divide: "Farms and settlements grew up on either side of the frontier."
Two easy mix-ups to lock down:
• across vs through — you go across a surface or open space (across a field, across a country); you go through something you are inside or that surrounds you (through a gate, through a forest, through a crowd).
• along vs beyond — along follows a line (walking along the wall); beyond takes you past its far end (the land beyond the wall).

1. Choose the preposition

Complete each sentence with along, across, through, beyond, between or on either side of.

1. The wall stretches the whole width of northern Britain, from coast to coast.

2. Approved traders were allowed to pass the guarded gateways.

3. The Caledonian tribes lived the wall, in land Rome never fully controlled.

4. A road for the army ran the southern side of the wall, following its whole length.

5. A watchtower stood each pair of milecastles.

1. across (from one side to the other)

2. through (in one side of the gateway and out the other)

3. beyond (on the far side of the wall)

4. along (following the wall's length)

5. between (in the space separating the two milecastles)

Choose the one that fits. Remember: across a surface or open space; through something you move inside or that surrounds you.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Rome guarded its northern edge with soldiers drawn from the very peoples it had conquered. Was that a sign of strength and confidence, or of an empire already stretched too thin?
  2. The wall worked as much through control and paperwork — who may cross, and with what — as through stone. Is that still how borders really work today?
  3. The wall outlived the empire that built it and became a symbol. Why do we protect and walk old frontiers instead of simply letting them disappear?
