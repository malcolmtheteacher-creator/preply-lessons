Never Rush a Greeting

In many African societies, a proper greeting is not small talk: it is the way you show respect, and skipping it can offend.

The stereotype: “Africans are always friendly and relaxed about everything, and they all greet in the same way.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A greeting is a conversation

In many parts of the region, a greeting is a short conversation, not a single word. In Swahili, people ask “Habari?” (what news?) and then follow with questions about work, family and health. In Zulu, “Sawubona” literally means “I see you”, and the reply, “Yebo, sikhona”, means “Yes, we are here”, which expresses the idea of ubuntu, that people exist through their relationships with others. In Ghana, it is considered polite to greet everyone in a room, one by one, usually with the right hand. It is expected that people ask after each other's family and health before turning to business. Skipping the greeting and getting straight to the point can be seen as cold, or even rude.

Part 2 — Age, hands and eye contact

Many customs are connected to respect for age. Elders are usually greeted first, and younger people may bow slightly, use two hands or add a title. In some Yoruba communities in Nigeria, younger men and women traditionally prostrate or kneel to greet older relatives, though this is less common in big cities. The right hand is widely used for greeting and giving, since the left hand is traditionally considered less clean. In some cultures, long eye contact with an elder is considered disrespectful, whereas in others it is expected. Touching is also common between friends: a hand on the shoulder, a long handshake or a hug. Visitors are not expected to know everything, but are expected to try.

1. The elder or oldest person.

Part 3 — Not the same everywhere

It would be a mistake to treat these customs as one rule for the whole continent. Africa has thousands of cultures, and greeting habits are different from one ethnic group to another, and between villages, towns and cities. In busy cities such as Lagos or Johannesburg, people have less time and a quick “hi, how are you?” is normal, especially in offices. Young people have also developed their own styles, from fist bumps to special handshakes. Visitors who want to behave well can simply watch what others do, greet first, listen carefully and ask politely if they are unsure. Nobody is expected to be perfect, and an honest attempt is usually appreciated.

Grammar — Impersonal Passive — It Is Considered, Is Expected To, Is Said To

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
The impersonal passive describes what people generally think or do.
• It is considered / thought / said that …: “It is considered polite that younger people greet elders first.”
• X is considered / expected / said to + infinitive: “Visitors are expected to greet everyone.” “It is considered rude to use the left hand.”
• It is customary to …: “It is customary to ask about the family.”
• Past reference: “The custom is said to have started long ago.”

Going deeper

Long greetings are often described as “African hospitality”, which is flattering but can be too general. The real principle is that a person is recognised as a person before the matter at hand: before the business, the favour or the question. Some societies call this ubuntu, others have different words, but the idea is the same. For visitors from places where time is scarce, it can feel slow. But it works as a way of building trust quickly, and it is also a good reminder that efficiency and relationship are different values, neither of which is automatically better.

Worth noticing: Impersonal passive: It is considered / believed / expected + that-clause and X is considered / expected / said to + infinitive: “It is considered rude to skip a greeting.” “Visitors are expected to greet everyone.”
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Greet first, and greet everyone. Offer your right hand, ask about the person's family or day, and wait for the answer. If you do not know the local greeting, ask someone to teach you: people are delighted when a visitor makes an effort.
