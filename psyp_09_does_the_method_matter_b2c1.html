Does the Method Matter?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — Everybody has won

In 1936, the psychologist Saul Rosenzweig made an observation that has since become one of the most debated ideas in psychotherapy. Borrowing a line from the Caucus race in Alice's Adventures in Wonderland, in which the Dodo declares that 'everybody has won, and all must have prizes', he suggested that the different schools of therapy, despite their rival theories, achieve similar results because they share something important.

The first large evidence came in 1977, when Mary Lee Smith and Gene Glass combined 375 studies and found that people who received therapy were, on average, better off than about three-quarters of those who did not. Comparisons between types of therapy showed much smaller differences than the rivalry between schools had led many to expect. Later meta-analyses, by Luborsky and others, repeated the pattern, and Bruce Wampold and colleagues built a detailed case that the equivalence was real.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the Dodo bird verdict?

2. What did Smith and Glass find in 1977?

1. That different well-delivered therapies often produce similar outcomes.

2. That people receiving therapy were, on average, better off than about three-quarters of those who did not, with only small differences between types.

Discuss:
  • Why might rival schools find equivalence an uncomfortable conclusion?

Part 2 — What does the work, then?

If techniques are not what matters most, the likeliest candidates are the common factors. The alliance has the strongest evidence. A meta-analysis of more than 300 studies found a moderate association of around r = .28 between the quality of the alliance and outcome, a figure that holds across different therapies. Empathy, agreement about goals and the client's expectation of improvement also predict outcome.

There is a further finding that clinicians may find uncomfortable: therapists differ. Some obtain consistently better outcomes than others using the same method, and these therapist effects appear to be larger than the differences between methods. What distinguishes the more effective clinicians is not yet clear, although studies point to interpersonal skill, responsiveness to feedback and deliberate practice.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the alliance–outcome link?

2. What are therapist effects?

1. A moderate association (around r = .28) found across many studies and therapies.

2. Differences in outcomes between therapists using the same method, apparently larger than the differences between methods.

Discuss:
  • If therapist effects are real, how should training and supervision change?

Part 3 — The case against

The verdict has serious critics. They point out that many comparisons are between treatments that are, in effect, all active, which makes a small difference harder to detect. For particular conditions, such as phobias, obsessive-compulsive disorder and post-traumatic stress, trials do show an advantage for treatments that include exposure. In addition, researcher allegiance, where investigators prefer the therapy they test, can bias outcomes and complicate any comparison.

The sensible position may be that both sides have part of the truth. Common factors explain much of the benefit across conditions, and specific techniques matter more for some conditions than others. For practitioners, the lesson is practical: build the alliance, monitor how the client is responding and be ready to change the plan when the evidence from the sessions says it is not working.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why do critics say the verdict is premature?

2. What is the practical lesson?

1. Because for specific conditions such as phobias, OCD and PTSD, treatments with exposure show an advantage.

2. Build the alliance, monitor the client's response and change the plan if it is not working.

Discuss:
  • Is it ethical to use a method that has an advantage for a condition if the client dislikes it?

Grammar — relative clauses

Relative clauses let you add information about a noun, in a defining way (to identify) or a non-defining way (to add extra detail). They are central to precise professional writing.

Defining clauses (no commas; essential to the meaning):
• "The therapist who builds a strong alliance tends to get better results."
• "The factors that all therapies share are called common factors."
You can often leave out that/who when it is the object: "the client (whom) she saw".
Non-defining clauses (commas; extra information):
• "Rosenzweig, who published in 1936, borrowed the idea from Alice."
• "The alliance, which is hard to measure, predicts outcome."
Use which (not that) and who/whom/whose. A which clause can also comment on a whole idea: "Therapist effects are large, which surprised many researchers."

Method or relationship?

Five cases. Say how far the method or the relationship should decide what to do. Then reveal.

1. A client with panic disorder asks for the most effective treatment.
Method matters. For specific conditions, treatments that include exposure and practice have good evidence; the alliance remains important to keep the client engaged.
2. A client with long-standing relationship difficulties asks which therapy is best.
Relationship matters. For broad, complex presentations, differences between methods are small and the alliance is a strong predictor.
3. A client says after three sessions, 'This doesn't feel right.'
Monitor and adapt. Feedback in early sessions predicts later outcome; discussing it openly can repair a weak alliance.
4. A clinic wants to improve results. It can fund one thing: a new manual or supervision in relationship skills.
Debatable. Evidence on therapist effects suggests that supervision and feedback may offer a good return, but both have value.
5. A researcher who developed a therapy publishes a trial that favours it.
Allegiance. Results should be read with care; independent replication and registered protocols reduce the risk of bias.
