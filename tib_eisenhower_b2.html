The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — A storm in June

In the spring of 1944, General Dwight D. Eisenhower had one of the hardest jobs in history. He was the Supreme Allied Commander in Europe, and he was responsible for Operation Overlord — the invasion of Nazi-occupied France. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers, thousands of ships and aircraft, and millions of tons of supplies all depended on his decisions.

The invasion was planned for 5 June. But the weather turned terrible, and Eisenhower had to postpone it. Then his weather experts told him there might be a short break in the storm. He listened, asked questions, and finally gave the order to go. On 6 June, D-Day, the landings began.

In his pocket, Eisenhower carried a short note he had written in case the invasion failed. It said that the decision had been his, and that “any blame or fault” belonged to him alone. He never needed it. But the note shows something about the man: he knew which decisions only he could make — and he made them.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Operation Overlord?

2. Why was the invasion moved from 5 June to 6 June?

3. What did Eisenhower's secret note say?

1. The Allied invasion of Nazi-occupied France in 1944.

2. Because of terrible weather; the experts predicted a short break in the storm.

3. That the decision was his, and that if the invasion failed, all the blame was his alone.

Discuss:
  • Eisenhower wrote the note before he knew the result. What does that tell you about him as a leader?

Part 2 — Two kinds of problems

After the war, Eisenhower spent several years as president of Columbia University in New York, and in 1953 he became President of the United States. His days were full of pressing problems, and he was known for protecting his time. He wanted short, clear reports and trusted his staff to deal with anything they could handle themselves.

In August 1954, he gave a speech in Evanston, near Chicago. In it, he quoted what he called the words of “a former college president”: “I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent.”

Nobody is completely sure who that college president was. Some people point to a university leader Eisenhower knew; others notice that Eisenhower himself had recently been one. Either way, the idea stayed with him. Some of his greatest achievements — the national highway system and the creation of NASA — were not emergencies. They were important projects that took years of patient planning.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Which university did Eisenhower lead after the war?

2. According to the quotation, what is the relationship between urgent and important problems?

3. Why were the highways and NASA good examples of his idea?

1. Columbia University in New York — he was its president.

2. Urgent problems are usually not important, and important problems are rarely urgent.

3. They weren't emergencies — they were important long-term projects that needed years of planning.

Discuss:
  • Do you agree that the important things are “never urgent”? Can you think of an exception?

Part 3 — From a sentence to four boxes

For decades, Eisenhower's sentence was simply a good quotation. Then, in 1989, the American writer Stephen Covey published The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People, which became one of the best-selling business books ever. In it, Covey made the idea famous as a grid: urgent or not urgent along one side, important or not important along the other. Four boxes. Over time, people began calling it the Eisenhower Matrix.

The grid gives each box an action. Urgent and important: do it now. Important but not urgent: schedule it. Urgent but not important: delegate it, if you can. Neither: drop it.

Covey's big point was about the second box. Exercise, learning, relationships, planning — these things are never urgent, so we procrastinate until they become emergencies. An American writer, Charles Hummel, had already given the problem a name in 1967: the tyranny of the urgent. Today, with a phone that buzzes every few minutes, that tyranny may be stronger than ever.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Which book made the four-box grid famous, and when?

2. What should you do with a task that is urgent but not important?

3. Why does Covey think the “important, not urgent” box is the most dangerous?

1. Stephen Covey's 1989 book, The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People.

2. Delegate it — give it to someone else, if possible.

3. Because nothing forces us to do those tasks, so we keep delaying them until they become emergencies.

Discuss:
  • The grid is named after Eisenhower, but Covey made it famous. Who deserves the credit?

How the tool works

Two questions, four boxes. Across the bottom: how urgent is it — does it need attention now? Up the side: how important is it — does it matter for your goals and values? Each box has its own action.

Using it, step by step

  1. Write down everything on your mind — work, home, people, ideas.
  2. Ask the importance question first: does this help my goals or the people I care about? Only then ask: must it happen soon?
  3. Put each task in a box. Be honest: most people put far too much in “do it now”.
  4. Act box by box: do, schedule (with a real time in your calendar), delegate, or drop — and don't feel guilty about dropping.

Try it yourself

Which box does each task belong in? Choose, then check.

1. Scrolling through random videos on your phone before bed.
2. Your child has a high temperature and needs to see a doctor this afternoon.
3. Starting regular exercise so that you stay healthy in ten years' time.
4. The report your boss needs by 5 p.m. today — and a big client is waiting for it.
5. A colleague keeps asking you to book meeting rooms — a quick job that anyone in the team could do.
6. Planning where you want your career to be in five years.

1. Neither — no deadline and no real value — drop it.

2. Urgent and important — health and family, and it can't wait — do it now.

3. Important, not urgent — it matters a lot, but nothing forces you to start today — schedule it.

4. Urgent and important — a real deadline with real consequences — do it now.

5. Urgent, not important — it feels urgent but doesn't need you — delegate it.

6. Important, not urgent — very important, never urgent — put a time in the calendar.

Where the tool breaks

Not everyone can delegate. The grid assumes you have someone to hand work to. A student, a parent or a small-business owner often doesn't.

Whose importance? Your boss's “important” and yours may be very different.

Life isn't a list. Some of the best moments — a long talk with a friend — look like “neither” on paper.

Talk it through:
  • Which box do you spend most of your time in? Which do you wish you spent more time in?
  • Is there anything in your life that the grid would call “drop it” that you would never drop?

Grammar — must, have to, need to, don't have to

Sorting tasks is really a question of obligation: what must happen, what we don't have to do at all. English has several ways to say this, and the differences matter.

must — strong obligation, often from the speaker (a personal rule or strong advice).
“I must call my grandmother this week — it's been too long.”
have to — obligation from outside: rules, laws, other people.
“Pilots have to retire at a certain age.”  Past: “We had to wait two hours at the border.” (must has no past form.)
need to — something is necessary to reach a goal.
“You need to book early if you want a window seat.”
don't have to / don't need to / needn't — it isn't necessary; you can choose.
“You don't have to bring anything — we have plenty of food.”
mustn't — it is NOT allowed, or a very bad idea. It is not the opposite of must!
“You mustn't use your phone while you're driving.”

1. Choose the right form

Write must, mustn't, have to, had to or don't have to in each gap. Use each once.

1. It's Saturday tomorrow, so I get up early. I can sleep until ten.

2. Yesterday the train was cancelled and I take a taxi.

3. In many countries, drivers wear a seat belt — it's the law.

4. This is a secret. You tell anyone.

5. I really stop eating so much chocolate — I've told myself a hundred times.

1. don't have to (it isn't necessary)

2. had to (past obligation)

3. have to (a rule from outside)

4. mustn't (it's not allowed)

5. must (a personal rule)

2. Same meaning or different?

Write same or different for each pair.

1. You don't have to pay. / You needn't pay. →

2. You mustn't touch it. / You don't have to touch it. →

3. We need to leave by six. / We have to leave by six. →

4. She had to work late. / She must work late. →

1. same — both mean it isn't necessary.

2. different — the first is forbidden; the second is only unnecessary.

3. same (almost) — both say it's necessary.

4. different — the first is in the past; the second is about now or the future.
