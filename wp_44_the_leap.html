The Leap

In which a melancholy Dane insists that truth is not a system but a decision—and that the most important things in life require a leap of faith

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855) rejected Hegelian systems. He insisted that existence cannot be rationalized. Truth is subjectivity—not a belief you hold, but a decision you make. The three stages of life (aesthetic, ethical, religious) culminate in a leap of faith. This is the birth of existentialism, and it takes Jesus seriously in a way no philosopher before him had dared.

I. Before We Begin

What is truth for you? A system of beliefs? A feeling? A commitment?

♦ ♦ ♦

Hegel said truth is a system—the self-realization of Spirit. Marx said truth is material conditions. But Kierkegaard says neither. Truth, he insists, is not objective. It is subjective—but not in the way we usually think. Subjectivity is truth means: truth is what you stake your existence on.

II. The Story — The Individual Before God

Søren Kierkegaard was born in Copenhagen in 1813. He lived most of his life in that city, rarely traveling, becoming increasingly alienated from conventional society. He was melancholic by temperament, prone to despair, yet driven by a burning need to understand what it means to live as a Christian in Christendom.

The problem: everyone in Denmark was Christian because everyone was born Christian. But true faith, Kierkegaard insisted, is not inherited. It is not rational. It is a leap—a radical, absurd, passionate commitment that cannot be justified by reason.

Against Hegel's System

Kierkegaard's first target was Hegelianism. Hegel had constructed a vast system that encompassed all of reality—past, present, future. Everything made sense within the system. But, Kierkegaard asked: where is you in this system? Where is the individual? Where is freedom? Where is anxiety?

Truth is subjectivity. — Kierkegaard, Concluding Unscientific Postscript

What did he mean? Not that truth is whatever feels true to you. Rather: truth is lived. Truth is inwardness. When you stand before God alone, all the Hegelian systems collapse. You must choose. And that choice is truth.

The Three Stages

Kierkegaard described three ways of living—three stages:

The Aesthetic Stage

The pursuit of pleasure, beauty, sensation. The aesthete seeks to experience all that life offers. But the aesthete always ends in despair: pleasure is fleeting, beauty fades, sensation becomes dull.

The Ethical Stage

The choice to commit to universal principles—duty, law, morality. The ethical person chooses to bind themselves to something larger than pleasure. But the ethical is not enough. It lacks passion. It lacks a ground.

The Religious Stage

The leap of faith. The individual stands before God in radical solitude. They choose to believe the absurd: that God became human, died, and rose. This is not rational. It is madness. But it is the only truth that matters.

Fear and Trembling

Kierkegaard wrote a masterpiece called Fear and Trembling about Abraham—who was asked by God to sacrifice his son Isaac. Abraham could not explain this to anyone. No system justified it. Yet he went forward in fear and trembling, trusting the absurd promise that God would provide.

This is the religious leap. Not a logical move. Apassionate commitment beyond reason.

III. The Ideas — What Kierkegaard Offers

Idea 1: Existence Precedes Essence

You are not defined by a system. You must choose who you are.

This will become the foundation of existentialism. Unlike things (a chair is defined by what it is), humans exist first and must define themselves through choice. You are not given a essence. You must create it.

Idea 2: Subjectivity Is Truth

Truth is not objective knowledge. It is passionate inwardness—a commitment you stake your life on.

This does not mean anything goes. Rather: the deepest truths (about God, about love, about your life) cannot be proven. They must be lived. They are truths you commit to in passion and fear.

Idea 3: The Leap of Faith

Faith cannot be reasoned into. It must be leapt into.

The absurd is the Christian claim: that God became human, suffered, and died. No rational system can make this make sense. Yet it is precisely here—in the absurd—that faith begins.

VI. Your Turn

The three stages in your life

Have you lived through the aesthetic stage (pleasure-seeking)? The ethical stage (duty)? The religious stage (faith)? Where are you now?

Subjectivity is truth

Kierkegaard said truth is not objective but subjective—lived passion. Does this make sense? Or is this just relativism?

The absurd leap

Can you believe something that contradicts reason? Have you ever made a commitment that you couldn't justify logically?

Abraham's choice

Abraham was told to sacrifice his son. He could not explain to anyone why this was right. Is faith this absolute? This irrational?

Against systems

Kierkegaard rejected Hegel's system. Is all philosophy just system-building? Can truth be systematic?

The individual vs. the system

Kierkegaard insisted on the individual—you alone before God. Does modern life make this possible? Or are we all absorbed into systems?
