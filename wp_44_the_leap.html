A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XLIV of L

The Leap

In which a tormented Dane insists that truth is not a system but a lived decision—and that the deepest truths require a leap beyond reason

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855) attacked Hegel and his grand systems. He insisted that existence cannot be rationalized. Truth is not objective doctrine but subjective—a passionate commitment, a decision you stake your life upon. The three stages of existence (aesthetic, ethical, religious) culminate in a leap of faith that defies reason. This is the birth of existentialism, and it takes Jesus seriously as the absurd center where all faith begins.

I. Before We Begin

How do you know what is true? Do you prove it logically? Feel it in your heart? Receive it from authority? And is there anything you believe that you cannot prove?

II. The Story — The Individual Before God

Søren Aabye Kierkegaard was born in Copenhagen in 1813. He lived almost his entire life in that city, rarely venturing far, becoming increasingly isolated and alienated from what he saw as the false piety of Danish Christendom. He was thin, melancholic, prone to dark moods, driven by an almost obsessive need to understand what it means to be a Christian.

His father had been a severe man, a wool merchant who made a fortune but lived in constant anxiety about his sins. Søren inherited this torment. He also inherited his father’s intellectual brilliance. At university he excelled, then spent years in what he called “the aesthetic stage,” pursuing pleasure and writing witty essays. But pleasure disgusted him. He grew convinced that he had to find truth—not comfortable truth, but real truth, the kind that demands everything.

The Problem: Christendom Is Not Christianity

In Denmark, everyone was Christian. You were born into the Lutheran Church. Bishops and priests sat in comfortable pews preaching comfortable doctrines. But Kierkegaard saw a massive deception: the crowd is untruth. When everyone claims to be Christian, nobody is. True faith cannot be inherited or assumed. It must be chosen in solitude, in fear, in trembling.

Christianity, Kierkegaard insisted, is not a doctrine to believe. It is an existence to live. It is not comfortable. It is radical, absurd, demanding.

Life can only be understood backwards; but it must be lived forwards. — Kierkegaard

Against Hegel’s System

Kierkegaard’s primary target was Hegelianism. Hegel had constructed a vast philosophical system that seemed to encompass all of reality. History was the self-realization of Spirit. Everything made sense. Everything fit into the rational whole.

But Kierkegaard asked a simple question: Where are you in this system? Where is the individual? Where is freedom? Where is anxiety? Where is the terror of choice?

In Hegel’s system, the individual is dissolved into the whole. But existence doesn’t work that way. You exist. Your choices are real. Your anguish is real. And this cannot be systematized away.

Truth is subjectivity. — Kierkegaard, Concluding Unscientific Postscript

This is his most famous claim, and it is wildly misunderstood. He does NOT mean truth is whatever feels true to you. Rather: truth is lived. Truth is inwardness. Truth is the intensity of your commitment. When you stand alone before God, all the systems collapse. You must choose. And that choice, made with your whole being, with passion and fear—that IS truth.

The Three Stages of Existence

Kierkegaard described three ways of living, three stages that the self must pass through:

Stage 1: The Aesthetic

The pursuit of pleasure, beauty, sensation, novelty. The aesthete seeks to experience all that life offers. They are brilliant, witty, cultured. But aesthetes always end in despair: pleasure is fleeting, beauty fades, sensation grows dull. The aesthete chases variety to escape boredom, but eventually they exhaust themselves.

Stage 2: The Ethical

The choice to bind yourself to universal principles. Duty. Law. Marriage. Morality. The ethical person chooses to renounce the pursuit of pleasure and instead commit to something larger than themselves. They become responsible, devoted, dutiful. But the ethical is incomplete. It is cold. It lacks passion. It lacks a ground that transcends reason.

Stage 3: The Religious

The leap of faith. Here the individual stands before God in radical solitude. They choose to believe the absurd: that God became human, suffered, died, and rose. This is not rational. It is madness. It contradicts reason. But it is the only truth that matters because it alone can justify and sustain existence.

Fear and Trembling: The Teleological Suspension of the Ethical

Kierkegaard wrote a masterwork called Fear and Trembling. In it, he meditates on Abraham—the father of faith. God commands Abraham to sacrifice his son Isaac, the child of promise. Abraham cannot explain this to anyone. There is no system that justifies it. No reason that makes sense of it. All morality says: “Do not kill your child.”

But Abraham believes. In fear and trembling, he goes forward, believing the absurd promise that God will provide. He prepares the altar. He raises the knife. And in that moment—faith.

This is the religious leap. It is not a logical move. It is a passionate commitment beyond reason. Abraham does not know what will happen. He has only the absurd promise and his own faith. And this faith is truth.

Regine Olsen and the Martyrdom of Inwardness

In 1840, Kierkegaard proposed to Regine Olsen, a beautiful and kind young woman. She accepted. But Kierkegaard broke the engagement. He could not marry her. His task, he believed, was to live out his philosophy, to become a “witness to truth,” and this demanded renunciation. He broke her heart and his own.

This is not mere philosophy for Kierkegaard. It is lived experience. He was not a system-builder sitting in comfort. He was a wounded man who staked everything on his beliefs, including his own happiness.

Writing Under Pseudonyms

Kierkegaard published much of his work under pseudonyms: Victor Eremita, Judge William, Johannes Climacus, Anti-Climacus. Why? Because he wanted to dramatize the different stages of existence. He wanted readers to experience the tension between perspectives, not simply absorb doctrine. Philosophy must be lived, enacted, suffered through. It cannot be transmitted as neutral information.

This is radical pedagogy. He refuses to be the authority who tells you what to think. Instead, he creates voices, perspectives, characters. You must choose which one to follow. You must make the leap yourself.

The Martyrdom of Inwardness

Kierkegaard died in 1855 at age 42, exhausted by his unrelenting struggle. He had battled not only against Hegelian philosophy but against the entire Danish church establishment. He called for true Christianity, for radical individual faith, for the renunciation of comfortable doctrine. Few listened in his lifetime. Now he is recognized as the father of existentialism.

III. The Ideas — What Kierkegaard Offers

Idea 1: Existence Precedes Essence

You are not defined by a system. You are not given an essence. You must choose who you are through your choices.

This will become the foundation of existentialism (you’ll see this phrase again in Sartre). Unlike objects (a chair is defined by what it is meant to do), humans exist first and then must define themselves through how they choose to live. You are not handed a nature. You must create yourself through commitment and decision.

Idea 2: Subjectivity Is Truth

Truth is not objective doctrine. It is passionate inwardness—a commitment you stake your life upon.

This does NOT mean relativism. Rather: the deepest truths (about God, about love, about your life) cannot be proven. They must be lived. They are truths you commit to with your whole being—in passion, in fear, in trembling. Truth is measured not by correspondence to some external reality, but by the intensity and authenticity of your commitment.

Idea 3: The Leap of Faith

Faith cannot be reasoned into. It must be leapt into. The absurd is precisely where faith begins.

The Christian claim is absurd: God becomes human, suffers, dies, rises from the dead. No rational system can make this make sense. No logic can justify it. Yet it is precisely here—in the absurd—that faith becomes possible. You cannot climb to faith by reason. You must leap.

Idea 4: The Individual Against the Crowd

The crowd is untruth. Truth requires standing alone.

When everyone believes something, no one has faith. True faith is always the choice of an individual standing before God. It cannot be mass-produced. It cannot be inherited. It demands solitude, risk, the willingness to be mocked. This is why Kierkegaard’s whole project was to unmask the false Christianity of Christendom—comfortable, collective, without passion.

Idea 5: The Paradox and the Absurd

The deepest truths contain contradiction. This is not a problem. It is the sign that we have encountered the infinite.

God became human. The infinite entered the finite. The eternal entered time. This is logically impossible. But it is the heart of Christianity. Kierkegaard celebrates the paradox. He does not try to resolve it. The paradox is the wound through which grace enters.

LESSON 44: KIERKEGAARD — THE LEAP

Five Core Images to Place in Location 44

Image 1: The Individual in Solitude A figure standing completely alone in a vast empty room, light falling from a single window. They cannot hide. They cannot escape. They stand naked before an unseen presence. This is the individual before God.

Image 2: The Aesthetic Stage’s Despair A woman dancing ecstatically in an ornate ballroom, her dress shimmering, colors blazing. Then the ballroom collapses into a grey shore, and she dances alone on ash-coloured sand as waves destroy everything behind her. Beauty fades. Pleasure exhausts itself.

Image 3: The Ethical Chains A man binding himself with heavy golden chains to a rock. His face is dutiful, serious. He has chosen. He is committed. But the chains are cold, and his eyes show no joy—only obligation.

Image 4: Abraham on the Mountain Abraham stands on a mountain holding an enormous knife over his son Isaac. Isaac lies on an altar made of stone. Abraham’s face is twisted in agony—tears streaming—but his eyes show faith. His lips are moving in prayer. This is the leap. This is the absurd made possible by faith alone.

Image 5: The Bridge Across the Chasm An impossible chasm opens before you. On the near side, you stand at the edge. On the far side, golden light. And a bridge made entirely of light spans the gap. You cannot see how it can hold your weight. All reason says it will collapse. But you leap, and it holds. This is faith.

Feel it now: The solitude. The despair of pleasure. The weight of duty. The agony and faith of Abraham. The leap across the impossible chasm. This is the journey of the self toward God. You have walked it in your palace.

VI. Your Turn

Choose one or more of these discussion prompts. Think deeply. Write honestly. There are no right answers, only authentic ones.

The Three Stages in Your Life

Have you lived through the aesthetic stage—chasing pleasure and beauty? The ethical stage—committing to duty and principle? Where are you now? Have you encountered anything you would call a religious leap?

Subjectivity Is Truth

Does Kierkegaard’s claim make sense? That truth is lived commitment, not objective fact? Or does this just sound like relativism? Can you give an example from your own life?

The Absurd Leap

Can you believe something that contradicts reason? Have you ever made a commitment you couldn’t justify logically? Love? Faith? A life decision? What made the leap possible?

Abraham’s Choice

Abraham was told to sacrifice his son. No system justified it. No morality sanctioned it. Yet he believed God would provide. Is faith this absolute? This demanding? Is it still faith if you hesitate?

Against Systems

Kierkegaard rejected Hegel’s system. He said systems miss the individual. But do we need systems to think clearly? Or do they always destroy the real complexity of life?

The Individual vs. the Crowd

Kierkegaard said the crowd is untruth. In what ways do you conform to the crowd? In what ways do you stand alone? What would it cost to truly be an individual?

VII. Final Retrieval

1. The Three Dates

Recall the three key dates. What happened in each?

1813 — Kierkegaard born in Copenhagen

1843 — Either/Or and Fear and Trembling published (his greatest works)

1855 — Kierkegaard dies at age 42, having witnessed to truth

2. The Three Stages

Name and describe the three stages of existence.

Aesthetic: Pursuit of pleasure and beauty; ends in despair as pleasure fades

Ethical: Commitment to universal duty and principle; incomplete without transcendence

Religious: The leap of faith into the absurd; the only stage that completes existence

3. Subjectivity Is Truth

What does Kierkegaard mean by this phrase? What is he NOT saying?

What he IS saying: Truth is lived commitment, passionate inwardness, the intensity with which you stake your existence on something. It is measured by how you live, not by abstract correspondence to facts.

What he is NOT saying: Truth is relative, or whatever feels true to you is true, or logic doesn’t matter. Truth demands the whole person. It is rigorous in a different way.

4. The Leap of Faith

Why does faith require a leap? What role does the absurd play?

Why a leap: Reason alone cannot justify faith. Evidence is ambiguous. You must choose to believe without sufficient rational grounds. The leap bridges the gap that reason cannot cross.

Role of the absurd: The absurd (God becomes human, infinite enters finite, death leads to resurrection) is where faith becomes possible. We don’t resolve the absurd. We leap into it. That’s faith.
