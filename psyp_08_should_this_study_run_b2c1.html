The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — Milgram's shocks

In 1961, the psychologist Stanley Milgram at Yale University advertised for men to take part in a study of memory and learning. Each volunteer was assigned the role of 'teacher' and told to give an electric shock to a 'learner' for every wrong answer, increasing the voltage each time, up to 450 volts. The learner, an actor, protested, screamed and finally fell silent. When teachers hesitated, the experimenter replied with a fixed series of prompts, the last of which was: 'You have no other choice; you must go on.'

In the best-known version, 26 of 40 participants, or 65%, continued to the maximum. Many were visibly distressed, trembling, laughing nervously and sweating. Milgram concluded that ordinary people may obey an authority even when they believe they are causing serious harm, a finding often linked to wider questions about wartime atrocities.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How was the study presented to participants?

2. What was the key result?

1. As a study of memory and learning, in which 'teachers' shocked a 'learner' for wrong answers.

2. 65% (26 of 40) continued to the maximum voltage.

Discuss:

Part 2 — The ethical argument

Critics, beginning with Diana Baumrind in 1964, argued that participants had been deceived, put under severe stress and could not freely withdraw, since they were repeatedly told to continue. Milgram replied that a follow-up questionnaire showed that most were glad they had taken part, and that none of the participants had been harmed in the long term. Later historians drew on the archives and argued that the accounts were more complicated: some participants had doubted that the shocks were real, and the experimenter's script had been applied less uniformly than reported.

The same debate surrounds the 1971 Stanford prison study, in which students played guards and prisoners and the study was stopped after six days. It has been taught for decades as a demonstration of how roles shape behaviour. Later research, including interviews and recordings, suggested that the guards had been coached on how to behave, which weakens the conclusion that they behaved this way spontaneously.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Baumrind argue?

2. What has later research suggested about the prison study?

1. That participants were deceived, stressed and could not withdraw freely.

2. That the guards were coached, which weakens the conclusion that they behaved that way on their own.

Discuss:
  • If a study's conclusions are weakened by later evidence, should the ethical cost be judged differently?

Part 3 — How the rules were built

Today's framework grew out of these controversies and out of earlier medical scandals. The Belmont Report of 1979 set out three principles: respect for persons, beneficence and justice. Professional codes require informed consent, the right to withdraw, minimisation of harm and debriefing, and independent review boards must approve research before it begins. Deception is permitted only when no alternative method exists and the harm is minimal.

Yet the principles do not settle every case. A board must still decide whether the potential benefit justifies the risk, and what counts as sufficient understanding. Some researchers argue that the system protects institutions more than participants, while others note that it has prevented real harm. Most would agree that the question to ask is not whether a study is famous, but whether it is proportionate and whether participants could really choose.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the Belmont Report establish?

2. When is deception allowed today?

1. Three principles: respect for persons, beneficence and justice.

2. Only when no alternative method exists and the harm is minimal, with a debrief.

Discuss:
  • Does the ethics review system protect participants, researchers, institutions or all three?

Grammar — obligation and permission in formal register

Ethics codes, contracts and professional guidelines use a formal register for what is required, forbidden and allowed. Recognising and producing it is essential in professional writing.

Obligation:
• be required to / be obliged to + verb: "Researchers are required to obtain consent."
• it is essential / necessary that + clause: "It is essential that participants understand the risks."
• shall (in rules): "The board shall review all proposals."
Permission and prohibition:
• be permitted to / be entitled to / have the right to: "Participants are entitled to withdraw."
• be prohibited from + -ing / may not: "Researchers are prohibited from disclosing identities."
• need not (formal) = don't have to: "The board need not review exempt studies."

Would it pass review?

Five proposals. Say whether a review board should approve each one, and what you would change. Then reveal.

1. A study will watch how strangers respond to a staged emergency in the street, with no prior consent.
Likely needs changes. Observation in public may be acceptable, but staging an emergency may cause distress; a debrief would be needed.
2. A study will tell participants that a task measures memory, but it actually measures obedience.
Deception. Possible only if there is no alternative, the risk is minimal and participants are fully debriefed; risk of distress must be low.
3. A researcher wants to use patient notes from a clinic for a new analysis.
Privacy. Data must be anonymised or consent obtained; the board must consider what patients agreed to originally.
4. A trial will give a placebo to a group with a serious condition when treatment exists.
Hard case. Withholding known treatment is ethically difficult; usually the control group receives standard care.
5. A study recruits students in the researcher's own class and offers extra credit.
Coercion risk. Students may not feel free to refuse; alternatives of equal value should be offered.
