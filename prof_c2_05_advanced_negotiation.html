Reciprocity The principle that concessions and gestures encourage reciprocal responses; a powerful influence mechanism in negotiation.
Logrolling Trading concessions across different issues to create value; each party gives on low-priority issues to gain on high-priority ones.

Principled Negotiation: The Fisher-Ury Model

Fisher and Ury's framework prioritises interests over positions, creating durable agreements that satisfy both parties.

Core Principles

1. Separate People from Problems
Criticism of an offer is not personal criticism of the proposer. Distinguish between the negotiator (a person worthy of respect) and the negotiating position (subject to rigorous challenge). This allows candid discussion without damaging relationships.

2. Focus on Interests, Not Positions
A position is what someone says they want ("We need £5 million"). An interest is the underlying need ("We need funding to expand capacity by Q4"). Multiple positions can satisfy a single interest. By exploring interests, you unlock creative options.

3. Generate Options for Mutual Gain
Before evaluating, brainstorm options without judgment. What combinations might work? Can you expand the pie? Can you trade across issues? Defer evaluation until a broad range of options is on the table.

4. Use Objective Standards
Ground the negotiation in objective benchmarks: market rates, industry precedent, regulatory requirements, comparable transactions. This depersonalises disagreement and creates legitimacy.

Language for Principled Negotiation

Shifting from positions to interests:

"I understand you're asking for a three-year contract with fixed pricing. Before we close on terms, I'd like to explore what's driving that request. Is your primary concern about cost predictability, supply security, or volume commitment?"

Proposing creative options:

"What if we structure this as a two-year base contract with a third-year option contingent on volume targets? This gives you certainty for two years while allowing us flexibility if demand changes. Does that address your underlying concern?"

Key insight: The best negotiations don't feel like compromises. Both parties walk away feeling they won because their true interests were satisfied, even if they conceded their stated positions.

Advanced Tactics and Counter-Tactics

Understanding manipulative tactics allows you to recognise them and respond strategically.

The Flinch

The tactic: React with exaggerated shock or disappointment to an offer, even if it's reasonable. This creates psychological pressure to improve the offer.

The counter: Stay composed. Acknowledge the offer professionally without reacting to the emotional display. "I understand this is lower than our expectation, but let's examine the assumptions. What's driving your number?"

Good Cop / Bad Cop

The tactic: One negotiator plays aggressive and demands, while a partner softens them. The "good cop" appears to be on your side, pressuring their partner to be reasonable. This manufactures false trust.

The counter: Recognise the dynamic and don't reward it. Treat both negotiators equally. "I appreciate the good cop role, but I won't factor personalities into my assessment. Let's focus on the commercial merit."

Strategic Silence

The tactic: After making an offer or proposal, remain silent. The discomfort often prompts the other party to offer concessions to fill the void.

The counter: Embrace silence yourself. Don't rush to fill uncomfortable pauses. "I've heard your proposal. I need time to consider it before responding." Use silence as a tool, not a vulnerability.

Anchoring Extreme Positions

The tactic: Open with a wildly unrealistic offer to set an anchor that biases the negotiation. The negotiation then moves toward the anchor, which is still better than your actual target.

The counter: Make your own anchor early and forcefully. Challenge unrealistic anchors with objective data. "That number is 40% below market rates. Let me share comparative data from three recent transactions."

Manufactured Deadlock

The tactic: Claim you cannot move on an issue, creating urgency for the other party to compromise elsewhere.

The counter: Test the deadlock. "If we could solve this issue, would you be prepared to move on payment terms?" If they say yes, explore solutions collaboratively. If no, you've exposed the bluff.

Information Asymmetry Exploitation

The tactic: Hide information that would weaken your position while extracting information from the other side.

The counter: Ask specific, targeted questions. Verify information independently. Be transparent about your constraints (within reason) to encourage reciprocal transparency. "I've shared our cost structure. What are your underlying economics?"

Ethical reflection: Which tactics are legitimate strategy, and which cross ethical lines? A skilled negotiator wins on substance and preparation, not manipulation. Tactics that create short-term advantage often damage long-term relationships.

Simulation: Multi-Million Pound Contract Renewal

You are renegotiating a critical supply contract worth £12 million annually.

Your Position (Buyer)

Current contract terms: £12M annual, 3-year agreement, fixed price, supplier covers logistics.

Your constraints: Budget increase capped at 3% annually. Growing volume (10% annually) means the contract is becoming expensive. Your BATNA is reshoring to a domestic supplier with lower volume costs but higher capital requirements (estimated £2M capex).

Your actual target: Maintain 3-year term, limit price increases to 2% annually, shift some logistics costs to supplier, add a performance-based discount (0.5-1% rebate for meeting SLA targets).

Key pressure points:

  • Supplier is profitable at current terms; they're not desperate
  • You're their largest customer (35% of their revenue)
  • You have genuine reshoring option if they don't move
  • But reshoring delays operations by 18 months and costs capex
10:00

Your Task: Opening Statement & Strategy (10 minutes)

You're meeting the supplier's CEO for the renewal negotiation. Prepare:

1. Opening Statement

You have 2 minutes. What do you say? Frame the negotiation as principled, not adversarial. Acknowledge their value. Set the tone for collaborative problem-solving.

2. Your Interests (Not Just Positions)

What do you really need from this renewal? Not "reduce price by 2%" but "sustainable cost structure as we scale." This invites creative solutions.

3. Negotiation Strategy

How will you use anchoring, logrolling, and objective standards? What concessions will you trade for price discipline? How will you leverage your BATNA without threatening?

If negotiations stall: The supplier refuses to move below 4% annual increases and wants to shift logistics costs to you. You're at impasse. What's your next move? Do you invoke your BATNA, or is there untapped value to explore? What interests might you have misunderstood?
