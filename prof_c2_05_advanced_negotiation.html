Why this matters

Advanced negotiation at C2 level isn't about leverage — it's about reframing the game entirely. You're supposed to move parties from positional bargaining ("I want X% royalty") to interest-based negotiation ("What I actually need is certainty about future growth"). Your language shifts the entire negotiation from a zero-sum fight to a joint problem-solving exercise.

Six advanced moves

1 Reframing positions as interests +
A position is a stated demand. An interest is the underlying need. Example: "We want 8% royalty" (position) vs. "We need to ensure sustainable cash flow to our investors" (interest). Reframing allows you to find solutions that address the interest without accepting the position.
How it sounds

"I hear you want 8% royalty. Help me understand the underlying interest. Is this about cash flow? Risk mitigation? Competitive positioning? Once I understand what you actually need, we might find creative ways to meet that need."

2 Anchoring strategy +
The first number in a negotiation disproportionately influences the outcome. Anchoring high (for your side) shifts the negotiation range. Use language that makes your anchor seem reasonable, not aggressive. Ground it in market data or precedent.
The move

"Based on comparable licensing deals in this sector, royalty rates have ranged from 4% to 6%. We believe a 4.5% opening position is consistent with market precedent and the capital intensity of the technology."

3 The principled concession +
Concessions need to be tied to reciprocal movement. Every concession you make should be traded for something of equivalent value from the other side. Use language that makes this exchange explicit and maintains your credibility.
The move

"We can move from 4.5% to 5.25% on base royalty. In exchange, we need you to move on the minimum annual guarantee. We need confidence in committed volume, not optionality."

4 Multi-party dynamics +
In multi-party negotiations, side coalitions are always forming. Use language that acknowledges shared interests across parties while protecting your own position. Position yourself as the rational actor in an irrational situation.
The move

"All three of us benefit from this deal. It solves a real market need. But we're stuck on terms that aren't actually in any of our interests. Can we step back and ask: what would a deal look like that actually works for all of us?"

5 Breaking deadlock +
Deadlock happens when parties are locked in positional stances. Break it by introducing a neutral third party, by calling a timeout, or by proposing a radically different deal structure. Use language that reframes the impasse as a shared problem.
The move

"We're in deadlock. We agree the underlying deal is valuable for all parties. We're stuck on how to share value. Let me propose bringing in an independent financial advisor. They set the royalty rate based on market comparables. Both sides live with their decision. That removes the negotiation."

6 BATNA as leverage +
BATNA (Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement) is your true power in a negotiation. Use language that clarifies what happens if the deal doesn't close — without threats, just fact. This reframes the negotiation around shared value creation, not winner-take-all.
The move

"If we don't reach agreement, we have a clear alternative: we license to a different partner. That's viable for us. But it's not the partnership we want. The question is: can we structure something that creates more value for both of us than either of our alternatives?"

High-stakes scenario

Three parties, three weeks in, two royalty positions locked: Party A wants 6%, Party B wants 3%. You're the neutral lead negotiator. You've been shuttling between them. Each thinks the other is unreasonable.

You've just had a breakthrough conversation with Party B. They revealed their true concern: they don't want high fixed royalties because their markets are cyclical. They can't guarantee volume. They need flexibility.

Now you need to redesign the deal structure to address this interest while keeping Party A satisfied. Instead of a flat 4.5% royalty, what about a tiered structure? Base of 3%, stepping to 5% if volume targets are met?

2:00

Your proposal presentation: 2.5 minutes

2:30

Speak now. You're presenting to Party A. You need to show them this structure actually serves their interests better than a flat 6% guarantee they won't get anyway.

Scaffold: How to structure this

Acknowledgment (20s)

Real You wanted high base royalty. I hear you. I have a better solution.

Problem (30s)

Why A flat 6% doesn't work if Party B can't guarantee volume. We'll get a deal on paper and watch them default.

Solution (40s)

Structure Tiered model aligned with performance. You get upside if Party B succeeds. You get downside protection if they don't.

Party A says: "That's just dressing up a 3% opening. We asked for 6%. If you lower our expectation to 3%, we'd rather not do the deal. You're asking us to bet on their performance, which we can't control."

After a breakthrough, a new person joins Party A's team with authority to undo prior agreements. "We want to revisit the royalty structure. 6% base is our floor." You're back to square one.

Respond. Deal momentum is collapsing. How do you prevent this from derailing weeks of progress?

After six weeks of negotiation, you've reached agreement: 3% base, 5% at 80% volume threshold, 6% at 95%. Everyone's exhausted. They sign.
