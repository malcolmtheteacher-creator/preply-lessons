At the Dentist

Four years since the last one. A tooth at the back that has started objecting to cold water, and a waiting room you have walked out of twice before. Today you're going in — and the useful thing to say first isn't about the tooth at all.

The Scene 🎬

You are forty-one years old and you are frightened of a chair.

It's the noise, mainly. Not the pain — you've had worse pain from a stubbed toe — but the sound of the drill and the fact that once it starts you have no way of saying stop. Your mouth is full, someone's hands are in it, and you are lying flat on your back with no idea what's happening or how long it will last.

So you've put it off. Four years. And in those four years a tooth at the back has gone from "a bit sensitive" to something that makes you flinch when you drink cold water, which means the thing you were avoiding has quietly become bigger than it needed to be.

Nadia Rahman has been a dentist for eighteen years. Roughly a third of the adults who sit in her chair are frightened, and almost none of them say so — they sit there rigid and silent and she has to guess. She would far rather be told. Telling her is the whole lesson.

Nadia Rahman Your dentist. Brisk, kind, entirely unsurprised by nervous adults. She can work around your fear easily — but only if she knows it's there.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for being a nervous adult in a medical chair — admitting it, describing the problem clearly, and taking back a bit of control. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Admitting fear works best said lightly and quickly, almost as an aside — the way you'd mention a mild allergy. Said heavily it sounds like you want reassurance, and you'll get sympathy instead of adjustments. Said lightly it sounds like useful information, which is exactly what it is. Try it: drop your voice slightly, say it at normal speed, and move straight on to the next sentence.

🫣 Admitting it

Phrase 1

She's looking at your notes and has just seen the gap. Get in first — own it before it becomes something she has to raise. Put the pieces in order:

I've been putting this offI'll be honest with you,for years.
"I'll be honest with you, I've been putting this off for years."
"I'll be honest with you" is a small English signal that means here comes something slightly awkward, and I'm choosing to tell you. It buys goodwill because you're volunteering the thing rather than being caught with it. Saying it first also stops the conversation opening with her asking why you haven't been — a question you'd have had to answer defensively.
Phrase 2

Now the actual admission. Which version gets you treated well without turning it into a performance?

A. "I have a genuine phobia, I should warn you, I've had panic attacks in this chair before."
B. "I'm not great with dentists, I'm afraid."
C. "No, I'm fine, honestly. Let's just get on with it."
B — "I'm not great with dentists, I'm afraid."
C is the commonest and the worst — it's a lie that guarantees nobody adjusts anything, and you spend the appointment rigid and silent. A is honest but it makes your fear the event: she'll spend the appointment managing your emotions rather than your tooth. B is the useful middle — mild, unembarrassed, and impossible to argue with. "I'm afraid" here isn't fear at all; it's a British softener meaning sorry to say, which is why the sentence can carry the word twice without sounding dramatic.
Phrase 3

"Not great with dentists" is vague. One specific detail lets her actually do something about it. Name the specific thing — the noise, the needle, the lying back, whatever it is for you. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "It's the drill more than anything."
"More than anything" is the useful part — it narrows a large vague fear down to one thing she can work with. A dentist can do nothing with "I'm scared"; she can do a great deal with "it's the noise", because now she can warn you before she starts it, tell you how long it will last, and let you wear headphones. Specific fears get accommodations. Vague ones get sympathy.

🦷 Describing the problem

Phrase 4

Now the tooth. Point at it and describe what it does — in the everyday way people actually say it, not in medical language. Complete the missing word:

"It's this one at the back — it's been giving me ___ when I drink anything cold."

"It's this one at the back — it's been giving me grief when I drink anything cold."
To give someone grief is to cause them trouble or nuisance — it's used about anything from a tooth to a knee to a colleague. It's warm, informal and slightly understated, which is how most British people describe a pain that isn't an emergency. Notice the structure: which tooth, then what sets it off. Those are the two things she actually needs, and giving them unprompted saves five minutes.

🖐️ Taking back some control

Phrase 5

The worst part of the chair is not knowing what's happening. Ask for a running commentary — and give the reason, because the reason is what makes it a reasonable request rather than a fussy one. Put the pieces in order:

I hate not knowing.Could you tell meas you go?what you're doing
"Could you tell me what you're doing as you go? I hate not knowing."
Most fear in a dentist's chair is not fear of pain — it's fear of the unknown, and a commentary removes almost all of it. "As you go" is the key phrase: not an explanation beforehand that you'll have forgotten, but a live one. And the short reason afterwards — "I hate not knowing" — turns a request into an explanation, which people grant far more readily.
Phrase 6

This is the single most useful sentence in the lesson: a way to say stop when your mouth is full and you can't speak. Ask for it as a question. One word is missing, and it's the thing you'd physically do:

"If I put my ___ up, could we stop for a second?"

"If I put my hand up, could we stop for a second?"
Every dentist knows this signal and most will offer it, but if they don't, you ask. It changes the whole experience — not because you'll necessarily use it, but because knowing you can stop removes the trapped feeling that is the actual problem. "For a second" keeps it modest: you're not asking to abandon the appointment, just to pause it.

💷 Asking the awkward questions

Phrase 7

She's told you it needs a filling. You need the price, and you'd rather not sound like you're haggling over your own teeth. Which version asks cleanly?

A. "How much are we talking, roughly?"
B. "That's not going to be expensive, is it?"
C. "I should say now, I haven't got a lot of money."
A — "How much are we talking, roughly?"
B asks her to reassure you rather than inform you, and she can't honestly answer it. C offers information she didn't ask for and puts you in a weaker position before you know whether there's even a problem. A is neutral and businesslike — "we're talking" makes it conversational rather than a demand, and "roughly" tells her you want a ballpark, not a written quote. It's the standard English way of asking the price of anything you're slightly nervous about.
Phrase 8

Before agreeing to anything, find out whether it's actually urgent — because "needs doing" and "needs doing today" are different things, and nobody will volunteer the difference. Say the question out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Is that something that needs doing now, or could it wait a bit?"
An either/or question is much harder to give a vague answer to than "is it urgent?" — you've offered two options and she has to pick one. It also quietly signals that you intend to make the decision yourself, which changes how the rest of the conversation goes. Ask this about any treatment, anywhere: it separates the genuinely necessary from the merely recommended.
Phrase 9

The question everyone in that chair wants answered and half of them don't ask. Complete it:

"Am I going to ___ anything?"

"Am I going to feel anything?"
Four words, and it gets you the most reassuring information available — which in this case is a proper answer: no pain, some pressure, forty seconds of noise. Notice that the honest answer is better than a vague "you'll be fine", because "forty seconds" is a number you can count through. Ask for numbers whenever you're frightened; a measured thing is smaller than an unmeasured one.

✅ Deciding

Phrase 10

She's offered you the choice: now, or book it in. Say yes to now — and say the real reason, which is not about the tooth. Put the pieces in order:

than sit and worry about itI'd rather have itfor a fortnight.done today
"I'd rather have it done today than sit and worry about it for a fortnight."
The sentence that undoes four years of avoidance, and it works by comparing two costs honestly. Twenty minutes of discomfort against fourteen days of dread — put like that, the choice makes itself. "I'd rather X than Y" is a genuinely useful structure whenever you're talking yourself into something unpleasant: it forces you to name what avoiding it actually costs.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

There's a particular kind of adult embarrassment about being frightened of ordinary things — a chair, a needle, a phone call — and it costs people a great deal. Not because the fear is unmanageable, but because admitting it feels childish, so nobody says anything and nothing gets adjusted.

The odd part is how little it takes. Nadia can't remove the drill, but she can tell you what she's doing, promise to stop when you raise your hand, and say "forty seconds" instead of "not long". None of that is medicine. All of it is language.

And the four years were never really about the tooth. They were about not having a sentence.
