Because it's already clear what "ones" means from what the customer just said — repeating "trainers" again would sound clunky. English constantly swaps out nouns for one/ones, and whole ideas for so/not, so speech doesn't get bogged down in repetition.
Today: three ways to avoid repeating yourself — one/ones for things, so/not after think, hope and be afraid, and so/neither (plus do it/do that) for reacting — so you can sound more natural in everyday conversation.

Talking About Things: One & Ones

For comparing or choosing between things — without saying the noun twice.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday moments:

  • "This bag's too small — have you got a bigger one?"
  • "I love these earrings. Do you have the silver ones too?"
  • "That mug's chipped. Can I use this one instead?"

Before you check: when does English use "one" and when does it use "ones"?

Check the pattern CLICK
one replaces a singular noun (a bag, a mug); ones replaces a plural noun (earrings, shoes). Both usually follow an adjective, "this/that," or "the": "a bigger one," "the silver ones," "this one."
one → replaces a singular noun
ones → replaces a plural noun
(adjective / this / that / the) + one(s)

From repeating the noun to swapping it out

"I don't like this jacket. I prefer that jacket."
→
"I don't like this jacket. I prefer that one."

The second "jacket" is already understood, so it's replaced with "one" instead of repeated.

Short Answers: I Think So, I Hope Not

For answering a yes/no question without repeating the whole idea.

Spot the pattern

Three quick exchanges:

  • "Is the train delayed again?" — "I think so."
  • "Will it rain later?" — "I hope not."
  • "Are we losing the game?" — "I'm afraid so."

What's replacing the whole question here? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
so stands in for the whole positive idea ("the train is delayed"); not stands in for the whole negative idea. After think, the negative is usually "don't think so"; after hope and be afraid, it's "hope not" / "afraid not."
think so / don't think so
hope so / hope not
'm afraid so / 'm afraid not

Agreeing Without Repeating Yourself

For reacting to what someone just said — without saying the whole sentence again.

Spot the pattern

Three quick reactions:

  • "I love this song." — "So do I."
  • "I can't eat spicy food." — "Neither can I."
  • "I've never been to Japan." — "Neither have I."

What comes first — the auxiliary or "I"? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
The auxiliary comes before the subject: So + do/does/did/have/can... + I. Use so to agree with a positive statement, neither to agree with a negative one — and match the auxiliary to the first speaker's verb.
So + auxiliary + subject (agree with a positive)
Neither + auxiliary + subject (agree with a negative)

From repeating the sentence to reacting

"I work from home on Fridays." "I work from home on Fridays too."
→
"I work from home on Fridays." "So do I."

Reacting to a request or instruction

Sometimes you're not agreeing — you're accepting a task. English uses do it or do that in everyday speech, and the more formal do so after "asked/told... to," especially when telling a story about it later.

do it / do that (everyday, spoken)
do so (more formal — often after "asked/told... to")
