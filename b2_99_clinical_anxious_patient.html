Reassuring an Anxious Patient

B2 Level · Clinical Practice · Patient Communication

Nursing / Clinical

First Thoughts

A patient is due for a routine procedure in twenty minutes. They are visibly anxious — breathing quickly, asking the same questions in slightly different ways, telling you about a relative who had ‘a bad experience’.

Your job is not to dismiss the anxiety, and not to over-promise. It is to bring the patient into the room calm enough to cooperate, and to trust what is about to happen.

Take a moment with each question below. Share as much detail as you like — specific examples, what you would actually say, what tripped you up last time.

1. What are the signs — in body language, in words — that tell you a patient is more anxious than they are admitting?

2. Is it helpful or harmful to say “Don’t worry, you’ll be fine”? Why?

3. What does a patient actually need from you in the ten minutes before a procedure? Information? Calm? Distraction? All three?

4. Think of a healthcare professional who made you feel genuinely safe. What did they do, specifically?

Quick opener — pick whichever you prefer:

  • The patient’s first words to you are: “I’ve had a bad feeling about this all week.” What is your very first response?
  • A senior nurse once said: “The most powerful tool in this room is your tone of voice, not the monitor.” Do you agree?

Key Vocabulary

Eight words and phrases you will actually use in this conversation. Click each card to reveal the meaning, then say the word aloud and try to use it in a sentence from your own experience.

a procedureA planned medical action, such as an operation, scan, or test. Softer than the word ‘operation’ for many patients.
to reassureTo help someone feel calmer and more confident, without lying to them about risk.
side effectsThe effects of a treatment or medication that are not the main intended effect — often mild but sometimes important.
anaestheticA medication that stops you feeling pain — either in one area (local) or by putting you to sleep (general).
consentThe patient’s formal agreement to go ahead, based on understanding the procedure and its risks.
recoveryThe period after the procedure when the patient rests and is monitored before going home.
to talk through (a procedure)To explain each step so the patient knows what to expect and feels prepared.
a drip / IV lineA small tube placed in a vein to give fluids or medication. Common before procedures.

Key Language

Useful chunks of language for this conversation. Read each phrase aloud and think about when you would actually reach for it.

Opening — naming the feeling

  • “I can see you’re feeling anxious — that’s completely understandable, and I’d rather you told me than kept it to yourself.”
  • “Before we go through the paperwork, can you tell me what’s going through your mind right now?”
  • “A lot of people feel exactly the way you’re feeling right now — you’re not being silly.”

Giving clear, honest information

  • “Let me talk you through what is going to happen in the next twenty minutes.”
  • “You will feel a small scratch, and then a cool feeling up your arm — that is completely normal.”
  • “I won’t tell you there’s zero risk — I will tell you the team doing this does it every single day.”

Checking understanding gently

  • “Is there anything I’ve said that doesn’t quite make sense, or that you’d like me to go over again?”
  • “What questions are still sitting in your head that you haven’t asked yet?”
  • “Before we sign the consent form, tell me in your own words what we’re about to do — just so I know I’ve explained it properly.”

Staying with the patient

  • “I’m going to be right there with you before and after — you’ll see me again in recovery.”
  • “If at any point you want to stop and ask a question, you can, and you won’t be slowing anyone down.”
  • “Take a slow breath with me now. In … and out. We’re not in a rush.”

Say it better

Blunt lines sound natural under pressure, but they cost trust. Compare each one with a stronger version.

Don’t worry, it’s fine.
It’s normal to feel nervous — what’s worrying you most?
Acknowledges before reassuring.
Nothing will go wrong.
This is a routine procedure, and you’ll be monitored closely throughout.
Honest and still reassuring.
We haven’t got time for this.
We have a few minutes — let’s go through it together.
Gives the patient time, even when there is little.

The Scenario

The situation: A day-case unit. Patient: 52-year-old, in for a routine endoscopy under light sedation. Pre-op bay. Twenty minutes until they go in. Patient is pale, hands shaking, keeps mentioning a relative who ‘had a bad experience’ with anaesthetic fifteen years ago. The anaesthetist is elsewhere; you are the nurse handling pre-op preparation.

Your task: Get the patient from this state to the point of walking calmly into the procedure room, without dismissing the fear, without over-promising, and without rushing. Keep your language plain, your tone warm, and your information honest.

Your response should include:

Step 1 · Plan your response

Use the timer. Make notes — do not write full sentences. Think about what you will say first.

Planning time: 5:00

Step 2 · Deliver

When you are ready, role-play the conversation out loud. Be ready to handle the follow-up questions below.

Follow-up questions to handle:

  • My aunt had this and she never really recovered. How do you know that won’t happen to me?
  • What if I panic when I’m in there? Will someone stop?
  • Can I change my mind right now?
  • Will you be there when I wake up?

Step 3 · Compare with one version

Once you have said your own answer aloud, look at one way it could sound.

Show Version

“I can see you’re feeling nervous, and that’s completely understandable. Would you like to tell me what’s worrying you most?”

“It sounds as if your aunt’s experience was frightening. Anaesthetic then was different from today: we monitor you closely throughout, and the dose is much lighter for this procedure.”

“I can’t promise that nothing at all will go wrong — nobody honestly can — but this is a very common, routine procedure, and you’ll have a nurse and an anaesthetist with you the whole time.”

“Let’s take it one step at a time. Take a slow breath with me. If you need to stop or ask a question at any point, just say so.”

Notice: Listen first; name the feeling; honest information; no false promises; small, calming steps.
