Joining Ideas Together

And, but, so, or — four small words that connect two complete ideas into one sentence.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "I finished work early, and I went straight to the gym."
  • "We booked a table for eight, but only six of us turned up."
  • "The car wouldn't start, so we called a taxi."

Each one joins two complete sentences — each half has its own subject and verb. What always comes right before the joining word? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
A comma, right before the joining word. When you connect two full sentences with and/but/so/or, put a comma just before the conjunction.
full sentence + , and / but / so / or + full sentence

Choosing the right word:

  • and — adds one idea to another
  • but — shows contrast or surprise
  • so — shows a result
  • or — shows a choice
Watch out — the comma splice: joining two full sentences with only a comma, and no and/but/so/or, is a common mistake. NOT "It was late, we went home." Instead: "It was late, so we went home."

Reasons, Contrasts & Conditions

Small linking words that explain why, show a contrast, or set a condition — and where the comma goes.

Spot the pattern

  • "Because the train was delayed, I missed my connection."
  • "I missed my connection because the train was delayed."
  • "Although it was raining, we still went for a walk."

Look at the comma in each sentence. When does a comma appear, and when doesn't it? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
When the small idea (with because/although/when/if...) comes first, put a comma after it. When it comes at the end, attached directly to the main idea, no comma is needed.
Because / Although / While / When / If / Since + clause , + main idea
main idea + because / although / while / when / if / since + clause

Words for each job:

  • Reason — because, since
  • Contrast — although, even though, while, whereas
  • Time — when
  • Condition — if
  • Purpose — so that

Example with purpose: "She left early so that she could catch the earlier train."

Front or end — same meaning, different punctuation

"I missed the bus because I woke up late."
→
"Because I woke up late, I missed the bus."

Same words, same meaning — just move the reason to the front and add a comma after it.

Watch out: "although" and "even though" already mean "but" — never use both together. NOT "Although it was cold, but we swam." Instead: "Although it was cold, we swam." or "It was cold, but we swam."
because vs. because of: "because" is followed by a subject + verb — a full clause: "because it was raining". "because of" is followed by a noun, with no verb of its own: "because of the rain".

Keeping a Conversation Going

Small words that don't change the grammar of a sentence — they manage the conversation itself.

Spot the pattern

  • "The film was long. However, I enjoyed every minute."
  • "I liked the hotel. It was expensive, though."
  • "Anyway, tell me about your weekend — how was it?"

These words don't join two clauses the way "but" does. What job do they do instead? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
They manage the conversation rather than the grammar. However and though both add a quiet contrast — however starts a new sentence, though sits alone at the very end. Anyway moves the conversation back to the main point, or on to something new.

More conversation connectors:

  • by the way — introduces a new, small extra topic: "By the way, did you get my message?"
  • actually — corrects something, or adds a slightly surprising true fact: "Actually, I've never been there."
  • well / so — a small word to start your turn while you think: "Well, I'm not sure..." / "So, what do you think?"
Watch out: "however" almost always starts its own sentence, with a comma straight after it — it doesn't sit in the middle of a sentence after just a comma. NOT "The hotel was lovely, however the pool was closed." Instead: "The hotel was lovely. However, the pool was closed." (Or simply use "but": "The hotel was lovely, but the pool was closed.")
