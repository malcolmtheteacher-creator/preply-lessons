Putting a word right at the front of a sentence is like raising your voice on it — a listener's attention lands on whatever comes first. Speakers move the surprising or important part forward (or split an ordinary sentence into two parts, or trim it down to a short introductory phrase) to control exactly what someone notices. That's the whole toolkit for today.
Today: cleft sentences, fronting with inversion, and participle clauses — so you can shape what people notice and connect your ideas the way a fluent speaker does when telling a real story or making a point.

Focusing What Matters: "It was the noise that..."

For pointing at exactly the thing you mean — not the whole idea, one part of it.

Spot the pattern

Three sentences, each built around one focused idea:

  • "It was the noise that finally drove us out of the flat."
  • "What really surprised me was how calm she stayed."
  • "All I want right now is five minutes of quiet."

Before you check: what does each sentence do with the important information?

Check the pattern CLICK
Each one splits a plain sentence into two parts so the important piece gets its own spotlight — a cleft sentence ("cleft" just means split). "It was the noise that..." — It + was + the focus + that + the rest. "What really surprised me was how calm she stayed" — What + a clause + is/was + the focus. "All I want is five minutes of quiet" works the same way.
It + was/is + [focus] + that ...
What + [clause] + is/was + [focus]
All + [subject] + want(s)/need(s) + is + [focus]
The reason (why) + [clause] + is/was + [focus]

From plain statement to focused sentence

"The rain ruined our plans."
→
"It was the rain that ruined our plans."

Nothing new is added — the sentence is just rearranged so "the rain" gets the stress, not buried in the middle.

Making a Point: "Never had I..."

For real emphasis — moving the surprising word to the front, even if it flips your word order.

Spot the pattern

  • "Never before had the town seen such a storm."
  • "Not until the last train had left did we admit we were stuck."
  • "So tired was I that I fell asleep before the film even started."

Look at the word order right after the bold-worthy opening word. What's changed?

Check the pattern CLICK
Each one moves a negative or limiting word or phrase to the very front, before the subject — this is called fronting. Once that happens, the verb flips: instead of subject-then-auxiliary ("I had"), you get auxiliary-then-subject ("had I"), exactly like a question — this is called inversion. English does this so the front position, where attention lands first, carries the weight.
Never / Rarely / Not until X + auxiliary + subject + verb
Only then / Only after X + auxiliary + subject + verb
So + adjective + was/is + subject + that ...

From plain statement to fronted statement

"I had never seen such a mess in my life."
→
"Never had I seen such a mess in my life."
This isn't only for storytelling — it turns up constantly in formal writing too: "Not until the results were verified were the findings released." (formal). The everyday version and the formal version work the same way; only the content changes.

Joining Ideas Smoothly: "Having finished..."

For connecting two related actions or states into one economical sentence.

Spot the pattern

  • "Having finished the report, she finally went home."
  • "Left alone in the office, he started to worry."
  • "Being new here, I didn't want to ask too many questions."

What's happened to the subject and the helping words of the first idea in each one?

Check the pattern CLICK
Each sentence trims a full first clause down to a short opening phrase — a participle clause — leaving the main action or state for the second part. Same information, fewer words, and the two ideas read as one connected thought instead of two sentences stuck together with "and" or "because".
Having + past participle, + main clause
[past participle alone], + main clause
Being + adjective/noun, + main clause

From two sentences to one

"I paid the bill. Then I left the restaurant."
→
"Having paid the bill, I left the restaurant."
Formal writing leans on this constantly: "Having reviewed the proposal, the committee approved the budget." (formal). In conversation the same structure just carries everyday content instead.

Your turn

1. I finished all my emails. Then I left for lunch.

Combine using "Having finished..."

2. The letter was written in French. It confused everyone.

Combine using a past participle alone — start with "Written..."

3. I don't know the city well. I got lost twice yesterday.

Combine using "Being new to the city..."

One more thing about flow: notice how "Having finished all my emails," clears the short, familiar information out of the way first, so the newer, more interesting part lands at the end, where a listener's attention naturally goes. That's the same instinct behind fronting and cleft sentences — put what's already known or less important early, save the important or surprising part for last, or, when you want it to land even harder, move it to the very front instead.
