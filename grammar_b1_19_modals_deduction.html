Listen in on this conversation

Ali: Whose jacket is this? Someone's left it on the chair.

Rosa: It must be Dan's — he was sitting there all evening.

Ali: It can't be Dan's. Dan was wearing his jacket when he left, remember?

Rosa: Hmm, true. Then it might be Priya's — she rushed off to catch her train.

Look again. Nobody has checked the jacket. So how sure is Rosa when she says "must be"? How sure is Ali with "can't be"? And "might be"? Put the three phrases in order of certainty out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
must be = almost 100% sure it's true, because of evidence ("he was sitting there").
can't be = almost 100% sure it's impossible ("he was wearing his jacket").
might be = just possible — maybe 50%.
This is the grammar of everyday detective work: working things out from clues, without checking.
Today: must be, can't be and might / could / may be — so you can work things out like a detective in everyday life: whose jacket is that, why isn't he answering, what's that noise?

Sure It's True: "You must be exhausted"

For when the evidence is so strong that you're sure — even though you haven't checked.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday conclusions:

  • "You've been up since five o'clock? You must be exhausted."
  • "There's a huge queue outside that new café. The food must be amazing."
  • "I can hear music and about forty voices — the neighbours must be having a party."

Two questions before you check: what's the structure? And is this the same "must" as "you must wear a seatbelt"?

Check the pattern CLICK
must be + adjective or noun ("exhausted", "amazing") — and must be + verb-ing for something happening right now ("must be having a party").
And no, it's a different "must": nobody is ordering the food to be amazing! This "must" means "I'm sure, because of the evidence."
must be + adjective / noun  (I'm sure it's true)
must be + verb-ing  (I'm sure it's happening now)

From evidence to conclusion

Evidence: "His curtains are closed and his car has gone."
→
Conclusion: "He must be away."
Watch out: it's must be, never "must to be". "Must" goes straight to the verb — no "to", ever.

Sure It's Not: "That can't be Tom"

For when you're sure something is impossible. And a famous trap hides here — let's walk straight into it and out again.

Spot the pattern

Three impossible ideas:

  • "That can't be Tom at the door — Tom's in Brazil until Friday."
  • "She can't be hungry again — she's just had a massive lunch."
  • "They can't be sleeping — I can hear their TV through the wall."

Here's the trap. The opposite of "must be" should logically be "mustn't be"... but that's not what these sentences use. What do they use instead — and can you guess why? Say your idea, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
The opposite of must be is can't be — never "mustn't be".
"Mustn't" belongs to rules only: "You mustn't park here." When you're working things out, English says it CAN'T be true — it's not possible. "Couldn't be" works too.
must be  ⇄  can't be  (sure it's true ⇄ sure it's impossible)
can't be + adjective / noun / verb-ing

Turning a guess around

"That must be the postman."
→
"That can't be the postman — it's Sunday!"
Say it once out loud to fix it in your memory: "Must be? No — can't be!" "Mustn't be" is the single most common mistake with this grammar.

Just a Guess: "It might be the fridge"

For when something is possible — maybe 50% — and you're honestly not sure.

Spot the pattern

Three honest guesses:

  • "There's a strange noise in the kitchen. It might be the fridge."
  • "Why isn't Sara answering? She could be driving."
  • "Don't throw that little key away — it may be important."

Three different modals this time. Do they mean three different things, or the same thing? Decide, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
might be, could be and may be all mean the same here: it's possible. ("May" sounds slightly more formal.) And just like the others, they take be + -ing for right now: "She could be driving."
might / could / may + be + adjective / noun
might / could / may + be + verb-ing
Trap 1: not "can be" for a guess about right now. "It can be the fridge" sounds wrong. "Can" describes what is generally possible — "This street can be noisy at weekends" — not one specific guess. For a guess: could / might / may.
Trap 2: "maybe" is one word and starts a sentence: "Maybe it's the fridge." Inside a sentence, you need the modal: "It might be the fridge" — never "It maybe the fridge" or "It maybe is the fridge".

Your turn

Complete each guess. Type all the missing words, using the words in brackets.

The situation

You're at a café. Your friend Jo is thirty minutes late — no call, no message. Now Jo's phone goes straight to voicemail. Jo is normally never late.

Talk it through out loud for one minute. Work through:

  • What are you sure of? (Think about the phone, and about Jo.)
  • What is definitely NOT the explanation? Why is it impossible?
  • What are two or three possible explanations — what could be happening right now?
