The Death Penalty and Forgiveness

Year 11 — Unit 28: Themes D & E

What You'll Learn

Examine Christian and Muslim responses to capital punishment. Analyse ethical debates on the death penalty and explore how forgiveness works in practice.

What is capital punishment?

Answer: Death penalty as punishment for serious crime (usually murder). Controversial across religions.


Do Christian traditions agree on the death penalty?

Answer: No. Catholics abolished it (Vatican II). Most Protestants oppose it. Some cite 'eye for eye' and Old Testament support.


How does Islamic law view capital punishment?

Answer: Qisas (retaliation) allows death penalty for murder. But forgiveness is spiritually preferred; families often choose diyah (compensation).

Christian Views on Capital Punishment

Catholic Position (Abolitionist)

Vatican II (1962–65): The Church acknowledged that death penalty contradicts dignity of human life, even though Old Testament endorses it. Pope John Paul II & Francis: Called for abolition worldwide. Reasoning: - Human life is sacred, created in God's image (Genesis 1:27) - Only God should decide life/death - Justice can be served through life imprisonment - Possibility of wrongful execution (irreversible error) - Jesus modelled mercy, not retribution - Modern prisons can protect society without killing Development: Catholic teaching evolved. Medieval theology supported death penalty; modern theology rejects it.

Protestant Diversity

Conservative position: - Old Testament: "eye for eye" (Exodus 21:24) - "Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man shall his blood be shed" (Genesis 9:6) - Government's duty to execute murderers (Romans 13:4) - These passages support capital punishment Progressive position: - Jesus exemplified mercy and forgiveness, not retribution - "Let anyone without sin cast the first stone" (John 8:7) - Life is sacred; only God takes it - Wrongful executions are real (DNA exonerations prove this) - Life imprisonment achieves protection and deterrence Mainstream Protestant view: Most major denominations (Methodist, Presbyterian, Lutheran) oppose capital punishment as incompatible with Christian values.

Islamic Perspectives on Capital Punishment

Qisas and the Death Penalty

Qur'an 5:45: "We ordained for them: Life for life, eye for eye, nose for nose, ear for ear, tooth for tooth, and wounds equal for equal."

Islamic law permits death penalty (qisas) for murder. Key conditions: - Murder must be proven beyond doubt - Family of victim must request qisas - Judge oversees process - Forgiveness is permitted at any stage

But Forgiveness Is Preferred

Surah 42:40: "The recompense for an injury is an injury equal to it: but those that forgive and make amends will have their reward from Allah."

Islamic tradition emphasizes that forgiveness is spiritually superior to retaliation. Families of murder victims often choose: - Diyah (compensation): Blood money paid to family - Life imprisonment: Without execution - Actual forgiveness: Release the murderer

Real example: In cases involving young perpetrators (age <15), Islamic law sometimes allows diyah instead of execution, recognising that youth deserves chance at redemption.

Modern Implementation

Varies by country: - Iran: Executes hundreds annually, including for crimes beyond murder (terrorism, apostasy, drug trafficking) - Saudi Arabia: Capital punishment for murder, but families can request clemency/diyah - Egypt, Palestine, Pakistan: Use qisas in principle but less frequently in practice - Turkey, Tunisia, Morocco: Abolished capital punishment, moving toward international norms

Key tension: Islamic law theoretically allows qisas, but practical applications and international human rights law push toward abolition.

Case Studies: Forgiveness in Action

Desmond Tutu and Apartheid Perpetrators (South Africa)

Context: After apartheid ended, victims faced murderers and torturers who had destroyed their families. Many wanted revenge.

Tutu's approach (Christian): Truth and Reconciliation Commission offered: - Perpetrators confess publicly - Victims share pain publicly - No death penalty; limited punishment - Emphasis on forgiveness and healing

Outcome: Some perpetrators were forgiven face-to-face. Some victims couldn't forgive but chose not to pursue revenge. South Africa chose reconciliation over retribution.

Religious foundation: Ubuntu (Zulu word): "I am because we are." Tutu's Christian theology of community healing shaped the TRC model.

Gordon Wilson: Forgiveness of IRA Bombers (Northern Ireland)

Tragedy: An IRA bomb killed 11 people in Enniskillen (1987). Gordon Wilson's 20-year-old daughter Marie was one of them.

Wilson's response (Christian): Within hours, he publicly forgave the bombers and the IRA. He said: "I have lost my daughter, but I bear no ill will. I have no desire for revenge. My personal faith is such that I must pray for these men and women that that they will come to realize the awful grief they have caused."

Impact: His public forgiveness shocked the nation and contributed to peace efforts. It showed that Christianity's message of forgiveness could transcend even tragedy.

Theological foundation: Lord's Prayer: "Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors." Forgiveness is mandatory, not optional.

Mohammad's Forgiveness of His Murderer's Cousin (Islam)

Historical example: Early Islamic period: A man's cousin was killed. The killer lived in fear. When the victim's family forgave him, they freed him from qisas (death penalty).

Islamic principle: Diyah (compensation) or forgiveness can replace execution. A murderer who sincerely repents (taubah) and the victim's family who forgive both perform spiritually virtuous acts.

Modern echo: In 2015, a Pakistani man sentenced to death for killing a Christian girl was forgiven by her family (with Islamic blessing). The forgiveness was celebrated as Islamic virtue.

Final Concepts

Capital Punishment

🔒 Tap to reveal

Definition: Death penalty as legal punishment, usually for murder. Christianity increasingly opposes it (Vatican II, most Protestant denominations). Islam theoretically permits qisas but prefers forgiveness and diyah (compensation).

Qisas vs. Diyah

🔒 Tap to reveal

Qisas: Retaliatory justice. Victim's family can demand eye-for-eye punishment (including death for murder).

Diyah: Compensation (blood money) paid instead. Qur'an explicitly prefers diyah and forgiveness (Surah 42:40).

Taubah (Islamic Repentance)

🔒 Tap to reveal

Definition: Sincere repentance for sin. Islamic theology holds that taubah is always possible and accepted by Allah. A murderer who genuinely repents may deserve forgiveness and clemency from victim's family and the state.

Summary: Death Penalty and Forgiveness

Christian development: Medieval theology accepted capital punishment; modern Christianity rejects it. Jesus modelled mercy and forgiveness. Vatican II, Pope John Paul II, and most Protestant denominations oppose the death penalty as incompatible with human dignity.

Islamic balance: Qur'an permits qisas (death for murder) but explicitly prefers forgiveness (taubah) and diyah (compensation). Islamic theology holds that forgiveness is spiritually superior to revenge.

Real-world examples: Desmond Tutu (South Africa), Gordon Wilson (Northern Ireland), and Pakistani families choosing forgiveness show that both traditions can move beyond retribution toward healing and redemption.

The case for abolition: Life imprisonment protects society. Wrongful executions are irreversible errors. Forgiveness and rehabilitation align with both Christian and Islamic values.
