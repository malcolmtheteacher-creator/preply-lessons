The Crusades

Two centuries of holy war for one city — and the two rival leaders whose respect for each other outlived the fighting.

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. For centuries, the city of Jerusalem was sacred to Jews, Christians and Muslims alike — a place each faith regarded as blessed and set apart from all others.
sacred (adj) — holy; connected with religion and treated with deep respect.
2. A pilgrim would travel for months across mountains and deserts, hoping only to pray at the holy sites once before returning home.
a pilgrim (n) — a person who travels a long way to a holy place for religious reasons.
3. Encased in steel from head to foot, the mounted knight was the most feared warrior on any medieval battlefield.
a knight (n) — a medieval soldier of high rank, usually mounted and heavily armed, bound by an oath of service.
4. The Pope preached a crusade, calling on all of Christian Europe to take up arms and march east in the name of their faith.
a crusade (n) — a medieval military campaign undertaken in the name of religion; (more widely) a determined campaign for a cause.
5. High walls, iron gates and a deep moat made the fortress almost impossible for any army to take by force.
a fortress (n) — a large, strongly defended building or town, built to withstand attack.
6. The two armies would besiege a city for months, surrounding it and cutting off its food until those inside could hold out no longer.
to besiege (v) — to surround a place with an army in order to force it to surrender.
7. When at last the defenders opened the gates, the two sides agreed on the terms of the surrender — who could leave, and what they could take.
terms (n, plural) — the conditions that both sides accept in an agreement, such as a surrender or peace deal.
8. Even his enemies praised the sultan's chivalry — the courtesy, mercy and honour he showed to those he had defeated.
chivalry (n) — the medieval code of honour, courage and courtesy expected of a knight, especially towards the weak or defeated.
9. Both leaders agreed to a truce, laying down their weapons for a fixed number of years so that ordinary people could rest from the fighting.
a truce (n) — an agreement between enemies to stop fighting for a period of time.
10. The long wars left a bitter legacy — mistrust and memories that would shape how East and West saw each other for centuries.
a legacy (n) — something handed down from the past; the lasting effects of an event or a person's actions.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A call to the East

In the year 1095, in a field in France, Pope Urban II gave a speech that would set two civilisations against each other for the next two hundred years. Jerusalem — sacred to Jews, Christians and Muslims alike — had passed under Muslim rule centuries earlier, though Christian pilgrims had long been allowed to visit its holy sites. Now, the Pope told the crowd, Christians in the East needed help, and the road to Jerusalem itself should be opened. Take up the cross, he urged, and go. Those who did would be called crusaders.

The response astonished everyone. Knights, lords and ordinary people alike sewed cloth crosses onto their clothes and set out on a journey of thousands of miles. Many had never travelled beyond their own village. They would march for years, through hunger, disease and unfamiliar lands, driven by a mixture of faith, adventure and the hope of land or forgiveness.

Against every expectation, the First Crusade reached its goal. In 1099, after a long and terrible siege, the crusaders took Jerusalem. The capture was followed by a massacre of many of the city's inhabitants — a brutal episode remembered with horror on all sides. The victors set up a string of Christian states along the coast of the Holy Land, and for a while it seemed the war was won.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who called for the First Crusade, and what did he ask people to do?

2. What did the crusaders achieve in 1099, and at what human cost?

1. Pope Urban II, in 1095, called on Christians in Europe to take up the cross, march east and open the road to Jerusalem.

2. They captured Jerusalem after a long siege, but the victory was followed by a massacre of many of the city's inhabitants.

Discuss:
  • Thousands of people left everything behind for a journey they might not survive. What mix of motives — faith, hope, fear, ambition — do you think moved ordinary people to go?

Part 2 — Saladin and the Lionheart

The Christian hold on the Holy Land did not last. In the decades that followed, a gifted Muslim leader rose to unite the divided lands around them. His name was Salah ad-Din — known in the West as Saladin. Patient, disciplined and widely admired even by his opponents, he rebuilt Muslim strength and, in 1187, met the crusader army at the Battle of Hattin, where he won a decisive victory. Soon afterwards, Jerusalem itself surrendered to him. This time there was no massacre; the terms allowed most of the city's Christians to leave in safety, and Saladin's restraint became famous across Europe.

The loss of Jerusalem shocked Christendom and set off the Third Crusade. Among the kings who answered was Richard I of England — Richard the Lionheart — a soldier of formidable reputation. For several years, Richard and Saladin fought a long campaign up and down the coast, besieging cities and manoeuvring their armies. Neither could destroy the other.

What made their struggle unusual was the respect that grew between the two enemies. Stories spread of Saladin sending fresh horses and even ice to his rival when Richard fell ill, and of the two men exchanging courteous messages across the lines. They embodied, for later generations, the idea of chivalry: that honour and mercy might survive even in the middle of a holy war.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was Saladin, and how did he treat the Christians of Jerusalem when the city surrendered to him?

2. What was remarkable about the relationship between Richard the Lionheart and Saladin?

1. A Muslim leader who united the region and retook Jerusalem in 1187 after the Battle of Hattin; he allowed most of the city's Christians to leave safely, and his restraint was widely admired.

2. Though enemies, they showed each other real respect — Saladin is said to have sent Richard horses and ice when he was ill, and the two exchanged courteous messages.

Discuss:
  • Saladin is remembered in both the Muslim world and the West as a model of honour. Why do you think some figures earn respect even from the people they fought against?

Part 3 — The long echo

The Third Crusade ended not in conquest but in compromise. In 1192, Richard and Saladin agreed a truce: Jerusalem would remain under Muslim rule, but unarmed Christian pilgrims would be allowed to visit its holy places in peace. Richard sailed home without ever entering the city he had fought so long to reach; Saladin died the following year, admired on both sides. Neither man had truly won.

Further crusades followed over the next century, but the tide had turned. One by one the remaining crusader strongholds fell, and by 1291 the last major fortress on the mainland was gone. The great project launched in that French field two hundred years earlier had, in military terms, failed.

Yet the Crusades left a long echo. They opened trade and the exchange of ideas, books and goods between East and West, feeding the learning that would reshape Europe. They also left a bitter legacy of mistrust between faiths and peoples — one still invoked, and still argued over, today. The Crusades are remembered very differently depending on where the story is told: as heroism, as tragedy, or as a warning about what people will do, for good and ill, in the name of what they hold sacred.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the truce of 1192 decide about Jerusalem and about pilgrims?

2. In what two very different ways did the Crusades shape the centuries that followed?

1. Jerusalem stayed under Muslim rule, but unarmed Christian pilgrims were allowed to visit the holy sites in peace.

2. They opened trade and the exchange of ideas between East and West, but also left a lasting legacy of mistrust between faiths and peoples.

Discuss:
  • The same events are remembered as heroism in one place and as tragedy in another. Why does the same history so often have completely different meanings depending on who is telling it?

Grammar — used to / would

To tell a story set nine hundred years ago, you need to describe a world that no longer exists: what people used to believe, the journeys knights would make, the way a siege would drag on for months. English has two neat structures for exactly this — the settled habits and states of the distant past.

The two structures:
• used to + base verb — a past habit or a past state that is no longer true: "Pilgrims used to travel for months to reach Jerusalem." "Jerusalem used to be ruled by different powers over the centuries."
• would + base verb — a repeated past action or habit, common in storytelling and description: "Knights would sew a cross onto their clothes before they set out." "An army would besiege a city for months."
The key difference:
• used to works for both actions and states: "He used to travel" ✓ and "He used to be poor" ✓.
• would works only for repeated actions, not states. "Knights would fight" ✓, but "Jerusalem would be sacred" ✗ — for a state, you need used to (or the past simple).
• So with verbs like be, have, know, believe (states), choose used to, not would.

1. used to or would? (some gaps allow only one)
