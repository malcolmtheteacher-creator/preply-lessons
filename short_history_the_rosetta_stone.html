The Rosetta Stone

A broken slab of stone that handed a whole lost civilisation back its voice.

Today: you'll tell the story of how one stone unlocked an entire writing system, and discuss who really owns the past — using cause & result connectors (thanks to, owing to, as a result, which meant that) and the language of discovery and translation.

Key words — guess first, then check

1. The king's decree was read aloud in every town square, and from that day no merchant dared raise his prices a single coin.

2. Egyptian hieroglyphs — those tiny pictures of birds, eyes and reeds — covered the temple walls, each delicate symbol carved with extraordinary care.
hieroglyphs (n, pl) — the picture-symbols used in ancient Egyptian writing. (adj: hieroglyphic)
3. The young couple inscribed their initials into the wet concrete of the new bridge, hoping the marks would still be there in fifty years.
to inscribe (v) — to write or carve words onto a hard surface such as stone or metal. (n: an inscription)
4. She moved from astronomy to music theory to ancient Persian without missing a beat, and her students agreed that no single subject could ever contain a polymath like her.
a polymath (n) — a person with deep, expert knowledge across many very different fields.
5. The doctor squinted at the prescription, struggling to decipher the scrawled handwriting before he dared hand over any pills.
to decipher (v) — to work out the meaning of something written in code or in an unknown script. (n: decipherment)
6. One quiet nurse ran the ward, the rota and half the doctors' decisions; when she finally retired, the whole hospital seemed to wobble, for she had been its linchpin.
a linchpin (n, figurative) — the single element that holds a whole system together and keeps it working.
7. Behind the museum glass sat a small clay artefact, its surface still showing the fingerprints of the potter who shaped it three thousand years ago.
an artefact (n) — a man-made object of historical or cultural interest, especially an ancient one.
8. For twenty years the two chess champions could not bear to lose to each other, and their bitter rivalry soured every tournament they entered.
a rivalry (n) — a long-running competition or hostility between people striving for the same prize. (adj: rival)
9. Grandmother's recipes, the old folk songs, the festival held every spring — all of it was the heritage she was determined to pass on to her grandchildren.
heritage (n) — the history, traditions and objects a society inherits from the past and values as its own.
10. After the war ended, the soldiers waited at the docks for their repatriation, longing for the ship that would finally carry them back home.
repatriation (n) — the return of a person or object to its country of origin. (v: to repatriate)

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A lucky find in the sand

In the summer of 1799, French soldiers were digging in the heat of the Egyptian Delta. Napoleon had invaded Egypt the year before, bringing not only an army but a small crowd of scholars, and the troops were rebuilding an old fort near the port town of Rosetta — Rashid, in Arabic. As they tore down a crumbling wall, one officer noticed a slab of dark stone covered in writing, and realised at once that it might matter. It was, in fact, well over fifteen hundred years old.

The surface was inscribed with the same passage three times over, in three different scripts. At the top stood ancient Egyptian hieroglyphs — the sacred picture-writing that had defeated every scholar for centuries. In the middle was Demotic, a flowing everyday Egyptian script. And at the bottom came ancient Greek, a language European scholars could still read perfectly well.

That last detail was everything. The text itself was fairly ordinary: a decree issued in 196 BC by a council of priests, honouring the young king Ptolemy V. But because the dull Greek could be read, and the same words were repeated above in the lost Egyptian scripts, the stone offered something no one had ever held before — a bridge from a language people knew into one they did not.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How and when was the stone found, and by whom?

2. What three scripts appear on the stone — and why did the Greek matter so much?

1. It was found in 1799 by French soldiers rebuilding a fort near the town of Rosetta (Rashid), during Napoleon's campaign in Egypt.

2. Egyptian hieroglyphs, Demotic, and ancient Greek. The Greek could still be read, and since the same decree was repeated in all three scripts, it offered a way into the two lost Egyptian ones.

Discuss:
  • The soldiers were not looking for anything ancient at all — they simply stumbled on it. How much of what we know about the past do you think is down to pure luck like this?

Part 2 — The race to read the past

The French did not keep their prize for long. When their army was defeated and surrendered to the British in 1801, the stone was handed over under the terms of the treaty, shipped to London, and within a year it was placed in the British Museum, where it has stood ever since. But copies of the inscription spread quickly across Europe, and the real contest began — a quiet, stubborn race to be the first to make the hieroglyphs speak.

An English polymath, Thomas Young, made the early running. He proved that some of the hieroglyphs inside the oval rings — the cartouches — spelt out the sounds of a royal name, Ptolemy, rather than standing for whole ideas. It was a vital crack in a wall that had held for over a thousand years.

But it was a young French scholar, Jean-François Champollion, who finally broke through. Obsessed with Egypt since boyhood and fluent in Coptic, a late descendant of the old Egyptian tongue, he realised in 1822 that hieroglyphs were neither pure pictures nor pure letters, but a clever mixture of both — some signs standing for sounds, others for things. With that single insight, the whole system unravelled. After fourteen centuries of silence, ancient Egyptian could be read again.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the stone end up in London?

2. What did Young contribute, and what was Champollion's decisive breakthrough?

1. The French surrendered to the British in 1801; the stone was handed over under the treaty, shipped to London, and placed in the British Museum.

2. Young showed that hieroglyphs in the cartouches spelt the sounds of the royal name Ptolemy. Champollion then realised, in 1822, that the script mixed sound-signs and idea-signs together — which finally cracked the whole system.

Discuss:
  • Two men, working in rival countries, chasing the same answer. Does a "race" like this help discovery along, or does the rivalry get in the way of the truth?

Part 3 — Whose stone is it?

The consequences were enormous. Champollion had not merely translated one dull decree; he had been handed the key to a whole civilisation. Suddenly the endless inscriptions on temples, tombs and papyrus — millions of words that had been mute for over a thousand years — could be read. Names, prayers, tax records, love poems and the lives of ordinary Egyptians came flooding back into history. The word "Rosetta" now means any key that unlocks a hidden language, from forgotten scripts to the code of life itself.

The stone itself, though, has never gone home. It remains one of the most visited objects in the British Museum, behind glass, far from the river it was found beside. And in recent years Egypt has asked for it back, with leading Egyptian voices describing it as an icon of national identity and calling its removal an act of plunder.

The museum has so far declined, pointing partly to a law that forbids it from giving away its collection, and partly to the old argument that such treasures belong to all humanity. So the stone sits at the centre of a much larger modern debate: when an object is taken from one land and made famous in another, where does it truly belong — and who has the right to decide?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the decipherment so much bigger than one short decree?

2. What is the modern argument over the stone, and what does each side say?

1. Reading the stone unlocked the whole hieroglyphic system, so the vast written record of ancient Egypt — on temples, tombs and papyrus — could suddenly be understood again.

2. Egypt wants the stone repatriated, calling it part of its heritage and identity; the British Museum has refused, citing the law on its collection and the idea that it belongs to all humanity.

Discuss:
  • The stone was deciphered in Europe but made in Egypt. Does where something is understood give anyone a claim to keep it?

The grammar this story runs on: cause & result connectors

This whole story is a chain of causes and effects. Because the same text appeared in three scripts, scholars could compare them; thanks to the readable Greek, Champollion cracked the code; the hieroglyphs had been unreadable for centuries, and as a result ancient Egypt was a closed book. To tell it well, you need the connectors that link a cause to its result.

Cause + a clause (subject + verb): use because or as — "Champollion succeeded because he could read the Greek."
Cause + a noun (no verb): use because of, thanks to, owing to or due to — "Thanks to the Greek inscription, the code was broken." (Note: thanks to = a welcome cause; owing to / due to are neutral or negative.)
Linking two sentences (result): use therefore, consequently or as a result — "The Greek could be read; therefore, it was a bridge to the lost scripts."
Inside one sentence: which meant that ties a result straight onto the cause — "The same text appeared three times, which meant that scholars could finally compare them."
