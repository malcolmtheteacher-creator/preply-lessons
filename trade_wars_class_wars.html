Trade Wars Are Class Wars

When countries fight over trade, who really loses?

When the US and China fight a trade war, who are they really fighting against - each other, or their own workers?

In 2020, a book called Trade Wars Are Class Wars by Matthew Klein and Michael Pettis made a provocative argument that shocked economists and policymakers alike. Their central claim: trade conflicts between countries are actually conflicts between rich and poor within those countries.

The Numbers That Challenge Everything

1.5 million

American manufacturing jobs lost to trade with China between 1990 and 2007 - about 25% of all manufacturing jobs lost during that period. But here's the twist: economists found that workers didn't move to new jobs as predicted. They stayed in their communities and were never fully employed again.

Source: Autor, Dorn & Hanson research, published 2013
45%

The share of total national income earned by the top 10% of Americans - making the US the most unequal country in the OECD. In Europe, the figure is 36%. Klein and Pettis argue this domestic inequality is the real driver of trade conflicts.

Source: World Inequality Database, 2023
$46 billion

The cost of US tariffs on China - paid almost entirely by American companies, not Chinese exporters. These companies then cut wages, delayed expansions, and raised prices for American consumers.

Source: Federal Reserve & Columbia University research

Real People, Real Impact

Liu Shengzun, a 42-year-old factory worker from Guangdong, China, lost two jobs in a single month when US tariffs spiked to triple digits in April 2025. First his lighting products factory reduced output. Then the footwear maker that hired him did the same. Now he's returned to his hometown to farm, earning far less than the 5,000-6,000 yuan ($700-830) he made monthly in the factories. "I can barely afford food," he told reporters.

Sarah Mitchell, a former auto parts worker from Michigan, lost her job in 2008 when her company moved production to China. The economists predicted she'd retrain and find new work. Fifteen years later, she works part-time at a grocery store for half her previous wage. "They told us trade was good for everyone," she said in an interview. "I'm still waiting to see the good part."

The Book's Radical Argument

Klein and Pettis don't blame China or the US. They blame policy choices within each country that transfer wealth from workers to the rich. When workers can't afford to buy what they produce, countries must export their surplus - creating the trade imbalances that lead to conflict.

Their conclusion? "To end the trade wars, end the class wars." Redistribute wealth more fairly within countries, and the global tensions disappear.

Vocabulary Focus A

Click each card to reveal the full explanation. Practice using each word before moving on.

trade deficit Click to reveal
MEANING

A situation where a country imports more goods and services than it exports. The country is spending more on foreign products than it earns from selling its own products abroad.

FORM

trade deficit (noun, countable) - often used with: run a ~, have a ~, reduce the ~, the ~ with [country]

Opposite: trade surplus

REGISTER

Formal/technical - used in economic discussions, news reports, and policy debates. Common in business English.

"The US has run a trade deficit with China for decades, meaning Americans buy far more Chinese goods than China buys American products."
"Politicians often promise to reduce the trade deficit, but economists disagree about whether this is actually a problem."
NOW YOU TRY

Does your country have a trade deficit or surplus with major trading partners? What products does your country import most? Use "trade deficit" in your answer.

trade surplus Click to reveal
MEANING

A situation where a country exports more goods and services than it imports. The country earns more from selling abroad than it spends on foreign products. Countries like Germany and China consistently run surpluses.

FORM

trade surplus (noun, countable) - often used with: run a ~, have a ~, a large/significant ~, the ~ economy

Related: surplus economy = a country that consistently exports more than it imports

REGISTER

Formal/technical - same register as "trade deficit." Note: Having a surplus isn't automatically "good" - Klein and Pettis argue surpluses can indicate that workers aren't being paid enough to buy what they produce.

"Germany's trade surplus is the largest in the world, which Klein and Pettis argue comes from suppressing workers' wages and consumption."
"China became a surplus economy by keeping wages low and encouraging exports over domestic spending."
NOW YOU TRY

Why might a government want to run a trade surplus? Can you think of any downsides to having a large surplus? Use "trade surplus" in your response.

purchasing power Click to reveal
MEANING

The ability to buy goods and services with your income. If prices rise but wages stay the same, your purchasing power decreases - you can afford less. Central to Klein and Pettis's argument: when workers lose purchasing power, they can't buy what they produce.

FORM

purchasing power (noun, uncountable) - often used with: lose ~, gain ~, erode ~, declining ~, consumer ~

REGISTER

Semi-formal - used in economics but also in everyday discussions about cost of living, inflation, and wages. Very useful in professional contexts.

"Inflation has eroded the purchasing power of middle-class families, who now struggle to afford housing and healthcare."
"The book argues that when workers lose purchasing power, their countries must export the goods they can no longer afford to buy."
NOW YOU TRY

Has your purchasing power increased or decreased in recent years? What factors have affected it? Explain using "purchasing power."

Vocabulary Focus B

Two more essential terms for discussing global trade and economics.

capital flows Click to reveal
MEANING

The movement of money across borders for investment, trade, or business purposes. When a Chinese company invests in US real estate, or a German bank lends to a Spanish business, that's capital flowing between countries. Klein and Pettis argue that when surplus countries send capital to deficit countries, it often creates debt bubbles rather than productive investment.

FORM

capital flows (noun, usually plural) - often used with: international ~, global ~, ~ into/out of [country], manage/control ~

Related: capital flight = sudden movement of money out of a country (often during crises)

REGISTER

Formal/technical - primarily used in finance, economics, and policy discussions. Essential vocabulary for anyone working in international business or finance.

"Massive capital flows from China to the US helped fuel the American housing bubble that caused the 2008 financial crisis."
"Developing countries often struggle to manage capital flows, which can flood in during good times and suddenly reverse during crises."
NOW YOU TRY

Where do you think capital flows to and from in your region? Do you see foreign investment as positive or potentially problematic? Use "capital flows" in your answer.

protectionism Click to reveal
MEANING

Government policies that restrict imports to protect domestic industries from foreign competition. This includes tariffs (taxes on imports), quotas (limits on import quantities), and subsidies for local producers. Often presented as protecting workers' jobs, but Klein and Pettis argue it doesn't address the root cause of trade imbalances.

FORM

protectionism (noun, uncountable) - often used with: trade ~, rising ~, embrace/reject ~, a wave of ~

Related: protectionist (adj/noun) = supporting or characterized by protectionism

Opposite: free trade

REGISTER

Formal - common in political and economic debate. Often carries negative connotations among economists, though this is contested. Highly relevant in current news.

"The rise of protectionism in both the US and EU reflects growing frustration with globalization among working-class voters."
"Critics argue that protectionism raises prices for consumers while failing to create the jobs it promises."
NOW YOU TRY

Do you support protectionism for certain industries in your country, or do you favor free trade? What are the trade-offs? Use "protectionism" in your response.

Key Insight from Today

Klein and Pettis challenge us to look beyond the headlines about trade wars between countries. Their argument: when we ask "who wins?" we should look at classes within countries, not countries themselves. The wealthy may benefit from policies that give them a larger share of national income, while workers on all sides struggle with declining purchasing power.
