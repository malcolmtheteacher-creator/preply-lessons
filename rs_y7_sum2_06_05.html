Key Terms: Sikh Ethics

Sewa: Selfless service; serving others with humility and without expectation of reward; foundation of Sikh ethics
Naam Japna: Remember God; meditate on God's name; spiritual practice central to Sikh faith
Kirat Karni: Earn an honest living; work hard with integrity; worldly success is blessed if earned honestly
Vand Chakna: Share with others; give to those in need; redistribute wealth for community wellbeing
Langar: Free community kitchen in the Gurdwara; open to all people regardless of religion, caste, or wealth
Khalsa: Community of baptized Sikhs; committed to high ethical standards and defending the faith and vulnerable
5 Ks: Five articles of faith worn by Khalsa Sikhs: Kesh (uncut hair), Kangha (comb), Kara (bracelet), Kachera (shorts), Kirpan (sword)

The Three Core Duties of Sikh Life

1. Naam Japna — Remember God

Meditation, prayer, and remembrance of God. Sikhs spend time daily in spiritual practice, reciting the morning prayers (Nitnem) and the Guru Granth Sahib. This connects you to the divine and shapes your character.

2. Kirat Karni — Earn an Honest Living

Work hard. Build wealth through integrity and hard work. Sikhism does NOT ask for poverty or monastic withdrawal. A Sikh should work, succeed, and support their family. But earnings must be honest—no exploitation, no fraud.

3. Vand Chakna — Share with Others

Give to those in need. Distribute wealth. Your earnings aren't just for you—they're for the community. The poor have a right to share in your success. Giving 10% (dasvandh) is encouraged, though not strictly required.

Together, these three create balance: Earn honestly, remember God, share generously. This prevents both poverty and selfishness. You're connected to God, grounded in work, and committed to community.

Sewa: The Heart of Sikh Ethics

What is Sewa?

Sewa is selfless service—doing good without expecting reward or recognition. It's not charity (which can imply the giver is superior). It's humble service, recognizing everyone's equal worth before God.

Examples of Sewa:

  • Working in the langar (kitchen) to feed others
  • Cleaning the Gurdwara
  • Helping in the community without seeking praise
  • Caring for the sick and elderly
  • Standing up for those who are oppressed

The Goal: Through sewa, you develop humility, compassion, and connection to others. Your ego dissolves. You understand everyone's equal worth.

Langar: The Revolutionary Meal

Every Gurdwara has a langar—a free kitchen serving all visitors without exception.

Why Langar Matters: In Guru Nanak's time (1500s), caste divided people. High castes wouldn't eat with low castes. Guru Nanak said: "Everyone eats the same langar." This was revolutionary—it proclaimed human equality. Even emperors and princes sit on the floor to eat with the poorest person, served the same food. This isn't charity—it's affirmation that all humans are equal.

The Khalsa: Ethical Warriors

In 1699, Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa—a community of baptized Sikhs dedicated to high ethical standards and protecting others.

The 5 Ks: Visible Commitments

  • Kesh (uncut hair): Accept God's creation; don't alter your body to fit society's beauty standards
  • Kangha (comb): Keep your hair clean and neat; self-discipline
  • Kara (steel bracelet): Strength and protection; reminds you of God
  • Kachera (shorts): Modesty and chastity
  • Kirpan (sword): Protection of the weak and oppressed; willingness to fight injustice

These aren't superstitions—they're ethical commitments made visible. They say: "I am committed to equality, modesty, and justice. I will stand up against oppression."

Sikh Ethics in Practice: Challenging Inequality

Example 1: Caste is Rejected

Guru Nanak directly challenged the Hindu caste system. Sikhism says all humans are equal before God, regardless of birth, wealth, or profession. This was radical in medieval India. The langar physically demonstrates this—high and low castes eating together, served equally.

Example 2: Gender Equality

Sikhism teaches that women and men are equal. Women lead prayers, serve in gurdwaras, join the Khalsa. In a time when women had few rights, this was revolutionary. Guru Nanak taught that God is not limited to male or female—God transcends gender.

Example 3: Standing Against Oppression

Sikh history is marked by resistance to oppression. The 10th Guru (Guru Gobind Singh) created the Khalsa specifically to protect religious freedom. The Gurus were willing to die for justice. Sikhism teaches that fighting injustice is a sacred duty.

Example 4: Langar as Social Action

Beyond the Gurdwara, langar serves anyone in need. During famines, disasters, or conflicts, Sikhs serve free meals to anyone—believer or not. This is sewa—selfless service as a form of justice and compassion.

How Sikh Ethics Differ from Other Traditions

Similarity: Both value community and law. Both have detailed practices (kashrut for Jews, 5 Ks for Sikhs).

Difference: Jewish ethics emphasize textual interpretation and law. Sikh ethics emphasize sewa (action) and equality. Judaism has complex rules; Sikhism is simpler and more action-oriented.

Similarity: Both emphasize love and service to others. Jesus taught the Good Samaritan; Sikhs practice sewa.

Difference: Christianity is based on grace and faith in Jesus. Sikhism is based on action (sewa) and meditation (Naam Japna). Both call for justice, but differently—Christians through love, Sikhs through equality and action.

Similarity: Both have detailed ethical frameworks. Both require generous giving (Zakah in Islam, Vand Chakna in Sikhism).

Difference: Islam is law-based (Shariah); Sikhism is principle-based (Naam, Kirat, Vand). Islamic law covers every detail; Sikh ethics are about spirit and commitment.

Discussion Questions

Choose at least TWO and write 3-4 sentences for each.

Question 1: Is sewa more powerful than rules?

Sikhism emphasizes sewa (selfless action) more than detailed rules. Is this more or less ethical than religions with detailed laws? Can you have justice without rules?

Question 2: Can visible symbols (5 Ks) make someone more ethical?

Wearing the 5 Ks is meant to show commitment to equality and justice. But can clothing make someone ethical? Or do the symbols just express inner commitment?

Question 3: Does equality require sameness?

The langar treats everyone equally. But people have different needs, abilities, and circumstances. Is complete equality fair? Or should ethical systems account for difference?

Question 4: How should Sikhs respond when justice requires conflict?

The Khalsa was created to protect the oppressed, even with violence. When is fighting for justice justified? When does protection become oppression?

The Big Question

"Selfless service (sewa) is a more powerful force for justice than detailed laws."

Do you agree or disagree? Explore both sides.

Arguments FOR (Sewa is more powerful):

  • Laws can be oppressive or unjustly enforced. But selfless service assumes good intentions and care for others.
  • Sewa changes hearts. When you serve others humbly, you understand their needs. Law is external; sewa is internal transformation.
  • Laws require enforcement. Sewa requires only compassion. It's more sustainable because it comes from love, not fear.
  • Sikh langar has been serving all people equally for 500+ years without coercion. That's powerful.

Arguments AGAINST (Laws are necessary):

  • People can't be forced to serve selflessly. Laws protect the vulnerable when people won't volunteer.
  • Without laws, powerful people can exploit others under the guise of "service." Laws set binding standards.
  • History shows that voluntary service doesn't reach everyone. Laws ensure systematic justice.
  • Sikh ethics work because they're embedded in community and tradition. But modern plural societies need formal law.
Your turn: Write 4-5 sentences on whether justice needs laws, service, or both. Can they work together?
