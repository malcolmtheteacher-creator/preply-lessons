Key Sikh Concepts

Sewa: Selfless service; serving others with humility and without expectation of reward; foundation of Sikh ethics

Naam Japna: Remember God; meditate on God's name; spiritual practice central to Sikh faith

Kirat Karni: Earn an honest living; work hard with integrity; worldly success is blessed if earned honestly

Vand Chakna: Share with others; give to those in need; redistribute wealth for community wellbeing

Langar: Free community kitchen in the Gurdwara; open to all people regardless of religion, caste, or wealth

Khalsa: Community of baptized Sikhs; committed to high ethical standards and defending the faith and vulnerable

5 Ks: Five articles of faith worn by Khalsa Sikhs: Kesh (uncut hair), Kangha (comb), Kara (bracelet), Kachera (shorts), Kirpan (sword)

Understanding Sikh Ethics in Context

The Three Core Duties

Sikh ethics revolve around three central principles:

1. Naam Japna – Remembering God through daily meditation and prayer

2. Kirat Karni – Earning an honest living through hard work and integrity

3. Vand Chakna – Sharing wealth and resources generously with those in need

Sewa: The Foundation of Sikh Practice

Sewa means selfless service—helping others without expecting reward or recognition. In Sikhism, sewa is not optional; it is central to being a Sikh. It is a way to:

  • Serve God through serving humanity
  • Break down ego and pride
  • Build community and social equality

Langar: Sewa in Action

The Langar is perhaps the most visible expression of Sikh values. It is a free community kitchen attached to every Gurdwara (Sikh temple) that serves meals to anyone who arrives—no questions asked, no payment required.

Why is Langar significant?

  • It demonstrates and practices radical equality: rich and poor sit side by side
  • It rejects the caste system, which traditionally excluded lower castes from eating with higher castes
  • It shows that serving others is a spiritual act, not a chore
  • It meets practical human needs with dignity

The Khalsa and the 5 Ks

The Khalsa is a community of baptized Sikhs committed to the highest ethical standards. Members of the Khalsa wear five articles of faith—the 5 Ks—as visible markers of their commitment:

Kesh: Uncut hair (represents natural beauty and respect for God's creation)

Kangha: A comb (represents cleanliness and discipline)

Kara: A steel bracelet (represents strength and commitment to God)

Kachera: Shorts (represents modesty and self-discipline)

Kirpan: A sword or dagger (represents justice, courage, and readiness to defend the vulnerable)

Sikh Ethics and Equality

A core teaching of Sikhism is that all humans are equal before God. The Gurus explicitly rejected:

  • The Hindu caste system
  • Gender discrimination (women can be Gurus, lead prayers, participate fully)
  • Religious hierarchy or superiority

This commitment to equality shapes everything in Sikhism—from Langar (where all eat together) to prayer (where all voices are heard).

Comparative Perspectives

Similarities:

  • Both emphasize community responsibility and mutual aid (Tzedakah in Judaism, Sewa in Sikhism)
  • Both value hard work and honest earning as spiritual acts
  • Both have rituals that reinforce community bonds

Differences:

  • Jewish Tzedakah is an obligation for the wealthy; Sikh Sewa is for all regardless of wealth
  • Langar explicitly breaks caste barriers (which were not part of Jewish tradition in the same way)

Similarities:

  • Both emphasize service to the poor and vulnerable
  • Both teach that serving others is serving God
  • Both value humility and reject pride

Differences:

  • Langar is a daily, universal practice; Christian charity is often organized through institutions
  • Sikh service is not tied to converting others to the faith

Similarities:

  • Both require Zakat (Islamic alms) and Sewa (Sikh service)
  • Both teach that wealth carries responsibility to others
  • Both emphasize justice and care for the poor

Differences:

  • Zakat is a fixed percentage (2.5%); Sewa is open-ended and continuous
  • Langar is publicly shared food; Zakat is often private charity

Lesson Summary

What We Learned

  • Sewa (selfless service) is at the heart of Sikh ethics and practice
  • The three core duties—Naam Japna, Kirat Karni, and Vand Chakna—guide how Sikhs live
  • Langar is a powerful example of putting equality into action
  • The Khalsa and 5 Ks represent a visible, committed community living these values
  • Sikhism explicitly rejects the caste system and teaches that all humans are equal
  • These values connect to similar teachings in Judaism, Christianity, and Islam
