Warm-up — Why Sound Matters

In the last lesson we said poetry is language where the SHAPE matters as much as the meaning. Today we go further: in poetry, the SOUND matters as much as the meaning. A poem is built to be heard.

To see what sound is doing, look at the same line written two ways:

Version A — Sound stripped
The waves arrived gently on the beach this morning.
Version B — Sound at work
Soft surf slides up the silver sand at sunrise.

The two lines describe the same thing. But Version B has been engineered for the ear. Read both aloud. What do you notice?

What's different?
  • Alliteration — Version B repeats the /s/ sound: Soft, surf, slides, silver, sand, sunrise. The line HISSES like real surf on sand.
  • Rhythm — short, sharp words make the line feel quick. Version A's longer words slow it down.
  • Imitation — the sound of the line imitates the thing it describes (a sea sound). That match between sound and meaning is one of the deepest pleasures in poetry.

The principle

Good poets do not just describe a thing — they make the words behave like the thing. A poem about water moves like water. A poem about grief is heavy. A poem about laughter is light.

Today's eight tools are the ways poets do this.

Vocabulary — Eight Sound Tools

Tap each card. These are the eight techniques you'll meet in today's reading and practise yourself.

Alliteration
Tap for definition
The same consonant sound repeated at the start of nearby words.
e.g. "Salt slap, sand-shift" — /s/ recurring.
Assonance
Tap for definition
The same vowel sound repeated in nearby words. The consonants can differ.
e.g. "the bones of the boat below" — /oʊ/ recurring.
Consonance
Tap for definition
The same consonant sound repeated anywhere in nearby words — not just at the start.
e.g. "click-clack" — /k/ at start, middle and end.
Onomatopoeia
Tap for definition
A word that sounds like the thing it names. The word IS its meaning, audibly.
e.g. slap, click, hiss, buzz, crash, whisper.
Anaphora
Tap for definition
The same word or phrase repeated at the START of consecutive lines or clauses.
e.g. "He whispers weather. He whispers wind."
Parallelism
Tap for definition
The same grammatical pattern repeated across consecutive lines, even when the words differ.
e.g. "The lamp clicks. The kettle ticks." (article + noun + verb)
Internal rhyme
Tap for definition
Rhyme that happens INSIDE a line, not just at the end.
e.g. "the lamp clicks. The kettle ticks." (clicks / ticks)
Slant rhyme
Tap for definition
A near-rhyme — the sounds almost match but not perfectly. Often more sophisticated than a perfect rhyme.
e.g. home / bone, alone / stone.

Reading — "The Lighthouse Keeper's Morning"

Read aloud — twice. Once for the meaning, once for the sound. Notice how the words behave like what they describe. The highlighted phrases are this lesson's vocabulary in action.

The Lighthouse Keeper's Morning
Salt slap, sand-shift, the sea
slow-singing its same low song.
The gulls cry. They cry. They cry —
words I cannot quite hear.
The lamp clicks. The kettle ticks.
The keeper, click-clack down the iron stair,
turns the key in a clean blue door.
He has done this for forty Marches alone.
He whispers weather. He whispers wind.
He whispers wait to the bones of the boat below.
The bones of the boat below
have been listening for forty Marches alone.
The sun cracks the cliff like an egg.
The sun cracks the cliff like an egg.
The keeper is a clock with one hand left,
the lighthouse a finger pointing toward home.

What's in this poem?

The eight sound devices — where they appear
  • Alliteration — "Salt slap, sand" (/s/; "shift" starts with a different sound, /ʃ/), "slow-singing its same low song" (/s/), "bones of the boat below" (/b/), "He whispers weather, wind, wait" (/w/).
  • Assonance — "bones of the boat below" repeats the /oʊ/ vowel three times in one line (bones, boat, below).
  • Consonance — "click-clack" — the /k/ sound recurs at start, middle and end. "lamp clicks... kettle ticks" — the /k/ sound recurs across the stanza.
  • Onomatopoeia — "slap", "click", "ticks", "cry". Words that sound like the action.
  • Anaphora — "He whispers weather. He whispers wind. He whispers wait..." (same opening, three consecutive sentences). Also "The gulls cry. They cry. They cry."
  • Parallelism — "The lamp clicks. The kettle ticks." (same grammatical shape). "The keeper is a clock... / The lighthouse a finger..." (same shape across lines).
  • Internal rhyme — "clicks / ticks" within the stanza.
  • Slant rhyme — "alone" (end of stanzas 2 and 3) and "home" (end of stanza 4) — same vowel sound, different consonants.

Discussion

  • Read the first line aloud slowly. Why does the /s/ alliteration suit the SUBJECT (the sea)?
  • Why does the poet repeat "The bones of the boat below" twice? What does the repetition do?
  • The line "The sun cracks the cliff like an egg" appears twice. Why both times in the same stanza?
  • Where in the poem are the SOUNDS sharpest (think /k/, /t/) and where are they softest (think /s/, /w/)? Does that match what the poem is describing?

Drill — Spot the Sound Device

1. "Wet wind whistled down the wide, white street." What device is this?
Alliteration
Assonance
Onomatopoeia
Alliteration: the /w/ sound begins five nearby words (wet, wind, whistled, wide, white).
2. "The moon through the roof of the blue room." What is the dominant device?
Consonance
Anaphora
Assonance
Assonance: the vowel sound /uː/ recurs in moon, through, roof, blue, room. The consonants are all different.
3. "Thud. Splash. Crack. The window shivers." What are the first three words?
Alliteration
Onomatopoeia
Slant rhyme
Onomatopoeia: each word sounds like the noise it names.
4. "We waited for the train. We waited for the rain. We waited for you." What is the structural device?
Anaphora
Parallelism only
Assonance
Anaphora: "We waited for" begins three consecutive sentences. (The train/rain rhyme is a bonus sound effect.)
5. Which pair is a slant (near) rhyme?
night / light
light / lamp
light / late
light / late: the consonants frame the word the same way (l…t) but the vowel is different, so the rhyme is only half-complete. night / light is a perfect rhyme.

Bonus — try a tricky one

"Buzz" and "hiss" — what are these?

Both are onomatopoeia — words that sound like what they mean. A bee buzzes. A snake hisses. The word IS the sound.

English has dozens of these: splash, crash, thud, ping, swish, rumble, click, snap, drip, thump, whisper, mumble, sigh. They are the most direct sound-to-meaning bridge in the language.

A Real Poem — Alfred, Lord Tennyson

So far you have looked at poems written for this lesson. Now read a real poem by a real poet. Read it aloud before you do anything else.

The Eagle
He clasps the crag with crooked hands;
Close to the sun in lonely lands,
Ringed with the azure world, he stands.
The wrinkled sea beneath him crawls;
He watches from his mountain walls,
And like a thunderbolt he falls.

Alfred, Lord Tennyson (1809–1892), 1851

Tennyson was Poet Laureate from 1850 until his death, and he was famous for the musical quality of his verse. This poem is only six lines long, but it is packed with sound.

A routine for reading any poem

  1. Read it aloud, slowly. Then read it again.
  2. Ask who is speaking, and to whom. What is the situation?
  3. Say what happens in plain English. One or two sentences, no technique words yet.
  4. Notice the techniques and their effect. Do not just name them; say what each one does.
  5. Respond. What did you feel? Which line stays with you? Do you agree with the poem?

Look closer

Think about each question and answer out loud, then open the reveal to compare.

1. Which consonant sound dominates the first line, and why does it suit an eagle?

The hard /k/ sound: clasps, crag, crooked. It sounds like claws on rock.

2. Look at the rhyme scheme. What does it do?

Each stanza has one rhyme sound across all three lines (hands, lands, stands; crawls, walls, falls). The rhyme locks the lines together tightly, like the eagle gripping the rock.

3. Why does the poem end on "falls"?

The first stanza is still (the bird "stands"). In the last line the movement is sudden: "like a thunderbolt he falls". The line is short, fast and the word falls drops away at the end. Sound and meaning match.

4. Find the personification and the simile. What does each add?

Personification: "crooked hands" and "mountain walls" make the eagle and the cliff human-sized. Simile: "like a thunderbolt" adds speed and danger. Notice that both are used sparingly; the sound does most of the work.

5. Which sounds are soft and which are hard in the second stanza?

"Wrinkled sea… crawls" is slow and soft (w, r, l). "Thunderbolt" is hard (th, b, t). The poem moves from calm to violence in the sounds themselves.

Language for talking about poems

Use these when you speak or write about any poem.

Say what is happening

  • The poem opens with…
  • The speaker is…
  • The poet addresses…
  • The mood shifts when…

Name a technique

  • The poet uses [technique] to…
  • There is a striking image of…
  • The repetition of "…" emphasises…

Explain the effect

  • This creates a sense of…
  • This suggests that…
  • The reader is left feeling…
  • It reminds me of…

Give your view

  • What struck me most was…
  • I found … effective because…
  • I'm not convinced that…
  • For me, the strongest line is…

Say it

  • Read the poem aloud twice: once like a calm nature documentary, once like a thriller. What changes?
  • Which word would you underline as the most important? Why?
  • Is a poem this short enough? What would you add, or is it perfect?

Write it: a short analysis

Write four or five sentences about the poem. Use this shape: what the poet does → an example from the poem → the effect on the reader → your view.

Your analysis
Start with: "In 'The Eagle', Tennyson uses sound to…". Choose one sound technique (alliteration, rhyme or rhythm), quote it, and explain the effect.
Compare with a model paragraph (after you have written your own)

In "The Eagle", Tennyson uses sound to make the reader feel the bird's power. In the first line the hard /k/ sounds in "clasps", "crag" and "crooked" suggest claws gripping rock. Because all three lines of each stanza rhyme, the poem feels tight and controlled, which suits an animal that is perfectly still before it attacks. The last line drops suddenly with "he falls", and this surprised me because the poem has been so quiet. For me, this is the strongest example in the poem of sound matching meaning.

Your Turn — Three Devices, Three Stanzas

Pick a place. A kitchen at breakfast. A bus stop in rain. A train station at night. A street market at lunch. Anywhere with its own sound-world.

Write three stanzas. Each stanza must USE one sound device, deliberately. The frame below tells you which device goes in which stanza.

Writing timer
12 minutes. Fill the slots. Edit later.
12:00
Stanza 1 — Alliteration
Pick a consonant sound that matches the place (/s/ for soft, /k/ for sharp, /b/ for heavy). Use it three or four times in close range.
Stanza 2 — Anaphora (repeated opening)
Start three short lines or sentences with the same word or short phrase. "She wears…" / "She wears…" / "She wears…" — anything.
Stanza 3 — Parallelism + slant rhyme
Write two lines with the SAME grammatical shape. End them on words that are slant rhymes (not perfect rhymes).

When you're done, read all three stanzas aloud. Does each device LAND, or do you need to push it harder?

Free Write — A Soundscape Poem

The frame is gone. Now you write a poem about a place using sound as the main tool.

Your prompt
Write a 4–6 stanza free-verse poem about a place you know well, where the SOUNDS of the words imitate the place itself. Aim to use AT LEAST FOUR of today's eight devices. Jot the names of the devices in the margin of your notebook as you go.
Free-write timer
15 minutes. Don't stop. You can fix it after.
15:00
Stuck? Open this for ways to start
  • Start with the loudest thing you can hear right now.
  • Begin with three words that start with the same sound.
  • Begin with a sound you cannot name.
  • Begin with a place at 5 a.m., using only what you hear.

Use one of these as your first line, or ignore them completely. A first line only has to get you moving. You can change it later.

After you've written — the sound checklist

  1. Underline every alliteration. Are there at least two clear runs?
  2. Circle any onomatopoeia. Even one or two is enough.
  3. Mark any anaphora or parallelism. These build rhythm.
  4. Check your line endings — any rhyme, slant rhyme, or assonance landing on the last word?
  5. Read it aloud. Where does the SOUND of the line match the meaning of the line? That's where the poem is working hardest.
