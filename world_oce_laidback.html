Laid-Back or Lazy? Work, Fairness and “She'll Be Right”

Australians and New Zealanders are known for being relaxed — but they work, volunteer and fight for fairness, and the relaxed style is partly a deliberate choice.

The stereotype: “Australians and New Zealanders are lazy: they just surf, drink beer and take it easy.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The numbers behind the image

People in both countries average roughly 1,700 hours of paid work a year, which is in line with other wealthy countries and well below some, so the idea of a lazy culture does not match the statistics. The eight-hour working day was first won in the world in 1856 by stonemasons in Melbourne, who marched for “eight hours work, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest”, and the minimum wage in Australia is among the highest in the world. Part-time work is common, and many people value time outdoors, so the image of laid-back living is partly a real preference for balance. But the stereotype ignores the large numbers of people who work long hours in cities, mines and on farms.

Part 2 — Fairness, humour and the tall poppy

Two values shape the relaxed style. The first is egalitarianism: managers and workers are on first-name terms, and there is a strong belief in a “fair go”, meaning that everyone deserves an equal chance. The second is a discomfort with showing off. Success is welcome, but boasting is not, and a person who seems to think they are better than others may be teased or “cut down”, an attitude known as tall poppy syndrome. This is why people tend to underplay their achievements. A person who has just won a medal might say, “Yeah, it went pretty well”. Understatement is not carelessness, but a social rule about humility.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is a “fair go”?

2. Why do people tend to underplay achievements?

1. An equal chance for everyone.

2. Because boasting is disliked and tall poppies may be teased.

Discuss:
  • Is it better to show confidence or to be modest? When does each work?

Part 3 — Serious when it counts

The relaxed style hides a strong sense of responsibility. Surf Life Saving Australia has well over 150,000 members, mostly volunteers, who rescue thousands of swimmers every year, and rural fire services depend on tens of thousands of volunteer firefighters, who have worked for weeks during bushfire seasons. New Zealanders have also shown great practical resilience after earthquakes in Christchurch. A phrase like “she'll be right” expresses optimism and a willingness to fix things, but it can also hide a reluctance to talk about problems, and mental health, especially among men in rural areas, is a real concern. The culture is not simply easy-going: it is practical, humorous and, when needed, extremely committed.

Discuss:

Grammar — Adverbs of Degree and Understatement — Quite, Rather, Pretty, A Bit, Not Bad

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
Adverbs of degree make statements stronger or softer.
• strong: “It was extremely difficult.” “She works really hard.”
• moderate: “The weather was quite good.” “It went pretty well.”
• mild: “It's a bit hot.” “That's rather surprising.”
• understatement: “That's not bad” (excellent), “We have a bit of a problem” (a serious one).
Remember: quite + gradable adjective means “fairly” in British English but can mean “very” in some contexts; listen to the tone.

2. “How was the exam?” — “Oh, it was tricky.” (a bit / extremely, as an understatement)

3. “How was the concert?” — “Not !” (bad / good, as an understatement)

Going deeper

Stereotypes of the “laid-back” kind often arise from short visits, when a tourist sees people at the beach, and from marketing, which sells relaxation. They also contain some truth, since informality and humour are real parts of the culture. The mistake is in treating a style as a measure of effort. In fact, many people who seem relaxed are working very hard, but have learned that a calm manner is often the most efficient way to deal with pressure. For a visitor or a new colleague, the useful lesson is not “people here don't care” but “people here don't make a fuss”.

Worth noticing: Adverbs of degree: very, extremely, really (strong), quite, fairly, pretty (moderate), a bit, slightly, rather (mild). For understatement: not bad, a bit of a problem, pretty good can mean very good or very serious.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Do not take understatement literally. “It was a bit tricky” may mean “very difficult”, and “not bad” may mean “excellent”. When you give feedback, a gentle, humorous tone usually works better than a loud one, and avoid boasting about your own success.
