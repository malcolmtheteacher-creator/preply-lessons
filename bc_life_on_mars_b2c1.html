What Do You Know About Mars?

Let's explore Mars colonisation and what it would mean to be part of humanity's next great adventure.

Discussion Questions

  • What do you know about Mars? What makes it different from Earth?
  • Have you heard about Mars One? What would a one-way mission mean for the people who applied?
  • How would you feel if a family member decided to apply for a Mars colonisation mission?
  • What are the biggest problems an ordinary citizen would face living on Mars?
  • If you could take three personal items with you to Mars, what would they be? Why?
  • Do you think humans will colonise Mars in your lifetime? What would it take?

Idioms About Difficulties

In this section, you'll spot and correct mistakes in idioms, then review the correct ones.

Spot the Errors

Click each box to reveal the correction.

1 "We've bitten off more than we can swallow with this project."

Correct: "We've bitten off more than we can chew with this project."

Meaning: We've taken on more responsibility than we can handle.

2 "The stumbling stone in our plan is lack of funding."

Correct: The stumbling block in our plan is lack of funding.

Meaning: Something that prevents progress or causes difficulty.

3 "The mission goes without a hiccups if we plan carefully."

Correct: The mission goes without a hitch if we plan carefully.

Meaning: Everything happens smoothly without problems.

4 "If something goes apple-shaped, we need a backup plan."

Correct: If something goes pear-shaped, we need a backup plan.

Meaning: If something goes wrong or doesn't work out as planned.

Correct Idioms

These idioms are all correct. Learn them:

In a tight spot — in a difficult situation or facing pressure

A thorny issue — a complex or difficult problem to solve

Unmitigated disaster — a complete failure with no redeeming qualities

Have teething problems — experience early difficulties with something new

Passive Reporting Verbs

When discussing Mars colonisation, we often use passive reporting structures:

  • is said to — "The project is said to be the next giant leap for humanity."
  • is believed to — "Mars is believed to have the potential for supporting life."
  • is thought to — "The mission is thought to require extraordinary courage."
  • is estimated to — "The trip is estimated to take six months."

Discuss Your Reasoning

Once you've ranked the candidates, explain your choices. Consider:

  • Why did you rank your top candidate first? What makes them essential?
  • Which candidate would you struggle without? Why?
  • How do different skills complement each other in a team?
  • What would happen if you had to leave someone out?

Review & Reflection

Let's reflect on what we've learned and discussed today.

Idiom Review

Which idiom describes each situation?

  • We have limited budget and too many projects:
    We've bitten off more than we can chew
  • The shortage of trained pilots is preventing the mission:
    That's a stumbling block
  • Everything in the launch sequence worked perfectly:
    It went without a hitch
  • If equipment fails unexpectedly:
    Things could go pear-shaped
  • The team is struggling with early operational difficulties:
    We're having teething problems
  • Negotiations are complicated and sensitive:
    That's a thorny issue
Final Reflection Questions
  • Tell me about a time when you bitten off more than you could chew. What happened?
  • Have you worked on a project that went without a hiccup? What made it run so smoothly?
  • What could potentially go pear-shaped on a Mars mission? What's the biggest risk?
  • If you were leaving Earth to colonise Mars, how would you spend your final week? What would you do?
  • Is Mars colonisation the solution to finding a habitable planet for humanity? Or are there better alternatives?

Lesson Complete

Key takeaways: Idioms help us discuss complex problems naturally. Team composition requires balancing skills, personality, and physical resilience. Space exploration pushes the boundaries of human capability and cooperation.
