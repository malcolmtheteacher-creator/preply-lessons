The Starry Heavens Above

Kant Part 1: The Copernican Revolution in philosophy, phenomena and noumena

Today: Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) saved philosophy from Hume's skepticism. He asked: how is knowledge possible? His answer: we do not conform to objects; objects conform to our minds. We impose space, time, and the categories of understanding on experience. This Copernican Revolution puts the mind at the center. We can know phenomena (things as they appear to us) but never noumena (things-in-themselves). Knowledge is possible, but it has limits. And those limits protect faith—God, freedom, and the soul lie beyond knowledge, in the realm of practical reason.

I. Before We Begin

How do we know anything? Can reason prove that God exists?

II. The Story

Hume showed that reason cannot prove causation or the self. Kant responded: Hume is right that reason has limits. But those limits are not a disaster. They are liberating. The mind does not passively receive impressions. It actively structures experience. We impose space, time, and the categories of understanding. Knowledge is possible, but knowledge of how things appear to us, not how they are in themselves.

The Copernican Revolution

Copernicus said: the Earth revolves around the sun, not vice versa. Kant made an analogous move: we do not revolve around objects; objects revolve around us. Our minds structure experience. Space and time are not features of reality itself; they are forms through which we experience reality.

Hitherto it has been assumed that all our knowledge must conform to objects. But all attempts to extend our knowledge of objects by establishing something in regard to them a priori, by means of concepts, have, on this assumption, ended in failure. We must therefore make trial whether we may not have more success in the tasks of metaphysics, if we suppose that objects must conform to our knowledge.— Immanuel Kant, Critique of Pure Reason, Preface to the Second Edition

Phenomena vs. Noumena

We can know phenomena—things as they appear to us through the structure of our minds. But we can never know noumena—things as they are in themselves. We are trapped, not in the solipsism Descartes feared, but in the structure of our own faculties. This is not a prison. It is how knowledge is possible at all.

III. The Ideas

Idea 1: The Copernican Turn

The mind actively structures experience, not passively receives it. Space and time are forms of intuition that we impose on experience. The categories of understanding (causation, substance, etc.) are structures of the mind, not features of reality.

Idea 2: Synthetic A Priori Knowledge

Knowledge can be both necessary and ampliative (adding new information). Mathematics and physics give us synthetic a priori knowledge. Hume said this was impossible. Kant showed it is the foundation of all knowledge.

Idea 3: The Limits of Knowledge Protect Faith

By showing what we cannot know, Kant makes room for what we must believe. We cannot prove God exists. But we can believe it, practically need it. The same with freedom and immortality. Knowledge is limited; faith is infinite.
