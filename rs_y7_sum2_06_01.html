This lesson is about exactly this question: How do we know what's right? Is there one answer for everyone, or does it depend on where you're from, what you believe, and who you ask?

Key Terms: The Language of Morality

Morality: Principles about right and wrong; beliefs about how people should behave
Ethics: Similar to morality; the study of what is right and how we should live
Conscience: An inner voice or feeling that tells you whether something is right or wrong
Moral Authority: A person, text, or principle that has power to tell people what is right and wrong
Absolute Morality: The belief that right is ALWAYS right and wrong is ALWAYS wrong, no matter the situation
Relative Morality: The belief that morality depends on circumstances, culture, or context—what's right in one situation might be wrong in another
Divine Command Theory: The belief that something is right because God commands it, and wrong because God forbids it

Sources of Moral Authority: Where Do We Learn Right from Wrong?

Sacred Texts

Religious believers turn to the Bible, Qur'an, Torah, or other texts for guidance on morality.

Religious Leaders

Priests, imams, rabbis, and gurus teach believers how to live ethically.

Conscience

An inner feeling or intuition about right and wrong that doesn't depend on external rules.

Reason

Using logic to work out what's right: "If I hurt people, I don't want to be hurt. So I shouldn't hurt others."

Law

Governments create laws that define legal right and wrong. Not all laws are moral, and not all moral things are legal.

Family & Culture

We learn morality from our parents, community, and the traditions we grow up in.

Empathy

The ability to imagine someone else's suffering: if I know how pain feels, I won't cause it to others.

Personal Experience

We learn what's right by making mistakes and experiencing consequences.

The Big Philosophical Question: The Euthyphro Dilemma

Here's a question philosophers have asked for 2,400 years:

Is something good because God commands it, OR does God command it because it's good?

Option A: Something is only good BECAUSE God says it is. Without God's command, nothing is good or bad.

Option B: God recognizes what IS good (goodness exists independently), and commands us to do it.

This matters because it asks: Does morality come from above (from God), or does goodness exist on its own? Does God CREATE morality, or DISCOVER it?

Absolute vs. Relative Morality

Absolute Morality View

Many religious believers hold that some things are ALWAYS wrong. Stealing is always wrong. Murder is always wrong. These are written into the universe itself—not human inventions. The Ten Commandments express eternal truths.

Relative Morality View

Many non-religious philosophers argue that morality is NOT absolute. Context matters. Is it wrong to steal? Not if you're stealing food to feed your starving child. Is it wrong to kill? Not if you're defending yourself or others. Morality depends on circumstances and consequences.

Reality: Most people, religious or not, hold a MIXED view. We believe some things are always wrong (cruelty for fun) but also recognize that circumstances matter (is self-defense justified?).

The Golden Rule: Morality Across All Traditions

One striking fact: nearly EVERY religious tradition and philosophy has a version of the same idea:

Christianity

"Do to others as you would have them do to you." — Jesus (Matthew 7:12)

Islam

"Not one of you believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself." — Prophet Muhammad (Hadith)

Judaism

"What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow. This is the whole Torah; all the rest is commentary." — Hillel the Elder

Buddhism

"Treat not others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful." — Dhammapada (Buddhist scripture)

Sikhism

"Nanak, by thinking of others as yourself, all doubts are removed." — Guru Nanak

Hinduism

"This is the sum of duty: do not do to others what would cause you pain." — The Mahabharata

Humanism

"Empathy and respect for others' wellbeing is the foundation of ethics." — Modern Humanist thinkers

What does this tell us? Even though traditions disagree on many things, they agree on a basic principle: treat others with the respect and kindness you want for yourself. This suggests that morality might come from something fundamental about human nature—our ability to empathize, to imagine ourselves in another person's place.

Moral Dilemmas: When Rules Conflict

Absolute morality is easier to follow when rules don't clash. But what happens when they do?

The Trolley Problem (Simplified)

A runaway trolley is heading towards five workers on the track. You're standing by a lever that can redirect the trolley to a different track, where it will hit ONE worker instead. Do you pull the lever?

This shows the clash between absolute rules ("never kill") and consequences ("save more lives").

Stealing to Survive

Is it wrong to steal bread to feed your starving family? The absolute rule says "thou shalt not steal." But another value says "preserve life." Which wins?

Telling the Truth

A stranger knocks on your door and asks where your friend is hiding. Your friend is hiding from someone trying to harm them. Do you tell the truth and betray your friend, or lie to protect them?

These dilemmas show why philosophers debate whether morality is absolute or relative. When rules conflict, we have to CHOOSE—and those choices reveal what we really believe is most important.

Discussion Questions

Choose at least TWO of these questions and write 3-4 sentences for each. There are no right answers—these are for you to think deeply.

Question 1: Does morality need a God?

Can you have a strong sense of right and wrong without believing in God? What about if you believe in God but don't follow a religion? Where would your morality come from?

Question 2: Is your conscience always right?

Sometimes your gut feeling tells you something is wrong. But what if your conscience is wrong? What if your culture taught you something was moral, but it actually isn't? How would you know?

Question 3: Should we follow laws we disagree with?

If a law seems immoral to you, is it okay to break it? Think of an example. Would you break that law?

Question 4: The Golden Rule seems universal. Does that prove it's true?

Nearly every tradition teaches "treat others as you'd want to be treated." Does that mean it's actually true? Or just that humans naturally feel this way?

The Big Question

"All morality is relative. What's right in one culture is wrong in another."

Do you agree or disagree? Explore both sides.

Arguments FOR (Why someone might agree):

  • What's considered right varies wildly across cultures and time. Slavery was once accepted; now it's condemned. Different cultures have different views on family, gender, and honour.
  • No single culture has a perfect moral system. Who are we to judge another culture's values?
  • If morality were absolute, we'd expect universal agreement. But we see constant disagreement, even among religions.
  • Morality seems to come from human culture, not from nature or God. Humans create it.

Arguments AGAINST (Why someone might disagree):

  • Some things ARE universally wrong: torturing children for fun, genocide, slavery. The Golden Rule appears in every tradition for a reason.
  • Just because cultures disagree on details doesn't mean there's no underlying truth. Cultures also used to disagree on whether the Earth was round—but it is.
  • If everything is relative, then you can't say slavery WAS wrong—it just was "different." That seems wrong.
  • Empathy and human wellbeing are not culturally invented. They're built into our nature as humans.
Your turn: Write 4-5 sentences explaining which view you find more convincing. Can you hold both views in tension? (Some things absolute, some things relative?)
