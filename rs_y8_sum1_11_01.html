Ethical Theories — An Introduction

What makes an action right or wrong?

The Question: What Makes Something Right or Wrong?

You probably have ideas about what's right and what's wrong. But where do those ideas come from? How do you know? And when you disagree with someone, how do you prove them wrong?

Question 1: Give an example of something you think is morally wrong. Why?

🔒Think of anything you believe is wrong!

Examples: Lying, stealing, hurting animals, breaking promises. But your reason might be: "because it causes harm", or "because I was told it's wrong", or "because it makes people unhappy", or "because it violates a rule". Notice how different reasons support the same judgment?

The Dilemma

Here's a puzzle: You find your friend has lied to someone. Is this wrong? Most people would say yes. But consider these scenarios:

  • Scenario A: Your friend lied about liking a gift so as not to hurt the giver's feelings. Wrong?
  • Scenario B: Your friend lied to a bully about knowing where another friend lives, so the bully couldn't find them. Wrong?
  • Scenario C: Your friend lied to get out of trouble they deserve to face. Wrong?

Same action (lying), but our judgment changes depending on context. Why? Because we care about outcomes, rules, intentions, relationships, and character. In this unit, we'll explore how different ethical frameworks help us think through these questions.

Meeting Three Major Ethical Frameworks

1. Consequentialism: The Ethics of Outcomes

Consequentialists say the morality of an action depends on its consequences. A good action is one that produces good outcomes. A bad action is one that produces bad outcomes.

The Idea: What matters is the result, not the rule or the intention. "The greatest good for the greatest number" is the ideal. Applied to lying: Lying might be wrong in Scenario C (it's just selfish), but could be right in Scenario B (it protects an innocent person). The outcome matters most. Strength: This makes intuitive sense. We do care about consequences and wellbeing. Challenge: Predicting outcomes is hard. What seems good might have bad effects we don't anticipate. And this approach could justify terrible things if the outcome is good enough.

2. Deontology: The Ethics of Rules and Duty

Deontologists say actions are right or wrong based on rules, not outcomes. Lying is wrong—period—because it violates the rule against lying. Duty matters, not results.

The Idea: There are moral rules that should always be followed, regardless of consequences. Some things are just right or wrong. Applied to lying: Lying is wrong in all three scenarios because lying is against the rule. Your duty is to be truthful. Strength: This protects people and prevents us from justifying horrible things because we think the outcome will be good. Challenge: What if following a rule causes serious harm? Should you always follow rules even when the consequences are terrible?

3. Virtue Ethics: The Ethics of Character

Virtue ethicists ask: what kind of person should I be? Rather than focusing on actions or outcomes, they focus on developing good character traits (virtues) like honesty, courage, compassion, and wisdom.

The Idea: A good person will act rightly because they've developed virtuous character. Ask not "what should I do?" but "what would a virtuous person do?" Applied to lying: An honest person would tell the truth—except if honesty would cause serious harm and compassion is needed. A virtuous person would have wisdom to know the difference. Strength: This seems realistic. Good people don't follow rules blindly or calculate consequences coldly. They've developed wisdom. Challenge: How do you teach virtues? What if virtuous people disagree about what to do?
Key insight: All three frameworks make sense and appeal to us. We care about consequences, we do follow rules, and we do try to develop good character. The question is which should take priority.

Exploring How Religions Approach Ethics

Different religions tend to emphasise different ethical frameworks:

  • Buddhism: Combines virtue ethics (developing compassion) with consequence-thinking (understanding karma as consequences of actions)
  • Christianity: Emphasises rules (the Ten Commandments), but also virtue (developing the fruits of the Spirit), and consequences (the ultimate outcome is salvation or damnation)
  • Islam: Based on divine command (deontology—follow the Shariah), but with wisdom about consequences
  • Humanism: Emphasises consequences (wellbeing and happiness) and virtue (developing empathy and reason)

Question 2: Which ethical framework do you think YOU use most often?

🔒Be honest with yourself!

Answer: There's no right answer. Most people use all three at different times. You might think "what's the rule?" sometimes, "what will happen?" other times, and "who do I want to be?" other times. That's normal.

Applying the Frameworks to a Real Dilemma

The Trolley Problem

A trolley is hurtling down tracks towards five people. It will kill them all. You're standing by a lever that can switch the trolley to another track—but there's one person on that track. The trolley will kill them if you pull the lever. What do you do?

Consequentialist answer: Pull the lever. Five deaths are worse than one death. The outcome of pulling the lever is better.
Deontological answer: Don't pull the lever. You have a rule against killing. Pulling the lever means you're directly killing someone. The rule against that matters more than the numbers.
Virtue ethics answer: A person of wisdom, courage, and compassion might agonise over this. Either choice involves harm. What matters is that they act from the right character, trying to reduce suffering while respecting the individual.

Question 3: What would YOU do in the trolley problem? Why?

🔒There's no single right answer!

This is a genuine dilemma. Most people pull the lever (consequentialist thinking), but some don't (deontological thinking). Both positions have strong reasons behind them. Your answer reveals which ethical framework you naturally use first.

Evaluating the Frameworks

Strengths and Challenges

  • Consequentialism: Practical and compassionate, but hard to predict consequences and risks justifying harmful acts
  • Deontology: Clear and protective of rights, but can be inflexible and ignore consequences
  • Virtue ethics: Realistic and develops character, but vague about what to do in conflicts
The truth: Probably we need all three. Good ethics needs rules (to protect people), consideration of consequences (because outcomes matter), and good character (to navigate complexity with wisdom).

Consolidating Your Understanding

Key Ideas

  • Ethics is about deciding what's right and wrong
  • Three major frameworks exist: consequentialism (outcomes), deontology (rules), virtue ethics (character)
  • Different religions emphasise different frameworks
  • Real dilemmas often involve trade-offs between these approaches

Key Vocabulary

  • Ethics: The study of right and wrong
  • Consequentialism: The idea that outcomes determine morality
  • Deontology: The idea that rules and duty determine morality
  • Virtue: A good character trait or quality
