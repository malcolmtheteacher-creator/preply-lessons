Encounter: Three Big Ideas About Right and Wrong

The Problem

Everyone agrees that some things are right and some things are wrong. But people disagree about why. Philosophers have developed three major frameworks to help us think about morality. Each one focuses on something different.

Consequentialism

Focus: Outcomes

An action is right if it produces good consequences. An action is wrong if it produces bad ones. The most famous version is utilitarianism: "the greatest good for the greatest number."

Example: Lying to protect an innocent person is right because the outcome (safety) is good.

Deontology

Focus: Rules and Duty

Some actions are right or wrong regardless of their consequences. If a rule says "don't lie," then lying is always wrong — even if lying would produce a better outcome.

Example: Lying is always wrong because it breaks the moral rule against dishonesty, no matter what the reason.

Virtue Ethics

Focus: Character

Instead of asking "what should I do?", virtue ethics asks "what kind of person should I be?" A good person develops virtues — honesty, courage, compassion, wisdom — and acts from those qualities.

Example: A virtuous person would weigh honesty against compassion and use wisdom to decide.

Key insight: You already use all three frameworks. Sometimes you follow rules. Sometimes you think about consequences. Sometimes you ask what a good person would do. The question is which should take priority when they conflict.

Where Do These Ideas Come From?

Religious Roots

Many ethical rules come from religion — the Ten Commandments (Christianity), the Five Precepts (Buddhism), Shariah law (Islam). Religious ethics often combines rules with consequences (heaven/hell, karma) and character development (becoming more like God or achieving enlightenment).

Philosophical Roots

Ethics can also be based on reason alone, without religion. Humanists argue that we can work out right and wrong through empathy, evidence, and rational thinking. They focus on human wellbeing rather than divine commands.

Apply: The Trolley Problem

The Classic Dilemma

A runaway trolley is heading towards five people who will be killed. You can pull a lever to divert it onto another track — but one person is standing on that track and will be killed instead. Do you pull the lever?

Consequentialist

Pull the lever. One death is better than five. The outcome with fewer deaths is the morally right choice.

Deontologist

Don't pull the lever. You have a duty not to kill. Pulling the lever makes you directly responsible for someone's death, which breaks the rule against killing.

Virtue Ethicist

A wise, compassionate person would agonise over this. Either choice involves harm. What matters is that you act from genuine care, not cold calculation or blind rule-following.

Evaluate: Which Framework Is Best?

Consequentialism is the strongest framework

It's practical and compassionate — it cares about real-world results and reducing suffering. It explains why we sometimes break rules (to prevent greater harm). Most people instinctively think this way: we pull the lever because five deaths are worse than one.

Deontology is the strongest framework

It protects individual rights. Without rules, powerful people could justify terrible things by claiming good outcomes. The rule "don't kill innocent people" must be absolute, or no one is safe. Consequentialism could justify horrific acts if the maths works out.

Virtue Ethics is the strongest framework

Real life isn't about trolley problems. Good people develop wisdom, compassion, and courage over time. They don't need to calculate consequences or follow rules blindly — they develop the judgement to navigate complex situations. Character is what matters most.

Question 1: What are the three major ethical frameworks?

Answer: Consequentialism (outcomes determine right and wrong), Deontology (rules and duty determine right and wrong), and Virtue Ethics (good character determines right action).

Question 2: What does "the greatest good for the greatest number" mean?

🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: This is the principle of utilitarianism (a form of consequentialism). It means that the right action is the one that produces the most happiness or wellbeing for the most people.

Question 3: Give one strength and one weakness of deontology.

🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Strength: it protects individual rights and prevents people from justifying terrible things by claiming good outcomes. Weakness: following rules rigidly can sometimes cause harm — for example, refusing to lie even when lying would save someone's life.

Question 4: How does virtue ethics differ from the other two frameworks?

🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Consequentialism focuses on actions and outcomes. Deontology focuses on rules and duties. Virtue ethics focuses on the person — asking "what kind of person should I be?" rather than "what should I do?" It's about developing good character traits like wisdom, courage, honesty, and compassion.

Key Terms

Ethics
The study of right and wrong — the branch of philosophy that asks what we ought to do and how we should live.
Consequentialism
The ethical theory that actions are right or wrong based on their consequences. Good outcomes = right action.
Utilitarianism
A form of consequentialism: the right action produces "the greatest good for the greatest number."
Deontology
The ethical theory that actions are right or wrong based on rules and duties, regardless of consequences.
Virtue Ethics
The ethical theory that focuses on developing good character traits (virtues) like honesty, courage, and compassion.
Virtue
A positive character trait or quality — such as honesty, courage, compassion, wisdom, or fairness.
Moral Dilemma
A situation where you must choose between two or more options and each one involves some harm or moral cost.
Morality
The principles or beliefs about what is right and wrong that guide a person's or society's behaviour.
