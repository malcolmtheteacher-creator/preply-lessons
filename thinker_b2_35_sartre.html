Are we truly free to choose who we become, or do our past and our situation decide for us?

"I'm just not a morning person." "I can't help it, that's how I was raised." "My boss made me do it." We say things like this every day. But are they true, or are they excuses? A French philosopher who wrote in Paris cafés said almost every one of them is a way of running away from our freedom.

Today: You'll meet Jean-Paul Sartre and his idea that we are "condemned to be free", so you can talk about taking responsibility for your choices and spot when an excuse is really hiding a choice.
Before you read on: what is your answer to the big question right now? Say it in two or three sentences. You'll come back to it at the end.

Meet Sartre

Read one part at a time. After each part, close your eyes and answer the question before you check.

I

A boy among books

Jean-Paul Sartre was born in Paris in 1905. His father died when he was a baby, and he grew up in his grandfather's house, surrounded by books. He was small and could hardly see with one eye, but he was a brilliant student. In 1929, while studying in Paris, he met Simone de Beauvoir. They stayed partners for the rest of their lives, but they never married and never lived together.

Who was Simone de Beauvoir to Sartre?

His partner for the rest of his life. They met in 1929 but never married or lived together.
II

Freedom in wartime

In 1940, during the Second World War, Sartre was captured by the German army and spent about nine months as a prisoner of war. Back in occupied Paris, he wrote for hours in cafés like the Café de Flore. In 1943 he published Being and Nothingness, a huge book about freedom. Its message was simple but shocking: there is no plan for human life. We exist first, and then we make ourselves through what we choose.

What is the main message of Being and Nothingness?

There is no plan for human life: we exist first, and then we make ourselves through our choices.
III

The prize he refused

After the war, Sartre became the most famous thinker in France. His 1945 lecture, Existentialism Is a Humanism, was so crowded that, according to reports, some people fainted. He wrote novels and plays, including No Exit, with its famous line "Hell is other people." In 1964 he was offered the Nobel Prize for Literature and refused it, saying a writer should not become an institution. When he died in 1980, around 50,000 people followed his funeral.

Why did Sartre refuse the Nobel Prize?

He said that a writer should not let himself be turned into an institution.
Talk: Sartre refused one of the most famous prizes in the world. Was that admirable, or a kind of showing off? Would you have accepted it?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. The manager took full responsibility for the mistake and did not blame his team.
responsibility (n) — a duty to deal with something, or the fact of being the cause of what happens. Example: "Parents have a responsibility to keep their children safe."
2. She always has an excuse for not exercising: too tired, too busy, too cold.
excuse (n) — a reason you give to explain why you did something wrong, often not the real reason. Example: ""The bus was late" is his excuse every single time."
3. He blamed his brother for the broken window, but everyone knew he had done it.
blame (v) — to say or think that someone is the cause of something bad. Example: "Don't blame the weather; we just left too late."
4. Fans love the singer because she seems authentic; she never pretends to be someone she isn't.
authentic (adj) — real and true to yourself, not pretending or copying other people. Example: "Her style feels authentic: she wears what she likes, not what's in fashion."
5. Signing a two-year contract is a big commitment, so think carefully first.
commitment (n) — a promise or decision to give your time and energy to something. Example: "Learning an instrument takes years of practice and real commitment."
6. He regrets selling his old guitar; he still misses it.
regret (v) — to feel sorry or sad about something you did or didn't do. Example: "I regret not learning to swim as a child."
7. Sartre said a knife is designed before it is made, so its essence comes first; humans have no design.
essence (n) — the most basic quality that makes something what it is. Example: "Trust is the essence of every good friendship."

The idea: Existence comes before essence

1
ObjectsA knife is designed first, then made: its purpose comes first.
2
HumansWe are born with no design: we simply exist first.
3
ChoiceWe make ourselves through what we choose and do.
4
ResponsibilitySo we are responsible for who we become: no excuses.

See it in action

Sartre describes a café waiter who moves a little too perfectly: quick steps, a bright bow, the tray balanced just so. He is playing at being a waiter, as if being a waiter were his fixed nature, like a knife's purpose. Sartre calls this "bad faith": pretending we are a thing with no choice. But the waiter could leave tomorrow. Each morning he chooses to be a waiter. Admitting that choice is the first step to living freely.

Your turn

Now you: explain Sartre's idea to a friend who has never heard of it. Speak for about a minute. Use the starters if they help.

Sartre believed that...Unlike a knife, a human being...For example...That's why he said we are condemned to be free, because...
Sartre believed that no plan or nature decides who we are. Unlike a knife, which is designed for cutting, a human being exists first and then makes itself through its choices. For example, a waiter who says "I'm just a waiter" is pretending he has no choice, but he could leave tomorrow. That's why he said we are condemned to be free, because we can't escape choosing, and we are responsible for what we choose.

Think it through

Thought experiment: The dream job

A young woman is offered her dream job in another country. But her father lives alone, is not well and needs her nearby. She can't do both, and no rule tells her which is right. Sartre discussed a very similar case from wartime and told the young man involved: "You are free, therefore choose."

Now give two reasons for your choice. Then argue for the other side for thirty seconds. Sartre said no rule could decide it for her. Do you agree, or is there a right answer?

Connect

Compare

Kierkegaard and Sartre: Kierkegaard, the 'father of existentialism', also said each person must choose and become themselves. But for him the great choice was a leap towards God, while Sartre was an atheist. Does freedom feel different with God or without?
Kierkegaard thought freedom brings anxiety, but the self finds its true shape before God. Sartre said there is no God and no fixed human nature, so we invent our own values and carry full responsibility for them.
Nietzsche and Sartre: Nietzsche said "God is dead" and that we must create our own values. How does Sartre take this idea further?
Sartre agrees that no values are written in the sky. He adds that this makes every person fully responsible: when we choose, we show what we think a human being should be, so we can't hide behind excuses.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From Ludwig Wittgenstein

What did Wittgenstein mean by "meaning is use"?

That a word gets its meaning from how people use it in real life, not from one fixed definition.
From Friedrich Nietzsche

What do the camel, the lion and the child stand for in Nietzsche's idea?

The camel carries values given by others, the lion says no to them and wins freedom, and the child creates new values of its own.
From G. W. F. Hegel

How did Hegel think history moves forward?

Through conflict: one idea meets its opposite, they clash, and out of the conflict comes something new that keeps the best of both. Step by step, humanity becomes more free.
From Thomas Hobbes

Why, according to Hobbes, do people agree to be ruled?

Because life without government would be "nasty, brutish and short", so we give up some freedom to a strong ruler in return for peace and safety.

Final review

From memory, out loud. Then check.

1. What did Sartre mean by "existence comes before essence"?

That humans have no fixed nature or plan at birth: we exist first, then make ourselves through our choices.

2. What is "bad faith", in Sartre's example of the waiter?

Pretending you have no choice, as if your role were your fixed nature, when in fact you choose it every day.

3. What did Sartre do when he was offered the Nobel Prize in 1964?

He refused it, saying that a writer should not become an institution.

4. What does 'excuse' mean? Give an example.

A reason you give to explain a mistake, often not the real one. For example: always blaming the bus for being late.
