🌍 The Language You Think In

How Words Shape Reality (And Who You Become)

Curious Conversations | C2 Level | 50 minutes | Reflection & Stories

Does the Language You Speak Change How You Think?

🗣️ There are approximately 7,000 languages in the world — and they don't just use different words. They carve up reality in completely different ways.

Here's a question that has haunted philosophers and linguists for centuries: Do you think in language, or is language just a tool for expressing thoughts that exist independently?

For people who speak multiple languages, this isn't abstract philosophy — it's daily life. You've probably noticed: some thoughts come easier in one language. Some feelings have no translation. Sometimes you switch languages mid-thought because the other language "fits better."

What does this tell us about the relationship between language and mind?

5 Fascinating Ways Languages Differ

🎨
1. Colors That Don't Exist
When your language can't see what's there

Russian has two separate words for light blue (goluboy) and dark blue (siniy) — they're as different as "red" and "pink" in English. Studies show Russian speakers can actually distinguish these blues faster than English speakers.

The Pirahã people of the Amazon have no words for specific colors at all. The Himba tribe in Namibia has no word for blue, but many words for green — and they can spot tiny differences in green that Europeans miss.

The question: If you don't have a word for something, do you see it differently? Or do you see it the same but just describe it differently?
⏰
2. Time Runs Differently
Past, present, future — or something else entirely?

In Mandarin Chinese, time is often described vertically: the past is "above" and the future is "below." English speakers think of time horizontally (left to right).

The Aymara people of the Andes do something stranger: they gesture behind them when talking about the future (you can't see what's coming) and in front for the past (you've already seen it).

Some Aboriginal Australian languages have no words for "left" and "right" — instead, they use cardinal directions (north, south, east, west) for everything, even body parts. Speakers always know which way north is.

The question: Do these languages create different experiences of time and space? Or just different ways of talking about the same experience?
🪞
3. The Self That Changes
Are you the same person in every language?

Many bilingual people report feeling like a different version of themselves in each language. Research confirms this: bilinguals often score differently on personality tests depending on which language they take them in.

In one famous study, Chinese-English bilinguals described themselves as more assertive and extroverted in English, and more modest and collective-minded in Chinese.

The question: Is this because language shapes personality? Or because each language connects you to different cultural contexts, memories, and versions of yourself?
💔
4. Untranslatable Words
The feelings only some languages can name

Every language has words that capture experiences other languages can only describe with long explanations:

German
Schadenfreude
Pleasure from others' misfortune
Portuguese
Saudade
Longing for something/someone absent
Japanese
木漏れ日 (Komorebi)
Sunlight filtering through leaves
Ukrainian
Тужити (Tuzhyty)
To grieve deeply, to yearn
The question: Do speakers of these languages experience these feelings more often or more intensely because they have words for them? Or do they just recognize them more easily?
🔀
5. Code-Switching
Why you change languages mid-sentence

If you speak multiple languages, you probably switch between them constantly — sometimes in the same sentence. This isn't laziness or confusion. Research shows code-switching is a sophisticated cognitive skill.

Speakers often switch to express concepts their other language handles better, to signal identity, to add emphasis, or because certain memories are "stored" in a particular language.

The question: When you switch languages, what triggers it? Is it the topic, the emotion, the person you're speaking to, or something else?

  • Do you feel like a different person when speaking different languages?

Expressions for Language & Identity

Click each expression to see what it means and practice using it. These are sophisticated expressions for discussing language, thought, and identity.

1. "There's no direct equivalent in [language]..."
Click to see meaning and practice
Formality: Neutral — perfect for language discussions
Meaning: Use this when explaining that a concept, word, or expression doesn't translate well between languages. It acknowledges cultural/linguistic gaps without oversimplifying.
Example:
"There's no direct equivalent in English for the German word Gemütlichkeit — it's not just 'cozy,' it's a whole atmosphere of warmth, belonging, and comfort. You have to describe it, not translate it."
NOW YOU TRY:
Think of a word or expression from Ukrainian or German that doesn't translate directly. Explain it: "There's no direct equivalent in English for..."
2. "I find myself switching to [language] when..."
Click to see meaning and practice
Formality: Neutral — personal and reflective
Meaning: Use this to describe patterns in your own code-switching behavior. It shows awareness of when and why you move between languages without making it sound random or accidental.
Example:
"I find myself switching to Ukrainian when I'm angry or emotional — it's like my brain defaults to my first language when feelings are intense. English feels too... distant for that."
NOW YOU TRY:
Complete this: "I find myself switching to [language] when I..." — What triggers your language switches?

📝 Quick Check:

  • "There's no direct equivalent in [language]..." — explaining untranslatable concepts
  • "I find myself switching to [language] when..." — describing your code-switching patterns

Expressions for Deeper Analysis

3. "The way [language] frames it..."
Click to see meaning and practice
Formality: Neutral to Formal — analytical and insightful
Meaning: Use this to highlight how different languages present the same concept differently. "Framing" suggests that language doesn't just describe reality — it shapes how we see it.
Example:
"The way German frames it, you don't just 'remember' something — you call it back into your mind (sich erinnern). The way English frames it, memories are more passive — they just come to you. It's a subtle difference, but it shapes how you think about the process."
NOW YOU TRY:
Compare how two languages frame a concept: "The way [language] frames [concept]..." versus "The way [other language] frames it..."
4. "It loses something in translation..."
Click to see meaning and practice
Formality: Neutral — widely understood idiom
Meaning: Use this to explain that when something is translated, part of its meaning, beauty, humor, or nuance disappears. Can be used literally (about words) or metaphorically (about cultural practices, jokes, etc.).
Example:
"Ukrainian poetry loses something in translation — not just the rhyme and rhythm, but the whole emotional weight of certain words. Доля doesn't just mean 'fate' — it carries centuries of collective experience."
NOW YOU TRY:
Think of something specific that loses something in translation: a song, a poem, a joke, a proverb. What gets lost?
5. "I can't quite articulate it in [language]..."
Click to see meaning and practice
Formality: Neutral — honest and reflective
Meaning: Use this when you have a thought or feeling that you struggle to express in a particular language. It shows linguistic awareness and acknowledges that some experiences resist verbal expression in certain languages.
Example:
"When I think about home, I can't quite articulate it in English — the feeling is connected to Ukrainian sounds and smells and memories. Trying to express it in English feels like describing a color to someone who's never seen it."
NOW YOU TRY:
What's a thought or feeling you struggle to express in English? "I can't quite articulate it in English, but in [language] it would be..."

📝 All 5 Expressions:

  • "There's no direct equivalent in [language]..." — untranslatable concepts
  • "I find myself switching to [language] when..." — code-switching patterns
  • "The way [language] frames it..." — different perspectives
  • "It loses something in translation..." — what gets lost
  • "I can't quite articulate it in [language]..." — limits of expression
