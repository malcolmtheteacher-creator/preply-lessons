The Saxon Shore

Migration, culture, and the myth of the Dark Ages

♦ ♦ ♦
Learning Intention: Today we watch Rome leave Britain and see what takes its place — not an "invasion" but a slow, complex migration. We expose the "Dark Ages" myth and discover that Anglo-Saxon Britain was far from dark: it had Sutton Hoo, sophisticated kingdoms, and the arrival of Christianity with Augustine.

Boudicca's Revolt (61 AD): A massive uprising by the Iceni queen against Roman rule, after Romans flogged her publicly. Her forces destroyed Londinium, Verulamium, and Camulodunum, killing 70,000-80,000 people. She was defeated by Gaius Suetonius Paulinus.

Marshall's errors about ancient Britons: She called them "wild" cave-dwellers (actually skilled farmers and metalworkers in well-built houses); she said Druids built Stonehenge (actually built 2,500 years before Druids existed); she said Britain was named after Brutus of Troy (actually probably from the Celtic word Pritani, "painted ones").

Hadrian's Wall (122 AD): A 73-mile defensive wall across northern England. It didn't represent Roman triumph — it proved Rome couldn't conquer all Britain. It was a limit, not a monument.

♦ ♦ ♦

The hook: What comes after Rome?

410 AD: The Silence. Rome withdraws its legions from Britain. No dramatic farewell. No fanfare. The Britons are told, in effect: you're on your own now. Within a generation, the stone cities crumble, Latin fades, and something new emerges from the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms to the east.

Marshall calls this the "Dark Ages." Modern historians call this period the "Migration Period." The truth is more interesting — and much less dark. The Anglo-Saxons brought sophisticated culture: the Sutton Hoo burial treasure, sophisticated law codes, and ultimately Christianity.

II. Marshall's Saxon Story

H.E. Marshall tells the Anglo-Saxon settlement as a dramatic invasion and conquest. It is a thrilling tale of heroes and treachery — but complicated by both history and archaeology.

The Departure of Rome and the Arrival of Saxons

When the Romans left Britain, the Britons thought they could defend themselves against their enemies. But they were mistaken. Fierce Saxon warriors from across the sea, seeing that Britain was no longer protected by the mighty Roman legions, sailed across and began to settle the land. The Saxons were fierce and warlike, and the Britons, grown soft by centuries of Roman rule, could not stand against them. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter III (1905)

This captures something real — there was a transition from Romano-British to Anglo-Saxon culture. But Marshall frames it as an invasion of fierce warriors overthrowing weak Britons. The reality was more gradual and complex.

Hengist and Horsa: The Legendary Invasion

A great king of the Britons, named Vortigern, fearing the invasion of fearsome Picts from the north, invited Saxon warriors to come and help him defend his kingdom. Two great Saxon heroes, named Hengist and Horsa, came with their people. At first they fought for Vortigern against his enemies. But after a time, being pleased with the land, they turned upon the Britons and drove them westward. Thus the Saxons took the land of Britain, and Hengist became a great king. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter III

This story of Hengist and Horsa comes from Bede and earlier chroniclers. It tells of an invited force that became an occupying army — a betrayal. Marshall presents it as fact, but historians now see it as a legendary origin story, reflecting anxieties about invasion.

Marshall's Vision of the Dark Ages

Now came what is called the Dark Ages, for during these days learning and civilisation seemed to disappear from Britain. The fine cities that the Romans had built crumbled to ruin. The roads grew overgrown with weeds. The people went back to living in rude huts and forgot the arts and crafts that the Romans had taught them. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter III

What Marshall captures:

She is right that Roman cities were abandoned and that there was a real cultural shift. But her language — "Dark Ages," "rude huts," "civilisation seemed to disappear" — reflects Victorian assumptions that Roman=civilised and Anglo-Saxon=primitive. Modern archaeology proves this deeply wrong.

III. Anglo-Saxon Britain: The Reality

The transition from Roman to Anglo-Saxon Britain was not an invasion but a gradual transformation. And the "Dark Ages" were anything but dark.

From Rome to the Saxons: A Gradual Change

In the late 4th and early 5th centuries, as Rome withdrew, Anglo-Saxon settlement in Britain accelerated. But "invasion" is misleading. Anglo-Saxons had been settling in Britain during Roman times — as soldiers in the Roman army, as traders, as refugees fleeing continental upheavals.

The transition was complex and regional. Some areas saw Anglo-Saxon settlement as a gradual assimilation (intermarriage, trade, cultural mixing). Some areas saw faster displacement of Romano-British elites. The Britons didn't "disappear" — many remained and were absorbed into Anglo-Saxon society. Others fled westward to Wales, which remained a centre of post-Roman British culture.

The story of Hengist and Horsa as invaders is legendary, not historical. It reflects anxieties about invasion, but the reality was messier and slower.

The "Dark Ages" Are Not Dark

The term "Dark Ages" comes from the fact that we have fewer written records from post-Roman Britain than from Roman or later medieval periods. But archaeology — pottery, jewellery, buildings, burials — tells a different story.

Sutton Hoo: The Treasure Ship

In 1939, archaeologists discovered an Anglo-Saxon burial mound in Suffolk containing a ship filled with treasures: gold, silver, fine metalwork, Byzantine coins. This was the burial of a 7th-century East Anglian king. It proves the "Dark Ages" were rich, sophisticated, and connected to Europe.

Sophisticated Kingdoms

The Anglo-Saxons didn't create a single kingdom. Instead, they formed the Heptarchy: seven major kingdoms (Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia, Essex, Sussex, Kent, Wessex) each with their own laws, coinage, and culture.

Metalwork and Craft

Anglo-Saxon metalworkers created some of the finest art of the early medieval period. The style is distinctive and sophisticated: interlaced designs, geometric patterns, and animal motifs that show real technical skill.

Christianity Arrives

In 597 AD, a missionary named Augustine (later Saint Augustine of Canterbury) arrived from Rome and began converting the Anglo-Saxons to Christianity. Within a century, all the major kingdoms had adopted the faith.

The Anglo-Saxon Kings

The Anglo-Saxon period produced kings of considerable power and vision. We will meet several — Offa of Mercia, Alfred the Great, and others — in future lessons. They were not crude barbarians. They issued law codes, struck coins, made treaties, and fought strategic wars.

The Heptarchy: Seven Kingdoms

The Anglo-Saxon settlement created a fragmented political landscape. By around 600 AD, seven major kingdoms had emerged:

The Heptarchy

Northumbria (the north) — formed from Deira and Bernicia.
Mercia (the Midlands) — often the largest and most powerful.
East Anglia (East) — home to Sutton Hoo's kings.
Essex, Sussex, Kent (South and Southeast) — smaller kingdoms.
Wessex (Southwest) — which would eventually come to dominate.
Wales (west) — remained unconquered, a centre of post-Roman British culture and Christianity.

This fragmentation meant constant warfare over supremacy — a constant theme of Anglo-Saxon history.

Augustine and Christianity (597 AD)

In 597 AD, Pope Gregory I sent a mission of about 40 monks led by Augustine to convert the Anglo-Saxons. They landed in Kent and converted King Æthelberht. From there, Christianity spread slowly northward and westward.

This was not a simple conversion. The Anglo-Saxons were pagan, worshipping gods like Woden and Thunor. Christianity did not erase these beliefs overnight. Instead, it merged with them: pagan festivals were renamed as Christian holidays (Yule became Christmas, Beltane became St. Brigid's Day). The process took centuries. And notably, Christianity arrived from both Rome (in the south, via Augustine) and from Irish missionaries (in the north), leading to different practices and a major dispute about when Easter should be celebrated.

Literacy and Learning

Here's a crucial point Marshall missed: the Anglo-Saxons were not illiterate. They developed their own alphabet, called runes (from the Germanic tradition), and later adopted the Roman alphabet through Christianity. Monasteries became centres of learning. Monks copied manuscripts, preserved knowledge, and created the beautiful illuminated books we still admire today (like the Lindisfarne Gospels).

VI. Your Turn: Think Like a Historian

Choose 2-3 of these questions to explore.

Invasion or migration?

Was the Anglo-Saxon settlement an "invasion" or a "migration"? Does the word choice matter? What's the difference between the two?

The "Dark Ages" myth

Why did Victorians call this period the "Dark Ages"? What does Sutton Hoo tell us about this label? Can a period be both "dark" (lacking written records) and "bright" (artistically rich)?

What makes a civilisation?

Marshall thought Roman cities = civilisation, and Anglo-Saxon kingdoms = barbarism. But what really defines a civilisation? (Trade? Writing? Art? Law? Something else?)

Hengist and Horsa

The legend of Hengist and Horsa is probably not historical, but it was believed for centuries. Why? What anxieties does this story express about invasion and betrayal?

Christianity's impact

Augustine arrived in 597 AD. Within a century, all the major Anglo-Saxon kingdoms had adopted Christianity. Was this peaceful conversion or gradual conquest? What was lost and what was gained?

Where did the Britons go?

After Roman rule, what happened to the Britons in the eastern and central parts of the island? Did they disappear, get absorbed, or flee? Why does this matter?
