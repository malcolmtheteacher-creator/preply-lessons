Before we dive deeper into Epicurean philosophy, we need to understand our sources. Where does our knowledge of Epicurus come from? The answer is: a tiny fraction of what once existed.

The Great Loss

Epicurus's Writings

Ancient sources credit Epicurus with about 300 scrolls of writings.

What Was Lost

Epicurus wrote extensively on physics, ethics, epistemology, and the gods. His main work was called On Nature — a massive 37-book treatise. Almost all of it is gone.

  • On Nature (Περὶ φύσεως) — 37 books, only fragments survive
  • The Canon — his epistemology, lost
  • On Lives — ethical treatise, lost
  • On the End (Goal) — on the good life, lost
  • Countless letters — only three survive
⚠️ Why Was It Lost?

Several factors contributed:

  • The rise of Christianity made pagan philosophy suspect
  • Epicureanism was seen as particularly dangerous — atheistic, hedonistic
  • Monastic scribes chose what to copy; they didn't choose Epicurus
  • The sack of Rome, the fall of the Western Empire, general destruction
Your turn: Why might Epicureanism have been considered more dangerous to Christianity than, say, Stoicism or Platonism? Think about the core teachings.

Why Epicureanism was especially threatening:

  • Mortal soul: Epicurus denied the immortality of the soul — a core Christian doctrine
  • No divine providence: The gods don't intervene — no prayer, no miracles, no salvation
  • Pleasure as the good: Could be (mis)read as endorsing hedonism
  • "Death is nothing": Undercuts the fear of hell and promise of heaven
  • Self-sufficient happiness: No need for God to achieve the good life

Stoicism and Platonism could be more easily adapted — Plato's immortal soul, Stoic divine providence. Epicureanism was harder to baptise (though Gassendi would try).

What Survives

Despite the losses, we have enough to reconstruct Epicurus's philosophy. Our sources fall into four categories:

Direct Source

Three Letters

Preserved by Diogenes Laërtius (3rd century CE):

  • To Herodotus — physics summary
  • To Pythocles — meteorology
  • To Menoeceus — ethics summary
Direct Source

Principal Doctrines

40 key statements (Kuriai Doxai) — the core teachings in condensed form. Also preserved by Diogenes Laërtius.

Direct Source

Vatican Sayings

81 maxims discovered in a Vatican manuscript in 1888. Some overlap with Principal Doctrines; many are unique.

Fragments

Herculaneum Papyri

Carbonised scrolls from a villa buried by Vesuvius (79 CE). Contain works by Epicurus and his follower Philodemus. Still being deciphered.

Secondary Source

Diogenes Laërtius

Lives of the Eminent Philosophers — Book 10 is entirely devoted to Epicurus. Our most important secondary source.

Poetic Exposition

Lucretius

De Rerum Natura — a Latin poem presenting Epicurean physics and ethics. The most complete account we have. (Next tab!)

📜
The Herculaneum Miracle
In 1750, excavators discovered a villa near Pompeii containing a library of carbonised scrolls. Many were Epicurean texts. Modern technology (X-ray imaging, machine learning) is slowly making them readable. We're still discovering "new" Epicurean writings.
Your turn: Given that we have only fragments, how should we approach studying Epicurus? What challenges does incomplete evidence create?

Methodological challenges:

  • Selection bias: We have summaries, not full arguments. Nuances may be lost.
  • Hostile witnesses: Many quotes come from critics (Cicero, Christian writers) who may distort.
  • Translation layers: Greek → Latin → modern languages. Terms shift meaning.
  • Filling gaps: Scholars must infer what's missing — room for error.

What this means for us: We should approach with humility. The Epicurus we reconstruct is a best effort. Multiple interpretations exist. Being honest about uncertainty is itself philosophical.

Lucretius: Our Gateway

Titus Lucretius Carus (~99–55 BCE) was a Roman poet about whom we know almost nothing. What we have is his masterwork: De Rerum Natura ("On the Nature of Things") — a 7,400-line Latin poem presenting Epicurean philosophy.

Mother of Aeneas and his race, delight of men and gods, nurturing Venus... since without you nothing rises into the shining shores of light, nothing grows to be lovely or glad...

— De Rerum Natura, Book I (opening)

Why Lucretius Matters

  • Most complete account: Where Epicurus's writings are fragments, Lucretius gives us a connected whole
  • Physics explained: Atomism, the swerve, the mortality of the soul — all detailed
  • Poetic power: Makes difficult ideas memorable and moving
  • Historical bridge: Transmitted Epicureanism to the Renaissance (more in Lesson 22)
📖
The Swerve
This poem's rediscovery in 1417 is the subject of Stephen Greenblatt's book The Swerve. Poggio Bracciolini found the only surviving manuscript in a German monastery. That discovery rippled through the Renaissance and into the Enlightenment. We'll explore this in Module 7.

The Six Books

De Rerum Natura is divided into six books:

  1. Book I: Atoms and void — basic physics
  2. Book II: Atomic motion, the "swerve" (clinamen)
  3. Book III: The soul is mortal — against fear of death
  4. Book IV: Sensation, perception, sex and love
  5. Book V: Cosmology, origins of life and civilization
  6. Book VI: Natural phenomena (thunder, plague) — against superstition
Your turn: Why might someone choose to present philosophy as poetry? What advantages and disadvantages does this have?

Advantages of poetic philosophy:

  • Memorable — rhythm and imagery stick in the mind
  • Emotionally moving — philosophy becomes felt, not just understood
  • Wider audience — accessible to those who might not read treatises
  • Beauty as argument — if the worldview produces such beauty, perhaps it's true?

Disadvantages:

  • Precision sacrificed — poetic language can be ambiguous
  • Arguments compressed — detailed reasoning may be lost
  • Lucretius's views may differ from Epicurus's — we can't always tell
  • Aesthetic pleasure may distract from critical evaluation

Revolutionary features:

  • Women accepted as equals
  • Enslaved people welcomed as philosophical equals
  • Living community (commune), not just a school
  • Simple living — modest food, no pursuit of wealth
  • Withdrawn from political life
From Lesson 2: Tetrapharmakos
Without looking back: What is the argument for "death is nothing to us"? Reconstruct the logic.

The argument:

  1. Good and bad require sensation (experience)
  2. Death is the complete end of sensation
  3. Therefore, death cannot be bad for the one who dies
  4. When we exist, death doesn't; when death comes, we don't
  5. Therefore, we never "experience" death
