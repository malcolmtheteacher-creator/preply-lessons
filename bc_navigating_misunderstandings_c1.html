When words say one thing but mean another

The same phrase can mean very different things depending on tone, context, and the relationship between the speakers. Look at these three sentences:

Sentence 1
"I think that's interesting."
Could be genuine — or potentially dismissive. The word "think" adds uncertainty. Tone is everything here: delivered flatly or slowly, it can signal polite disinterest.
Sentence 2
"That's very interesting."
Could be enthusiastic agreement — or heavy sarcasm. The word "very" can be emphasised positively or with irony. Without context, it's ambiguous.
Sentence 3
"That's quite interesting."
"Quite" in British English often softens or even downgrades a statement — "quite interesting" can imply mild doubt or reserved approval, rather than strong enthusiasm.

From Vague to Clear

Vague language creates misunderstandings. Look at each vague statement and the clearer alternative. What makes the difference?

Vague
"That could be improved."
Clearer: "We need to refine our strategy to be more specific about our target audience and the marketing channels we're using."
Vague
"It's a bit confusing."
Clearer: "I'm not sure I understand the timeline for the project phases. Could you walk me through each step in order?"
Vague
"Let's touch base later."
Clearer: "Can we schedule a 30-minute call tomorrow at 2pm to go through the project progress and any blockers?"

Passive-aggressive statements

Some statements are vague in a different way — they communicate displeasure indirectly. How would you respond to this without creating conflict?

Statement
"I'm not sure that's the best approach, but whatever you think is fine."
You might invite specificity: "It sounds like you have some reservations — I'd really value your honest thoughts. What concerns you about this approach?" This opens the door without being confrontational.

What you've explored today

Key ideas from this lesson:

Responding assertively and curiously — rather than defensively — is usually the most effective approach.
