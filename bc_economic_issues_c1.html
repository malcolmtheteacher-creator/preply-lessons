Six Key Determinants of Economic Growth

Economists look at these six factors to understand whether a city or country is likely to grow economically. Let's explore each one and think about how your city performs.

1. Legal and Political Framework

What it means: Are property rights protected? Is the government stable? Can contracts be enforced fairly?

A strong legal system means businesses can invest with confidence. If laws are unpredictable or corrupt, companies won't risk their money.

How would you rate your country's legal and political stability? Why?

2. Economic Stability

What it means: Is inflation controlled? Is the currency stable? Do prices stay roughly the same?

When prices jump wildly or money loses value fast, people and businesses panic. They can't plan. Economic stability gives confidence.

Has inflation affected you personally? How does it feel to live with economic uncertainty?

3. Ease of Doing Business

What it means: How long does it take to register a company? Can you get a permit quickly? Are there unnecessary fees?

If starting a business takes 6 months and 15 forms, talented entrepreneurs look elsewhere. Simplicity attracts innovation.

Do you know anyone who started a business? What were the biggest obstacles?

4. Financial and Information Flow

What it means: Can businesses get loans? Do they have access to market information? Is there transparency?

If banks won't lend or if information is hidden, good ideas never get funded. Healthy financial systems pump money where it's needed.

Is banking accessible where you live? Do small businesses struggle to get financing?

5. Convenience as a Business Centre

What it means: Are transportation and logistics efficient? Is there reliable internet? Good infrastructure?

Companies need to move goods, meet clients, communicate globally. Poor infrastructure makes everything slower and more expensive.

How good is your city's infrastructure for business? Roads, ports, airports, internet?

6. Liveability

What it means: Is the city safe? Is there good healthcare and education? Is pollution low? Is housing affordable?

Top talent—the people who build successful companies—choose places where they want to live. They need safety, good schools for their kids, clean air.

Would a top entrepreneur or scientist want to live in your city? Why or why not?

Economic Vocabulary

Click each card to flip between the term and its definition. Learn these ten essential words for discussing economics.

Bailout Package Government or financial help given to a failing company or bank to prevent collapse
Recession A period when the economy shrinks, businesses close, unemployment rises, and people feel poorer
Skills Shortage When there are more jobs than qualified workers, making it hard for companies to find staff
Housing Market The system of buying, selling, and renting homes; prices depend on supply, demand, and the economy
GDP Gross Domestic Product—total value of all goods and services produced in a country in one year
Service Sector Jobs and businesses that provide services (banking, healthcare, tourism, education) rather than making physical goods
Deregulation Removing government rules to let businesses operate more freely—seen by some as innovation, by others as risky
Quality of Life How well people live: health, education, safety, environment, leisure—not just money
Privatisation Selling government-owned services (railways, hospitals) to private companies; supposed to increase efficiency
Standard of Living Average level of wealth, comfort, and material goods available to people in a society

The Relocation Challenge

Your international clothing company is expanding and needs to relocate its headquarters. You've narrowed it down to three cities: Seoul, Mexico City, and London.

Your task: Evaluate each city using the economic factors we discussed, then make your final decision.

Your Decision

Now it's your turn. Based on the six economic factors and the additional considerations (cost, talent, market access, quality of life), which city should your clothing company relocate to? Write your argument.

Structure your response:

  • Which city you choose and why (in 2–3 sentences)
  • The strongest advantage of your chosen city
  • Why you rejected the other two cities
  • Any risks or challenges you'd need to manage
Great work! You've made a well-reasoned business decision. Remember to explain your thinking clearly when presenting to the company board.

Think Deeper

Economic decisions aren't just about numbers. They involve ethics, fairness, and real people's lives. Let's explore the bigger picture.

What advice would you give to your city's mayor?

If you could influence economic policy where you live, what would you change? What's the biggest barrier to economic growth in your city?

Which industries would you like to see grow in your city?

What industries would create good jobs and add value? Think beyond just money—think about meaning, sustainability, and community.

Are there any industries you'd like to disappear?

Pollution? Exploitation? Low wages? What's damaging your city or country, and why should it go?

Which city would you prefer to live in: Seoul, Mexico City, or London?

Remember, choosing a city for business is different from choosing it for yourself. What matters more to you personally?

Local vs. Global: Which matters more?

Should your country prioritize its local economy or compete globally? Can you do both? What are the trade-offs?

Reflection

Economic discussions quickly become personal. They touch on fairness, your future, your family's security. That's not a bug—it's a feature. The best economic decisions balance numbers with humanity.
