What the work questions really ask

After the general questions, the interview turns professional: How do you avoid errors? What happens in an emergency? How does bad weather affect flights? These are graded on accuracy and clarity — can you explain your professional world so that any aviation colleague, from any country, follows you exactly?

Every work question is one of four types

The procedure question
“What are the steps…?” “What would you do if…?” — wants a sequence: first, then, meanwhile, finally.
The cause-and-effect question
“How does X affect Y?” — wants chains: leads to, results in, which means that, as a consequence.
The comparison question
“What is the difference between…?” — wants both sides named, then contrasted: whereas, while, on the other hand.
The opinion question
“Do you think…? Are some flights harder…?” — wants a position plus reasons: in my experience, I'd say… mainly because…
Name the type before you speak. Half a second of “this is a procedure question” gives you the connectors, the tense, and the shape of the whole minute. Answering a procedure question with an opinion — or an opinion question with a memorised procedure — is how good English earns a mediocre mark.
Warm up — spot the type, then answer (90 seconds)

Question: “What factors do you consider to ensure the safety of flights?” Say which type it is, then answer it in that shape — a list of factors, each with one sentence of why it matters.

The language of how — procedures that sound professional

Work answers live on two grammatical engines: sequence words that keep a procedure in order, and the passive that describes how things are done when the doer is obvious or unimportant — reports are issued, the runway is inspected, traffic is suspended.
EngineSounds likeWhy examiners reward it
Sequence“First we…, then…, meanwhile…, once that's confirmed…, finally…”Order is clarity — a sequenced answer is automatically easy to follow.
Passive“A special report is issued… the alert is passed to… the runway is closed until it has been inspected.”It is how professionals describe systems — the action matters, not the actor.
Condition“If the reading falls below…, we… · Unless it improves, the airport will…”Real procedures are full of if — conditional control is procedure control.

Question bank A — safety, errors & communication

The first family of work questions: how professionals stop small mistakes becoming big ones. Answer each for the full minute; the phrase boxes carry the load-bearing vocabulary.
1. What methods are used in aviation to avoid errors?
01:00
Load-bearing words: standard phraseology (everyone uses the same words) · readback (instructions are repeated back and checked) · checklists · cross-checking (two people, one task) · “If anything is unclear, we ask — say again, confirm — instead of guessing.”
2. How do pilots and controllers communicate when they don't speak the same first language?
01:00
Load-bearing ideas: English as the shared language of the sky · standard words with fixed meanings (roger, wilco, standby) · the phonetic alphabet so letters can't be confused · numbers spoken digit by digit · “and when plain English is needed, it must be simple and clear — which is exactly what this test measures.”
3. What is the relationship usually like between controllers and pilots?
01:00
Load-bearing ideas: professional trust between strangers · both sides working from the same picture · cooperation, not hierarchy — “each depends on the other's information” · and under pressure, courtesy gets shorter but respect stays.
4. What would you do if communication with the other party suddenly failed?
01:00
A sequence shape: “First I'd check my own equipment — the fault may be on my side. Then I'd try the alternative frequency, or another station as a relay. Meanwhile, both sides follow the procedures for communication failure, so behaviour stays predictable. The principle is: when you can't talk, you follow the plan both sides already know.”
Judge this answer — “How do professionals avoid misunderstandings?”: “We are always very careful and we concentrate a lot.” What's missing?

Question bank B — weather, emergencies & change

The second family: when things go wrong, and how the job is changing. This is where your daily expertise becomes your best English — you know this material better than the examiner does.
1. How do adverse weather conditions affect flights?
01:00
Chain language: “Low visibility leads to greater spacing between aircraft, which means fewer landings per hour, and as a consequence delays and diversions. Strong crosswinds can force go-arounds; icing results in de-icing queues; thunderstorms can shut down operations entirely.” One hazard, one chain — three hazards fill the minute.
2. In which circumstances is an emergency declared, and what happens next?
01:00
Load-bearing ideas: distress vs urgency — immediate danger versus a serious problem that isn't yet dangerous · “the aircraft gets priority; other traffic is moved out of the way; the emergency services are alerted; and everyone's job becomes making that one aircraft's situation simpler.”
3. How can technology help prevent collisions — and how has it changed your own work?
01:00
Change language (present perfect country): “Technology has taken over much of the routine — alerts, automatic warnings, better sensors — but it hasn't replaced judgement. When I started, we used to…, whereas now… The biggest change has been…”
4. Are some flights or situations more difficult to manage than others? Why?
01:00
Opinion shape: position first — “In my experience, yes — by far the hardest are…” — then two reasons, then one example, then a concession: “Having said that, a routine day can turn difficult in minutes, which is why…” A concession sentence is a Level 5 fingerprint.
One trap in this family: the question asks about aviation in general, and you answer only about your own desk — or the opposite. Do both, explicitly: “In general, … — and in my own work, that means…” That one pivot sentence shows range and keeps you exactly on the question.
