Reframing Conflict Situations

"Our key to transforming anything lies in our ability to reframe it." — Maryanne Williamson

Your Learning Goals

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:

Question assumptions and beliefs to frame conflict objectively

Learn to separate facts from interpretations when conflict arises.

Use the Ladder of Inference to question your reasoning

Understand how we move from observable facts to conclusions, and where errors can happen.

Use positive language techniques to keep conversations less adversarial

Express concerns without blame, making dialogue more productive.

Skills Focus 1: The Ladder of Inference

The Ladder of Inference shows how we build beliefs from facts. At each step, we risk making incorrect assumptions. Understanding this helps us reframe conflict.

The Ladder (from bottom to top)

Step 1: Observable Data

The facts. What actually happened. What you can see, hear, or measure.

Step 2: Selected Data

You notice certain facts and ignore others. What you choose to focus on.

Step 3: Interpret Data

You add meaning to the facts based on your experience and beliefs.

Step 4: Make Assumptions

You assume you understand the other person's motives or beliefs.

Step 5: Form Conclusions

You conclude what the situation means based on your assumptions.

Step 6: Adopt Beliefs

The conclusion becomes a belief you hold as true.

Step 7: Take Action

You act based on your beliefs, often without checking facts.

Critical questions to ask at each step:

  • Which information is factual, and which is interpreted?
  • What other reasons could explain this behaviour or situation?
  • What information am I missing?
  • What other actions could the other person have chosen?

Skills Focus 2: Reframing Conflict Language

Compare adversarial language (accusatory and emotional) with objective language (factual and outcome-focused). Reframing helps keep dialogue open.

Example 1: Micromanagement Concern

Adversarial stance (too harsh):

"You are always micromanaging me. Don't you have anything better to do?"

Objective, reframed stance:

"I would like to discuss differences in our approach to managing work tasks and the impact this has on how we work together. I realise that both of us seem to have a different understanding of task management. I want to explain my concerns about how this affects my work and understand your perspective."

Example 2: Professional Experience Not Valued

Adversarial stance (too harsh):

"I was really irritated about the way you discounted my professional experience at the client meeting."

Objective, reframed stance:

"I wanted to talk to you about yesterday's client meeting. I was surprised by what you said about the relevance of my professional experience to the project. I acknowledge I may not have all the product knowledge and wanted to discuss this with you."

Example 3: Ideas Not Being Implemented

Adversarial stance (too harsh):

"You accept everyone's ideas except mine. I can propose 10 great initiatives, but you'll still implement a substandard one suggested by someone else. It's not about my ideas, is it? You just think I'm too old!"

Objective, reframed stance:

"I wanted to talk to you about how we select ideas for implementation within the team. I'm concerned that my ideas are unwelcome and would like to share my apprehensions about this process. Could you help me understand your perspective on this?"

Key differences:

  • Adversarial: Uses "you" accusations, assumes motives, emotional language
  • Objective: Uses "I" statements, focuses on specific behaviour, invites dialogue

Practice Task: Reframing a Conflict

Here are two real conflict scenarios. For each one, discuss how to reframe the adversarial language into objective, outcome-focused language.

Scenario A: Team Embarrassment (Front-Line vs. Office)

Adversarial Version

"You can't go around embarrassing my team in front of customers! It destroys their image and demeans their work. Don't you know how hard everyone on my team works? You sit in a fancy office all day with no idea about what happens on the front line!"

Objective reframe: "I wanted to talk about a situation at the customer meeting. I felt that feedback about our team was delivered in front of the client, and I'm concerned about how that affected team morale and confidence. I'd like to understand your perspective on how we can address performance issues more privately, and also explain some of the constraints we face in the field."

Scenario B: Regulatory Changes (Vendor vs. Buyer)

Adversarial Version

"I don't care about changes in laws and regulations. You made a commitment in the vendor agreement, and you'd better stick to it. I want suppliers to take ownership and solve problems. If there are regulatory changes, deal with them yourself. Don't come begging to me for solutions."

Objective reframe: "I want to discuss how we handle the impact of recent regulatory changes on our vendor agreement. I understand the commitment we made, and I also recognise that new regulations present challenges for you. I'd like to explore how we can adapt the agreement to address these changes while maintaining our partnership and ensuring compliance for both parties."

Reflect on your reframing:

  • What techniques did you use to make the text less adversarial?
  • How objective do you think your version is?
  • What do you think the effect of your version would be?
