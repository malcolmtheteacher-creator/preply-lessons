The Ladder of Inference

The "Ladder of Inference" is a tool for understanding how we form opinions from facts. We often jump to conclusions, skipping important steps. By recognizing these steps, we can understand where misunderstandings happen.

The Six Rungs of the Ladder (Bottom to Top)

1. Facts and Data — What actually happened. Observable reality.

2. Selected Data — We notice only certain facts (we can"t remember everything).

3. Meanings — We add our own interpretation to what we notice.

4. Assumptions — Based on those meanings, we make assumptions about reasons.

5. Conclusions — We jump to conclusions about why something happened.

6. Beliefs and Actions — These conclusions become fixed beliefs, which drive our future actions.

A Real Example

Fact: Your colleague did not respond to your email for two days.

Meaning you add: "She"s avoiding me."

Assumption: "She must be angry about the project."

Conclusion: "She doesn"t respect my work."

Belief + Action: You become defensive and send a sharp follow-up email.

But the truth: She was at a conference without email access. Your interpretation was wrong.

Breaking the Ladder: Ask Better Questions

To question your assumptions, ask:

  • "What are the facts exactly? What did I actually see or hear?"
  • "What other meanings could this have?"
  • "What other reasons could explain this behaviour?"
  • "What other information would I need to understand this better?"
  • "What other possibilities could there be?"

Reframing Conflict Language

When in conflict, we often use accusatory, emotional language (adversarial stance). By reframing with more objective language, we keep conversations constructive and solution-focused.

Adversarial vs. Objective Language

Conflict Scenario A:

❌ Adversarial: "You can"t go around embarrassing my team in front of customers! It destroys their image and demeans their work. Don"t you know how hard everyone on my team works? You sit in a fancy office all day with no idea about what happens on the front line!"

✓ Objective: "I"ve noticed some concerns about how feedback is being delivered in front of clients. Could we discuss how to address performance issues in a way that maintains team morale and client confidence? I"d like to understand your approach better and share what works well on the front line."

Conflict Scenario B:

❌ Adversarial: "I don"t care about changes in laws and regulations. You made a commitment in the vendor agreement, and you"d better stick to it. I want suppliers to take ownership and solve problems. If there are regulatory changes, deal with them yourself. Don"t come begging to me for solutions."

✓ Objective: "I understand there have been changes in regulations that affect the agreement. I"d like to discuss how we can adapt together. What"s changed in your operating environment, and how might we work toward solutions that honour our original partnership while addressing new compliance requirements?"

Key Techniques for Reframing

1. Replace blame with curiosity:

❌ "You never listen to me."

✓ "I"d like to understand your perspective on this. What concerns do you have?"

2. Focus on the issue, not the person:

❌ "You"re being unreasonable."

✓ "I think we have different views on this issue. Can we explore what"s causing the difference?"

3. Use outcome-oriented language:

❌ "You always mess this up."

✓ "We need to find a way forward that works for both of us. What would success look like?"

4. Acknowledge the other person"s experience:

❌ "That"s not a real problem."

✓ "I hear that this is important to you. Help me understand why this matters."

Practice: Reframe These Statements

Rewrite using the ladder and reframing techniques:

1. "You completely ignored my email and didn"t care about my concerns."

"I didn"t hear back from you about my concerns. I"d like to understand what happened and find a time to discuss this with you."

2. "You"re always late and it shows you don"t respect my time."

"I"ve noticed that we"ve had some timing challenges with our meetings. What"s happening on your end that makes punctuality difficult? Let"s find a solution together."

Speaking Task: Reframe a Conflict

You will be given an adversarial statement about a conflict. Your task is to reframe it into objective, outcome-focused language while discussing it out loud.

Scenario to Reframe

Your manager says to a colleague: "You"ve been making excuses all quarter. You clearly don"t care about meeting your targets. The team can"t rely on you, and your attitude is bringing down morale. Either you step up or we need to have a serious conversation about your future here."

10:00

Your Task

In 5-7 minutes, do the following:

  • 1. Analyze: What"s the underlying issue? (Look at the facts vs. the judgments.)
  • 2. Reframe: Rephrase the same concerns in objective, non-accusatory language.
  • 3. Discuss: Explain why your reframed version would lead to a more productive conversation.
  • 4. Suggest: What would you add to make this a collaborative problem-solving conversation?

Useful Phrases for Your Response

"The underlying issue seems to be…"

"A more objective way to express this would be…"

"Instead of blaming, the manager could…"

"This reframing would help because…"

"To move toward a solution, the conversation could include…"

Next Steps

To develop your conflict resolution skills further:

  • Practice the Ladder of Inference in your daily life — notice when you jump to conclusions
  • Read or watch examples of conflict mediation to see reframing in action
  • Role-play more complex conflict scenarios with feedback
  • Keep a journal of conflicts you observe and how they might be reframed
  • Learn about other conflict resolution models (active listening, non-violent communication, etc.)
