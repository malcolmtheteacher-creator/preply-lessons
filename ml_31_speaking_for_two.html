Speaking for Two

Your mother has lived here six years and her English still hides when officials appear. Today it's the housing office: her name, her flat, her problem — your English. The hardest part isn't the vocabulary. It's doing the talking without taking the conversation away from her.

The Scene 🎬

Millions of people do this every week and nobody teaches it: being the English for someone you love.

If you're the strongest English speaker in an immigrant family, you know this job. School letters, doctor's appointments, phone calls to the bank — somewhere along the way you became the family's interpreter, unpaid and untrained, often since you were twelve.

Today it's your mother and the housing office. The heating in her flat has been broken for three weeks, her emails get form replies, and she's asked you to come and talk to them. She's not helpless — in her own language she's the sharpest person you know, and that's exactly what gets lost when other people talk past her to you, about her, as if she were her own luggage.

So the job has two halves, and they pull against each other. Carry the meaning accurately in both directions — including the difficult questions and the answers she'd rather soften. And keep her the owner of her own conversation: spoken to, deciding, present. The phrases in this lesson manage the officials, the pace, and the traffic — but the deepest one is six words long: talk to her, not to me.

The Conversation 💬

The housing office, desk 2. Your mother sits beside you, handbag on her lap, letters in a folder — organised, as ever, in everything but English.

Mr OseiRight — so you're here about the heating issue. Can you tell me when the problem started?
YouBefore we start — I'm here with my mother. It's her flat and her appointment; I'll be doing the English for both of us. So please — talk to her, not to me. I'll keep up.
Mr Osei…Of course. My apologies. *turning* When did the heating stop working?
You*to her, then back* She says three weeks ago last Tuesday — and she wrote the date down, here. She reported it the same day through the portal, and twice since. These are the reference numbers.
Mr OseiThree reports… yes, I see them. They were logged as "non-urgent". Standard for heating between May and September.
YouGive me a second to tell her what you said. *a quiet exchange — her answer comes fast and sharp* She's asking: is it also standard for a woman of sixty-eight with arthritis to wash in cold water for three weeks in May?
Mr Osei…When you put it like that. Look, the category is the issue — if there's a medical condition, it should have been logged as vulnerable-resident, which is a different queue entirely.
YouCould you say that more simply? I have to translate it for her, and "vulnerable-resident categorisation" won't survive the trip.
Mr OseiFair enough. Tell her: because of her health, her case should be in the fast queue. It's in the slow one. I can move it today.
You*translating; her face changes; she speaks quickly* She understands more than she speaks, by the way — she caught "fast queue" before I translated it. She's asking: how fast is the fast queue?
Mr Osei*smiling, to her now* Five working days, with a phone call the day before the engineer comes. …She's nodding before you translate again. I see what you mean about understanding.
YouShe does that. One correction, though — I said it wrong just now: it's not arthritis in her hands, it's her hips. That's not quite what she means about the washing — let me try again. The problem isn't taps, it's that cold water and her hips make the bath dangerous. She's afraid of falling.
Mr OseiThat changes things again, actually — a falls risk gets an emergency marker, not just fast-queue. I'll be honest, most people don't tell us the falling part. They're embarrassed.
You*to her — a pause — she looks at her hands, then nods* She says: write it down. Embarrassed is cheaper than broken. …That's word for word, by the way. She's quotable in two languages.
Mr Osei*laughing, writing* Emergency marker, falls risk, engineer within forty-eight hours, call beforehand. Anything else?
YouTwo things. Don't decide anything yet on the older complaint — the one about the windows — we need a minute together first; that's her decision and she wants to think. And could we get today's outcome in writing before we go? We'll go through it at home this evening, at her speed, in her kitchen.
Mr OseiPrinted and stamped. *standing, to her, carefully and directly* Thank you for coming in — and I'm sorry it took three weeks and a translator to be heard properly.
You*after her reply* She says: you listened today, that's what counts. And from me — thank you for being patient with the two of us. The two-language version of a meeting takes twice as long and gets it right once.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for interpreting for someone you love — offices, clinics, banks, parents' evenings from the other side. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. You're doing three jobs at once — translator, advocate, and guardian of her dignity — and the phrases keep them from tangling. The rule underneath all ten: you are the bridge, not the destination. Traffic crosses a bridge; it doesn't stop there.

🎬 Setting the ground rules

Phrase 1

The job: declaring the arrangement before business starts. Complete it:

"I'm here with my mother — I'll be doing the ___ for both of us."

"I'm here with my mother — I'll be doing the English for both of us."
Ten seconds at the start that shape the whole meeting: who's who, whose appointment it is, and what your role is. Without it, officials guess — and they always guess that the fluent one is in charge. "Doing the English" is a lovely plain way to name the job: not "translating" (formal), not "helping" (which makes her sound helpless) — just handling one specific part, the way someone else might carry the folder.

Take it anywhere: "I'm here with ___ — I'll be doing the English for both of us." Every counter, every clinic, first thing.
Phrase 2

The job: the six words that protect her dignity all meeting long. Complete it:

"Talk to ___, not to ___ — I'll keep up."

"Talk to her, not to me — I'll keep up."
The heart of the lesson. Officials drift towards whoever answers fastest — not from cruelty, from efficiency — and within a minute the person the meeting is ABOUT has become furniture in it. This sentence fixes the eyes and the grammar of the whole room: questions in the second person, to her face. "I'll keep up" handles their worry (won't this be slower?) — that's your problem, and you're good at it. Expect to repeat it, kindly, twice.

Take it anywhere: "Talk to her, not to me." Doctors, teachers, waiters — anyone talking past a person you're helping. It works for wheelchairs and hearing aids as well as languages.
Phrase 3

The job: claiming the pause translation needs. Complete it:

"Give me a ___ to tell her what you said."

"Give me a second to tell her what you said."
Two-language meetings have a rhythm problem: the official keeps talking while you're still carrying the last sentence across, and suddenly she's three answers behind her own appointment. This phrase claims the space — openly, unapologetically — and trains the room to work in turns. Say it as often as you need. The pause isn't a delay in the meeting; for her, it IS the meeting.

Take it anywhere: "Give me a second to tell her what you said." The traffic light of every interpreted conversation.

🌉 Carrying meaning faithfully

Phrase 4

The job: delivering her words as HERS. Complete it:

"She's ___: is it also standard for a woman of sixty-eight to wash in cold water?"

"She's asking: is it also standard for a woman of sixty-eight to wash in cold water?"
The frame matters as much as the content. "She's asking:" — then her actual question, sharpness intact — keeps her the author of her own best lines. The lazy alternative ("my mum wants to know about the water") summarises her into the third person and sands off her edge. She was sharp in her language; the officer deserves to meet that sharpness, and she deserves to be its owner. Carry the sting. It's hers.

Take it anywhere: "She's asking: ___" / "He says: ___" — first person preserved, edge intact, credit where it belongs.
Phrase 5

The job: catching and fixing your own translation error. Put the pieces in order:

let me try again.That's not quite what she means —
"That's not quite what she means — let me try again."
The bravest phrase in the amateur interpreter's kit, because it means admitting your last sentence was wrong. But look what the correction was worth here: hands became hips, discomfort became a falls risk, and the case jumped two priority levels. Meaning that arrives ninety percent right can be a hundred percent wrong in effect. Correcting yourself isn't failure — it's the job done properly, and officials trust interpreters who do it more, not less.

Take it anywhere: "That's not quite what she means — let me try again." Also, unchanged, for your own words: "that's not quite what I mean."
Phrase 6

The job: making the official translatable. Complete it:

"Could you say that more ___? I have to ___ it for her."

"Could you say that more simply? I have to translate it for her."
Officials speak in categories — "vulnerable-resident categorisation" — and jargon that barely survives ONE language dies completely in transit through two. This request isn't a confession of weak English; it's professional: you're telling him the sentence has a journey ahead and needs packing properly. Watch the effect — he immediately produced the human version ("fast queue, slow queue"), which was clearer for everyone, including him.

Take it anywhere: "Could you say that more simply? I have to pass it on." Also glorious in meetings with no second language at all.

🛡️ Guarding the person and the decisions

Phrase 7

The job: the sentence that changes how the room sees her. Complete it:

"She ___ more than she ___."

"She understands more than she speaks."
True of almost every immigrant parent on earth, and the room needs telling — because people quietly equate halting speech with halting mind, and start performing their sentences AT a person who is following every word. This phrase corrects the room's maths: she caught "fast queue" before the translation landed. Once the officer knew that, he started talking TO her — and the meeting became hers again. Say it early; it does its best work before the misjudgement settles.

Take it anywhere: "She understands more than she speaks." For parents, patients, and anyone whose comprehension outruns their production — which is every language learner alive.
Phrase 8

The job: stopping a decision being made over her head. Complete it:

"Don't decide anything yet — we need a ___ together first."

"Don't decide anything yet — we need a minute together first."
Desks generate momentum — while you're all here, shall we just settle the windows issue too? — and momentum plus a language gap is how people get signed up to things they'd never have chosen at their own speed. This phrase stops the conveyor. The decision isn't refused; it's relocated — to her kitchen, her evening, her language, with the papers in front of her. Anything that must be decided THIS second at a desk is almost always better decided tomorrow.

Take it anywhere: "Don't decide anything yet — we need a minute." Salesmen, landlords, and every form with a signature line.
Phrase 9

The job: taking the meeting home on paper. Complete it:

"Could we get that in ___, to go through at home?"

"Could we get that in writing, to go through at home?"
A spoken outcome exists only in memory — yours, doubly loaded from working in two languages at once. The written version does two jobs: it's the evidence if the forty-eight hours becomes a fortnight, and — the quieter, bigger one — it lets her re-take the whole meeting at her own speed, at her kitchen table, with a dictionary and no queue behind her. That evening read-through is where she becomes fully the owner of her own case again.

Take it anywhere: "Could we get that in writing, to go through at home?" Every appointment where the language moved faster than one of you.
Phrase 10

The job: closing for both of you. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Thank you for being patient with the two of us."
"The two of us" is the phrase doing the work: it closes the meeting as a unit — not a helper and a helped, but two people who did this together. And the thanks is deserved where it's given: a two-language meeting genuinely asks more of the official, and the ones who slow down and speak to her directly should hear that it landed. You'll be back at this desk someday. Leave it warmer than you found it.

Take it anywhere: "Thanks for being patient with the two of us." The close for every appointment you attend as somebody's English.

⚡ The twist

Round 1 again — and mid-meeting the officer says: "There's actually a hardship payment she may qualify for, backdated — but I have to ask some questions about her finances. Savings, family support, everything." Your mother listens to your translation, then says quietly, in your shared language: "Don't tell him about the money your brother sends. Say there's nothing."

She's asking you to mis-translate — to lie in her name, in a language she can't check. It might even be harmless; it might not be. And you're not just her child in this room — you're the bridge both sides are trusting. What do you say — to her, in your language, and then to the officer, in English?

The Echo 🕰️

The Ptolemies ruled Egypt for three hundred years — Greek kings who never once learned the language of the country they governed. Every decree, every audience, every judgement travelled through interpreters. For fifteen generations, the people who actually ruled Egypt, in one practical sense, were the ones carrying the words across.

Then came Cleopatra. Ancient sources record it with astonishment: she was the first of her whole dynasty who spoke Egyptian — one of nine languages she commanded, receiving ambassadors from Ethiopia to Parthia without an interpreter in the room. Her power wasn't only beauty or politics; it was that nobody could stand between her and what was actually being said. She met every conversation as its owner.

Most of the world can't be Cleopatra — most of the world is her subjects, depending on somebody to carry the words faithfully. Which is why the person doing your family's English holds a quiet kind of power, every appointment: the power to carry meaning whole, edge intact, decisions returned to their owner — or not. Carry it like it's a kingdom. For the person beside you, it is.
