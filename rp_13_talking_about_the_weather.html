Talking About the Weather

It starts with a grey sky and the one morning you forgot your coat. Where it goes from there is entirely up to you.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

You moved into the ground-floor flat about six weeks ago, and so far your whole social life on this street is a collection of half-nods in the hallway. You know faces, not names. It's a friendly enough place — you can feel that — but nobody has quite opened the door yet, and neither have you.

This morning the sky can't make up its mind. It was golden an hour ago; now it's the colour of wet slate, and you've come out without a coat. Standing at the bus shelter is one of your neighbours — the one who's always coming back from somewhere with muddy hands and a wheelbarrow. You've got a good three minutes until the bus. Say nothing and it's three minutes of polite silence. Or say something — and the weather, that oldest, safest icebreaker in the world, might just open into something real.

Because that's the secret of small talk: nobody actually cares whether it holds off till lunch. The weather is just the door. What matters is what you do once it's open.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Where you're from, is chatting about the weather with a stranger normal, odd, or faintly suspicious? If people there DON'T open with the weather, what do they use instead?
  2. Think of a friend you now couldn't imagine life without. Can you remember the very first, utterly forgettable thing one of you said to the other? How much of friendship, honestly, starts by accident?

Who's who

You
The New Neighbour 📦

Six weeks in, still living out of boxes, quietly hoping this place will start to feel like home. Warm, a little shy — but ready to say the first word if you can find it.

The other voice
The Old Hand 🌿

Twenty years on this street, runs the communal garden round the back, knows everyone's business and most of their names. Unhurried, dry, and quietly working out whether you're the sort worth knowing.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Watch the weather do its job and then quietly bow out, leaving two neighbours actually talking.

NeighbourOoh, that sky's turned, hasn't it? It was glorious not an hour ago.
YouDoesn't look like it's going to hold, does it? And naturally it's the one morning I've come out without a coat.
NeighbourHa — typical, isn't it. Mind you, we can't really complain after the summer we've had. I'd half forgotten what rain looked like.
YouTrue enough. Honestly, I'll take a grey morning over that heat any day — I was melting by June. Are you waiting for the number 30 as well?
NeighbourThe 46, me — I only get the 30 if it's really coming down. Here, you've just moved into the ground-floor flat, haven't you? Number 4?
YouNumber 3, just across the hall. We've been in about six weeks — still surrounded by boxes, if I'm honest.
NeighbourSix weeks! Well, that explains why I keep half-recognising you and can't place it. Whereabouts were you before, if you don't mind me asking?
YouOh, only the other side of town — nothing exotic. But it feels like a different world over here, to be honest. Quieter. People actually say good morning.
NeighbourThat's this street for you — bit of a village, really. I've been here going on twenty years. I look after the little garden round the back, for my sins.
YouThat gorgeous garden's yours? Funnily enough, I was admiring it from my window only yesterday, wondering who kept it looking like that. It must be a lot of work.
NeighbourIt keeps me out of trouble, put it that way. You'd be welcome to a corner of it, you know — half the beds go begging by August. Do you grow anything?
YouI've killed every plant I've ever owned, but I'd genuinely love to learn. Small world — I've been meaning to find some way of actually meeting people round here, and here you are at a bus stop.
NeighbourWell, there you are, then. Ah — that's my bus grumbling up the hill. Look, you'll have to pop round for a cup of tea one of these days. Number 11, blue door, you can't miss it.
YouI'd like that very much. Right — I'd better let you make a dash for it before the heavens open. Lovely to finally put a name to a face!
NeighbourAnd you, love. Now go and buy yourself a coat — this one's not going anywhere. See you in the garden.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The chat opens with nothing but the sky. Trace how it turns into a real conversation — what's the first true thing the two neighbours discover about each other, and what small offer, right at the end, opens the door for next time?

2. Twice, one speaker refuses to only moan about the weather — first "we can't really complain after the summer we've had", later "I'll take a grey morning over that heat". What does turning a grumble positive actually do for the conversation?

3. Neither person ever says "let's be friends", yet by the end both plainly want to meet again. Which lines do the work of "I'd like to see you again" without ever saying it out loud?

1. The weather is just the runway. The first real discovery is that they're neighbours in the same building — the new one in number 3, the old hand in number 11 — surfaced by "you've just moved into the ground-floor flat, haven't you?". The door-opening offer at the end: a corner of the communal garden, and "pop round for a cup of tea — number 11, blue door."
2. A shared grumble bonds two strangers for a second, but a grumble that only grumbles is a dead end. Turning it positive signals you're easy, warm company — not someone who complains — and it keeps the exchange light and open, so it can move on to something that matters.
3. "You'd be welcome to a corner of it", "you'll have to pop round for a cup of tea", "I'd like that very much", "lovely to finally put a name to a face", and "see you in the garden" — every one a soft, no-pressure invitation or a warm goodbye that quietly means let's do this again.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1883, a volcanic island in the Sunda Strait called Krakatoa blew itself apart with what is still reckoned the loudest sound in recorded history — heard nearly 3,000 miles away. Millions of tonnes of ash were flung into the sky and carried right around the planet.

For months afterwards, people from London to New York stepped outside, glanced up, and remarked to complete strangers on the strangest, reddest sunsets anyone had ever seen. They were making small talk about the weather — with no idea they were admiring a dead volcano's ash circling the whole Earth. Some think those very skies gave Edvard Munch the blood-red backdrop of The Scream.

So the next time you nod at a neighbour and try "doesn't look like it's going to hold" — remember you're reaching for the oldest icebreaker there is, and that the sky above the two of you is never quite as small as the chat.

Two bigger questions

  1. The weather is the one thing every stranger genuinely shares — you're both standing under the very same sky. Is that why it's the universal icebreaker? What else could ever do the same job as safely?
  2. Krakatoa quietly changed the weather across a whole planet that had no idea why. Today we blame strange weather on the climate — and sometimes on each other. Has talking about the weather stopped being innocent small talk?
