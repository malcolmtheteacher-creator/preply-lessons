Because "so" already carries that whole idea — "that I am going to the leaving party." Repeating it in full would sound stiff and over-explained. Not Repeating the Noun

Small words that stand in for a noun you've already named, or that sum up a whole situation.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday moments:

  • "I need a new umbrella — mine broke in the wind, so I borrowed my sister's old one."
  • "These shoes pinch a bit — could I try the brown ones instead?"
  • "The queue took forty minutes, the machine was broken, and the woman at the counter was rude about it — I've never had such a frustrating morning at the bank."

What job is one doing in the first sentence? What about ones in the second? And is such a naming something new, or summing something up? Say your answer before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
one replaces a singular countable noun already mentioned ("umbrella"); ones does the same job for a plural ("shoes"). such a + noun sums up a whole situation just described — "such a frustrating morning" packages up the queue, the broken machine and the rude woman into one phrase, instead of listing them all again.
adjective + one / ones  (singular / plural)
such a + adjective + noun  (summing up what was just said)

From naming it twice to naming it once

"My phone screen cracked, so I need a new phone."
→
"My phone screen cracked, so I need a new one."

Once the listener knows you mean "phone", repeating the word sounds clumsy — "one" carries the same meaning in a single syllable.

Referring Back to a Whole Idea

For answering, reacting and reminding — without repeating everything that was just said.

Spot the pattern

Three real exchanges:

  • "Is the meeting still happening at three?" "I believe so — nobody's told me otherwise."
  • "Did they fix the leak?" "I'm afraid not — the plumber couldn't come until Thursday."
  • "We might move the party outdoors if the weather's good." "And if so, we'll need extra chairs."

Do "so" and "not" replace a single word, or a whole idea? Say it out loud before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
so stands in for a whole positive idea already mentioned — "that the meeting is still happening", "that we move the party outdoors". not does the same job negatively — "that they didn't fix the leak". Repeating the full idea each time ("I believe the meeting is still happening at three") would sound stiff in conversation.
think / believe / hope / guess / suppose / expect + so
afraid + not  ·  if so / if not
One special case worth knowing: "I told you so" — used, often a little smugly, when something you warned about turns out to be true. "The bridge is closed again." "I told you so — I said they'd never finish the repairs on time."

this / that / these / those for a whole idea

  • "She quit her stable job to travel for a year. That took real courage."
  • "He never apologised, which surprised me — I hadn't expected that from him."
  • "Interest rates are rising and rents keep going up. This is making it harder for young people to buy a home."

These words aren't replacing a single noun here — what are they pointing back to? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
They point back to a whole idea, action or situation just described — not one thing. this/these often feels close or current — something happening now, or your own situation. that/those often feels a little more distant — someone else's words, or something already finished.
this / these → close, current, your own situation
that / those → more distant, someone else's words, something finished

Not Repeating the Verb

For actions someone already mentioned — and for dropping words the listener can fill in themselves.

Spot the pattern

  • "She asked me to close the window, so I did so straight away."
  • "He told the child not to touch the exhibit, but she did it anyway."
  • "I said I'd call her back, and I fully intend to do that this evening."

What action does each bold phrase replace? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Each one replaces the whole action already mentioned — closing the window, touching the exhibit, calling her back — so the speaker doesn't have to repeat the full verb phrase again.
did / do / done + so / it / that
A note on tone: "do so" sounds a little neutral-to-formal — natural in writing or a considered reply. In quick, casual conversation, people usually say "do it" or "do that" instead. All three are correct; the difference is how formal the moment feels.

Leaving words out altogether

  • "Have you told your parents about the move?" "Not yet, but I will this weekend."
  • "Can you actually fix a leaking tap?" "I can, yes."
  • "I don't think he's coming." "He isn't — he texted me an hour ago."

What's missing after "will", "can" and "isn't" in each reply? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
After "will" you'd expect "tell them"; after "can", "fix a leaking tap"; after "isn't", "coming". English simply drops the repeated part after and / but / or plus an auxiliary, once the meaning is already obvious.
and / but / or + auxiliary  (repeated verb phrase left out)
"He asked me to lock the door. I locked the door."
→
"He asked me to lock the door. I did so."
