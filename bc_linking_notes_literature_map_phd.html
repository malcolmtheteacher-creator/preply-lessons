Linking Notes & Building a Literature Map

From atomic notes to a networked thesis — the move that makes year 3 easier than year 1

The problem with atomic notes alone

The Cornell + Zettelkasten lesson taught you to write one idea, one note. That's the foundation. But atomic notes alone don't make a thesis. They make a pile.

The move that makes year 3 of a PhD easier than year 1 is linking: every new note hooks into existing notes. The pile becomes a network.

💬 Discuss before we go further

Right now, when you read a paper, do your notes connect to what you read last week — or do they sit in their own folder, never to be seen again?

Be honest. Most PhD researchers describe their notes as "graveyards." We're going to change that.

A demonstration: two notes from different papers

202403-15-A
Diaspora identity: Anderson on imagined communities
Anderson argues national identity is "imagined" — sustained by print capitalism and shared symbols rather than face-to-face contact (1983).
202404-22-B
Post-conflict legitimacy: Mac Ginty critique
Mac Ginty (2010) challenges the "liberal peace" model — argues imposed institutional templates fail to capture local legitimacy.
💬 Question for you

These two notes look unrelated. But for a researcher working on identity in post-conflict contexts, they are deeply linked. How?

Both critique top-down models of legitimacy — Anderson by showing identity is constructed (not given by states), Mac Ginty by showing peace can't be imposed (it has to be locally legitimate). Together they suggest your literature gap: nobody has yet examined how diaspora-imagined identities interact with the local-legitimacy critique in post-conflict reconstruction.

That sentence is the seed of a research question. It came from linking, not from either note alone.

⚡ The core principle

A single note has information. Linked notes have argument. Your thesis lives in the links.

✍ Now you try

Pick two papers you've read this month from different angles of your topic. Write one sentence that names what they have in common — even if it's a tension, not an agreement.

If you can't write that sentence yet, you haven't read the papers as a researcher — you've read them as a student. The link makes the difference.

Four types of link — pick the right one

Not all links are equal. There are four useful types in academic linking. The discipline is to name the type when you make the link, not just connect notes invisibly.

1. Agreement Two sources converge on the same claim. Strengthens the consensus argument.
2. Tension Two sources disagree. Surfaces a debate you can position yourself in.
3. Consequence Note B is what happens IF note A is true. Builds the chain of reasoning.
4. Method Note A is the question; note B is a methodological approach to answer it.
💬 Quick check

Which of the four would you expect to appear MOST in a strong thesis literature review — and why?

Tension links. A literature review that only shows agreement is a textbook. A literature review that surfaces tensions — that names the debate — is where YOUR contribution becomes visible. The gap you fill is almost always sitting between two tensions.

Worked example — building each type

📄 Two source notes

Note A (Castells, 1996): network society is replacing hierarchical structures of power.

Note B (Mann, 2013): states retain unprecedented infrastructural reach in the digital age — networks have not flattened power.

💬 You name the link

What TYPE of link is this — agreement, tension, consequence, or method? Say it out loud.

Tension. Castells says power is flattening; Mann says it's not. The link sentence:

"Castells (1996) frames the network society as displacing state hierarchies, yet Mann's (2013) infrastructural-power thesis directly challenges this — suggesting digital infrastructures have entrenched, rather than dissolved, state reach."

That sentence belongs in your literature review. It exists nowhere in either source — you wrote it by linking.

Bad link vs good link

⚠ Bad link

"See also: 202404-22-B"

No type named. No reason given. In six months you'll see this and have no idea why you connected them.

✓ Good link

"Tension with 202404-22-B: Mac Ginty's local-legitimacy critique cuts against this note's assumption of institutional transferability."

Type named. Direction of the tension stated. Reusable two years later.

✍ Now you try

Take two notes from your own reading this week. Pick the type (agreement / tension / consequence / method) and write the link sentence as if it had to land in your literature review unchanged.

⚡ The discipline

Every link names its type and includes a sentence you could lift straight into your thesis. If a link is too short to do that, it's not finished.

The literature map — making the field visible

Once you have linked notes, you can draw the map. The map is not the literature — it's your reading of the literature. It will change as you read more, and that's the point.

💬 First, name the camps

In your field, who are the major camps — the schools of thought or methodological positions your work has to engage with? Name three.

If you can't name three, you haven't read enough breadth yet — and that's useful information.

A simple visual structure

💬 Read the map

The gap sits in the middle. The camps surround it. The dashed lines are links you can already make. The red line is the tension you're going to exploit to argue for your contribution.

Where on this map does your most recent paper sit? Which camp? Which empirical territory?

How to draw your own (right now)

  1. Name 2-3 theoretical camps your field is split between
  2. Name 1 methodological tradition you're drawing from
  3. Name the empirical case literature on your specific topic
  4. Mark the tensions between camps with red lines
  5. Find the empty space — that's where your contribution sits
✍ Sketch yours now (just words, no drawing yet)
⚡ Why the map matters

The map is what your supervisor will ask to see in month 6. The viva panel will ask the same in year 4. Build it now, update it every month, and you'll never write the literature review from scratch — you'll just transcribe the map.

Apply to your thesis — live

Everything we've covered is theory until you apply it to your own work. We're going to do that now, with one paper you've read recently.

💬 Pick one paper

Name a paper or chapter you've read in the last 7 days. Tell me the author, the year, and the central claim in one sentence.

If you can't summarise it in one sentence, that's the first finding — the note isn't done yet.

✍ Step 1 — Write the atomic note

One claim, one note. Author + year + central claim. No quotes — paraphrase in your own words.

✍ Step 2 — Find one note to link to

Which existing note in your system does this connect to? Name it. Then name the link TYPE and write the link sentence.

💬 Read it back

Read your link sentence aloud. Could it land in your literature review unchanged? If not — refine it now. The discipline is to write notes that are thesis-ready the first time.

✍ Step 3 — Place it on the map

Looking back at your sketched map (Tab 3): which camp does this paper belong to? Which tension does it speak to?

Before you close this lesson — checklist

⚡ The compound effect

One linked note per paper. 30 papers a week. 1,560 linked notes a year. By month 18, your map shows you the argument of your thesis before you've written it.

The viva test — does the system work?

You'll know your linking system is working when you can answer viva-style questions instantly, from memory, by walking through your map. Let's test it.

Speaking time
2:00
🎓 Viva question 1

"Walk me through how the literature on your topic is split. Who are the main camps, and where does your work sit?"

💬 Speak it out

Use your map as the structure. Camp 1 says X. Camp 2 says Y. The tension is Z. My contribution sits HERE because [link sentence].

If you stumble, the map needs more work — not your speaking. Keep going.

🎓 Viva question 2

"What is one paper that, if it didn't exist, would change your argument?"

💬 The hidden test

This question tests whether you've identified your load-bearing sources — the ones your literature map depends on. If your linking is good, you'll name one in three seconds.

"If Mann's 2013 infrastructural-power thesis didn't exist, my argument that digital infrastructures entrench rather than dissolve state authority would lose its principal counterweight to Castells. I'd be left arguing against a position with no representative — the tension would collapse, and with it the gap I'm filling."

Notice: the answer names the source, the link type (tension), and the consequence for the thesis. That's three years of linking talking through one minute of viva answer.

🎓 Viva question 3

"Where would you locate yourself methodologically — and why have you chosen that camp over the alternatives?"

💬 Final speak-out

This is Tab 3 of the dashboard's Defending Methodology lesson — the rationale and alternative-considered structure. If you've linked your methodological notes properly, the answer should already be on your map.

⚡ The proof

If you can answer all three viva questions in two minutes each from your map alone — no notes, no slides — your linking system is working. If you can't, the next session of reading needs to be slower, more linked, more deliberate.
