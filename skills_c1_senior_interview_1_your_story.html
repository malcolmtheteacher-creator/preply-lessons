The Two-Minute Headline

“Tell me about yourself” is the most important answer of the interview — and the one most senior candidates get wrong.

Today: you’ll work through the language of senior career stories — and sharpen the grammar that connects your past to your present: I’ve led… since 2019 · I spent six years… · what connects these roles is…
Before we start. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • When did you last introduce yourself to someone whose opinion could change your career? What did you say first, and would you say it again?
  • If someone read only the first sentence of your CV, what should they know about you?
  • Is it better to be remembered for being brilliant at one thing, or competent at many? Does the answer change as you get more senior?

The vocabulary of this lesson

Ten items. Work through the activities and say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Her remit had grown every year: first one product line, then the whole European region, and now two other divisions as well.
remit (n) — the area of responsibility officially given to a person or team. Within / outside my remit; to widen / narrow the remit. “That decision falls outside my remit.”
2. The board gave her a clear mandate: halve the delivery time and she would have a free hand to restructure the department.
mandate (n) — formal authority and instruction to do something. A clear / broad mandate; to have a mandate to…; to receive / give a mandate. Stronger than “task”.
3. He was brought in to spearhead the company’s move into Asia, and by the second year he was running it.
to spearhead (v) — to lead an important effort or initiative, usually a new or difficult one. “She spearheaded the merger.” Slightly formal, but standard in senior CVs.
4. Instead of reciting her career in order, she opened with a single headline: “I turn ambitious strategies into results that a board can measure.”
headline (n) — here, a one-sentence summary of what you stand for, borrowed from newspapers. Lead with a headline; a career headline. A headline sells the story; it is not the story.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: lateral move · track record · to position yourself. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means a history of past results that shows what someone can do?
a track record — usually “a proven / strong / solid track record of (doing) something”. Preposition: of + noun or -ing. “A strong track record of delivering on time.”
2. Which term means a move to a job at the same level, in a different area?
a lateral move — sideways, not up. Often used to explain a gap in progression honestly: “It was a lateral move, but it gave me the P&L experience I lacked.” Compare a step up and a step down.
3. Which phrasal-sounding verb means to present yourself so that others see you in a particular way?
to position yourself (as…) — borrowed from marketing. “She positioned herself as the person who fixes broken partnerships.” Noun: positioning.

C · Choose the meaning: a / b / c

1. He wanted a role in which he could scale the practice from twenty people to a hundred. To scale something is to…
  (a) reduce it to a manageable size   (b) grow it substantially while keeping it working well   (c) measure its performance against a standard
(b) — to scale (up) a business, team or process. Noun: scalability; adjective: scalable. The opposite: to scale back (reduce).
2. After eight years as a deputy, she was ready to step up to the top job. To step up means to…
  (a) accept a bigger responsibility   (b) resign   (c) move to a new country
(a) — to take on a more senior or demanding role. “When the director left, she stepped up.” Opposite: to step down (leave a post).

D · Collocation & word building

1. Complete the collocation. Careers have a shape, and you can describe it: “my career has followed an upward ___ rather than a straight line.” What is the noun, and what other adjectives can describe it?
trajectory (n) — the path of development over time. An upward / steep / unconventional / flat trajectory. “My trajectory has been unusual: engineering first, then strategy.”
2. A role you do not plan to stay in forever, but that prepares you for something better, is a ___ stone. Finish the idiom, and say whether it is positive or negative.
a stepping stone — usually positive: “That job was a stepping stone to the executive team.” Be careful in an interview: calling this role a stepping stone suggests you will not stay.

Now use them: out loud

Discuss:
  • Say your own one-sentence headline, then say what your remit is today and what mandate you were given when you took it on.
  • Sort the terms by register. Which would you use in a CV and a board interview (mandate, spearhead, trajectory)? Which are more natural in conversation (step up, scale, lateral move)? Which one would sound odd if you overused it?

Two finalists, one opening question

A composite scene, built for teaching: the people and figures are invented, but the pattern is one that panels describe again and again.

Part 1 — The same CV, a different first minute

A European advisory firm is choosing a partner for its healthcare practice. Two finalists reach the last round, and on paper they are almost twins: fifteen years in the sector, a division of around forty people, revenue growth of roughly a third over three years, and both are fluent, charming and well prepared.

The panel’s first question is the same for both: “Tell me about yourself.” The first candidate begins with her school and university, then walks through five employers in order, with every title, every date and every reorganisation. She is clear and honest, and she speaks for six minutes. Two panel members look at their notes by the third minute.

The second candidate begins differently: “I’m a transformation leader. For fifteen years I’ve helped healthcare organisations turn strategy into results a board can measure.” She adds two proofs — a division she has led since 2019 and the third by which it has grown — and ends with direction: “I’m now looking for a role where I can shape strategy, not only deliver it.” She stops after ninety seconds.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the two candidates have in common on paper?

2. How did the two openings differ in length and in shape?

1. Fifteen years’ experience in the sector, a team of about forty, roughly a third revenue growth in three years, and strong preparation.

2. The first was six minutes of chronology. The second was ninety seconds: headline, two proofs, and a direction.

Discuss:
  • Which opening would you rather listen to at 4 p.m., after five interviews? Why?
  • What does an interviewer learn about judgement from how a candidate chooses what to leave out?

Part 2 — What the panel remembered

That evening the panel compares notes. Nobody disputes that the first candidate has the experience: it is all there, and she did answer every follow-up. But when one panel member is asked to sum up each candidate in a single sentence, she can do it for the second candidate without a pause — “the one who fixes strategy that has stalled” — and cannot do it for the first.

Another panel member points out that the second candidate’s headline did something clever. It was specific enough to be tested, and so it gave the panel its questions: “You said strategy that a board can measure — give us an example where the board disagreed.” From that point the interview followed the story she had chosen. The first candidate, by contrast, spent the later questions answering whatever the panel happened to notice on her CV.

It is not that one candidate was cleverer. It is that one controlled the interview’s agenda by offering a frame and keeping her promises, and the other left the frame to the panel.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What could the panel say about the second candidate that they could not say about the first?

2. Why did the headline make the later questions easier for her?

1. They could summarise her in one sentence: the one who fixes strategy that has stalled.

2. It was specific enough to be tested, so the panel’s follow-up questions came from the story she had chosen.

Discuss:
  • What is the difference between a headline and a slogan?
  • If your headline makes a promise, which of your later answers must keep it?

Part 3 — The question that stays open

There is an uncomfortable detail. One panel member argues the second candidate may have been too polished, and that ninety seconds is not enough time to understand a career built on six difficult moves. Another replies that the panel had the full CV in front of it and the rest of the hour to ask. “The CV tells us what she did. The opening tells us how she thinks about herself.”

A third raises a harder question: would the verdict have been the same if the candidates had been judged by a panel from a different organisation or country? Norms about modesty, directness and rank do differ, and a first answer that sounds confident in one room can sound self-promoting in another.

The firm offers the role to the second candidate. Whether it was the right decision is, as one panel member admits, impossible to prove — nobody ever gets to see the other timeline.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the panel member’s worry about the second candidate’s opening?

2. What was the third panel member’s question about the verdict?

1. That ninety seconds might be too polished and too short to represent a career built on six difficult moves.

2. Whether the same verdict would hold with a panel from a different organisation or country, where norms about modesty and directness differ.

Discuss:
  • How do you judge, in your own sector, how much confidence is the right amount in the first minute?
  • If you could only keep one: a strong headline or a strong first example? Argue it out.

Grammar: past, present, and the thread between them

The most common grammar error in a senior career story is not a missing article. It is the wrong tense: it makes a career that is still running sound finished, or one that is finished sound unfinished.

Present perfect = a story that is still running. I’ve led the division since 2019. · I’ve worked in healthcare for fifteen years. · Over the past three years we’ve grown revenue by a third. Time markers: since, for, over the past…, so far, in recent years, ever since.
Past simple = a closed chapter. I spent six years in advisory roles. · In 2016 I moved to the regional office. · I left because the structure had no room for growth. Time markers: in 2016, from 2012 to 2018, back then, at that point, after that. If the period is finished, use the past simple, even if the effect lasts.
The thread: a cleft sentence. Use a cleft to name what stays constant across your roles: What connects these roles is… · What I’m best known for is… · What has stayed the same throughout is… After is you can put a noun phrase, a clause with that, or a to-infinitive: “What I’ve always done is to rebuild trust.”

1 · Headline and proof

Put the verbs into the correct tense. Read each paragraph aloud when you have finished.

“I (lead) a division of forty people since 2019, and over the past three years we (grow) revenue by a third. Before that, I (spend) six years in advisory roles, where I (learn) to win the trust of boards.”

have led · have grown · spent · learnt / learned

Two present perfects for the story that is still running (since 2019 · over the past three years); two past simples for the closed chapter (before that · six years). “Learnt” and “learned” are both correct.

2 · Finding the thread

Complete each sentence with the two words that are missing.

“What all my roles that I’m the person who is asked to fix partnerships that have stalled.”

“What I’ve always is rebuild trust before I rebuild the plan.”

“Looking back, the thing that has the same throughout is the question I ask first: what does the client actually need?”

connects · is · done · to · stayed

The cleft puts the important information at the end. After “is”, a to-infinitive is natural when the cleft begins with “What I’ve done”.

3 · The full answer

A panel asks: “Why are you looking to move now?” Complete the answer with the correct form.

“For the past four years I responsible for the practice’s delivery, and I it. But I the point last year where I built what I to build. What I want now a broader mandate.”

have been · have enjoyed · reached · had · had come · is

“Have been”, “have enjoyed” — the story is still running. “Reached”, “had” — closed point in time. “had come to build” — the past perfect is used for something that happened before the point we are describing.

Going deeper

This is a composite scene, so there is no real balance sheet to check. What follows is the argument about what an opening question actually tests.

How panels score an answer

Most senior panels score on the same five things, whatever the question. You will use this scorecard to mark your own mock interview in the Speak tab.

CriterionWhat the panel is listening for
StructureThe listener can follow the shape of the answer (headline, steps, result).
EvidenceA specific example, with a number, instead of a general claim.
OwnershipIt is clear what I personally decided or did, and what the team did.
RelevanceIt connects to the role and the organisation I am talking to.
DeliveryCalm pace, no long fillers, finished within the time.

What the first answer is really measuring

Panels rarely score “tell me about yourself” directly. It is a probe for four things at once: whether you can prioritise (what you choose to say), whether you can summarise (how much time you need), whether your self-image matches your CV, and whether you can direct a conversation. A chronological answer proves you remember your career, which nobody doubted.

The promise nobody wrote down

A headline is a contract. If you open with “I turn strategy into measurable results”, every later example will be judged against that sentence. The skill is not choosing a grand headline but choosing one you can support in three different follow-up stories — and being ready for the follow-up that tests it.

Two readings of the same ninety seconds

The sceptical reading: an opening that tidy sounds rehearsed, and a rehearsed senior leader may be hiding the messy parts of a career. The supportive reading: choosing what to say is the work of seniority; the messy parts come up in follow-ups. A good candidate lets both readings be true: tight, then candid when asked.

Discuss:
  • What is the one detail on your own CV you would most like a panel not to ask about, and what is your honest, composed answer?
  • Does a strong headline make you more or less approachable in the room? Does it depend on the culture of the organisation?
  • Which would be harder for you: cutting your story to ninety seconds, or extending a short one to three minutes?

Speak

All of it out loud, against the clock. These are the three rooms in which a senior person has to tell their story: the panel, the headhunter and the dinner table.

Useful language: I’m a [role] with… years’ experience in… · What connects these roles is… · I’ve led… since… · I took on responsibility for… · That experience led me to… · Which is why this role appeals to me.

How to rehearse every answer

  1. Write key words for your answer, not the full text.
  2. Say it aloud in two minutes and record it on your phone.
  3. Listen back and mark one thing to fix.
  4. Say it again in ninety seconds.
  5. Say it a last time in forty-five seconds, without notes.

Which answer would you trust?

Decide before you click, and say why.

“Tell me about yourself.”
A“I was born in a small town. I studied economics at university. Then I got my first job in a bank, and after that I moved to another company, and then another one…”
B“I’m a transformation leader with fifteen years’ experience helping organisations turn strategy into measurable results. Most recently I led a division of forty people and grew its revenue by a third. I’m now looking for a role with broader strategic responsibility.”
B opens with a headline, adds proof and ends with direction. A is a chronological list without focus or result.
“What makes you different from other candidates?”
A“I combine deep sector knowledge with experience running large teams. Few people can both design the strategy and lead the people who deliver it.”
B“I’m just a normal manager. I try to do my best, and I hope it is enough.”
A names a specific, defensible combination. B hedges and doubts itself, which weakens credibility at senior level.
“What were you personally responsible for in that project?”
A“We did everything together as a team. The team achieved a great result and everyone worked very hard.”
B“I designed the approach and won the sponsor’s support. My team of ten delivered it, and my specific contribution was resolving the budget conflict that was blocking us.”
“We” for the team, “I” for your own decisions. A hides your individual contribution.
