Telling Your Story

Position yourself as a senior candidate in the first two minutes.

Today: you will deliver a clear, confident career story at senior level so you can open any interview with impact.

How you begin sets the frame

The first answer in an interview shapes everything that follows. Whatever you say first becomes the thread the interviewer pulls on, so it needs to be deliberate.

Language you can reuse

Each card describes a moment in the interview. Decide what you would say, then check the phrases.

Situation: You open with a single sentence that says who you are professionally.

Say how you would handle it first.

  • “I’m a [role] with [number] years’ experience in [field].”
  • “My career has been built around [theme].”
  • “I specialise in [area], particularly where [context].”
  • “I’m best known for [result or reputation].”

Lead with the headline, not with where you were born or where you studied.

Situation: You describe a career move and show it was a deliberate step, not an accident.

Say how you would handle it first.

  • “I was brought in to [fix / build / lead] [challenge].”
  • “I took on responsibility for [scope].”
  • “That experience led me to [next step].”
  • “I made the move because [reason that shows ambition].”
  • “I was promoted to [role] after [result].”
Situation: You link the pattern across your roles into one thread.

Say how you would handle it first.

  • “What connects all of these roles is [theme].”
  • “Looking back, a common thread has been [theme].”
  • “Each step gave me a broader remit.”
  • “I’ve consistently been the person asked to [type of challenge].”
Situation: You explain why you are ready for more responsibility.

Say how you would handle it first.

  • “I’m ready to take on a broader mandate.”
  • “I’ve reached the point where I want to shape strategy, not only deliver it.”
  • “The natural next step is [scope].”
  • “I’m looking for a role where I can [impact].”
Situation: You finish by tying your story to this particular role.

Say how you would handle it first.

  • “Which is why this role appeals to me.”
  • “What draws me to this position is [reason].”
  • “I’d like to bring [strength] to [what the organisation needs].”
  • “That’s the experience I’d want to build on here.”

End with direction. The interviewer should know where the story is going.

Upgrade the sentence

Rewrite each sentence so it sounds like a confident senior candidate. Then compare.

Make it sound senior: “I worked in many projects and I managed some people.”
Over the past eight years I’ve led portfolios of complex projects, most recently with a team of twelve across three countries.
Make it sound senior: “I was responsible for sales in my company.”
I owned a sales portfolio worth around €15 million and grew it by 22% in two years.
Make it sound senior: “I changed company because I wanted something new.”
I moved because I had gone as far as I could in that structure, and I wanted broader scope and more influence over strategy.
Make it sound senior: “I am very good in communication and leadership.”
My strength is turning complexity into a clear direction that senior stakeholders and teams can act on.

Two ways to answer the same opening question

Read both answers. Decide which one an interviewer would rate higher, and say why before you click.

“Tell me about yourself.”
A“I was born in a small town. I studied economics at university. Then I got my first job in a bank, and after that I moved to another company, and then another one…”
B“I’m a transformation leader with fifteen years’ experience helping organisations turn strategy into measurable results. Most recently I led a division of forty people and grew its revenue by a third. I’m now looking for a role with broader strategic responsibility.”
B opens with a headline, adds proof and ends with direction. A is a chronological list with no focus and no result.
“What makes you different from other candidates?”
A“I combine deep sector knowledge with experience running large teams. Few people can both design the strategy and lead the people who deliver it, and that combination is where I add the most value.”
B“I’m just a normal manager. I try to do my best and I hope it is enough.”
A names a specific, defensible combination. B uses hedging and self-doubt, which weakens credibility at senior level.
“What were you personally responsible for in that project?”
A“We did everything together as a team. The team achieved a great result and everyone worked very hard.”
B“I designed the approach and won the sponsor’s support. My team of ten delivered it, and my specific contribution was resolving the budget conflict that was blocking us.”
Using “we” for the team and “I” for your own decisions shows ownership. A hides your individual contribution.

Build your career story

A senior-level opening has three parts. Write each part in your own words, with your own details.

Headlinewho you are, in one sentence
Prooftwo or three moves with results
Directionwhy this role, why now
Interview question

“Tell me about yourself.”

Build your answer in three parts

Headline: “I’m a transformation director with fifteen years’ experience helping mid-sized organisations turn strategy into measurable results.”

Proof: “Most recently I lead a division of forty people and a €30 million portfolio, which we’ve grown by a third in three years. Before that I spent six years in advisory roles, where I learnt to win the trust of boards. What connects all of it is that I’m the person asked to fix complex problems.”

Direction: “I’m now ready for a broader mandate, which is why a role with responsibility for growth and for building a team appeals to me.”

Notice: a headline under twenty seconds, numbers in the proof, “I” for own decisions, and an ending that points forward.

Speak

Close the notes and give your answer aloud in about two minutes. Then give it a second time with one improvement.

Explain a gap or a sideways move on your career path so that it sounds like a strategic choice. Write two or three sentences.

“After seven years in one sector, I deliberately moved into a different one to broaden my perspective. It meant a lateral step, but I came back with a wider network and skills I couldn’t have built by staying.”

Frame the move around what you gained and what you chose, not what you lost.
