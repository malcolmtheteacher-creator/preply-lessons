What Is the Academic Word List?

The Academic Word List (AWL) contains 570 word families essential for academic English across all disciplines. These words appear frequently in textbooks, lectures, research, and essays at university level. Mastering the AWL dramatically improves your academic language competence.

Why the AWL Matters

Unlike subject-specific technical terms, AWL words are used across disciplines. You'll encounter "analyze," "significant," and "mechanism" in history, biology, literature, and economics. Building this vocabulary is your fastest path to academic fluency.

Common AWL Words You Should Know

These appear in virtually every academic text:

Analyze / Analysis: "The study analyzes the economic factors influencing climate policy."
Significant / Significance: "The results were statistically significant."
Identify / Identification: "Researchers identified three key variables."
Establish / Establishment: "The hypothesis was established through repeated testing."
Acquire / Acquisition: "Students acquire language through exposure and practice."
Theory / Theoretical: "The theoretical framework guides the research design."
Evaluate / Evaluation: "We must evaluate the evidence before drawing conclusions."
Method / Methodology: "The research methodology combined qualitative and quantitative approaches."

Key insight: AWL words appear 10x more frequently in academic texts than in everyday English. Focusing your vocabulary study on the AWL is the smartest investment of your study time.

Understanding Word Families

AWL words organize into families. A word family includes different forms of the same root word. Learning one family teaches you multiple words.

Example: ANALYZE Word Family

Analyze (verb): "Researchers analyze the data."
Analysis (noun): "The analysis reveals important patterns."
Analytical (adjective): "Analytical thinking is essential."
Analyst (noun): "A financial analyst studies market trends."

Why This Matters

Reading an academic text, you'll encounter all four forms. Understanding they're related helps you grasp meaning. Better yet, learning one form helps you recognize and understand the others.

More Word Families

ESTABLISH: establish → establishment → established → establishing
SIGNIFICANT: significant → significance → significantly → insignificant
CONCLUDE: conclude → conclusion → conclusive → conclusively
REQUIRE: require → requirement → required → requiring

Collocations: Words That Go Together

Academic language isn't just about knowing individual words—it's about knowing which words naturally go together. These combinations are called collocations.

Common Academic Collocations

Conduct research — "They conducted extensive research on climate change."
Draw a conclusion — "Based on the evidence, we can draw several conclusions."
Significant findings — "The study reported significant findings."
Theoretical framework — "The theoretical framework shaped our research design."
Identify factors — "We identified key factors contributing to the problem."
Demonstrate evidence — "The data demonstrates compelling evidence."
Underlying assumption — "An underlying assumption is that all participants have prior knowledge."
Subsequent analysis — "Subsequent analysis revealed unexpected patterns."

Learning tip: Don't learn words in isolation. Learn them with common collocations. Write them in sentences. This is how native speakers use language, and it's how your brain remembers it.

Practice: Academic Vocabulary in Context

Fill in the correct word form:

"The (analyze) shows that the treatment had a (significant) effect on patient outcomes."

Analysis; significant. The first is a noun (follows "The"), the second is an adjective (modifies "effect").

Which collocation is correct?

A) "Conduct an investigation" or B) "Make an investigation"?

A is correct. "Conduct an investigation" is the standard collocation. In academic writing, we don't "make" investigations.

Identify the AWL word and its family:

"We must establish clear criteria for evaluation before undertaking the study."

AWL words: "establish" (establish, establishment, established), "evaluation" (evaluate, evaluation, evaluative). Both are key academic words appearing across disciplines.

Replace the informal word with an academic alternative:

"The results show that exercise helps mental health."

"The findings demonstrate that physical activity positively impacts mental health." Better academic register: "show" → "demonstrate," "helps" → "positively impacts," "results" → "findings," "exercise" → "physical activity."

Academic writing requires a particular register—level of formality and vocabulary choice. Shifting from informal to academic language is a core B2 skill.

Building Your Academic Vocabulary System

Strategy 1: Master Word Families

Focus on 20-30 key word families. Learn the noun, verb, adjective, and adverb forms. Review until automatic.

Strategy 2: Learn Collocations

When you encounter an AWL word, note what it typically combines with. Write example sentences with the collocation, not isolated words.

Strategy 3: Active Use

Don't just read academic vocabulary—use it. Write practice essays. Discuss with study partners. Try using new words in your next written assignment.

Strategy 4: Spaced Repetition

Review new words at increasing intervals: after 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, 1 month. This hardwires them into memory.

The 80-20 rule: 80% of academic texts use about 20% of the vocabulary. Master the AWL and you'll understand most academic reading. Subject-specific terms come after.

Your Action Plan

  2. Learn all forms (noun, verb, adj., adv.) and one strong collocation for each
