The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The golden age of the airship

In the years between the two world wars, the fastest and most glamorous way to cross an ocean was not by aeroplane — those were small, noisy and short-ranged — but by airship. Germany led the world in building them. The company behind them, Zeppelin, had already sent its earlier ship, the Graf Zeppelin, around the globe and across the Atlantic hundreds of times without a single passenger death. For a decade, the giant silver ships of the sky seemed to be the future of travel.

The crowning achievement of that age was the Hindenburg. Completed in 1936, it was the largest aircraft ever to fly — more than 800 feet long, close to the length of the Titanic. Passengers did not sit strapped into seats; they strolled along promenades with slanted windows, ate fine meals in a proper dining room, slept in private cabins, and could even relax in a sealed smoking lounge. Crossing from Germany to America took two and a half days in extraordinary comfort, floating serenely above the waves.

There was, however, a hidden danger built into the design. To lift such an enormous hull, the ship was filled with hydrogen — cheap, wonderfully light, and terrifyingly flammable. The safer gas, helium, was controlled almost entirely by the United States, which refused to sell it to Germany. And so the most luxurious machine of its era flew, season after season, buoyed up by seven million cubic feet of the most explosive gas known.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was airship travel so appealing in the 1930s, and what made the Hindenburg special?

2. Why was the ship filled with hydrogen rather than the safer helium?

1. Airships were faster and far more comfortable than early planes; the Hindenburg was the largest aircraft ever built, offering ocean-liner luxury — dining room, cabins, promenades — over a smooth 2.5-day Atlantic crossing.

2. Hydrogen was cheap and very light, but highly flammable; the safer gas, helium, was controlled by the United States, which would not sell it to Germany.

Discuss:
  • The builders knew hydrogen was dangerous but used it anyway. When is it acceptable to accept a known risk in the name of progress or profit?

Part 2 — Thirty-seven seconds at Lakehurst

On the evening of 6 May 1937, the Hindenburg was approaching its landing field at Lakehurst, New Jersey, at the end of yet another Atlantic crossing. Thunderstorms had delayed the arrival for hours, and the air was damp and charged. A crowd of reporters, photographers and ground crew was waiting below. Among them stood a radio reporter named Herbert Morrison, who was recording a calm, admiring description of the great ship as it drifted in and dropped its landing ropes.

The ground crew were reaching for the lines and beginning to haul the ship down when, near the tail, something changed. A flicker of flame appeared high on the hull. In an instant the hydrogen ignited, and fire raced forward through the ship. The tail sank, the nose lifted, and the entire structure was engulfed. Morrison's calm commentary broke apart into the anguished cry that history never forgot — "Oh, the humanity" — as he struggled to describe what he was seeing.

The whole ship was destroyed in little more than half a minute. And yet, astonishingly, of the ninety-seven people aboard, sixty-two survived: many leapt or were pulled from the wreck as it settled onto the field. Thirty-five people on board lost their lives, along with one member of the ground crew below — a heavy toll, borne with dignity by the crews and rescuers who did what they could in those desperate seconds.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were the conditions and the scene as the Hindenburg approached Lakehurst?

2. How quickly was the ship destroyed, and roughly how many of those aboard survived?

1. It was the evening of 6 May 1937; thunderstorms had delayed the landing, the air was damp, and a crowd of reporters and ground crew was waiting — including radio reporter Herbert Morrison.

2. The ship was destroyed in a little over half a minute (around 35 seconds); remarkably, 62 of the 97 people aboard survived.

Discuss:
  • The disaster happened in front of cameras and a live radio microphone. How does being filmed change the way the world remembers a tragedy?

Part 3 — The end of an era

What made the Hindenburg disaster so different from earlier accidents was not the number of lives lost — other crashes had taken more — but the fact that it was captured so vividly. The newsreel footage of the burning ship played in cinemas across the world, and Morrison's shaking voice was broadcast the next day. For the first time, millions of people did not just read about a disaster; they watched it happen, again and again.

An official inquiry was held, but no single cause was ever proven beyond doubt. The most widely accepted explanation is that a spark — most likely a build-up of static electricity in that charged, stormy air — ignited hydrogen that was leaking from the ship. Sabotage was suspected at the time but never established. What everyone could agree on was simpler and more damning: a ship kept aloft by so much hydrogen was, in the end, a catastrophe waiting to happen.

Public confidence collapsed overnight. Almost no one was willing to step aboard a giant hydrogen airship again, and within a few years the great passenger Zeppelins had stopped flying for good. The future of long-distance travel passed instead to the aeroplane. The age of the airship — elegant, unhurried, and briefly the height of luxury — had come to an end, and it never returned.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did this particular disaster have such a huge impact on the public?

2. What is the most widely accepted explanation for the fire, and what happened to airship travel afterwards?

1. It was captured on film and radio; millions could watch it happen repeatedly in cinemas, which shattered public trust in airships in a way earlier crashes had not.

2. Most likely a static-electricity spark in the stormy air ignited leaking hydrogen (no single cause was ever proven); passenger airship travel collapsed and the aeroplane took over.

Discuss:
  • The disaster ended an entire technology almost overnight. Can you think of another invention that was abandoned after one dramatic failure? Was that reaction fair?

Grammar — past continuous vs past simple

The Hindenburg story lives or dies on one contrast: the long, calm scene, and the sudden event that tears through it. English marks exactly that contrast with two past tenses. Master the pairing and you can hold a listener in the quiet moment before everything changes.

The two tenses, side by side:
• Past continuous (was/were + -ing) — an action in progress, the background scene that was already going on: "The crowd was waiting below." "The ground crew were reaching for the ropes."
• Past simple — a completed action, often sudden, that interrupts or moves the story forward: "A flame appeared." "The fire raced through the ship."
How they work together:
• Use the past continuous to set the scene — what was happening around the moment.
• Use the past simple for the event that suddenly happened. Words like suddenly, then, at that moment, without warning usually go with the past simple.
• The classic shape: "The airship was approaching the field when a flame appeared." The long action (was approaching) is interrupted by the short one (appeared).

1. Choose the tense

Put the verb in brackets into the past continuous or the past simple.

1. The crowd (wait) on the field when the airship finally came into view. (background action)

2. As the ground crew (haul) the ship down, a flame suddenly (appear) near the tail. (background + sudden event)

3. Within seconds, the fire (spread) the whole length of the hull. (completed event)

1. was waiting (past continuous — the scene in progress)

2. were hauling … appeared (background action + sudden interrupting event)

3. spread (past simple — a completed, sudden action)

2. Join the two clauses

Combine each pair into one sentence using when or while, with one verb in the past continuous and one in the past simple.

1. The reporter / describe the landing — the fire / break out.

2. Thunderstorms / delay the arrival — the passengers / wait for hours.

1. was describing … broke out (long action interrupted by a sudden one)

2. were delaying … were waiting (two long background actions happening at the same time — both past continuous)

3. Build the scene — complete the paragraph

Put each verb into the past continuous or the past simple. Read it aloud once you're done.
