Made Redundant

A meeting turned up in your calendar this morning with no agenda on it. You're about to be told your job has gone — and the twenty minutes that follow are the ones you'll be living off for the next six months.

The Scene 🎬

It's a Tuesday, about half ten. The meeting room is the small one by the lifts, the one nobody ever books. There's a jug of water on the table that somebody has thoughtfully put there, and a printed sheet lying face down in front of the woman sitting opposite you. She turns it over, and you work out what the room is for about two seconds before she starts speaking.

Here's the bit nobody warns you about. You will be winded. Your ears will work and your brain won't, and the words coming across the table — restructure, at risk, consultation, enhanced terms — will sail straight past you while you sit there nodding along. Then in a fortnight, at your kitchen table, trying to work out what you're owed and whether anyone will speak for you, the only thing you'll have is whatever you managed to ask for in this room.

So today isn't about dignity, or grace, or having the right feelings about it. It's smaller and more useful than that. It's being able to say four or five plain sentences while you're in shock, so that the version of you who wakes up tomorrow has something to work with.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of a time someone told you something big and unexpected — good or bad. How much of what they said afterwards do you honestly remember? What does that tell you about how far you can trust yourself in a room like this one?
  2. When you're thrown, what's your default in English — do you go quiet and polite, do you go very formal, or do you fill the silence? Be honest, because whatever it is, it'll turn up today.

Who's who

You
The One Being Let Go 📦

Eleven years here. You've done nothing wrong and it's happening anyway. You've got twenty minutes and a head full of noise, and your only job is to leave the room knowing more than you did walking into it.

The other voice
Elaine Marsh · HR Director 📄

Reading carefully from a script she didn't write and doesn't much like. She isn't your enemy, and she isn't your friend either. Ask her a straight question and she'll mostly give you a straight answer — but she won't volunteer a single thing you don't ask for.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't follow the news — you already know the news. Watch how the speaker handles being winded: where he stops the room, what he asks first, and how he keeps putting short, flat, unheroic questions while he's plainly nowhere near alright.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Notice that almost nothing the speaker says is clever. It's short, it's a bit clumsy, and it does the job.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Elaine had already promised to email everything through — and yet in his very last turn the speaker asks for it in writing all over again. Why do it twice? What's the second one for?

2. Before he asks anything about money, dates or who else is going, he asks whether it's the role or him. What does the answer to that actually change for him?

3. Elaine says she's already had one of these this morning and it didn't go well, then tells him to say what he wants to say. She's braced for something. What does he give her instead — and what does he get out of doing it that way?

1. The first one is him asking for the email, which she agrees to; the second one puts it last, so it's the thing she carries out of the room with her. It also costs him nothing to repeat, because he's already said out loud that he won't remember any of this by the evening — and once you've admitted that, asking twice looks like sense rather than distrust.
2. It tells him whether he was judged or simply unlucky, and that changes what he does next: if the whole layer has gone then there's nothing about him to argue with, whereas if he'd been picked out of a group doing the same work, somebody made a choice, and a choice can be questioned during the consultation. It also decides how he tells the story to the next employer.
3. He gives her a flat promise not to make a scene, and then asks for two things in the same breath. Because she was braced for a row and doesn't get one, she's relieved, and the two requests land on somebody who now rather wants to be helpful — which is how he ends up with a reference from the person who has just made him redundant.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten sentences for a room you can't think straight in. They're deliberately short, because long ones fall apart when you're shaking. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it — which today means flat. All of these work better said slowly, with very little music in the voice, because you're not performing being fine and you're not performing being wronged either. A short question in a level voice is hard to brush off, whereas the same question asked brightly invites a bright non-answer and asked angrily invites a defensive one. And leave the silence alone afterwards. Let her fill it.

🧱 Getting your feet back under you

Phrase 1

She's said the sentence. You heard every word and absolutely none of it went in. You need her to say it again — but you're not deaf, and you don't want to pretend you followed it either. Which line does that?

A. "Right. Yes. Understood."
B. "Sorry — can you say that again? I don't think I took it in."
C. "Sorry, could you speak up a bit?"
B — "Sorry — can you say that again? I don't think I took it in."
To take something in means to absorb it and understand it, which is a different thing from hearing it, and that difference is the whole point here. C asks her to be louder, when volume was never the problem, so you'll get the same sentence again at the same useless speed. A is what most people actually say, and it's a disaster, because you've just told her you understood and she'll move on to the next paragraph. B is honest about what's happening in your head, and it buys you the sentence a second time, when you might be ready for it.
Phrase 2

It's landed. You need the room to stop for a bit — not for a coffee, not for a walk outside, just for a few seconds while your head catches up. Say it out loud now: four words, no apology, and no explaining why you need it.

"Give me a second."
It's an instruction rather than a request, which is exactly why it works — "would it be alright if I took a moment?" hands her the decision, whereas this simply tells her what's about to happen and she hasn't really got an option. It looks blunt written down, but in the room it isn't, because you're both perfectly aware of what has just been said. The thing to resist is explaining yourself afterwards, since the four words are complete, and whatever you add is you looking after her feelings at the one moment you should be looking after your own.

🔎 Finding out what this actually is

Phrase 3

She's used a lot of careful language and you genuinely can't tell whether this has already happened or is still being discussed. Everything else depends on the answer. Put it in order:

or aIs thisconsultation?a decision,
"Is this a decision, or a consultation?"
The careful language exists so that this question doesn't get asked, which is why asking it early tells her you're still functioning and she can stop reading at you. A consultation is a period where the company is formally supposed to be listening before it decides, while a decision has already been taken and you're being informed of it — and because those two need completely different behaviour from you over the next month, you can't afford to leave the room unsure which one you're in.
Phrase 4

Everyone in the building is about to tell you it's nothing personal. You want to know if that's true — whether the job itself has been abolished, or whether people doing your job still exist here and you were the one chosen. Which line is most likely to get you a straight answer?

A. "So am I being sacked, or what?"
B. "Just so I'm clear — is it the role that's going, or is it me?"
C. "Why have you chosen me and not somebody else?"
B — "Just so I'm clear — is it the role that's going, or is it me?"
C is the trap, and a tempting one, because it feels like the brave question — but it has already assumed the answer, so all she can do is defend a choice you've accused her of making, and you'll learn nothing you didn't walk in believing. A gets you a flat correction about the word and not much else. B lays the two possibilities side by side and asks her to point at one, which is much harder to slide out of than a question with an accusation buried in it, and "just so I'm clear" leaves her a way to answer that isn't a confrontation.
Phrase 5

She's said "over the coming weeks" and "in due course" twice each now. You want dates — the real ones, not the comfortable version. Put it in order:

here?timelineWhat's theactual
"What's the actual timeline here?"
Everything hangs off this — when the money stops, when you tell your family, when you start looking, whether there's a summer in the middle of it. The word doing the work is actual, which quietly says you've noticed the vagueness and you'd like whatever's underneath it, while stopping short of calling her evasive, so she can just give you the dates and you can both carry on as though the word wasn't there.

💷 The questions you'll wish you'd asked

Phrase 6

Money. There's the notice, the redundancy pay, the holiday you never took. You're not asking her to be generous — you're asking what your contract and the law already say is yours by right. Complete the chunk: "What am I ___ ___?"

"What am I entitled to?"
To be entitled to something is to have a right to it, and that's why this is the question rather than "how much will I get" — one asks what's owed to you and the other asks what she feels like offering, and they won't get you the same answer. It also moves the conversation onto ground where she has to be accurate, because entitlements are written down somewhere and she knows you can go and read them. People skip this one out of embarrassment and regret it inside a fortnight.
Phrase 7

She's just said several numbers at you and you've retained none of them. You want it on paper — not because you distrust her, but because your memory has left the building. Complete the chunk: "Would you put that ___ ___ for me?"

"Would you put that in writing for me?"
Say it the moment any number is mentioned, and say it every time. It reads as practical rather than suspicious, particularly if you add the reason — that you won't remember — which is both true and impossible to take offence at. There's a quieter benefit too, which is that things written down tend to stay the shape they were, whereas things said in a small room by the lifts on a Tuesday have a way of turning out to have been slightly different.
Phrase 8

You want to know whether it's just you or half the floor. She may well not be allowed to tell you, and you'd rather not make her refuse you. Which line is most likely to get you something?

A. "Who else are you letting go?"
B. "Am I allowed to ask who else?"
C. "Come on — I'll find out this afternoon anyway."
B — "Am I allowed to ask who else?"
It asks permission to ask, rather than asking, and that small step backwards changes what she has to do: instead of refusing you, she gets to explain where her line is, and people explaining their line will very often hand over the part that sits on your side of it — a number, a team, an "it isn't only you". A forces a straight no. C turns it into a fight she wins by saying nothing, and you'll have spent the last of your goodwill on it. Notice how much of this room is about not backing her into corners.

🚪 Leaving with something

Phrase 9

You'll need someone to speak for you, and the woman across the table is senior enough for it to count. You want the warm kind, not the bare confirmation that you existed here. Put it in order:

a reference —Would youa proper one?give me
"Would you give me a reference — a proper one?"
Companies will often only confirm your dates and your job title, so a proper one is doing something specific: it means the kind that says what you were actually like to work with, and those two words mark the difference without you having to spell it out. Ask now, in this room, while she's feeling exactly how she's feeling. In three weeks you'll be a name in her inbox and it'll be a form somebody else fills in.
Phrase 10

She's braced. She's had one of these already today and it went badly, and she's waiting for you to shout or cry. You're going to do neither — but you're not walking out with nothing either. Say the two sentences out loud: one that promises her calm, one that asks for the paperwork anyway.

"I'm not going to make a scene. I would like the details in writing, though."
To make a scene is to lose your temper in front of other people, and naming it is what makes this work, because the moment you say you're not going to do it, the thing she's been tensed against all morning stops being in the room. Then comes "though", which is really the whole sentence — it quietly says that being calm isn't the same as being finished with you, and that she doesn't get the relief without the paperwork. Two flat sentences, and you leave with the thing you came for.

The Twist

🎯 She stops reading

You're back in your own chair, and you've got your plan: ask the questions, take the letter, go home, work it out at the kitchen table with someone sensible. Then Elaine turns the sheet face down and says: "Off the record. There's a job going in Priya's team. It's two grades down and it's yours if you want it — but I need to know before you leave this room, because I've got someone else in here at twelve. If you go through the consultation instead, you'll probably come out with the money. Probably. I can't promise that and I'm not going to." Start the timer. You don't get the kitchen table, you don't get the sensible person, and you don't get to say you'll think about it. Talk it through with her out loud and decide — and whichever way you go, get something in writing before you stand up.

  • I found out whether my role had gone or whether I'd been picked — before anything else.
  • I asked about dates and about money out loud, without apologising for asking.
  • I got at least one thing promised in writing.
  • I stayed calm and still asked for what I wanted — being calm didn't cost me the asking.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one to run again. Do the twist a second time and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Nobody is at their best in that room, and it's worth knowing that beforehand, because the fantasy version — where you say the devastating thing and leave with your coat over your arm — has never once happened to anybody.

What happens is that you sit there feeling oddly polite while your working life is described in the passive voice, and you thank her, and you leave, and about four hours later you think of everything you should have asked.

Which is the whole reason for having a handful of short sentences ready. Not so you can win it. So that the twenty minutes produce something — dates, a figure, an email, a reference — rather than just a feeling you'll be carrying about all summer.

Two bigger questions

  1. We tend to judge people by how they behaved in the worst twenty minutes of their week, and to admire the ones who were dignified about it. Is that fair? What does the pressure to take bad news gracefully quietly cost the person taking it — and whose interests does that pressure actually serve?
  2. Elaine didn't make this decision and can't undo it, but she's the one in the room and the only face you'll ever have for it. Is she a person doing an unpleasant job as decently as she can, or is being that face part of how the thing gets done at all? Could both be true at once?
