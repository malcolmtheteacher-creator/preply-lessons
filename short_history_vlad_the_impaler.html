The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A prince raised in a dangerous world

He was born around 1431, most likely in Transylvania, and given the name Vlad. His father belonged to a knightly order called the Order of the Dragon — in the old Romanian language, Dracul, "the dragon" — and so the son became known as Dracula, "son of the dragon". Long before the vampire, the name simply meant that.

Vlad's homeland was Wallachia, a small principality in what is now southern Romania. It sat in a perilous position, caught between two giants: the Christian kingdom of Hungary to the north and, to the south, the vast and expanding Ottoman Empire. To survive, Wallachia's princes had to balance carefully between these powers — and the throne changed hands constantly, often violently.

As a boy, Vlad and his younger brother were handed over to the Ottoman court as hostages, a guarantee of their father's loyalty. He spent formative years in captivity, learning the language, the tactics and the politics of the very empire he would one day fight. When his father and older brother were killed in the vicious power struggles back home, Vlad emerged from those years hardened, watchful, and determined to take — and hold — the throne that was his by blood.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did the name "Dracula" originally come from, and what did it mean?

2. Why was Vlad's homeland, Wallachia, in such a dangerous position?

1. From his father's membership of the Order of the Dragon — Dracul meant "the dragon", so Vlad became Dracula, "son of the dragon". It had nothing to do with vampires.

2. Wallachia was a small principality caught between the Christian kingdom of Hungary and the expanding Ottoman Empire, and its throne changed hands often and violently.

Discuss:
  • Vlad spent his childhood as a hostage in the court of the empire he later fought. How might years of captivity shape the ruler a person becomes?

Part 2 — The prince who defied an empire

When Vlad finally secured the throne of Wallachia, he ruled a country weakened by feuding noblemen and threatened on every side. He set about crushing opposition at home and refused to pay the tribute that the Ottoman Sultan, Mehmed II — the same sultan who had conquered Constantinople — demanded of him. It was an act of open defiance from a tiny state against the most powerful empire of the age.

In 1462 Mehmed marched north with an enormous army to punish him. Vlad could not win a battle of numbers, so he waged a ruthless campaign of a different kind. He burned his own crops and villages so the invaders would find no food, poisoned wells, and struck at night in swift, terrifying raids. His most infamous tactic was psychological: he cultivated a fearsome reputation for punishing enemies and traitors without mercy, and he used the sheer horror of that reputation as a weapon. According to the chronicles, when the Ottoman army advanced on his capital, it came upon a grim field of the executed — a sight so dreadful that, some accounts say, even the battle-hardened Sultan was shaken and turned back.

It is here that history and legend blur. Vlad became known as Vlad Țepeș — "the Impaler" — from the method of execution most associated with his name. We should treat the darkest stories with real caution: many were spread by his enemies and by early printers who found that lurid tales sold well. What is clear is that he was, even by the harsh standards of his time, feared as a merciless ruler — and that this very fearsomeness was, for a while, the shield of his small country.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Vlad provoke the Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II, and how did he fight such a huge army?

2. Where does the name "the Impaler" come from, and why should we be cautious about the darkest stories?

1. He refused to pay the tribute Mehmed demanded. Unable to win by numbers, he fought a ruthless campaign — burning crops and villages, poisoning wells, night raids, and using his fearsome reputation as a weapon.

2. From the method of execution most associated with him (Țepeș = "the Impaler"). Many of the worst stories were spread by his enemies and by early printers who profited from shocking tales, so they may be exaggerated.

Discuss:
  • Vlad turned his own frightening reputation into a weapon of war. Can fear alone protect a country — and what are the limits of ruling that way?

Part 3 — From history into legend

Vlad's triumph over Mehmed did not last. Betrayed by allies and imprisoned for years in Hungary, he regained his throne only briefly before he was killed in battle, probably in the winter of 1476–77. In Romania, memory of him is complicated: to some he is a national hero who stood up to a mighty empire and imposed order on a lawless land; to others he is a cruel tyrant. Both readings have survived for over five hundred years.

But his afterlife took an extraordinary turn. In 1897, the Irish author Bram Stoker published a novel about a blood-drinking Transylvanian count. Stoker borrowed the resonant name — Dracula — and a few scraps of half-remembered history, and wove them into something entirely new. His Count Dracula is not a portrait of the real prince; it is a work of Gothic imagination, drawing on folklore, other vampire tales, and the eerie reputation of the Carpathian mountains. Yet the name stuck, and it immortalised Vlad in a way no chronicle ever could.

And so we are left with two figures who share one name. There is the historical Vlad III — a real, complex, ferocious 15th-century ruler fighting for the survival of his principality. And there is Count Dracula — a fictional monster who has never died on screen or page. To understand either properly, we have to disentangle them: to separate the prince from the myth, and to ask why the world remembers the legend far more vividly than the man.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How is Vlad remembered in Romania, and why is that memory "complicated"?

2. What exactly did Bram Stoker take from Vlad — and what did he invent?

1. To some he is a national hero who defied a great empire and brought order; to others he is a cruel tyrant. Both views have survived for over five centuries.

2. Stoker borrowed the name Dracula and a few fragments of history, but Count Dracula — the blood-drinking vampire — is a Gothic invention drawn from folklore and other vampire tales, not a portrait of the real prince.

Discuss:
  • The world remembers the fictional vampire far more vividly than the real prince. Why do legends so often overtake the historical people behind them?

Grammar — Word formation (prefixes & suffixes)

The story of Vlad is full of built words: fear becomes fearsome and fearless; power becomes powerful and empower; mercy becomes merciless. English constantly builds whole families of words from a single root by adding pieces to the front (prefixes) or the end (suffixes). Master this and your vocabulary grows in leaps rather than steps.

Prefixes change the meaning — often to the opposite:
• un-, in-, im-, dis-, mis- reverse or negate: loyal → disloyal, merciful → unmerciful, possible → impossible, trust → mistrust.
• em-/en- often means "to give / to make": power → empower, rich → enrich, danger → endanger.
• over-, out- add "too much" or "beyond": power → overpower, number → outnumber.
Suffixes usually change the word class:
• -ful / -less make adjectives (with / without): fear → fearful / fearless, power → powerful, mercy → merciless.
• -some, -ous make adjectives meaning "causing / full of": fear → fearsome, fame → famous.
• -ity, -ness, -ment, -ion make nouns: captive → captivity, ruthless → ruthlessness, punish → punishment, invade → invasion.
• -ise/-ize, -en make verbs: mortal → immortalise, hard → harden.
