Off to a Great Start

Creating Memorable Presentation Openings — C1 Level

What Makes a Beginning Memorable?

Research shows that audiences remember information from the beginning and end of presentations most clearly. This is called the recency and primacy effect.

Your opening is your opportunity to capture attention, establish credibility, and set the tone for everything that follows.

Think about presentations you have attended or given. What made the beginning memorable or forgettable?

Take a moment to consider your experience. In this lesson, we'll explore techniques that turn opening statements into powerful first impressions.

The Structure of Effective Openings

A well-structured opening typically includes three key elements:

"Today I'd like to give you information about the new factory we will be moving to. I'll be explaining the improved facilities and how they will help us with our jobs and answer any concerns or questions you have to help you feel positive about our new environment."

Identify the Structure

What parts of opening structure can you find in this extract?

The Three Elements:

  • Announcement: "Today I'd like to give you information about..."
  • Outline/Purpose: "I'll be explaining the improved facilities and how they will help us..."
  • Engagement/Reassurance: "...answer any concerns or questions you have..."

Positive & Inclusive Language

Look at the same extract again. How many positive adjectives can you find?

Positive adjectives found:

  • new (factory, environment)
  • improved (facilities)
  • positive (about our new environment)

What do you notice? The speaker uses forward-looking, optimistic language that helps the audience feel reassured and included in the conversation. This is positive and inclusive language — it builds rapport and goodwill from the very start.

Hooking Techniques: Capturing Attention

A "hook" is an opening technique that grabs your audience's attention and makes them want to listen. Here's an example:

"Do you know how many countries the British Council has worked in since it started in 1934?... The British Council now works in more than 100 countries. That's more than half of the countries that are in the whole world."

Which Hooking Techniques Are Used Here?

Choose the two techniques you can identify in this opening:

Techniques used:

  1. Rhetorical Question: "Do you know how many countries...?" — This engages the audience directly and makes them think.
  2. Surprising Statistic: "More than 100 countries... more than half of the countries in the whole world." — This is unexpected and memorable.

(A third technique visible here is the historical reference — mentioning "since it started in 1934" — but the two main hooks are the rhetorical question and the surprising statistic.)

Common Hooking Techniques

Rhetorical Question
A question asked for effect, not expecting an answer. It engages the audience and makes them think. Example: "Have you ever wondered why...?"
Surprising Statistic
An unexpected number or fact that challenges assumptions. Example: "Did you know that 80% of..." Makes the audience sit up and listen.
Historical Reference
Mentioning relevant history or context. Example: "Since our company started in 1985..." Creates credibility and perspective.
Personal Story
A brief, relevant anecdote that connects emotionally with the audience. Example: "Three years ago, I was where you are now..."
Striking Statement
A bold or thought-provoking claim that demands attention. Example: "Everything you think you know about this is wrong."

Your Opening Statement

Draft your opening. Consider:

  • Which hooking technique(s) will you use? (rhetorical question, surprising statistic, personal story, etc.)

Feedback for Your Partner

As a listener, when your partner presents their opening, note:

  • Which hooking techniques did they use?
  • What opening structure elements were present?
  • How effectively did they capture your attention?
  • What advice would you give to make it even more memorable?

Try these sentence starters when giving feedback:

  • "I was immediately interested when you..."
  • "The most memorable part was..."
  • "You could strengthen this by..."
  • "I'd suggest adding... because..."
  • "This opening made me want to listen because..."

Key Takeaway: The beginning of your presentation is not just an introduction — it's your first chance to build trust, spark curiosity, and establish why your audience should care. Whether you use a rhetorical question, a surprising statistic, or a personal story, the goal is the same: make them want to listen.
