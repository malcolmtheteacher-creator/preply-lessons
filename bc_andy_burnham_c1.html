Andy Burnham & the Power of Cities

C1 Level · Speaking & Discussion Focus

Who runs your city?

Most of us can name the leader of our country. Far fewer can name the person who actually runs the place we live — who decides where the buses go, how the housing money is spent, how the police are held to account. Today we look at one figure who has made that local job unusually visible.

Warm up — talk it through:

Who has real power over your daily life — the national government, or your city and region? Where do you notice each one?

Can you name the mayor or leader of your city or region? What are they actually responsible for?

Think of a leader — in any field — who you genuinely respect. What is it that makes them a leader, and not just a boss?

A phrase to weigh up

When a regional leader publicly stood up to the national government, some newspapers nicknamed him “the King of the North.” What do you imagine a person has to do to earn — or attract — a nickname like that?

A note on this
We will meet the moment behind that nickname in a moment. Notice that it can be read two ways: as admiration (a bold defender of his region) or as mockery (a local politician acting grander than he is). Keep both readings in mind — today is about discussing a real, living figure fairly.

The Story

Read the passage, then work through the questions. The aim is not to memorise dates but to have something worth arguing about.

Andy Burnham was born near Liverpool in 1970 and grew up in a working-class family in the north-west of England. He studied English at Cambridge, entered Parliament as a Labour MP in 2001, and rose through government to become Health Secretary. Twice he ran to lead his party; twice he lost. For a while, his career looked like a familiar story of near-misses in Westminster.

Then, in 2017, he did something unusual for an ambitious politician: he left national politics to run a city-region. He became the first elected Mayor of Greater Manchester — a “metro mayor” with real, if limited, power over transport, housing and policing for nearly three million people. Devolution had given the north of England a new kind of leader, and Burnham set out to make the role matter.

The moment that made him nationally famous came in October 2020. As the government prepared to place Greater Manchester under tough pandemic restrictions, Burnham argued that the financial support on offer for local workers and businesses was simply not enough. He held a press conference in the open air and refused to back down — and, in a piece of political theatre, the exact sum the government was offering was reportedly announced to journalists while he was still speaking. To his supporters, he had stood up for his region against a distant government; to his critics, he was grandstanding for the cameras. The newspapers reached for a nickname: “the King of the North.”

There is an older thread to his story, too. As a lifelong Liverpool supporter, Burnham spent years pressing for the truth about the 1989 Hillsborough football disaster, in which 97 fans were unlawfully killed; he is widely credited with helping to force the independent review that finally reopened the case. Whatever one makes of his politics, that campaign points to the question at the heart of this lesson: what is a leader actually for — to climb, or to represent?

1. Why was leaving Westminster to run a city-region an unusual choice?

Show Answer
Ambitious politicians usually see national politics as the top. Choosing a regional, local role instead ran against that expectation — it treated a city-region as a place to lead, not a step down from Parliament.

2. The passage gives two readings of the 2020 stand-off. What are they?

Show Answer
Supporters saw a leader bravely defending his region against a distant national government; critics saw a politician “grandstanding” — performing for the cameras. The lesson deliberately holds both, rather than deciding for you.

Now argue it out:

Is a leader who publicly defies the national government being brave, or simply putting on a show? Can it be both at once?

Should power sit close to people, in cities and regions — or is a strong central government fairer, because it can treat every area the same?

“What is a leader for — to climb, or to represent?” Which leaders you know of have chosen which?

Saying It Fairly

Talking about a living politician is a test of good English: you want to report what people say and think without stating contested opinions as if they were facts. These are the tools that let you do it — the language of attributing and distancing.

1. Attribute the claim — don't own it

Put the opinion in someone else's mouth, clearly, so you stay neutral.

  • Supporters argue that he defended his region; critics counter that he was performing.
  • He is widely regarded as a strong communicator.
  • According to his opponents, the stand-off achieved little.
  • He is often credited with helping to reopen the case.

Your turn: attribute it

Turn this bald opinion into a fair, attributed one: “He is the best leader the region has had.”

Show a model
“Many of his supporters regard him as the best leader the region has had.” (Anything that names whose opinion it is — supporters / critics / commentators say… — does the job.)

2. Distance yourself from unproven facts

When you are not certain something is true, mark it — don't assert it.

  • The figure was reportedly announced while he was still speaking.
  • He is said to have considered leaving politics altogether.
  • The deal, it is claimed, was worth far less than the region needed.
  • Allegedly, no warning was given.

Spot the difference

What changes between “The government offered too little” and “Critics claim the government offered too little”?

Show Answer
The first states a contested judgement as plain fact. The second reports it as someone's position — you pass on the claim without endorsing it. In discussion of politics, the second is almost always the safer, more credible choice.

3. Reporting verbs carry an attitude

The verb you choose quietly tells the listener how much you believe it. Match each verb to what it signals.

Reporting verb

1. He admitted that…
2. He claimed that…
3. He pointed out that…

What it signals

a. The speaker treats what follows as a true, relevant fact.
b. It was something awkward to confess.
c. Doubt — you are not sure it is true.
Show Answers
1–b (admitted = a difficult confession), 2–c (claimed = the speaker signals doubt), 3–a (pointed out = treated as an established fact). Same news, three different attitudes.

Task 1 — The balanced view

Power close to people

Local leaders know the place; decisions fit real needs; easier to hold to account.

Power at the centre

One nation treated equally; no rich regions racing ahead of poor ones; a single clear voice.
