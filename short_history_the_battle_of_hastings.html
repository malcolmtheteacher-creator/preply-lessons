The Battle of Hastings

One October day in 1066 that decided who would rule England — and reshaped the language you are reading now.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of the single day that ended one dynasty and began another in England — and you'll master narrative tenses and time clauses, the tools that let you say exactly when one event cut across another.
Warm up — a few words worth carrying with you. These are ideas any historian of 1066 leans on. Know it already? Say what it means and use it in a sentence of your own. New to you? Even better — it's a useful word to add today. Reveal to check, then use each one out loud before we begin.
a throne
What does it mean? Use it to talk about who rules a country.
a throne (n) — the ceremonial chair of a king or queen; by extension, royal power itself ("to seize the throne").
an heir
What does it mean? Use it about the person who comes next in a family or a business.
an heir (n) — the person legally entitled to inherit a title, property or throne when someone dies. (The "h" is silent.)
to claim
What does it mean? Use it about someone who says a thing is rightfully theirs.
to claim (v) — to state firmly that you have a right to something, such as a crown or a piece of land.
to conquer
What does it mean? Use it about taking control of a country by force.
to conquer (v) — to take control of a land or people by military force. (William became known as "the Conqueror".)

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. When the old king died with no son of his own, several powerful men each announced that the crown was theirs — and the succession was suddenly in dispute.
succession (n) — the process or order by which one person follows another into a throne, title or office.
2. He gathered his fleet on the coast — hundreds of ships lined up on the sand, waiting for the wind to change so they could cross the sea.
a fleet (n) — a large group of ships acting together, especially for war or transport.
3. The men who fought on foot with spears and swords, rather than on horseback, made up most of the infantry.
infantry (n) — soldiers who fight on foot, as opposed to cavalry (mounted soldiers).
4. The soldiers locked their shields edge to edge to form a solid shield wall, a barrier no rider could easily break through.
a shield wall (n) — a defensive line in which soldiers stand shoulder to shoulder and overlap their shields.
5. Exhausted after a long march, the tired army struggled to hold its ranks — the neat, ordered lines that kept the soldiers together.
ranks (n, plural) — the ordered rows or lines in which soldiers stand or move; "to break ranks" is to leave that formation.
6. The attackers pretended to run away in a feigned retreat, drawing their enemies out of position before turning to strike.
a feigned retreat (n phr) — a false, pretended withdrawal used as a trick to lure the enemy into a weaker position.
7. As the sun began to set, the exhausted line finally gave way, and what had been an ordered defence collapsed into chaos.
to give way (phr v) — to stop resisting and break or collapse under pressure.
8. The new ruler ordered stone strongholds to be built across the country, so that a small force could control the land around each one.
a stronghold (n) — a strongly defended, fortified place that is hard for an enemy to capture. (Also figurative: "a stronghold of support".)
9. Weavers recorded the whole campaign in a long embroidered tapestry, stitching the ships, the horses and the battle into cloth.
a tapestry (n) — a heavy cloth with pictures or scenes woven or embroidered into it. (The Bayeux Tapestry tells this story.)
10. The victory marked a turning point — a watershed after which the country's language, laws and rulers were never quite the same again.
a watershed (n) — a moment or event that marks an important dividing line, after which things change decisively.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A crown with three claimants

On 5 January 1066, King Edward the Confessor died in London, leaving no children behind him. For a kingdom, that was dangerous: with no clear heir, the succession was open to argument. The very next day, the English council crowned Harold Godwinson, the most powerful nobleman in England, as king. But two other men believed the throne was rightfully theirs.

Across the Channel, Duke William of Normandy was furious. He claimed that Edward had promised him the crown years earlier, and — more damaging still — that Harold himself had once sworn an oath to support that claim. Far to the north, a third contender, King Harald Hardrada of Norway, was also preparing to fight for England. While Harold watched the southern coast, waiting for William, the danger arrived first from the opposite direction.

In September, the Norwegians landed in the north. Harold marched his army the length of the country at astonishing speed and crushed the invaders at Stamford Bridge. He had won a great victory. But as his exhausted soldiers rested, a messenger arrived with the news he had been dreading: while he was fighting in the north, the wind had finally changed in the south, and William's fleet had crossed the sea.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the death of Edward the Confessor such a problem?

2. What had Harold just done in the north when he heard that William had landed?

1. Edward died with no children and no clear heir, so several powerful men each claimed the throne, making the succession a matter of dispute.

2. He had marched north and defeated a Norwegian invasion at Stamford Bridge; while he was doing so, William's fleet crossed the Channel and landed in the south.

Discuss:
  • Harold faced two invasions from opposite ends of the country at almost the same time. How much of what followed do you think was bad luck, and how much was poor planning?

Part 2 — The long day at Senlac Hill

Harold turned his weary army around and marched south again, covering hundreds of miles in a matter of days. On the morning of 14 October 1066, the two sides met on a ridge near Hastings. Harold's men took the higher ground and locked their shields into a dense shield wall along the top of the hill. Below them, William had cavalry, archers and infantry — a more varied army, but one that had to attack uphill against a wall of shields.

For most of the day, the English line held. Wave after wave of Norman attacks broke against the shield wall and fell back down the slope. At one point a rumour spread through William's army that the duke himself had been killed, and his men began to flee. William reportedly lifted his helmet so his soldiers could see his face and know he was still alive, and rallied them to turn back.

That moment seems to have taught the Normans a lesson. Twice more, groups of William's horsemen pulled back as if beaten — a feigned retreat — and each time, some of the English broke ranks to chase them down the hill. Once those men had left the safety of the wall, the Norman riders wheeled around and cut them off. Slowly, gap by gap, the great shield wall began to thin.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What advantage did Harold's army have at the start of the battle?

2. How did the feigned retreats help William break the English line?

1. Harold held the higher ground on the ridge and formed a strong shield wall, forcing the Normans to attack uphill.

2. When the Norman horsemen pretended to flee, some English soldiers broke ranks to chase them; the riders then turned and cut them off, weakening the shield wall.

Discuss:
  • A single false rumour nearly lost William the battle, and a clever trick helped him win it. What does the day tell you about how much battles turn on nerve and information rather than pure strength?

Part 3 — The Conqueror and the world he made

As the light faded, the English line finally gave way. King Harold was killed near his banner at the top of the hill — famously, later tradition said an arrow struck him in the eye, though the truth is uncertain. With their king dead and their wall broken, the surviving English scattered into the dusk. William had won.

Winning the battle was not the same as winning the country. William marched on London, and on Christmas Day 1066 he was crowned king in Westminster Abbey. Over the years that followed, he broke repeated English resistance and secured his hold on the land, ordering a network of castles to be built so that a Norman minority could control a far larger English population. He also commissioned the Domesday Book, a vast survey recording who owned what across the whole kingdom.

The long echo of that October day is still audible today. A French-speaking ruling class settled over an English-speaking country, and over centuries the two tongues fused — which is why English now carries thousands of French-derived words alongside its older Germanic core. The Bayeux Tapestry, embroidered soon after, still lets us follow the whole story stitch by stitch. Few single days have divided a nation's history so cleanly into "before" and "after": 1066 remains the great watershed of English history.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Winning the battle wasn't the same as winning the country. What did William do to secure his control?

2. Why is 1066 described as a "watershed", especially for the English language?

1. He was crowned in London, then put down English resistance, ordered castles built across the country, and commissioned the Domesday Book survey of who owned what.

2. A French-speaking Norman ruling class settled over English-speaking England, and the two languages gradually fused, giving English thousands of French-derived words; it split English history into "before" and "after".

Discuss:
  • The battle lasted a single day, yet its effects on England lasted centuries. Why do you think some short events cast such a long shadow over history?

Grammar — narrative tenses & time clauses

This is a story of things happening while other things were already under way: Harold fights in the north while William crosses the sea; the wall holds until it suddenly breaks. To tell it well, you need to control two tenses and the little words that link one moment to another.

The two narrative tenses, side by side:
• Past simple — the main, completed events that move the story forward, one after another: "Edward died. The council crowned Harold. William crossed the sea."
• Past continuous (was/were + -ing) — a longer background action already in progress when something else happened: "While Harold was fighting in the north, the wind changed." The past continuous sets the scene; the past simple is the event that cuts across it.
Time clauses — linking the moments:
• when — one thing happens, then (often) another: "When Edward died, the throne was empty."
• while / as — two things happen at the same time (often one continuous): "While he was resting, a messenger arrived." "As the sun set, the line gave way."
• by the time — one action was already complete before another point (often with the past perfect): "By the time Harold reached the coast, William had built a camp."
Note: in a time clause we use a past form, never would — "When he arrived…", not "When he would arrive…".

1. Past simple or past continuous?

Put the verb in brackets into the correct narrative tense.

1. While Harold (fight) in the north, William's fleet crossed the Channel.

2. The two armies (meet) on a ridge near Hastings on 14 October.

3. As the Norman horsemen (pretend) to flee, some English soldiers left the shield wall.

1. was fighting (a longer background action in progress)

2. met (a single completed event that moves the story on)

3. were pretending (an ongoing action that another event cut across)

2. Choose the time clause

Complete each sentence with when, while, as or by the time. More than one may be possible — pick the best fit for the meaning in brackets.

1. Edward died, England was left without a clear heir. (one event, then the result)

2. Harold marched south, his soldiers were already exhausted. (the second army was tired before he even arrived)

3. the light was fading, the English line finally gave way. (two things happening together)

1. When (one event triggers the next)

2. By the time (one situation was already true before another point)

3. As (two actions unfolding at the same moment; "While" also works)

3. Build the scene — complete the paragraph

Put each verb into the past simple or past continuous, and choose a time word where a gap needs one. Read it aloud once you're done.

Harold (rest) his troops in the north when a messenger (arrive) with terrible news. While his men (recover) from one battle, William (land) in the south. By the time Harold (reach) Hastings, the Normans had already made their camp.

was resting (background) · arrived (the event that cuts across it) · were recovering (ongoing) · landed (completed event) · reached (past simple in the "by the time" clause; the main clause "had already made" is past perfect).

Notice the pattern: past continuous sets the scene, past simple delivers the event, and the time words tell your listener exactly how the moments overlap.
