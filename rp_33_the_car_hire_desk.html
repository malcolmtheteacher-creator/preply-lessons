The Car-Hire Desk

You booked the car in March. Paid in full. It said so in the email. And yet here at the desk, somehow, everything is still for sale — the cover, the upgrade, the tank of fuel. Fifteen minutes between you and the road.

The Scene 🎬

The one counter in travel where the price you paid is treated as an opening offer.

Airport arrivals, holiday finally begun. The car-hire desk is the last obstacle: a queue of pale families, a rack of key fobs, and Dario, who is friendly, quick — and paid partly on what he sells you at this desk.

None of what's coming is a scam, exactly. The extra cover is real cover. The excess — the amount you'd pay yourself before insurance covers the rest — is really a thousand euros. Dario will describe that thousand euros vividly. What he won't mention is that you can decline everything, hand over the card, and drive away with exactly what you booked in March.

Two skills, then: asking what things actually cost and include, before deciding — and saying no twice, warmly, because the first no is never the end of it. Plus one habit that outranks both: walking round the car before you drive it, and getting every scratch in writing.

  1. Why do we find it so hard to say no to someone friendly? What is the friendliness for?
  2. "You'd only pay if something happened." When is peace of mind worth buying — and when is it just fear with a card machine?
YouBooked, paid, confirmation email saved on your phone. Wants the road, not a debate — but won't be rushed past a signature.
DarioCharming, quick, on commission. Every objection you raise, he's heard today already. Respects the customers who ask real questions.
First: read the conversation and watch what You does with every offer: never argues, never gets cross — just asks what it includes, what it costs, and then decides. The questions are the armour.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for any desk that sells extras — car hire, phone shops, gyms, airlines. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The hard-sell has a rhythm: urgency, a story, a discount. Your rhythm is slower — include what? cost what? decided. You never need to be rude, because you never need to be defensive: a person asking precise questions is visibly not a person about to panic-buy.

🧾 Establishing what's already yours

Phrase 1

The job: opening with the booking and the key fact about money. Complete it:

"I've got a booking under Weber — everything should be paid ___ ___."

"I've got a booking under Weber — everything should be paid up front."
Up front = in advance, before arriving. Saying it in your first sentence plants the flag the whole conversation happens around: the buying is finished; today is collection. "Should be" isn't doubt — it's the polite invitation for him to confirm it on screen, out loud.

Take it anywhere: "I've got a booking under ___ — everything should be paid up front." Hotels, tours, restaurants.
Phrase 2

The job: before hearing about the upgrade — find out what you already have. Which question?

A. "Is the basic cover bad?"
B. "What exactly does the basic cover include?"
C. "Do I really need the extra insurance?"
B — "What exactly does the basic cover include?"
A and C both hand the pen to the salesman — you've asked him to characterise the thing he's paid to make sound frightening, and "do I need it?" begs for "yes". B asks for contents, not opinion: a list of facts you can weigh yourself. "Exactly" politely warns him you'll notice what's missing.

Take it anywhere: "What exactly does ___ include?" Before any upgrade of anything, audit what you already own.
Phrase 3

The job: turning a scary story into a number. Complete it:

"And what would I ___ pay if something happened?"

"And what would I actually pay if something happened?"
The salesman's version is a story — the trolley, the war-zone car park, the boom. Actually is the word that ends the story and demands the figure: worst case, in euros, full stop. Once fear becomes a number, you can compare it with another number — eighty-four euros of cover — and decide like an adult.

Take it anywhere: "What would I actually pay if ___?" The question that shrinks every scary pitch to its real size.

🙅 The warm no — twice

Phrase 4

The job: declining the extra — owning the risk cheerfully. Complete it:

"I'll take my ___ with the standard cover, thanks."

"I'll take my chances with the standard cover, thanks."
To take your chances — to accept a risk knowingly. That's what makes this no so hard to argue with: it doesn't claim the risk isn't real, it claims the risk is yours to take. There's no fear in it for the pitch to work on. The "thanks" keeps the whole thing friendly.

Take it anywhere: "I'll take my chances with ___, thanks." Extended warranties, travel add-ons, premium anything.
Phrase 5

The job: the second no — after the discount and the trolley story. Put the pieces in order:

and I'm stillYou've talked mehappy as we are.through it,
"You've talked me through it, and I'm still happy as we are."
The crucial move: you do not repeat your reasons. Reasons are handholds — every one you give, he can argue with. This sentence credits his effort, confirms you heard everything, and closes the door with no handle on his side. Said pleasantly, it ends the sale without ending the warmth.

Take it anywhere: "You've talked me through it, and I'm still happy as we are." The second no, for every salesman who doesn't stop at the first.

🛡️ Paper, fuel, and small print

Phrase 6

The job: the walk-round — nothing undocumented leaves the car park with your name on it. Complete it:

"I'd like these scratches ___ on the ___ before I take it."

"I'd like these scratches marked on the form before I take it."
Not a question — a condition, politely stated. "Before I take it" is the load-bearing phrase: it makes the paperwork part of the handover, not a favour afterwards. Every scratch not on that form is, as far as Saturday's inspection knows, yours. Photos too, with today's date on them.

Take it anywhere: "I'd like ___ marked/noted in writing before I ___." Flats, equipment, anything borrowed that gets inspected on return.
Phrase 7

The job: your copy of the evidence. Complete it:

"Can I get a ___ of that once it's ___?"

"Can I get a copy of that once it's signed?"
Their form, in their filing system, protects them. Your copy protects you — and "once it's signed" means the copy shows the scratches and the signatures, which is the version that wins arguments in August about a bumper you photographed in July.

Take it anywhere: "Can I get a copy of that once it's signed?" Contracts, condition reports, anything you initialled at a counter.
Phrase 8

The job: checking the fuel rule — and naming the fair version yourself. Complete it:

"What's the arrangement for fuel — ___ to ___?"

"What's the arrangement for fuel — full to full?"
Full to full: you collect it full, you return it full, you pay only for what you burned. Knowing the term and saying it first tells the desk you know the fair option exists — which quietly retires the "buy our tank, bring it back empty" pitch, where the unused half-tank is a gift to them.

Take it anywhere: "What's the arrangement for ___?" Fuel, keys, deposits, cleaning — ask for the rule before you're surprised by it.
Phrase 9

The job: finding the flexibility that's never advertised. Complete it:

"Is there any ___ on the return time?"

"Is there any leeway on the return time?"
Leeway = room to bend without breaking; flexibility inside a rule. Deadlines at desks come in two layers — the official one and the real one — and this word is the key to the second layer. Ask it while everyone's friendly and you learn about the twenty-nine minutes. Discover it at 6:15 on Saturday and you're paying for a day.

Take it anywhere: "Is there any leeway on ___?" Deadlines, checkout times, baggage limits, late fees.
Phrase 10

The job: closing the desk — settled, not surrendered. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "That all seems fair — where do I sign?"
Notice the order of the words: the judgement first, then the pen. "That all seems fair" is a small audit passed out loud — you sign because you've checked, not because the queue was sighing. It also leaves the desk on good terms, which matters: Saturday's inspection is done by someone, and better it's someone who remembers you kindly.

Take it anywhere: "That all seems fair — where do I sign?" The close that follows checking, never replaces it.

⚡ The twist

Round 1 again — and just as you're pocketing the keys, Dario beams: "Actually — good news. For you, a free upgrade. The Corsa is small; I can give you the new SUV, automatic, same price. It's a beautiful car."

It's free. It's bigger. He's being nice. And nothing at this desk has been free yet. What do you ask before you say yes — about the excess on the bigger car, the fuel it drinks, and why bay twelve suddenly needs emptying?

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you find out what you already have before hearing what you could buy?
  • Can you turn a frightening story into a worst-case number?
  • Can you say no twice without repeating your reasons?
  • Can you make paperwork a condition, politely — and keep your copy?
  • Do you check gifts for wheels before rolling them into your holiday?

The Echo 🕰️

The Greeks had camped outside Troy for ten years and taken nothing. Then one morning the beach was empty — the army gone, the ships gone, and standing at the gates a magnificent wooden horse, left as a gift. The Trojans debated. One priest, Laocoön, begged them to check it: I fear Greeks, even bearing gifts.

They didn't check. The horse was free, it was flattering, and after ten years everyone badly wanted the war to be over — which is exactly the state of mind free gifts are designed for. They rolled it through the gates themselves. That night the soldiers hidden inside opened Troy from within, and the city that ten years of siege couldn't take was finished by an upgrade nobody had questioned.

Three thousand years on, the horse still turns up wherever things are signed in a hurry — bigger, shinier, free, and needing only your yes. Laocoön's advice still stands, and at a hire desk it costs one question: what does this gift include?
