Look at what Sam and Alex do in four short lines. Five small, easy-to-miss words are doing a lot of work here. Can you spot them? Say what you notice before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "It's no use panicking" — it as an empty subject, just to get the sentence started.
2. "I did that myself" — a reflexive used for emphasis: alone, no one else.
3. "something off" — a compound word (something) with the adjective AFTER it.
4. "there's no point in messing" — there as another empty subject, for something pointless.
5. "another one" — one standing in for "printer" so nobody has to repeat it.

These five small words are everywhere in natural spoken English — and today they're the whole lesson.

It's No Use Worrying

A sentence that starts with "it" — but "it" doesn't mean anything yet. The real information comes after.

Spot the pattern

  • "It's no use complaining about the bus — it happens every day."
  • "It's worth double-checking the address before we set off."
  • "It took nearly an hour to find parking on Saturday."

What is "it" actually pointing at in each one? Say your idea, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Nothing! It here is just a placeholder subject — English sentences need a subject, so "it" fills the slot while the real content (complaining, double-checking, finding parking) comes later in the sentence. This "it" is empty, not the "it" that means a specific thing.
It + is/was + no use + -ing
It + is/was + worth + -ing
It + takes/took + (time) + to + infinitive
One trap: after no use and worth, always use -ing, never "to + infinitive". "It's no use to worry" is wrong — say "It's no use worrying".

Quick check

1. A friend keeps changing his mind and won't listen to reason. Point that out, starting the sentence exactly like this:

asking him twice — he never changes his mind.

2. Your sister keeps stressing about something that's completely out of her hands. Tell her — use the no use pattern with stress:

It's about something you can't control — it won't help.

3. This morning you queued at the post office for what felt like forever. Tell someone about it — use take in the past:

This morning, nearly forty minutes just to post one parcel.

There's No Point in That

Another empty subject — but this time "there", used when something exists, happens, or is pointless.

Spot the pattern

  • "There's no point in arguing with him — he never listens."
  • "There's no need to bring anything, we've got it all covered."
  • "There seems to be a problem with the wifi again."

What comes right after "no point", and what comes right after "no need"? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
no point is followed by in + -ing. no need is followed by to + infinitive. And there seems to be introduces something that appears to exist, without saying who noticed it. "There" isn't a place here — it just lets the sentence begin before the real subject appears.
There's no point in + -ing
There's no need to + infinitive
There seems/seemed to be + noun
One trap: "there is no point to go" is wrong. After no point, always use in + -ing: "there's no point in going".

Quick check

1. A friend keeps ringing the manager, but nothing ever changes. Tell her — use no point in with complain:

There's no point to the manager — he never changes anything.

2. Something wasn't your friend's fault at all, and she keeps saying sorry. Reassure her — use no need with apologise:

There's no need — it really wasn't your fault.

3. You've just noticed something's wrong with a restaurant booking. Say so, out loud first, using seem:

Hang on — to be a mix-up with our table.

  2. Something there's no need to worry about right now…
  3. A situation where "there seems to be a problem with..." fits your day so far…

Something, One, Myself

Three small tools that stop you repeating yourself: compound words that describe things, "one" that replaces a noun, and reflexives that add emphasis.

Compound words + adjective

  • "There's something fishy about that email — don't click the link."
  • "I want nothing complicated for dinner tonight — just toast."
  • "We met someone interesting at the party."

Where does the adjective go, before or after the compound word? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
After. Ordinary nouns put the adjective first ("a fishy story"), but compound words like something/nothing/someone/anyone/everything flip it: the adjective always comes AFTER — something fishy, nothing complicated, someone interesting.
something / nothing / someone / anyone / everything + ADJECTIVE
One trap: putting the adjective first, like "fishy something", is the wrong order. The adjective always comes after the compound word.

1. The new neighbour never says hello and always looks over his shoulder. Describe your first impression — use something + odd:

I noticed about the new neighbour.

One / ones for reference

  • "My old phone broke, so I bought a new one."
  • "Which trainers are you getting — the black ones or the white ones?"

"One" replaces a singular noun already mentioned; "ones" replaces a plural noun — so nobody has to repeat the same word twice.

a/an/the/this/that + ADJECTIVE + one (singular)
these/those/the + ADJECTIVE + ones (plural)

2. Your umbrella broke in the wind this morning. Tell someone what you need to do — without repeating "umbrella":

My umbrella broke in the wind, so I need to buy

Reflexives for emphasis

  • "I fixed the leak myself — I didn't call a plumber."
  • "She wrote the whole report herself, with no help at all."

These reflexives don't mean "to me/her" — they mean "alone, no one else helped". That's different from a true reflexive object like "I hurt myself".

subject + verb + object ... + myself/herself/himself/itself/ourselves/yourselves/themselves  (= alone, no one else)
One trap: "hisself" isn't a word in standard English. The correct form is himself.

3. A neighbour is proud that he didn't need to hire anyone to fix his roof. Say it for him:

He repaired the roof — he didn't hire anyone.

What Do You Remember?

From before — a quick refresher

1. Your street actually has two good cafés on it. Say so out loud, using existential there:

two good cafés on this street, actually.

2. Someone checked their appearance before leaving the house. Use a plain reflexive object:

He looked at in the mirror before leaving.

Say first, then check — fresh situations, no looking back

3. A friend is about to sign a contract without reading the fine print. Warn her — use worth with check:

the small print before you sign anything.

4. You've cooked plenty for everyone. Tell a guest not to bother — use no need with bring:

anything — we've already got plenty of food.

5. He kept insisting everything was fine, so nobody worried. Use nothing + important:

He kept saying was going to happen, so nobody worried.
