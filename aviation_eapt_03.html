The photo task — exactly as the exam sets it

Section 3 is the shortest part of the exam and the easiest to lose marks in. It is not a knowledge test and not a vocabulary test. It is a precision test: six sentences that all sound right, and only two that the picture actually supports.
Before anything is explained — say what comes to mind (45 seconds)

Today: the photo section, so that on the day you can find the two true statements in 45 seconds, five times in a row, without a single guess.

How Section 3 works

WhatThe rule
PhotographsFive. Aircraft, airfields, ground operations, incidents — on the apron, on the runway, in the air, in the water.
StatementsSix per photo, a) to f). Exactly two are correct.
Time45 seconds per photograph. You click your two answers and can change them as often as you like inside the 45 seconds. When the clock ends, the choice is locked.
LengthAbout 5 minutes for the whole section.
ScoringMarked automatically, and it is a pass-or-fail section: zero errors keeps Level 6 open, up to two errors keeps Level 5, up to four keeps Level 4. More than four errors in this section fails the whole test — however good the interview was.
What it testsLanguage structure and vocabulary: prepositions, tenses, quantity words, aircraft and airfield words — read fast and read exactly.
The shape of the trap. Every wrong statement is grammatical, plausible, and wrong about one thing: a position word, a number, a stage, who is doing what, or something the camera simply cannot show. The whole skill of this section is finding that one word in under eight seconds per sentence.

Which two can a camera never prove?

No picture this time — on purpose. Read these six statements about an aircraft on a stand. Two of them describe something that no photograph could ever confirm. Say which two and why, then check.

b) waiting for is an intention inside someone's head. A photo can show people standing near an aircraft; it cannot show what they are waiting for. e) about to take off is the future. A photo shows one frozen moment, never the next one.

The other four are all checkable: a position (beneath, behind), a state (missing), a colour (orange). They may be true or false — but a camera can settle them. In the exam, a statement about intentions, futures, or things outside the frame is almost always one of the four wrong ones.

The five traps — every wrong option is one of these

📍
1 · WHEREA position word is wrong: behind / in front of / beneath / beyond / on the left side / in the foreground.
🔢
2 · HOW MANYA quantity word is wrong: both / all / more than one / no other / three / neither.
⏳
3 · WHAT STAGEThe moment or state is wrong: is landing / has landed / has not touched down yet; extended / retracted; intact / damaged.
🔄
4 · WHO DOES WHATThe actor is swapped: the tug is towing the aircraft vs the aircraft is moving the tug.
🕶

What stage, whose action — the grammar of a frozen moment

A photograph is one instant. The statements test whether you can read which instant: before, during, or after — and who is doing what to whom.

Three verb forms, three different moments

FormWhat it claimsAs the test writes it
Present continuous
is landing, is being towed, are evacuating		Happening now, in the frame. Passive for the thing acted on: is being de-iced, is being marshalled, is being refuelled.“The pushback tug is towing the aircraft” · “The de-icing procedure is in progress”
Present perfect
has landed, has clipped, has been deployed		Finished — and the result is visible. The event is over; you see what it left.“The aircraft has overshot the drainage ditch” · “The wing tip has clipped the truck” · “Chocks have been inserted”
State adjectives
extended / retracted, deployed, deflated, intact, submerged, jammed, shattered, contaminated		A condition you can check by looking.“The main landing gear is retracted” · “Both nacelles are intact” · “The fuselage appears undamaged”
The “not yet” family. has not completed touchdown yet · has yet to be extended · not quite over the threshold · on the verge of / on the brink of taking off · about to — every one of these describes the moment before. They are only true if the picture shows the thing still undone: wheels still in the air, gear still up.

Who does what — the actor swap

Two statements can contain exactly the same words and mean opposite things. Find the verb, then ask: who does it, to whom?

Same picture, opposite claimsWhich is true?
“The pushback tug is towing the aircraft” / “The aircraft is moving the pushback tug”Only the first. The tug has the engine doing the work; the aircraft is passive. “The aircraft is being towed by the tug” says the same as the first.
“The high-wing aircraft is towing the glider” / “The glider is towing the high-wing aircraft”Only the first. Then the test rewrites it: pulling, dragging, tugging, the lead aircraft, the towing plane — five ways to say one relationship, and only the versions with the right actor are true.
“The vehicle has clipped the right engine” / “The impact has damaged the fire truck”Look at what is broken. Damage on the engine, not the truck — only the first.

Hedges and degree words — small, and they decide everything

  • seems / appears / looks — the honest size for a photo: the tyres seem deflated, the fin appears damaged. A hedged statement claims only what a surface can show, so it is often one of the true pair.
  • fairly / quite / rather / significantly / extremely / completely — degree words are killers in disguise. The sky is extremely clear is wrong if there are two clouds. The setting is fairly rural is wrong with a city behind the fence. The fuselage is engulfed in flames is wrong if only the engine is burning.
  • still / already / yet / no longer — they fix the moment: the left engine is still burning needs flames now, not smoke after.

Read the moment — three frozen instants

1. The picture: an aircraft with its wheels about a metre above the runway, nose slightly high, over the painted threshold. Which statement is true?
2. The picture: a small aircraft on its back in a field, wheels pointing at the sky, both wings still attached. Which statement is true?
3. The picture: a tug attached to the nose of a jet, the jet moving backwards away from the terminal. Which statement is true?

Hunt the killer word — three photos, no clock

Exam format, without the pressure: six statements, choose two. After you check, read every wrong option again and find the single word the picture refuses to support. Being able to name the trap is what makes you fast later.

Photo 1 — on the stand

b) attached — WHERE / WHO: the tug is parked beyond the tail, connected to nothing.  d) boarding — NOT IN THE PICTURE: stairs at a door prove nothing about people; the steps are empty.  e) in front of — WHERE: the belt is at the rear cargo door, behind the wing.  f) both…covers — HOW MANY + NOT IN THE PICTURE: one engine is visible, uncovered; the other cannot be seen at all.

True: a) forward door, and the side facing you is the left side (nose to the left); c) the yellow wedges under the nose wheel — a present-perfect result you can see.

Photo 2 — off the end

a) on the runway — WHERE: the runway is on the left; the aircraft has left it and stopped on the grass.  c) smoke — NOT IN THE PICTURE: none, anywhere.  d) rear door — WHERE: the slide hangs from the forward door.  f) still on board — WHAT STAGE: five people are already on the grass, moving away; the slide has been used.

True: b) no nose gear visible and the nose is down — and note the hedge, appears to have, which is exactly the size of claim a photo supports; e) two red appliances, one each side. Attending means present and dealing with it — it does not require hoses.

Photo 3 — over the runway

a) already touched down — WHAT STAGE: there is clear air between the wheels and the runway.  b) not been extended — WHAT STAGE: the landing gear is clearly down.  d) surrounded by high-rise — degree: one control tower and low terminal buildings; surrounded promises tall buildings on every side.  e) significantly cloudy — degree word: there is not a cloud in the sky.

True: c) the control tower stands on the right, beyond the aircraft's nose; f) a second, smaller aircraft is on the ground on the far left. Notice that neither true statement says what the aircraft is doing — landing or taking off — because a single photo cannot prove which.

The 45-second method — say it back

  1. Look first, read second (5 seconds): nose direction, what is on the ground, what is in the air, what is in the background. Decide which side of the aircraft you can see.
  2. Read each statement hunting one word — the position word, the number, the tense, the actor, the claim you cannot see.
  3. Prefer hedged, checkable claims: seems, appears, can be seen. Distrust intentions, futures and degree words: waiting to, about to, extremely, completely, surrounded.
  4. Choose two, then re-check the two in the last ten seconds. You can change your answer until the clock ends.
