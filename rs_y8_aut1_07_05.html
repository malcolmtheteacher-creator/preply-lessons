What Unites and Divides?

Understanding the three Abrahamic faiths

What Do You Remember?

Before we compare the three faiths, let's recall what you learned about each one. Try to answer from memory first, then click to check your answer.

Question 1: Abraham's role in all three faiths

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Abraham is the founder figure for Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. Jews trace descent from Abraham through Isaac; Christians see Abraham as a model of faith; Muslims see Abraham (Ibrahim) as one of the greatest prophets and the father of Ishmael.

Question 2: Judaism's core belief

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Monotheism—belief in one God (Yahweh). The Torah is the most sacred text. Jewish practice is guided by mitzvot (commandments) and includes rituals like Sabbath observance, keeping kosher, and celebrating festivals like Passover and Hanukkah.

Question 3: Christianity's core belief

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Christians believe in Jesus Christ as the Son of God and Saviour. The Trinity (Father, Son, Holy Spirit) is central to most Christian theology. The Bible (Old and New Testaments) is sacred scripture. Key practices include prayer, worship, and the sacraments of Baptism and Communion.

Question 4: Islam's core belief

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Muslims believe in one God, Allah, and follow the teachings of the Prophet Muhammad. The Qur'an is the holy scripture, revealed directly by God. Core practices include the Five Pillars: declaration of faith, prayer five times daily, alms-giving, fasting during Ramadan, and pilgrimage to Mecca.

Quick Check

Who is the key figure in Judaism? Moses led the Exodus, but Abraham is the founding patriarch.
Why is Jesus important to Christians? Christians believe Jesus is the Son of God who died for humanity's sins.
What is the Five Pillars of Islam? The five core duties all Muslims should follow, from faith declaration to pilgrimage.
What does "Abrahamic faiths" mean? Faiths that trace their roots to Abraham and share a commitment to monotheism.

Three Traditions, One Root

Let's examine the three Abrahamic faiths side by side. What unites them? What divides them?

Beliefs About God

Christianity

God's Nature: The Trinity—one God in three persons: Father, Son (Jesus), and Holy Spirit. God is both transcendent and incarnate (present in Jesus).

God's Relationship with Humanity: God loves humanity so much that He sent Jesus to die for human sin and offer salvation.

Islam

God's Nature: Tawhid—absolute monotheism. Allah is one, indivisible, and beyond human comprehension. No partners, no division.

God's Relationship with Humanity: Allah is merciful and just. Humans are created to worship God and submit to His will (Islam = submission).

Judaism

God's Nature: Monotheism—belief in one God (Yahweh/Adonai). God is transcendent yet present in covenant relationships with the Jewish people.

God's Relationship with Humanity: God made a covenant with Abraham and renewed it with Moses. Humans are partners in fulfilling God's purposes.

Sacred Scriptures

Tradition Primary Scripture Status & Authority
Judaism Torah (first five books of Hebrew Bible); Tanakh (full Hebrew scriptures); Talmud (rabbinic interpretation) Torah is most sacred. Talmud guides interpretation and practice.
Christianity Bible: Old Testament (Hebrew scriptures) + New Testament (Gospels, Epistles, Revelation) Jesus Christ is the "Word of God." Scripture testifies to Jesus. Interpreted through church tradition.
Islam Qur'an (God's direct revelation to Muhammad); Hadith (sayings & deeds of Muhammad) Qur'an is God's literal, eternal word in Arabic. Hadith provides guidance for living.

Prophets & Revelation

Christianity

Jesus Christ is God's Son and the ultimate revelation. Old Testament prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah) are honoured but fulfilled in Jesus. Revelation ended with the apostles.

Islam

Muhammad is the final and greatest prophet (the "Seal of the Prophets"). Earlier prophets (Abraham, Moses, Jesus) are respected but Muhammad is God's last messenger. Revelation ended with Muhammad.

Judaism

Moses is the greatest prophet. Abraham, Isaac, Jacob are patriarchs. Prophets like Isaiah and Jeremiah conveyed God's word. Rabbinic Judaism emphasizes ongoing interpretation rather than new revelation.

Prayer & Worship

Tradition Prayer Practice Central Worship Acts
Judaism Formal prayers (liturgy) in synagogue; personal prayer; blessing over daily activities. Shabbat prayers are central. Sabbath (Friday sunset–Saturday); Torah readings; High Holy Days (Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur)
Christianity Personal and communal prayer; the Lord's Prayer is fundamental. Styles vary (liturgical to spontaneous). Worship services (Sunday); Baptism and Communion (Eucharist); Christmas and Easter
Islam Salah: five daily prayers at set times (dawn, noon, afternoon, sunset, night). Face Mecca; perform ritual ablutions (wudu). Friday prayers (Jumu'ah); Hajj (pilgrimage); Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha; month of Ramadan

Dietary Laws & Food

Judaism

Kashrut (keeping kosher): Only kosher animals (split hooves, chew cud). No shellfish or pork. Separate utensils for meat and dairy. Strict rules about slaughter. Observed by observant Jews; less strictly by others.

Islam

Halal (permissible): No pork or alcohol. Animals must be slaughtered in God's name. Similar to kosher but distinct. All observant Muslims follow halal. Ramadan fasting (sunrise to sunset) is mandatory.

Christianity

Dietary law: Most Christian traditions have no dietary restrictions (Jesus declared all food clean). Historically, some Catholics fast on Fridays in Lent. Fasting is spiritual discipline, not law.

What Links & What Divides?

Use this interactive tool to explore what unites the three Abrahamic faiths and what creates division between them.

Shared Beliefs (Unity)

Monotheism

All three faiths believe in one God. This is foundational and non-negotiable across Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

Abraham as Patriarch

All three trace spiritual and/or biological descent through Abraham. He is the symbol of faith, covenant, and the beginning of God's chosen people.

Sacred Scripture

All three have holy texts that are treated as divinely inspired or revealed. They use scripture to guide belief and behaviour.

Ethical Monotheism

All three emphasize that God is just, merciful, and demands ethical behaviour. Moral law is bound to religious obligation.

Prophecy

All three believe God communicates with humanity through prophets. Moses, Jesus, Muhammad—all are prophetic figures across these faiths.

The Afterlife

All three believe that human actions have eternal consequences. Judgement, heaven, and hell are concepts found in all three traditions.

Key Differences (Division)

The Nature of God

Christianity: Trinity (God in three persons). Islam & Judaism: Absolute monotheism—God is one and indivisible.

Who is the Messiah?

Christianity: Jesus is the Messiah, Son of God, died for humanity's sins. Judaism: Messiah hasn't come yet; Jesus is not the Messiah. Islam: Jesus was a prophet but not God's son.

The Role of Jesus

Christianity: Divine; central to salvation. Judaism: Not divine; a teacher or false messiah. Islam: Respected prophet but not God's son; Muhammad is the final prophet.

Revelation & Scripture

Islam: Qur'an is God's literal, unchanging word; final revelation through Muhammad. Christianity: Revelation culminated in Jesus. Judaism: Torah is most sacred; ongoing rabbinic interpretation.

Salvation

Christianity: Salvation through faith in Jesus Christ. Judaism: Salvation through covenant obedience; less emphasis on afterlife. Islam: Salvation through submission to Allah and righteous deeds.

Law & Practice

Judaism & Islam: Detailed law (Torah/Shariah) governs daily life—diet, dress, prayer times. Christianity: Less emphasis on ritual law; saved by grace, not works.

Key Insights

What unites the three Abrahamic faiths is deeper than what divides them at first glance. All three share belief in one God, the importance of Abraham, sacred texts, and ethical law. But differences about the nature of God (especially Jesus), revelation, and the path to salvation are profound and have shaped centuries of history.

Real-World Scenarios

How do these beliefs shape the lives of followers? Explore scenarios where the differences and similarities matter.

Scenario 1: The Sabbath Question

The Situation

Three students at your school—Sarah (Jewish), Marcus (Christian), and Amir (Muslim)—are planning to study together for an exam on Friday evening. Sarah cannot study; Marcus would prefer Sunday morning; Amir is flexible.

Why?

📖 Think about each faith's practices first!
  • Sarah (Jewish): Shabbat (Sabbath) begins Friday at sunset and ends Saturday at nightfall. Religious observance forbids work, travel, and writing. Studying is work.
  • Marcus (Christian): Most Christians worship on Sunday but have no daily restrictions like Sabbath. Friday evening is fine; he just prefers Sunday.
  • Amir (Muslim): Friday is important (Jumu'ah—communal prayers at noon). Evening is fine. No daily restrictions like Sabbath or Sunday Sabbath.

Scenario 2: The School Meal

The Situation

Your school is serving pork chops at lunch. Amir and Sarah cannot eat them; Marcus will happily eat them.

Why?

📖 Think about dietary laws first!
  • Sarah (Jewish): Kashrut (keeping kosher) forbids pork. Pigs are considered ritually unclean in Jewish law.
  • Amir (Muslim): Halal law forbids pork. The Qur'an explicitly mentions pork as haraam (forbidden).
  • Marcus (Christian): Jesus declared all foods clean. Most Christian traditions have no dietary restrictions. Pork is permissible.

Scenario 3: Prayer in the School Day

The Situation

During free period, Amir asks the school to provide a quiet space for prayer. Why might he need this more urgently than Sarah or Marcus?

Why?

📖 Think about prayer frequency and timing first!
  • Amir (Muslim): Salah (prayer) is mandatory five times daily at specific times: dawn, noon, afternoon, sunset, and night. During school hours, he may need to pray at noon and afternoon.
  • Sarah (Jewish): Prayer is important, but there is more flexibility. Formal liturgy is usually in synagogue morning/evening. Daily prayer is encouraged but not as time-specific as Islam's five daily prayers.
  • Marcus (Christian): Prayer is encouraged throughout the day but not mandated at specific times. No fixed prayer schedule.

Scenario 4: The Christmas Nativity Play

The Situation

Your school is staging a nativity play about Jesus's birth. Marcus might participate fully; Sarah and Amir might not.

Why?

📖 Think about Jesus's role in each faith first!
  • Marcus (Christian): Jesus's birth is central to Christian faith. The nativity is a core celebration of incarnation and salvation.
  • Sarah (Jewish): Jesus is not recognized as the Messiah or as divine. Participating in a play that venerates Jesus as God's son would conflict with Jewish monotheism.
  • Amir (Muslim): Jesus (Isa) is respected as a prophet but not as God's son. Muslims believe Jesus was human, not divine. Portraying him as the Son of God contradicts Islamic belief.

Summary

The three Abrahamic faiths—Judaism, Christianity, and Islam—share a profound foundation of monotheism, ethical law, and reverence for Abraham. Yet they diverge significantly on the nature of God (Trinity vs. absolute monotheism), the role of Jesus (Saviour vs. prophet vs. not the Messiah), and the path to salvation. Understanding both unity and division helps us respect people of faith while recognizing the depth of their convictions.

Practice & Recall

Use these final questions to consolidate everything you've learned.

Recall Cards: Answer Before Revealing

1. What is Tawhid?

🔒 Answer first!

Answer: Tawhid is the Islamic concept of absolute monotheism—the oneness of God (Allah). It is the core belief that Allah is one, indivisible, and has no partners or equals.

2. What are the Five Pillars of Islam?

🔒 Answer first!

Answer: The Five Pillars are:

  • Shahada (declaration of faith)
  • Salah (five daily prayers)
  • Zakat (alms-giving)
  • Sawm (fasting during Ramadan)
  • Hajj (pilgrimage to Mecca)

3. What is the Talmud?

🔒 Answer first!

Answer: The Talmud is a vast collection of Jewish rabbinic interpretations and discussions of the Torah. It provides guidance on how to apply Torah law to daily life and includes both legal discussions (Halakha) and stories (Aggadah).

4. What is the significance of the Incarnation in Christianity?

🔒 Answer first!

Answer: The Incarnation is the Christian belief that God became human in Jesus Christ. It represents God's ultimate act of love and redemption, making salvation possible for humanity. Jesus is fully God and fully human.

5. Name three similarities between Judaism and Islam.

🔒 Answer first!

Answer: Three similarities:

  • Both believe in absolute monotheism (one God, indivisible).
  • Both have detailed dietary laws (Kashrut and Halal) that forbid pork.
  • Both emphasize law and ethical behaviour as central to religious life (Torah and Shariah).
