The Group Admin

Three hundred runners, one no-advertising rule, and one member who keeps forgetting it. You didn't ask for the little shield next to your name — but it's there, so this call is yours.

The Scene 🎬

You started the group four years ago so that six of you could sort out a Sunday route without forty texts. It's three hundred people now. Somebody set up a rule about advertising back when it was fifty of you and nobody's had to think about it since. Then Tomasz joined, and Tomasz is a sports massage therapist, and Tomasz posts.

Not once. Most days. A discount code, a photo of a treatment table, ten percent off if you mention the group. And he's lovely — he's the one who drives out to fetch people when they've hobbled to mile eleven with a dead calf. But this week three separate members have messaged you privately, all slightly apologetically, all saying more or less the same thing: is this allowed?

And it isn't. It's your rule, on your group, and you're the one with the button that takes posts down and the button that takes people out. You don't want to press either. You want him to stop, stay, and still turn up on Sunday. So you do the thing nobody does any more: you ring him.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Have you ever had to enforce a rule you didn't personally care about, just because it was your job to? How did it feel — reasonable, or ridiculous?
  2. When someone breaks an unwritten rule in a group you're in, what usually happens: a quiet word, a public comment, or three hundred people saying nothing for six weeks?

Who's who

You
The Admin 🛡️

You have the power here and you'd rather nobody remembered that. You need the posts to stop, you need Tomasz to stay, and you'd quite like to run beside him on Sunday without either of you thinking about this call.

The other voice
Tomasz, the Massage Therapist 💆

Generous, useful, and self-employed. To him the group isn't a rule book, it's three hundred runners with sore legs — and his rent. He won't shout. He'll just ask, quite reasonably, who complained.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for holding a rule without becoming the sort of person who enjoys holding rules. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. The whole thing turns on tone, because the words themselves have power behind them and everybody knows it. Say them fast and flat and you sound like someone reading out a verdict; say them slowly, at ordinary volume, with the pauses left in, and you sound like a person who'd rather be talking about Sunday's route. Do not sound sorry, though, because an apology invites an argument. Somewhere between "reading a verdict" and "asking a favour" is the voice you want, and it's closer to the middle than you'd think.

🎩 Opening it properly

Phrase 1

He's picked up and he can already hear that this isn't a normal call. You need to flag, in one breath, that you're ringing in your official role rather than as a mate — without sounding like you've been promoted to God. Which opener does that?

A. "So, as you know, I'm one of the administrators of the group, and there's an issue I'm required to raise with you."
B. "Can I have a quick word about the group — wearing my admin hat?"
C. "Don't take this the wrong way, but a few of us have been talking about you."
B — "Can I have a quick word about the group — wearing my admin hat?"
To be "wearing your admin hat" means you're speaking from the role rather than from the friendship, and the lightness of the image is doing real work, because it says the hat comes off again afterwards. A is so formal that it turns a phone call into a disciplinary meeting, and C makes him feel he's been discussed behind his back before he's said a word.

🧊 Taking the personal out of it

Phrase 2

He needs to hear, straight away, that you haven't picked him out — that anybody doing this would be getting this call. Complete the chunk: "It's not a ___ thing, and I'd say the same to anyone."

"It's not a personal thing, and I'd say the same to anyone."
The first half denies the accusation he hasn't made yet, which is why it works so well before he's had a chance to make it. The second half is the proof: you're not asking him to trust you, you're telling him the test you'd apply to anybody, and he can check that against everything he's ever seen you do.
Phrase 3

He's arguing that his posts are useful, which they are — and you can't win that argument, so you step round it. You're not judging whether his posts are good; you're pointing at something that applies regardless. Put the pieces in order:

for everybody —I'm ringing.The rule's the samethat's the only reason
"The rule's the same for everybody — that's the only reason I'm ringing."
Notice that you never disagree with him, because you don't have to: the rule doesn't ask whether a post is useful, so his best argument simply doesn't apply. And "that's the only reason I'm ringing" quietly rules everything else out — no history, no grudge, no hidden second complaint — which is usually exactly what the other person is bracing for.
Phrase 4

"Who complained?" You're not naming anybody, but you do want him to understand that you didn't go hunting for this and that it isn't one person with a grudge. Put the pieces in order:

which is whymessage me about it,I've had three peopleit's landed on me.
"I've had three people message me about it, which is why it's landed on me."
If something "lands on" you, it arrives as your problem without you asking for it — the picture is of a thing dropping onto your desk. So the phrase answers the question he really asked, which was why are you doing this to me, while refusing the question he actually asked, which was for a name. Three is also a number he can't wave away, and it's a number without a face attached.

🚪 Leaving him room

Phrase 5

You don't want him silent, you want him occasional — so give him the space that's genuinely available and sound like you mean it, rather than like you're handing over a crumb. Which line does that?

A. "You're welcome to post it once, and that's genuinely fine."
B. "I suppose one post wouldn't kill anybody."
C. "One. And I'll be watching, so."
A — "You're welcome to post it once, and that's genuinely fine."
"You're welcome to" is an offer rather than a permit, so it doesn't remind him who's holding the button, and "genuinely fine" closes the door on the suspicion that you'll be annoyed with him afterwards for doing the thing you just allowed. B sounds like it's being dragged out of you, and C turns the concession into a threat, which means he'll never use it.
Phrase 6

He says it's how he gets work — and he's right, this isn't a hobby for him, it's his rent. Before you go anywhere near consequences, say the thing that shows you've understood what's actually at stake for him, and that you're not out to damage it. In your own words: what would you say?

e.g. "I know it's your livelihood — I'm not trying to make that harder."
Your "livelihood" is how you earn your living, and it's a weightier word than "job" — it carries the rent and the food, which is exactly why it's the right one here. The second half matters just as much, because he's braced for you to say his work doesn't belong here, and instead you've said the opposite: you can see what this costs him, you're not indifferent to it, and you're still going to ask.

⚖️ Naming the consequence

Phrase 7

Now the awkward part. He needs to know what happens if nothing changes — but said as something you'd hate to do, not something you're itching to do. Put the pieces in order:

taking them down,I'll have to startand I'd rather not.If it carries on
"If it carries on I'll have to start taking them down, and I'd rather not."
Three small things are doing the softening. "If it carries on" puts him in charge of whether it ever happens; "I'll have to" makes it the rule's doing rather than yours; and "I'd rather not" tells him you take no pleasure in it. Say the same fact without those three and you're threatening him — which is why people so often skip the consequence altogether and then have to spring it on someone later.
Phrase 8

Removing him from the group is the thing you both know you could do, and it's sitting there unsaid, doing damage. How do you deal with it?

A. "Obviously I can remove people, just so we're clear where we are."
B. "Nobody's talking about removing anyone, don't be silly."
C. "I don't want to remove you. That's the honest position."
C — "I don't want to remove you. That's the honest position."
The word he's afraid of is already in the room, so saying it yourself takes it off him, and saying it in a sentence that begins "I don't want to" tells him where you stand before he can imagine somewhere worse. "That's the honest position" is a small promise that you're not managing him — which is what the other two are doing, since A holds the power up so he can see it, and B pretends it doesn't exist and insults him in the process.

🤝 Getting a real yes

Phrase 9

You've said everything you came to say and you've put a workable arrangement on the table. Now you need his actual agreement out loud — not silence, and not delight, because you're never getting delight. Ask him for it in one short question. What would you say?

e.g. "Is that something you can live with?"
To "live with" something is to accept it without having to like it, so the question asks for exactly as much as you can honestly expect and no more — which is why he can say yes without pretending. Asking "are you happy with that?" would force him either to lie or to say no, and "so we're agreed?" isn't a question at all, it's a minute of the meeting.
Phrase 10

He's agreed, the business is done, and you'll be standing next to him at a car park at eight on Sunday morning. Land it. Complete the chunk: "Right — no ___ ___, I hope. See you Sunday."

"Right — no hard feelings, I hope. See you Sunday."
"Hard feelings" are the resentment left behind after a disagreement, and adding "I hope" is what stops it being an instruction, because you can't order somebody not to mind. Then the ordinary detail — Sunday, the car park, the usual — does the real repairing, since it puts the two of you back where you were before the call and treats that as the obvious outcome.

🔑 Make them yours

Holding a rule you didn't write, over someone you like, is a small piece of work that comes round again and again — at work, in a family, in any group that lasts long enough to have rules. Pin three of these to your own life so they're there when you need them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"It's not a personal thing, and I'd say the same to anyone." — where have you had to tell somebody something they might easily take as an attack?

"If it carries on I'll have to start taking them down, and I'd rather not." — a consequence you've been avoiding naming. What is it, and how would you say it?

"Is that something you can live with?" — an agreement you need from someone who won't be thrilled about it.

The Twist

😬 The post everybody loves

Tomasz kept his word. One post — and it's a good one. Free injury clinic, Sunday morning after the run, no charge, group members only, bring your dodgy knees. It's been up four hours. Forty likes. Denise has commented "this man is a legend". Two people have said it's the best thing the group's ever done.

It is also, quite plainly, an advert — his name, his table, his business, and everyone who limps over on Sunday leaves with his card. One of the three has already messaged you: so is the rule real or not? Take the post down and you're the person who deleted the free clinic. Leave it up and the rule you enforced on Tuesday died on Thursday, in public, in front of three hundred people.

Start the timer. Ring him again. There is no version of this where you get everything — decide what you're actually protecting before you speak, and be able to say it out loud when he asks. One rule: you can't ask him to decide for you.

  • I gave him something he could actually do, and I sounded like I meant it.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Nobody applies to be an admin. You start something small, it grows, and one day you look up and you're holding a rule over three hundred people you mostly like, with a button that can remove any of them and no training whatsoever in when to press it.

It's an odd little job, and it's everywhere now — the group chat, the forum, the residents' page, the club that used to be six people. Real power, no salary, no authority anybody voted for, and the person you're enforcing the rule on is somebody you'll see on Sunday morning in the cold.

Which is probably why so many of these rules go quietly unenforced for years. It isn't that people don't notice. It's that saying the thing out loud costs something, and deleting the post costs nothing — so the post gets deleted, and the person never gets told why, and they leave.

The phone call is more expensive. It's also the only version where he's still there in the car park.

Two bigger questions

  1. Who should hold the rules in a group that nobody officially runs — the founder, whoever's willing, or everybody? And does the answer change when the group gets to three hundred people, or three thousand?
  2. Is there a real difference between a useful post and an advert, or is it only ever about who's making money? Where would you have drawn the line with Tomasz — and could you have written that line down in advance?
