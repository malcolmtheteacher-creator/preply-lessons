A Photo of You

It was a good night and it's a nice photo and you don't want it on the internet. Asking is twelve seconds of awkwardness; not asking is it being there forever. The trick is making it small enough that nobody has to feel got at.

The Scene 🎬

You're tagged. It's already got forty likes. It is a genuinely lovely photo.

Nina posted it this morning — the four of you outside the pub, everyone laughing, you in the middle. She did it out of affection. She has no idea it's a problem, because for her it obviously isn't.

You keep your face off social media. The reason is yours and you don't particularly want to explain it, which is the awkward part: you're asking for something without wanting to justify it.

Two ways this goes wrong. Make it too big and she's mortified and apologising for an hour. Make it too vague and it doesn't come down. What you want is small, clear, and over in about a minute.

  1. Do you mind photos of you online? Has that changed as you've got older?
  2. Should you ask before posting a photo of someone else? Does it depend who they are?
YouNot cross, slightly awkward, and not wanting to turn a nice night into A Thing. You'd rather not explain your reasons.
NinaPosted it out of pure affection. Will be embarrassed the moment she realises, and will over-apologise unless you stop her.
First: read the conversation and time it — the whole ask is about four lines. Notice that You never explains why, and Nina never actually asks.

The Conversation 💬

A phone call, the following morning.

NinaMorning! God, my head. Was that not the best night?
YouIt was brilliant. Genuinely — best I've laughed in ages. Listen, tiny favour, and it's genuinely no big deal.
NinaGo on?
YouThe photo you put up — would you mind taking that one down?
NinaOh! Oh God, is it a bad one of you? I thought you looked—
YouNo, it's a lovely photo. It's nothing you did — I just keep my face off there.
Nina…Right. Do you? I didn't— sorry, I feel awful now, I should have—
YouPlease don't feel daft — how would you have known? I've never made a thing of it.
NinaI could just crop you out? Or there's that blur thing—
YouJust the whole thing down, if that's alright. Whenever you get a sec, honestly, there's no rush at all.
NinaNo, course. Doing it now. …Done. Is there anything else I've put up? I've probably—
YouI'll have a look and tell you if there is. That'd be easier than you going back through five years.
NinaYeah. God. Sorry again.
YouWe're totally fine — thank you for getting it. And send me that photo, would you? I'd love to actually have it. Just for me.
NinaSending it now. …It is a good one, isn't it.
YouIt's a great one. That's why I want it.

From memory — don't scroll back up.

  1. Nina offers a compromise instead of taking it down. What is it, and how does You handle it?
  2. Nina offers to go back through everything she's posted. What does You suggest instead, and why is that better?
  3. How does the call end — and what does You ask for?
1. Cropping you out, or blurring. You declines it without debating it — "just the whole thing down, if that's alright" — and immediately softens it with "no rush at all" so the refusal doesn't sting.

2. That You will look and report back. It saves her a guilt-driven trawl through five years, and it means the checking is done by the only person who actually knows what they object to.

3. By asking her to send the photo privately. It's the move that proves the objection was to the posting, not the picture — and it turns the whole thing from a rebuke into a compliment.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for asking someone to undo something they did out of kindness. Each works on a photo, a mention, an introduction, a bit of news shared too widely. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Fast and light. This whole conversation should take under a minute, and the speed is the reassurance — if you slow down and get careful, she'll conclude it's serious and start apologising properly, which is the outcome you're trying to avoid. Say it the way you'd ask someone to pass the salt.

🪶 Making it small before you ask

Phrase 1

The job: sizing the request before she hears it. Which opening?

A. "Can I ask you something? It's a bit awkward."
B. "Tiny favour — genuinely no big deal."
C. "So I saw you posted that photo."
B — "Tiny favour — genuinely no big deal."
A announces awkwardness and creates it; she'll now brace for something serious. C is an observation with an unmistakable edge and she'll hear a complaint coming. B does the sizing before the content — "tiny" and "no big deal" tell her exactly how much to care, and "genuinely" stops those words reading as politeness. She stays relaxed, which is the whole game.

Take it anywhere: "Tiny favour — genuinely no big deal."
Phrase 2

The job: making the ask itself — short, specific, and as a question. Complete it:

"Would you ___ taking that one down?"

"Would you mind taking that one down?"
"Would you mind" is the standard English softener for a request that slightly inconveniences someone, and it's a genuine question rather than an instruction. "That one" is precise without being pointed — you're not naming and shaming the post. Six words, no explanation attached, which is what keeps it small.

Take it anywhere: "Would you mind ___?" The workhorse polite request in British English.

🛡️ Taking the blame off them

Phrase 3

The job: clearing her of fault and giving a reason — without explaining yourself. Put the pieces in order:

I just keep my faceIt's nothing you did —off there.
"It's nothing you did — I just keep my face off there."
Two jobs in one sentence. The first half absolves her, which is what stops the apology spiral. The second gives a reason that is complete but not explanatory — it states a policy of yours rather than a justification, and "just" makes it sound routine. You're not obliged to say why, and this phrasing means she won't ask.

Take it anywhere: "It's nothing you did — I just ___." Any time you need something undone without blaming the person who did it.
Phrase 4

The job: stopping the over-apology before it gets going. Complete it:

"Please don't feel daft — how would you have ___?"

"Please don't feel daft — how would you have known?"
The question is doing the work: it isn't rhetorical comfort, it's a fair point. She genuinely couldn't have known, and saying so out loud removes the embarrassment rather than just telling her not to feel it. Daft is a gentle British word for silly — affectionate rather than critical, which is exactly the register you want.

Take it anywhere: "How would you have known?" Kinder and more effective than "don't worry about it."
Phrase 5

The job: taking a share of the responsibility, honestly. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "I've never made a thing of it."
True, and it converts the situation from her error into a gap in what she knew — which you're responsible for. It's a very cheap sentence that costs you nothing and buys her a lot of relief. Say it immediately after clearing her of blame, while she's still deciding how bad to feel.

Take it anywhere: "I've never made a thing of it." / "I've never actually said, to be fair."

🚫 Declining the compromise

Phrase 6

The job: refusing the half-measure without a debate about it. Which version?

A. "No, cropping doesn't really solve it — the whole thing needs to go."
B. "Just the whole thing down, if that's alright."
C. "Well, cropping would probably be okay I suppose…"
B — "Just the whole thing down, if that's alright."
A explains why her idea won't work, which invites her to defend it and turns a favour into a negotiation. C accepts something you don't want because refusing felt rude — and you'll be annoyed about it for a month. B simply restates the ask, unchanged, and adds "if that's alright" so the firmness doesn't land as sharpness. Don't argue with a compromise; just repeat the original request warmly.

Take it anywhere: "Just ___, if that's alright." The polite way to decline a half-measure.
Phrase 7

The job: removing all urgency, so the ask doesn't feel like a demand. Complete it:

"Whenever you get a ___, honestly, there's no rush at all."

"Whenever you get a sec, honestly, there's no rush at all."
A sec is a second — informal and very common. Adding this immediately after a firm refusal is what keeps the whole exchange gentle: you've held the line on what and given way completely on when. People almost always do it straight away, which is the small paradox — remove the pressure and you get faster compliance.

Take it anywhere: "Whenever you get a sec — no rush at all."

🧹 Handling the offer to fix everything

Phrase 8

The job: declining a guilt-driven offer and replacing it with something practical. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "I'll have a look and tell you if there is — that'd be easier than you going back through five years."
She's offered to trawl her whole account, which she'd do badly and resentfully. Taking the job yourself is both kinder and more accurate, since you're the only one who knows what you object to. Framing it as saving her effort means it doesn't read as you not trusting her.

Take it anywhere: "I'll have a look and tell you — that'd be easier than you ___."

💛 Closing it warmly

Phrase 9

The job: declaring the matter closed, and thanking her for the right thing. Put the pieces in order:

thank youWe're totally fine —for getting it.
"We're totally fine — thank you for getting it."
"We're totally fine" is an explicit statement about the friendship, not the photo, and it's the thing she actually needs to hear. "Thank you for getting it" thanks her for understanding rather than for complying — which treats her as someone who saw the point, not someone who obeyed.

Take it anywhere: "We're fine — thank you for getting it."
Phrase 10

The job: proving your objection was to the posting, not the picture. Complete it:

"Send it to me though, would you? I'd love to actually ___ it. Just for me."

"Send it to me though, would you? I'd love to actually have it. Just for me."
The single best move in the conversation, and it takes four seconds. Asking for the photo says the night was good, the picture is nice, and the only problem was where it lived — which retrospectively makes your request smaller and warmer than it seemed. "Just for me" draws the line clearly without a word of explanation.

Take it anywhere: "Send it to me though — I'd love to have it. Just for me."

🔑 Make them yours

These work whenever you need someone to undo something they did meaning well — a photo, a bit of news passed on, an introduction you didn't want. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"Tiny favour — genuinely no big deal." — what have you not asked for because it felt awkward?

"It's nothing you did — I just ___." — a reason that's complete without being an explanation.

"Just ___, if that's alright." — where have you accepted a half-measure you didn't want?

⚡ The twist

Nina takes it down straight away. An hour later she rings back: her sister had already shared it to a group of about two hundred people, and someone in that group has commented with your full name.

It's no longer in Nina's hands, she's upset, and the thing you were quietly avoiding has happened anyway. What do you say to her?

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you size a request before you make it?
  • Can you give a reason that's complete without being an explanation?
  • Can you stop someone over-apologising in one sentence?
  • Can you decline a compromise by repeating the original ask, warmly?
  • Can you finish so the friendship is visibly untouched?

The Echo 🕰️

Tutankhamun was buried to be left alone. The tomb was sealed, hidden and forgotten for three thousand years, which is roughly the point of a tomb.

In 1922 Howard Carter opened it. The boy king's face has since been photographed more than almost any face in history — printed, projected, put on posters and mugs and the sides of buses. He was eighteen when he died and nobody has ever asked him about any of it.

You are not a pharaoh and Nina is not an archaeologist. But there's something in it: a face, put somewhere permanent, by someone who meant no harm and never thought to ask.
