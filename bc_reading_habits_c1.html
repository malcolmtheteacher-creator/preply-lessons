Reading Phrasal Verbs

Click each card to flip and see the definition and example.

flip through

To turn pages quickly without reading carefully.

"I was flipping through the magazine while waiting for my appointment."

skim

To read quickly, looking for the main ideas without reading every word.

"I skimmed the article to find the key points about climate change."

read up on

To learn about a topic by reading about it carefully and thoroughly.

"I need to read up on the latest research before the meeting."

couldn't put down

To be so absorbed in a book that you don't want to stop reading; gripping.

"The novel was so good—I couldn't put it down!"

get into

To become interested in or involved with something; to start doing.

"I really got into fantasy novels after reading the first book in the series."

go off

To stop liking or enjoying something that you previously enjoyed.

"I used to love detective stories, but I've gone off them lately."

skip bits

To miss parts of a text while reading, usually parts you find boring or unimportant.

"The descriptions were too long, so I skipped bits and focused on the dialogue."

read cover to cover

To read a complete book from beginning to end without skipping anything.

"I read the entire novel cover to cover in two days."

have your nose in a book

To be reading deeply or intensely; being absorbed in a book.

"She always has her nose in a book during her commute."

Opinions on Reading

For each statement, decide your opinion: Agree or Disagree? Then justify your position and consider counter-arguments.

1. Reading makes you a better communicator.

Consider: Reading exposes you to varied vocabulary, sentence structures, and ways of expressing ideas. But does this automatically make you a better communicator?

Counter-argument: Someone might read passively without actively working to adopt new language patterns. Communication also requires practice in speaking and listening.

2. People read less today than they did 20 years ago.

Consider: Digital devices and social media compete for attention. Less time on traditional books is measurable.

Counter-argument: People read more text overall now (emails, articles, messages, news feeds). The medium has changed, not necessarily the amount.

3. Online reading is taking over from printed books permanently.

Consider: E-readers and digital platforms are convenient, accessible, and sustainable. Many people prefer screens.

Counter-argument: Print books offer advantages: no eye strain, no distractions, tactile pleasure, and don't require batteries. Both formats likely coexist long-term.

4. Readers have higher IQs than non-readers.

Consider: Reading requires and develops cognitive skills: focus, comprehension, analytical thinking. Regular readers may show higher performance in certain cognitive areas.

Counter-argument: Intelligence is multifaceted. Non-readers may excel in emotional intelligence, practical skills, visual-spatial reasoning, or other domains. Reading ≠ intelligence.

5. Reading regularly reduces stress and improves mental health.

Consider: Reading is a form of escapism and mindfulness. It can lower cortisol levels and provide comfort, especially fiction.

Counter-argument: Not all reading is relaxing—dense academic texts or stressful news articles can increase anxiety. The type of reading matters, as does the reader's state of mind.

6. There is too much to read nowadays; it is impossible to stay informed.

Consider: Information overload is real. The volume of published content daily exceeds what one person could read. Curation becomes necessary.

Counter-argument: Curated sources, summaries, and selective reading strategies exist. You can be well-informed without reading everything. Quality matters more than quantity.

Extended Speaking Task

Give a 3-minute talk about your reading life. Use the timer and planning framework below to organize your thoughts.

3:00

Planning Framework — Cover These Topics:

Childhood reading: What are your earliest memories of reading? Who introduced you to books? What did you enjoy?
First books & family reading: What were some books you remember from childhood or school? Did your family read together?
School books: What did you have to read in school? Did you enjoy assigned reading?
Subscriptions & current reading: Do you subscribe to any platforms? What are you reading now?
All-time favourites: Which books or authors have stayed with you? Why do they matter to you?
Online vs. print: How do you prefer to read? Where do you get your books?
Reading in English: How has learning English affected your reading habits? Do you read in English by choice now?
Reading purpose: Do you read mainly for work, study, or pleasure? Or a mix?

After Speaking — Reflect:

How was your fluency? What phrases did you use naturally? What would you like to practice next time?
