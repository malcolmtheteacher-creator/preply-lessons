Six Moves for Remote Leadership

Click each move. These are the practices that build trust and clarity across distance.

1. The Check-In That Isn't a Check-Up +

Why it matters: Remote people feel surveilled. They're waiting for the "are you actually working" question. A real check-in is different — it asks how they're doing, not whether they're on track.

Check-up (surveilling): "How's the project moving? Are you on schedule?"
Check-in (genuine): "How are you getting on? What's working? What's not?"

The benefit: They feel seen as humans, not productivity units.

2. Visibility Without Surveillance +

Why it matters: You need to know what's happening. But constant monitoring kills culture. The move: regular brief updates on what they're working on and blockers.

Set expectation: "Every Friday, a 30-second update: what you shipped, what you're working on next, what's blocking you. That's all I need." Not: constant Slack messages asking for status.

The benefit: You have visibility. They don't feel watched. It becomes routine, not surveillance.

3. The Async Brief +

Why it matters: Synchronous communication (meetings) is impossible at scale across time zones. Async communication (writing) is essential. But it has to be structured so nothing is lost.

Structure: Context → Decision → Rationale → Next Steps → Question for them
NOT: rambling email with unclear point. Clear email with clear structure. Takes 5 minutes to write. Saves hours of back-and-forth.

The benefit: Everyone understands. Nobody is confused. Time zones don't matter.

4. The Remote 1:1 +

Why it matters: 1:1s on camera can feel awkward remotely. But they're crucial. The move: make them genuine conversations, not status reports.

Wrong: "Status update on your projects."
Right: "How are you doing? What are you working on that excites you? What's frustrating? What do you need from me?"

The benefit: You build relationship across distance. You find out what's really going on — not just what's in the tickets.

5. Timezone Fairness +

Why it matters: If you always schedule meetings at times that are terrible for some people, they'll feel they don't matter. Be intentional about it.

Rotate meeting times so one timezone doesn't always get the worst slot. Make async the default, sync only when needed. If you schedule at 9am your time and it's 10pm theirs, acknowledge the cost and be grateful.

The benefit: People feel valued. They know you're not taking their time for granted.

6. Rebuilding Trust at a Distance +

Why it matters: When someone goes quiet, trust erodes fast remotely. You need to rebuild it proactively.

"I've noticed you've been quiet the last two weeks. No judgment — just checking in. What's going on? What would help?"
Not: "Your output is down." Not: "Are you okay?" Just: genuine check-in from real care.

The benefit: You're signalling you notice, you care, and you're not assuming the worst.

Your Remote Leadership Scenario

Pick one scenario. Speak your way through it — a conversation or an async message. You have 2 minutes to prepare, then 2-3 minutes to deliver. Record yourself.

A. The Disengaged Team Member

The situation: Two weeks of minimal engagement. You don't know what's going on. Could be burnout, personal issue, quiet quitting, or time zone adjustment.

Your challenge: Check in genuinely. Don't assume. Don't accuse. Signal you care and want to understand. Open space for them to be honest.

B. The Conflict Between Remote Team Members

The situation: Two team members who have never met in person are having a conflict. Via Slack, they're being passive-aggressive. It's affecting team dynamics.

Your challenge: Intervene. Name the conflict. Make it safe for them to be honest. Create clarity on what needs to change.

C. Delivering Difficult Feedback Remotely

The situation: A talented remote team member is good at their job but is dismissive in meetings, talks over others, and is affecting the team dynamic. You've never met them in person.

Your challenge: Deliver feedback in a way that lands — not defensive. Help them see the impact. Show you're invested in them improving.

Reflection & Language Bank

Remote Leadership Is Real Leadership

It's not about time-zone management or async tools. It's about building trust and clarity without the spontaneous moments that in-person teams get.

The core principle: Be intentional. What you don't say explicitly, they won't know. What you don't ask directly, they won't tell you. Assume nothing. Ask everything.
