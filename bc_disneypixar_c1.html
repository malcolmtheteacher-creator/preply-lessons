The Studio That Bought Its Rescuer

Disney's own animation was failing and its most successful partner was walking away. In 2006 it paid about $7.4 billion for Pixar — and then let the company it bought run its animation.

Easier version: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll work through an acquisition in which the buyer chose to be changed by what it bought — and you'll practise the language of the future seen from the past, which every deal story and every post-mortem needs: they were going to walk away, the deal was about to collapse, it was a decision that would shape the next twenty years.
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • Think of a partnership — at work or outside it — that was successful and still ended. What really ended it: money, control, or personalities?
  • When a large organisation buys a smaller one, who usually changes whom? Is that the right way round?
  • Would you rather buy the competitor that is beating you, or try to beat it yourself? What would you be admitting if you bought it?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. The small studio made the films, but it had no way to get them into thousands of cinemas around the world, or to sell the toys and videos. The big studio handled all of that in exchange for a large fee. It controlled the distribution.
distribution (n) — the business of getting a product to the places where customers can buy or see it. Distribution rights, a distribution deal, a distribution fee. In film, the distributor often earns a large share even when it did not make the film.
2. The buyer promised that the studio could keep its name, its offices, its working methods and its own leaders. Nobody from head office would tell it how to make films. Its autonomy was written into the plan.
autonomy (n) — the freedom to make your own decisions without control from outside. Adjective: autonomous. In acquisitions, the key question is how much autonomy the acquired business keeps — and for how long.
3. Talks about a new contract failed, and the smaller company announced that it would find a different partner once its existing films were released. It had decided to walk away.
to walk away (from) (phrasal v) — to leave a negotiation or relationship, choosing no deal over a bad one. To walk away from the table, a walk-away price (the point at which you stop negotiating). Your willingness to walk away is often your strongest bargaining power.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: synergy · creative control · shareholder revolt. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means the power to make the final decisions about the content of a film, a book or a product?
creative control (n) — to have / keep / give up creative control. In entertainment deals it is often more fiercely negotiated than money.
2. Which term means a situation in which a large number of investors publicly oppose the company's leadership, for example by voting against them?
a shareholder revolt (n) — also a shareholder rebellion. Related: a proxy fight (a battle for shareholders' votes), activist investors, to withhold votes.
3. Which term means the extra value that two businesses are supposed to create together, more than they could create separately?
synergy (n) — cost synergies, revenue synergies. Register note: very common in deal announcements, and often treated with suspicion, because promised synergies frequently fail to appear.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. The buyer did not pay cash. Instead, the owners of the smaller company received new shares in the larger one. It was an all-stock deal. An all-stock deal is…
  (a) a deal in which the buyer purchases only the other company's stock of products   (b) a deal that includes every shareholder of both companies   (c) an acquisition paid for entirely with the buyer's own shares
(c) — an all-stock deal (n), also an all-share deal (UK). Consequence: the sellers become shareholders in the buyer, so they share its future success or failure. Contrast an all-cash deal.
2. After the purchase, managers had to combine two companies' systems, teams and ways of working without destroying what made the smaller one successful. This process of integration took years. Integration here means…
  (a) including people from many different countries   (b) bringing an acquired company's people, processes and systems together with the buyer's   (c) paying the acquired company's debts
(b) — integration (n), often post-merger integration. The most common reason acquisitions fail is not the price but the integration — especially when two cultures meet.

D · Collocation & word building

1. The whole value of the acquired company seemed to depend on two or three people. If they left, much of the value could leave with them. Analysts call this key-___ risk. Complete the term.
key-person risk (n), also key-man risk (older) — the danger that a business depends too heavily on a few individuals. Companies reduce it with succession plans, contracts that keep leaders for several years, and by spreading knowledge across teams.
2. A film that continues an earlier story is a ___quel. A film that tells what happened before it is a ___quel. Complete both words.
a sequel and a prequel. Sequel rights — the right to make follow-up films — were at the centre of this case. In business generally, a sequel can also mean a follow-up product: “The sequel to last year's campaign.”

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — The partner that was too successful

In 1986 Steve Jobs, recently forced out of Apple, bought a small computer-graphics group from the film-maker George Lucas's company for around ten million dollars. It became Pixar. For years it lost money; it sold expensive graphics computers and made short animated films to show what its software could do, and Jobs kept paying to keep it alive.

In 1991 Disney agreed to fund and distribute three computer-animated feature films made by Pixar. The first, Toy Story, was released in 1995 — the first feature film made entirely by computer — and took more than 350 million dollars worldwide. A week later, Pixar sold shares on the stock market. In 1997 the two companies signed a new deal for five more films. They would share costs and profits roughly equally, but Disney would own the films and the characters, earn a distribution fee, and hold the rights to make sequels.

Pixar then produced hit after hit: A Bug's Life, Toy Story 2, Monsters, Inc. and Finding Nemo, which took more than 850 million dollars. Over the same years, Disney's own animation studio — the company's creative heart since Walt Disney's first feature — was releasing expensive films that disappointed at the box office. The partner was becoming more important to Disney's animation than Disney itself. And its owner knew it.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Disney receive under the 1997 agreement, apart from half the profits?

2. Why did the balance of power in the partnership shift towards Pixar?

1. Ownership of the films and characters, a distribution fee, and the rights to make sequels.

2. Pixar's films were consistently huge successes, while Disney's own animated films were disappointing, so Disney's animation business depended more and more on Pixar.

Discuss:
  • A contract signed when you are small can feel unfair when you become successful. Should a strong partner renegotiate, or honour the terms it agreed? Think of a situation from your own working life.

Part 2 — The break

Relations between Jobs and Disney's chief executive, Michael Eisner, became openly hostile. The companies argued about money, about control, and about whether Toy Story 2 counted as one of the films in the contract. In January 2004, after months of negotiation, Pixar announced that it was ending talks. Once its remaining films under the contract had been released, it would find another distributor. Disney would keep the rights to make sequels to Pixar's existing films — and it set up a separate unit to make them without Pixar, starting work on a third Toy Story.

At the same time, Eisner was facing a shareholder revolt. Roy E. Disney, Walt Disney's nephew, had resigned from the board and was campaigning to remove him, criticising, among other things, the damage to the Pixar relationship and the state of Disney's animation. At the 2004 annual meeting, around forty-three per cent of shareholder votes were withheld from Eisner. He lost the role of chairman and, the following year, left the company.

His successor, Bob Iger, took over in October 2005. Iger later wrote that, at the opening of Hong Kong Disneyland shortly before he became chief executive, he watched the parade and realised that almost none of the characters created in the previous decade came from Disney's own studio; the popular new ones were Pixar's. Disney had a choice: rebuild its animation slowly, let Pixar go to a rival, or try something that looked like an admission of failure.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Disney plan to do after Pixar ended the negotiations?

2. Why was Eisner's position weakened in 2004–2005?

1. Use its sequel rights to make follow-ups to Pixar films without Pixar, through a separate unit that began working on a third Toy Story.

2. A shareholder campaign led by Roy E. Disney criticised his leadership, including the Pixar relationship and the decline of Disney animation; at the 2004 annual meeting about 43% of votes were withheld from him, he lost the chairman role, and he left in 2005.

Discuss:
  • Iger's realisation came from watching a parade, not from a report. Where do you get the information that changes your mind about strategy — and how much of it appears in formal reports?

Part 3 — The buyer that let itself be changed

In January 2006 Disney agreed to buy Pixar for around 7.4 billion dollars, paid in Disney shares. Jobs became Disney's largest individual shareholder and joined its board. The unusual part was the arrangement for people. Pixar's co-founder Ed Catmull became president of both Pixar and Disney's own animation studio, and Pixar's creative leader, John Lasseter, became chief creative officer of both. Pixar kept its name, its campus in California and its way of working; the unit that had been making Pixar sequels without Pixar was closed. In effect, Disney bought a company and then asked it to rescue Disney's animation.

The results took several years. Disney Animation, under Pixar's leaders, released Tangled in 2010 and Frozen in 2013, which took around 1.3 billion dollars, followed by Zootopia and Moana. Pixar continued to produce major hits. The deal became a model of acquisition: buy a culture, protect it, and let it spread. But the model depended on people. In 2018 Lasseter left the company after allegations about his behaviour at work; Catmull retired the following year.

In the 2020s Pixar's position changed. During the pandemic, several of its films were released directly on Disney's streaming service, and some Pixar staff said publicly that this made their work feel less special. Lightyear, in 2022, performed well below expectations, and in 2024 Pixar cut a significant number of jobs as Disney reduced costs. That same year Inside Out 2 took around 1.7 billion dollars, and Pixar announced more sequels. So the question is still open: in the long run, did the company Disney bought change Disney — or did Disney, slowly, change Pixar?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was unusual about the way Disney organised the leadership after the purchase?

2. Why does the case show that the success of the deal depended heavily on individuals?

1. Pixar's leaders were put in charge of Disney's own animation studio as well as Pixar, Pixar kept its name, campus and methods, and Disney's unit making Pixar sequels was closed — the acquired company led the buyer's creative business.

2. The revival was led by two Pixar executives; when one left in 2018 after workplace-conduct allegations and the other retired in 2019, the protection of Pixar's culture no longer rested with the people who had designed it, and Pixar's direction in the 2020s changed.

Discuss:
  • Protecting an acquired team's culture depends on the people who promised to protect it. What happens to such promises in your experience when those people leave?

Grammar — the future in the past

Deal stories and post-mortems are told from a point in the past, looking forward: what people planned, what nearly happened, and what they could not yet know. English has a precise set of structures for this. At C1 they let you tell a story with suspense and honesty — separating what was intended from what actually happened.

1 · was / were going to — a plan or intention, often not carried out
• “We were going to open in Madrid, but the permit never came.”
• Also a prediction based on evidence at the time: “It was clear the quarter was going to be difficult.”
2 · would — the future seen from the past
• In reported speech: “The CEO said the merger would close in March.”
• In narrative, often with hindsight: “Nobody knew then that the small investment would transform the business.”
3 · was about to / was on the point of — very near, often interrupted
• “The bank was about to withdraw its offer when the call came.”
• “They were on the point of signing when the auditors raised a concern.”
4 · was to / was due to / was to have + past participle — formal arrangements, and destiny
• A schedule: “The plant was due to open in 2020.”
• A plan that did not happen: “She was to have led the project, but she resigned.”
• Destiny, in formal narrative: “It was a meeting that was to change the direction of the company.”

1. Choose the structure

Complete each sentence with the structure described in brackets. Write only the missing words.

1. In 2004 the smaller studio find a new distributor once its contract ended. (an intention — be going to)

2. At the time, nobody guessed that the smaller company's leaders end up running the bigger company's animation. (future seen from the past — one word)

3. The negotiators leave the building when a new offer arrived. (very near future — be about to)

4. The film open in November, but it was delayed until the following spring. (a schedule — be due to)

In sentence 3, were agrees with the negotiators. Check the subject before you choose was or were.

Going deeper

A note on the figures: the purchase price, box-office takings and voting percentages are rounded and taken from public reporting, and film revenues are not adjusted for inflation. The relationship between the two studios is still developing.

What the case actually tests. The first reading is that the buyer showed rare humility. Most acquirers buy a company for its products and then absorb it into their own systems, often destroying what made it valuable. Disney did the reverse: it protected Pixar's autonomy, put Pixar's leaders in charge of its own struggling studio, and closed the unit that had been designed to replace Pixar. It paid a very high price for what was, in effect, a culture and a group of people — and the revival of Disney Animation suggests that was the right thing to buy.

The second reading is less generous. Disney did not choose humility from strength; it had very few options. Its own animation was weak, its partner was leaving, and Pixar's owner could take the studio to a rival. Part of the price reflected what Disney had given up in earlier negotiations and lost in a public conflict. And the success depended heavily on two individuals, which is exactly the kind of key-person risk that deal analysis warns about. When those people left, the protection became a policy without its authors.

A third point is about time. A deal can succeed for ten years and still change shape over twenty. In the 2020s the parent company's priorities — a streaming service to fill, costs to cut, reliable sequels to release — began to shape the acquired studio. That is not necessarily failure; a company that keeps succeeding may have to change. But it raises the question every acquisition eventually faces: autonomy is easy to promise when the acquired business is saving you, and much harder to maintain when you need it to serve the group.
Talk it through:
  • Humility or lack of options? Take one side and argue it with at least two facts from the case.
  • If your organisation bought a smaller, more creative competitor, what would you protect — and what would you insist on changing from the first day?
  • How would you reduce key-person risk in an acquisition whose value depends on a small number of leaders?
  • Is it inevitable that, over time, a parent company shapes the business it bought? What would stop it?

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

It is 2006. You are the new chief executive announcing the deal. An analyst says: “You're paying over seven billion dollars for a company that releases about one film a year, and you're handing your own animation studio to its managers. Isn't that simply admitting that Disney can no longer do the one thing it was founded to do?”

Answer properly: concede what is true about your studio's recent record, explain what you are really buying, describe how the value will be protected, and end with how the deal should be judged. Then do it again in ninety seconds — the compression is the exercise.

Task C · Four rooms, one acquisition (4 minutes)

Take one acquisition — real or invented — and announce it four ways, one minute each. In at least two rooms, tell a short story about what was about to happen before the deal.

  • To the board: the price, the risk, and what you expect in five years.
  • To the buyer's own staff, whose department will now be led by the acquired company's managers: honest about the reason, respectful about their work.
  • To the acquired company's staff: what you promise to protect, and how they can hold you to it.
  • To customers or partners: what will be better, and what will stay exactly the same.

Prefer not to use your own organisation? Take one of these instead:

  • A publishing group buying an independent games studio.
  • A national hotel chain buying a small boutique hotel group famous for its service.
