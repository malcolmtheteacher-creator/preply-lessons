Rhetorical Devices

C1 Level · Spotting & Using Persuasive Language

Why does some writing stick?

A newspaper article from last week has already blurred in your memory. But a speech from decades ago, an advert jingle, a single sentence from a novel — those can stay with you for years.

That is not an accident. Writers and speakers use rhetorical devices — deliberate patterns of language — to make their ideas land harder, travel further, and stay longer in the reader's head.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to name the main devices, spot them in an unseen text, and use them in your own writing and speaking.

1. Think of an advert, speech, or line from a book that has stayed with you. What made it memorable?

Common reasons include: a surprising image, repetition, a short punchy rhythm, a question aimed at you directly, a strong emotional word, or a contrast between two ideas. These are all rhetorical devices doing their job.

2. In your own first language, what makes a speaker sound persuasive? Is it the same or different in English?

The devices are mostly universal — rhythm, repetition, contrast, emotional language — but the register differs. English persuasion often prefers understatement, rhetorical questions, and the rule of three, while some languages prefer long, layered sentences or stronger emotional language.

3. When you read an argument you disagree with, what makes you still finish reading it? What makes you stop?

You keep reading when the writer sounds credible (ethos), reasonable (logos), and occasionally makes you feel something (pathos). You stop when the language feels exaggerated, aggressive, or dishonest — often because the devices are too heavy-handed or one-sided.

Before moving on:

Rhetorical devices are tools. They are not tricks. Good writers use them to make a true point clearer. Bad writers use them to make a weak point sound stronger than it is. Part of being a strong reader is noticing the difference.

Ethos, Pathos, Logos

Aristotle identified three ways to persuade an audience. Nearly every rhetorical device works by strengthening one of these three appeals. Learn to name the appeal first, and the device will be easier to spot second.

ETHOS

Credibility — "You can trust me."

What it does: Builds the writer's authority. Makes you believe the person speaking, regardless of the argument.

How to spot it: References to experience, qualifications, shared values, or balanced acknowledgement of the other side.

"As a doctor who has treated this condition for twenty years, I can say with confidence that..."

Why writers use it: If readers don't trust the speaker, they will not accept any argument, no matter how clever.

PATHOS

Emotion — "Feel what I feel."

What it does: Moves the reader. Makes them angry, hopeful, scared, or inspired enough to act.

How to spot it: Vivid imagery, personal anecdotes, emotionally loaded words (e.g. children, suffering, home, dream), exclamations.

"A child shouldn't have to choose between a textbook and a hot meal. Yet that is the choice thousands made last winter."

Why writers use it: Decisions are rarely purely logical. Emotion is what turns agreement into action.

LOGOS

Logic — "The numbers agree with me."

What it does: Appeals to reason. Uses evidence, statistics, cause-and-effect reasoning, or examples.

How to spot it: Numbers, percentages, research citations, logical connectors (therefore, as a result, it follows that), cause-and-effect chains.

"Three studies in the last decade — in Helsinki, Oslo, and Toronto — show the same result: later school start times raise test scores by roughly 7%."

Why writers use it: Gives the reader permission to agree. Logos lets people say "I'm not being emotional; the evidence is clear."

Spot the appeal

For each short quote, decide which appeal is doing most of the work. Most texts mix all three, but one will usually dominate.

A. "Having spent fifteen years in classrooms across three continents, I know how quickly a tired child disengages."

Primary: Ethos. The writer establishes personal credibility — fifteen years, three continents — before making any claim. There is a small touch of pathos at the end ("tired child disengages"), but the function of the sentence is to earn the reader's trust in the speaker.

B. "Imagine waking up tomorrow and finding that the river outside your window is gone. No sound. No movement. Just dry stones where generations fished."

Primary: Pathos. Direct address ("your window"), sensory imagery ("No sound. No movement."), and an emotional loss ("generations fished") all target the reader's feelings rather than their reason.

C. "If wages rise by 3% but prices rise by 5%, workers are poorer in real terms. This is not opinion; it is arithmetic."

Primary: Logos. Numbers, cause-and-effect logic, and the explicit contrast with "opinion" all position the argument as mathematical, not emotional. The final sentence is itself a rhetorical move: it dares the reader to disagree with arithmetic.

The Device Toolkit

Click each card to flip it. On the back you will find a short definition and an example. Learn these twelve and you will have a name for almost every device you meet in an unseen text.

Metaphor A direct comparison without "like" or "as". e.g. "Her words were daggers." Effect: makes the abstract concrete and emotionally charged.
Simile A comparison using "like" or "as". e.g. "The room felt as cold as a server farm." Effect: softer than metaphor — shows the comparison is deliberate.
Personification Giving human qualities to something non-human. e.g. "The city refused to sleep." Effect: makes abstract forces (cities, markets, nature) feel alive and morally responsible.
Imagery Vivid sensory description — sight, sound, touch, smell, taste. e.g. "The ash tasted like old coins." Effect: puts the reader inside the scene.
Rule of Three Three parallel items or phrases in a row. e.g. "Blood, sweat and tears." / "Faster, cheaper, safer." Effect: rhythmic, memorable, feels complete.
Anaphora Repeating the same word or phrase at the start of successive clauses. e.g. "We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds..." Effect: builds momentum, hammers the message home.
Rhetorical Question A question that isn't meant to be answered — the answer is assumed. e.g. "How much longer can we ignore this?" Effect: pulls the reader in and makes them agree silently.
Juxtaposition Placing two contrasting ideas side by side. e.g. "Record profits; record redundancies." Effect: exposes hypocrisy, forces a judgement.
Hyperbole Deliberate exaggeration for effect. e.g. "I've said this a million times." Effect: signals strong feeling or frustration. Overuse sounds childish; sparing use lands hard.
Direct Address Speaking straight to the reader with "you". e.g. "You already know how this ends." Effect: breaks the distance, makes the reader feel implicated.
Inclusive "We" Using "we", "our", "us" to build a shared identity with the reader. e.g. "We cannot afford to look away." Effect: puts writer and reader on the same team.
Loaded Language Word choices with strong positive or negative connotations. e.g. "regime" vs "government", "scheme" vs "plan". Effect: smuggles a judgement into what looks like a neutral description.

A quick reminder

A device is not an effect. "Metaphor" is a tool; "makes abstract danger feel physical" is an effect. In analysis you always need both: what it is and what it does to the reader.

Spot the Technique

Three short passages from different genres. For each one, try to name at least two devices and say what effect they have on the reader. Then reveal the analysis and compare.

Passage 1 — Political speech (closing lines)

We did not come this far to stop here. We did not raise a generation on broken promises to hand them another one. We owe them better, we owe ourselves better, and — yes — we owe history better.

So tonight the question is not whether change is possible. The question is whether we are ready to be the ones who change it.

— Fictional speech extract, built for practice

Name the techniques. What effect do they have?

  • Anaphora — "We did not... we did not..." and "we owe... we owe... we owe..." Hammers the obligation into the reader.
  • Rule of three — "we owe them... we owe ourselves... we owe history." Gives the sentence rhythm and a sense of completeness.
  • Inclusive "we" — binds speaker and audience into one group, making refusal feel like betrayal of the self.
  • Rhetorical question — "whether we are ready to be the ones..." doesn't expect an answer; it creates the feeling that the audience has already said yes.
  • Overall appeal: mostly pathos (emotional duty) supported by ethos (the speaker speaks as one of "us").

Passage 2 — Travel writing

The market wakes before the city does. By five, the fishmongers are already sharpening knives, and the air smells of salt and diesel and yesterday's coffee. A woman calls out prices in a voice that has clearly been doing this since before I was born.

The tourists come later, cameras up, looking for something authentic. They are, in a way, too late — the market has already sold its best hour to the locals.

— Fictional travel piece, built for practice

Name the techniques. What effect do they have?

  • Personification — "The market wakes"; the market "has already sold its best hour." The market is treated as a living character, which makes the writer's affection for it feel almost personal.
  • Sensory imagery — "salt and diesel and yesterday's coffee" lands smell and taste simultaneously; the rule of three inside the image gives it a rhythm.
  • Juxtaposition — locals in the early hour vs tourists "later, cameras up." Sets up the final judgement without stating it outright.
  • Ethos — "in a voice that has clearly been doing this since before I was born" positions the writer as an observer who notices; it earns authority quietly.
  • Overall effect: the reader feels present in the scene (imagery) and gently agrees with the writer's sly critique of tourism.

Passage 3 — Opinion column (opening)

Last year, a single tech firm generated more revenue than the entire gross domestic product of Portugal. Let that sit for a moment.

We are no longer dealing with companies. We are dealing with private nations — nations without citizens, without elections, and without any duty to us beyond a quarterly earnings call. And yet we still, politely, call them "companies." Why?

— Fictional opinion column, built for practice

Name the techniques. What effect do they have?

  • Logos / statistic opening — comparing a firm's revenue to Portugal's GDP. Grounds the argument in a number so the writer can later make a bigger claim.
  • Metaphor — "private nations." Reframes companies as political entities rather than economic ones; a whole argument in two words.
  • Rule of three / anaphora — "without citizens, without elections, without any duty." The repetition makes each absence feel heavier than the last.
  • Loaded language — "politely" is the crucial word. It implies our language is complicit — we are too well-mannered to call the thing what it is.
  • Rhetorical question — "Why?" Short, blunt, forces the reader to keep reading.
  • Overall appeal: logos (the number) opens the door, but pathos (outrage, complicity) carries the reader through it.

Pattern to notice:

In every passage above, two or three devices are doing the work together. One device alone is a trick. Two or three devices working in the same direction is rhetoric.

Self-check — once you've finished writing

Read your paragraph aloud. Then tick honestly:

1. Which devices did you actually use? Can you underline them in your own text?

If you cannot point to the device on the page, you did not really use it. Strong writing has devices you can highlight — a clear rule of three, a named metaphor, a specific rhetorical question. Vague "emotional language" does not count.

2. Which appeal does your paragraph lean on most — ethos, pathos, or logos?

Most C1 writers over-rely on pathos. If your paragraph is mostly emotional, add one sentence of logos (a statistic, a cause-and-effect link) or a touch of ethos (a reason the reader should trust the voice). A mix of two appeals almost always beats a pure one.

3. Where did you resist the temptation of hyperbole? Where did you let it in too easily?

If every sentence is a dramatic claim, the dramatic claims stop working. One moment of hyperbole, placed carefully, does more than five unplaced ones. The same applies to rhetorical questions — one is sharp, three in a row sounds desperate.

Next time you read an unseen text

Read it twice. On the first reading, note what it is saying. On the second, note what it is doing — where it uses anaphora, where it shifts to pathos, where a metaphor is quietly doing the real argument. That second reading is where rhetorical analysis lives.
