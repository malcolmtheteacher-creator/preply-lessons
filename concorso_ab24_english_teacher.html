The Three Parts of Your Oral

Part 1 — Lezione Simulata (Simulated Lesson)

You receive a traccia (topic rubric) 24 hours before your exam. You prepare and then deliver a lesson in English to the commission as if they were your students. The topic could be a grammar point ("the present perfect"), a literary text, a cultural topic ("Romanticism"), or a pedagogical scenario ("a B1 class with three DSA students").

You must present a full lesson plan and deliver part of the lesson. Up to 22 minutes maximum.

Part 2 — Disciplinary Questions

After your lesson, the commission asks questions in English about: your methodological choices (why did you use that approach?), ELT theory (SLA, CLT, CLIL, formative assessment), English linguistics, and Anglo-American literature and culture. You draw a separate topic at the start of the exam for this section.

Part 3 — Oral Interaction in English

Throughout the exam, the commission assesses your spoken English: fluency, accuracy, range of vocabulary, pronunciation, and ability to discuss complex topics at C1 level. This is not a separate section — it runs through everything you say.

📋 Model Lesson Plan Framework

Class
Year 3, Liceo Scientifico — 22 students, B1+ level (3 DSA students with PDPs)
Topic
Reported Speech — Backshift of Tenses
Prerequisites
Students can form direct speech correctly. They know basic tense forms (present simple, past simple, present perfect).
Learning Objectives
By the end of the lesson, students will be able to: (1) convert direct speech to reported speech applying correct tense backshift; (2) use reporting verbs (said, told, asked, explained) accurately; (3) identify when backshift is optional.
Methodology
PPP (Presentation–Practice–Production) with communicative elements. Inductive grammar discovery at presentation stage.
Lesson Stages
Warm-up (5 min): Telephone game — a message changes as it passes; students notice meaning is preserved but language changes.
Presentation (10 min): Students read two versions of a dialogue (direct/reported) and discover the backshift rule inductively. Teacher elicits and formalises.
Practice (15 min): Controlled gap-fill, then semi-controlled transformation exercise. Pair checking.
Production (15 min): Role play — one student interviews another; a third reports the conversation to the class.
Assessment (5 min): Exit ticket — two sentences to convert independently.
ICT Integration
Padlet for collecting production examples (anonymous — reduces anxiety). Short authentic video clip (news report) for input at presentation stage.
Differentiation (BES/DSA)
DSA students receive a colour-coded reference card showing tense backshift patterns. Extended time on written tasks. Audio version of the video with subtitles.
Assessment
Formative: teacher monitoring during practice, peer correction, exit ticket. Summative: incorporated into next written test.

ELT Methodology — Key Concepts

The commission will ask you to justify your choices and explain theories. Know these thoroughly — and be ready to connect theory to your classroom practice.

Communicative Language Teaching (CLT)

The dominant paradigm in ELT since the 1970s. The primary goal of language learning is communicative competence — the ability to use language appropriately in real contexts. Activities are meaning-focused; errors are tolerated if communication succeeds.

Key figures: Hymes (communicative competence), Canale & Swain (four components: grammatical, sociolinguistic, discourse, strategic).

Exam question: "Why did you choose a communicative approach for this lesson?" → "Because at B1+ level, students already have the structural knowledge; the priority is activating that knowledge for genuine communication. CLT reflects the understanding, from Hymes onwards, that knowing the rules of a language is insufficient — one must know when and how to use them."

Presentation–Practice–Production (PPP)

A three-stage lesson structure: the teacher presents a new language item, students practise it in controlled exercises, then produce it freely. Traditional, widely used for grammar teaching.

Criticism: Skehan and others argue it over-emphasises form and doesn't reflect naturalistic acquisition. But at secondary school level, with rule-governed grammar points, PPP remains appropriate and efficient.

Know when to defend it: "While PPP is sometimes criticised as behaviourist in orientation, for explicit grammar instruction at this level, the structured sequence gives learners the cognitive clarity they need before attempting free production."

Task-Based Language Teaching (TBL / TBLT)

Students complete a meaningful task (not a language exercise) as the vehicle for learning. The task comes first; language focus is derived from what students needed to complete it. Based on Ellis's and Willis's frameworks.

Stages: Pre-task → Task → Planning → Report → Language focus → Practice.

Contrast with PPP: "Where PPP begins with a language item and builds towards communication, TBL inverts this — communication drives the lesson and language is focused on in response to what learners needed."

Content and Language Integrated Learning (CLIL)

Teaching a non-language subject (history, science, geography) through the medium of English. The 4Cs framework: Content, Communication, Cognition, Culture. Increasingly mandated in Italian secondary schools.

Key principle: Language is learned incidentally while attention is on content; there is a "dual focus" on both simultaneously.

Common exam question: "How would you apply CLIL principles in a history lesson about the British Empire?" Be ready to distinguish between hard CLIL (full subject delivery in English) and soft CLIL (some English integrated into Italian-medium lessons).

Form-Focused Instruction (FFI)

Deliberate attention to language form within a communicative context. Two types: Focus on FormS (isolated grammar teaching, PPP-style) vs. Focus on Form (reactive correction or grammar attention within communicative tasks).

Know Long's (1991) argument that purely communicative instruction is insufficient — learners need some explicit attention to form to notice features they would otherwise skip over.

Second Language Acquisition Theory

The commission may ask you to ground your methodology in SLA theory. Know these and be able to connect them to classroom decisions.

Krashen's Input Hypothesis Acquisition occurs when learners receive "comprehensible input" — language slightly beyond their current level (i+1). Comprehension precedes production. Monitor Model: acquisition vs. learning distinction.
Swain's Output Hypothesis Comprehensible input alone is insufficient. Learners need to produce output — speaking and writing — to notice gaps in their knowledge and develop accuracy. "Pushed output" forces precision.
Vygotsky's ZPD Zone of Proximal Development — the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with support. Scaffolding helps learners operate in the ZPD. Basis for cooperative learning and teacher support strategies.
Long's Interaction Hypothesis Acquisition is facilitated by interaction, especially negotiation of meaning — when learners clarify, confirm, and repair communication breakdowns. Justifies pair and group work.
Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis Learners must consciously notice a feature in the input for it to be acquired. Mere exposure is insufficient. Justifies consciousness-raising tasks and form-focused instruction.
Affective Filter (Krashen) A metaphorical barrier that rises when learners are anxious, unmotivated, or lack confidence — blocking input from reaching the "language acquisition device." Low-anxiety classrooms facilitate acquisition.

Commission Questions — Methodology

"Why did you use pair work rather than teacher-led instruction for the practice stage?"

Draw on: Long's Interaction Hypothesis + Vygotsky's ZPD.

"Pair work creates opportunities for negotiation of meaning — learners clarify, confirm, and repair communication in ways that teacher-led drill cannot replicate. From a Vygotskian perspective, learners can support each other in the Zone of Proximal Development; a peer explanation is often more accessible than a teacher's because it comes from someone who has just worked through the same confusion. It also dramatically increases individual speaking time — in a class of 22, teacher-fronted practice means each student may speak for under a minute, while pair work gives everyone sustained practice."

"What is the difference between acquisition and learning in Krashen's model, and does it matter for classroom teachers?"

Key distinction + classroom implication:

"Krashen distinguishes between acquisition — subconscious internalisation through meaningful exposure, the way children acquire their first language — and learning, the conscious study of rules. His controversial claim is that learned knowledge cannot become acquired knowledge: the 'non-interface' position. While this has been challenged — most notably by Ellis, who argues for a 'weak interface' — the distinction does have practical value: it reminds teachers that explicit grammar rules alone will not produce fluent speakers. Learners need sustained meaningful input and output opportunities, not just metalinguistic knowledge."

"How would you handle error correction in a communicative activity without inhibiting fluency?"

Distinguish recasts vs. explicit correction + affective filter:

"During a communicative activity, the priority is fluency — Krashen's affective filter hypothesis tells us that anxiety blocks acquisition, so stopping students mid-utterance to correct form is often counterproductive. I use recasting: I reformulate the error correctly in my response without drawing explicit attention to it. I also use delayed feedback — I note errors on a slip of paper and address them after the activity in a brief 'language focus' phase, asking the class to correct anonymised examples. This preserves flow during the task while ensuring accuracy is still addressed."

If the commission asks "Which theory of second language acquisition do you find most convincing, and why?", what would your answer be? Think it through before looking at any notes.

English Literature — Key Periods & Authors

The commission may draw a literary topic and ask you to discuss it, connect it to a teaching context, or analyse a brief extract. Know the major periods and their most important figures.

Renaissance & Elizabethan (1485–1660)

Key figures: Shakespeare, Christopher Marlowe, Edmund Spenser, John Donne, John Milton.

Characteristics: Humanism, classical influence, exploration of individual identity, blank verse, the sonnet form. The theatre as public discourse.

Teaching angle: Shakespeare's language is excellent for teaching figurative language, dramatic monologue, and inference at B2–C1 level.

Romanticism (1785–1830)

Key figures: William Blake, Wordsworth, Coleridge, Byron, Shelley, Keats.

Characteristics: Reaction against Enlightenment rationalism; elevation of emotion, imagination, and nature; the sublime; the Romantic hero; the Gothic.

Teaching angle: Romantic poetry is accessible at B1+ for reading comprehension; excellent for descriptive and persuasive writing tasks.

Victorian Age (1830–1901)

Key figures: Dickens, the Brontës, George Eliot, Thomas Hardy, Oscar Wilde, Robert Browning.

Characteristics: Social realism, industrialisation, class critique, empire, the rise of the novel, dramatic monologue in poetry.

Teaching angle: Dickens extracts work well for narrative analysis and social comment at B2 level.

Modernism (1900–1945)

Key figures: T.S. Eliot, Virginia Woolf, James Joyce, W.B. Yeats, D.H. Lawrence, Ezra Pound.

Characteristics: Stream of consciousness, fragmentation, rejection of linear narrative, interior monologue, mythic method (Eliot), free verse.

Teaching angle: Challenging at C1 level — useful for close reading, inference, and discussing narrative technique.

Post-War & Contemporary (1945–present)

Key figures: Orwell, Beckett, Harold Pinter, Seamus Heaney, Kazuo Ishiguro, Zadie Smith, Salman Rushdie.

Characteristics: Absurdism, post-colonialism, multiculturalism, unreliable narrators, dystopian fiction, confessional poetry.

Teaching angle: Contemporary fiction is highly motivating; Orwell's essays and Ishiguro's prose work at C1 for argument and style analysis.

American Literature — Brief Overview

Key figures: Hawthorne, Whitman, Emily Dickinson, F. Scott Fitzgerald, Hemingway, Faulkner, Toni Morrison, Arthur Miller, Langston Hughes.

Themes: The American Dream, race and identity, Puritanism, the frontier, the Lost Generation, the Harlem Renaissance, the Civil Rights Movement.

Teaching angle: Miller's Death of a Salesman and Fitzgerald's The Great Gatsby are frequently used in Italian licei at C1 level.

Linguistics — Key Areas

Questions may come from any branch of linguistics. Know the terminology and be able to give examples.

Phonology The study of sound systems. Key terms: phoneme (the minimal unit of sound), allophone (variants of a phoneme), minimal pair (e.g. /p/ vs /b/ in "pin/bin"), syllable structure, connected speech (assimilation, elision, linking).
Morphology The study of word structure. Morpheme = smallest unit of meaning. Free morphemes (can stand alone: "cat") vs. bound morphemes (prefixes/suffixes: "un-", "-ing"). Derivation (new word: "happy → happiness") vs. inflection (grammatical change: "walk → walked").
Syntax The study of sentence structure. Phrase structure, constituent analysis, transformation rules. English is a Subject–Verb–Object (SVO) language. Key concepts: subordination, coordination, embedding, passivisation.
Semantics The study of meaning. Lexical semantics: synonymy, antonymy, hyponymy, polysemy, homonymy. Propositional meaning vs. connotation. Sense vs. reference.
Pragmatics Meaning in context — what speakers mean vs. what sentences mean. Key concepts: speech acts (Austin, Searle), Grice's Cooperative Principle and maxims (quantity, quality, relation, manner), implicature, deixis, politeness theory (Brown & Levinson).
Discourse Analysis How texts and conversations are structured. Cohesion (grammatical/lexical links within a text) vs. coherence (logical/semantic unity). Turn-taking, adjacency pairs, genre conventions.

Commission Questions — Literature & Linguistics

"How would you teach a poem by Keats to a Year 5 Liceo Classico class at C1 level?"

"I would begin with a pre-reading task to activate schema — asking students what they know about Romanticism and what emotions they associate with autumn or beauty. Then I'd use a scaffolded close reading: students read the poem once for gist, then again with guiding questions targeting figurative language (the extended metaphor, the personification). A key C1 skill is moving from surface comprehension to interpretive analysis — so I'd ask them to evaluate how Keats uses sound (the open vowels in 'Season of mists and mellow fruitfulness') to create mood. For production, I might ask them to write a short comparative paragraph or a creative imitation. ICT: the British Library has audio recordings of Romantic poems — listening to a professional reading before visual analysis often unlocks the musicality students miss when reading silently."

"Explain the difference between phonetics and phonology."

"Phonetics is the study of speech sounds as physical phenomena — it describes how sounds are produced (articulatory phonetics), transmitted (acoustic phonetics), and perceived (auditory phonetics). It is language-neutral. Phonology, by contrast, is language-specific: it studies how sounds function within a particular language system — which distinctions are meaningful, how sounds vary in context, and how they pattern. The classic example is aspiration in English: [p] and [pʰ] are separate sounds phonetically, but in English they are allophones of the same phoneme /p/ — the distinction is phonologically irrelevant in English, though it is phonemic in other languages such as Thai."

"What do you understand by Grice's Cooperative Principle, and how is it relevant to language teaching?"

"Grice proposed that conversation works because participants implicitly follow a Cooperative Principle: make your contribution what is required. This operates through four maxims — Quantity (say enough, not too much), Quality (say what you believe to be true), Relation (be relevant), and Manner (be clear and orderly). When speakers appear to violate a maxim, listeners infer an implicature — an unstated meaning. For example, if someone asks 'Can you pass the salt?' and I respond 'Yes' without moving, I've flouted Relation. Teaching this to C1 students unlocks a huge area of pragmatic competence: humour, irony, indirect speech acts, and politeness strategies all depend on the ability to read implicatures. It's directly applicable to teaching authentic dialogue and literary texts where characters say one thing and mean another."

Post-Simulation Commission Questions

After your lesson, the commission will ask follow-up questions. Practise answering these without preparation time.

"You used PPP. Are you aware of the criticisms of this approach, and would you consider an alternative?"

"Yes — critics such as Skehan and Willis argue that PPP imposes a linear path onto a process that isn't linear. Learners don't acquire structures in the order we teach them, and the 'practice makes perfect' assumption reflects a behaviourist model that SLA research hasn't fully supported. For this particular class and topic, however — a first encounter with a clearly rule-governed structure at A2+/B1 — I believe PPP is defensible. The alternative I would consider is a TBL approach: give students a task that requires the present perfect before any explicit teaching, observe the gaps in their output, then use those gaps as the focus of a form-focused phase. This is arguably more acquisition-friendly, but for a formal exam context and a lower-level class encountering a new form for the first time, I chose PPP for its clarity and manageability."

"How does the present perfect function differently in British English compared to American English?"

"In standard British English, the present perfect is typically used for recently completed events with present relevance — 'I've just eaten', 'Have you seen the news?' In American English, the simple past is frequently used in identical contexts — 'Did you eat yet?' 'I just ate.' This is a genuine grammatical divergence, not simply informal usage. For Italian learners following an Italian curriculum aligned to British English norms, this is worth acknowledging: they will encounter American English in authentic input, especially in media, and should understand that the past simple in those contexts is not an error but a regional variant. It also makes for a genuinely interesting discussion of World Englishes and the status of the CEFR, which is primarily based on European norms."

"What digital tools would you use to assess learning during and after this lesson?"

"During the lesson, I would use Mentimeter or Kahoot for quick concept checks — multiple-choice questions projected on the board give me immediate class-level data. For the production stage, a shared Padlet allows students to post their role-play transcripts anonymously, which I can review in real time and highlight strong examples or persistent errors without singling anyone out. After the lesson, Google Forms works well for a brief exit ticket — five targeted sentences to convert — and the automated data lets me identify which learners are struggling before the next class. For DSA students, I would allow voice-recorded responses rather than written exit tickets, using a tool like Vocaroo or the voice memo function in Google Classroom."

"Tell me about a literary text you would use in a Year 5 class at C1 level and explain how you would approach it."

"I would use the opening chapter of Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. For a C1 class, Ishiguro is ideal: his prose is syntactically clear enough to be accessible, but his meaning is layered — Stevens, the narrator, is famously unreliable, and everything he says must be read against what he is clearly not saying. This trains students in the C1 skill of inference and critical reading. I would structure the lesson around the concept of the unreliable narrator: first, close reading for what Stevens explicitly claims about dignity and professional service; then discussion of what his language reveals about repressed emotion; finally a writing task asking students to rewrite the same scene from a different character's perspective, forcing them to confront the gap between Stevens's version and a more objective account. The novel also opens into discussions of post-war British identity and the decline of empire — rich cultural content for a Liceo Classico or Scientifico."
