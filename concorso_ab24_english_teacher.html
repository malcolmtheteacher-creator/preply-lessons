Lesson Planning — Say it before you see it

🎙 Speak first
Imagine the commission asks: "Walk us through the structure of your lesson plan." Name every component you'd include and briefly explain why each one matters.
Model lesson plan framework:
  • Classe & livello — e.g., Classe 3ª liceo linguistico, B1+/B2
  • Argomento / Topic — the traccia topic, stated clearly
  • Obiettivi di apprendimento — specific, measurable learning outcomes (not activities)
  • Prerequisiti — what learners already know / assumed prior knowledge
  • Materiali e risorse — board, slides, handouts, audio, video — with rationale
  • Fasi della lezione — Warm-up → Presentation → Practice → Production → Wrap-up (or PPP / TBL sequence)
  • Valutazione formativa — how you check understanding during the lesson (ICQs, CCQs, peer feedback)
  • Inclusione / DSA — adaptations for learners with dyslexia, attention difficulties, etc.
  • Compito a casa — meaningful extension task (optional for 22-min simulation)
🎙 Speak first
What is the difference between a learning objective and a learning activity? Give one example of each for a B2 grammar lesson on reported speech.
Objectives vs activities:

An objective is what the learner will be able to do by the end — it is measurable and learner-focused.
An activity is what happens in the classroom to help them get there — it is teacher/task-focused.

  • Objective: "By the end of the lesson, students will be able to convert direct speech into reported speech using correct backshifting and pronoun changes in written sentences."
  • Activity: "Students work in pairs to transform six direct speech sentences from a tabloid article into reported speech."

Tip: Write objectives using Bloom's verbs — identify, produce, distinguish, evaluate — not vague verbs like understand or know.

🎙 Speak first
The commission asks: "How would you adapt this lesson for a student with dyslexia?" Speak for 60 seconds without stopping. Use real strategies, not generalities.
DSA adaptations — concrete examples:
  • Font and layout: use Arial or OpenDyslexic, minimum 14pt, increased line spacing, avoid justified text
  • Colour overlays / backgrounds: cream or pastel backgrounds reduce visual stress
  • Chunking: break tasks into small, numbered steps rather than long multi-part instructions
  • Extended time: students with DSA have a legal right to 30% extra time on written tasks in Italy (L.170/2010)
  • Oral alternatives: allow spoken responses instead of written ones where the objective permits
  • Graphic organisers: mind maps, tables, and sentence frames reduce cognitive load
  • Text-to-speech / audio: provide reading tasks as audio alongside text
  • No penalty for spelling errors in communicative tasks unless spelling is the explicit learning goal

ELT Methodology & SLA — Speak it out

🎙 Speak first
Explain Communicative Language Teaching (CLT) to the commission as if introducing it to a colleague who has never heard of it. Include: core principle, typical activity types, and one criticism.
CLT — key points:

Core principle: Language learning happens through meaningful communication. The goal is communicative competence — the ability to use language appropriately in real social contexts — not just grammatical accuracy.

Typical activities: information-gap tasks, role plays, discussions, problem-solving tasks, jigsaw reading — all require learners to exchange real information.

Criticism: CLT can neglect explicit grammar instruction. In high-stakes exam contexts (like Italian licei with State exams), learners may develop fluency but have persistent accuracy gaps. Critics argue a balance of form and meaning is needed — hence the emergence of Focus on Form.

🎙 Speak first
Compare PPP and TBL. The commission asks: "Which would you choose for a B1 class learning the present perfect for the first time, and why?" Speak your answer before checking.
PPP vs TBL:

PPP (Presentation–Practice–Production)

Teacher presents the form → controlled practice (gap-fills, drills) → freer production. Linear, predictable, good for beginners and new structures. Risk: practice doesn't always lead to real acquisition.

TBL (Task-Based Language Teaching)

Pre-task → task → planning → report → language focus. Learners use whatever language they have to complete a real task. Language emerges from need. Better for fluency and noticing gaps.

For a B1 class, new present perfect: A case can be made for either, but many would argue PPP is appropriate here — learners need the form explicitly presented before they can use it. A good answer acknowledges the debate: "I'd use a PPP frame but move quickly to a meaningful task that requires the form, rather than drilling in isolation."

🎙 Speak first
Name six SLA theorists and, for each one, say one sentence that summarises their main contribution. Do this from memory.
SLA theorists — one-line summaries:
  • Krashen — acquisition (unconscious) differs from learning (conscious); comprehensible input (i+1) drives acquisition; the affective filter can block it.
  • Swain — Output is not just a product of acquisition but a trigger for it; pushed output forces learners to notice gaps and test hypotheses.
  • Vygotsky — Learning happens in the Zone of Proximal Development through interaction with a more capable other; scaffolding is central.
  • Long — Interaction and negotiation of meaning (the Interaction Hypothesis) provide the best conditions for acquisition.
  • Schmidt — Noticing is necessary for acquisition; learners must consciously attend to a form for it to enter the interlanguage system.
  • Selinker — Coined interlanguage: the systematic, rule-governed language system learners create between L1 and L2; fossilisation can occur.
🎙 Speak first
The commission asks: "How do you handle student errors in your classroom? Give us a principled answer." Think about: when to correct, how to correct, and what the research says.
Error correction — principled answer:

When to correct: During accuracy-focused tasks (controlled practice), correct immediately and explicitly. During fluency tasks (role plays, discussions), delay correction or use recasts — interrupting breaks communicative flow and raises the affective filter.

How to correct:

  • Recast — repeat the utterance correctly without explicitly flagging the error (implicit)
  • Clarification request — "Sorry, could you say that again?" — signals something was unclear
  • Elicitation — "You said you goed — can anyone help?" — students self- or peer-correct
  • Metalinguistic feedback — "That's almost right — think about the tense" — explicit without giving the answer

Research context: Long's Focus on Form suggests brief, timely attention to form within a communicative context is more effective than isolated drilling. Lyster & Ranta (1997) found recasts are common but elicitation leads to more uptake.

🎙 Speak first
Explain CLIL to the commission. Cover the four Cs and give one concrete example of a CLIL activity you could use in an English class in an Italian liceo scientifico.
CLIL — Content and Language Integrated Learning:

CLIL teaches a non-language subject through the medium of a foreign language. The four Cs framework (Coyle, Hood & Marsh) covers:

  • Content — subject matter knowledge (e.g., evolution, the water cycle)
  • Communication — language needed to access and discuss the content
  • Cognition — higher-order thinking skills: analysis, evaluation, problem-solving
  • Culture — intercultural awareness and the wider context of the topic

Example activity: In a liceo scientifico, students read a short English article on climate tipping points (Content), identify key vocabulary and discourse connectors (Communication), then debate in groups whether international agreements are sufficient (Cognition), comparing Italian and UK climate policy perspectives (Culture).

Key Terms — tap to flip

Affective Filter
Krashen: emotional barriers (anxiety, low motivation, poor self-image) that block comprehensible input from reaching the Language Acquisition Device. Lower filter = more acquisition.
Scaffolding
Temporary support (Vygotsky/Wood): sentence frames, partially completed tasks, worked examples. Removed gradually as learner gains independence (fading).
Focus on Form
Long: brief, reactive attention to a linguistic form during a communicative task — triggered by a learner error or communication breakdown. Contrasts with Focus on FormS (pre-planned grammar syllabus).
Interlanguage
Selinker: the evolving, rule-governed linguistic system a learner creates between L1 and L2. It is systematic, permeable, and dynamic — but can fossilise.
Noticing Hypothesis
Schmidt: intake only occurs when learners consciously notice a linguistic feature in input. Awareness is a prerequisite for acquisition — not just exposure.
Formative Assessment
Assessment for learning: CCQs, exit tickets, peer feedback, observation during tasks. Informs teaching in real time. Contrasts with summative (assessment of learning — grades, exams).

Literature & Linguistics — Your voice first

🎙 Speak first
Give the commission a brief map of English literary history. For each period, say: the approximate dates, two key features, and one author. Cover at least five periods.
Literary periods — overview:
  • Renaissance / Early Modern (1485–1660): humanism, the sonnet, drama. Authors: Shakespeare, Marlowe, Sidney.
  • Restoration & 18th century (1660–1785): satire, the novel as form, reason and order. Authors: Defoe, Swift, Fielding, Pope.
  • Romanticism (1785–1830): nature, emotion, the sublime, the individual. Authors: Wordsworth, Keats, Shelley, Blake.
  • Victorian (1830–1901): industrialism, social criticism, realism, serialised fiction. Authors: Dickens, Eliot, Hardy, Brontë.
  • Modernism (1901–1945): fragmented form, stream of consciousness, loss of certainty post-WWI. Authors: Woolf, Joyce, Eliot, Lawrence.
  • Postmodernism (1945–present): metafiction, intertextuality, unreliable narrators, cultural plurality. Authors: Pynchon, Carter, McEwan, Rushdie.
🎙 Speak first
The commission asks you to discuss Virginia Woolf. Speak for 90 seconds. Cover: her significance, the technique of stream of consciousness, and one work in detail.
Virginia Woolf — key points:

Significance: Central figure of British Modernism. Woolf rejected the plot-driven, chronological novel in favour of interiority — the texture of consciousness, perception, and time as felt experience.

Stream of consciousness: A narrative technique that mimics the continuous, associative flow of a character's thoughts. Unlike an interior monologue, it doesn't follow grammatical logic — it moves freely by association, sensation, and memory. Key devices: free indirect discourse, fragmented syntax, sensory detail as memory trigger.

Mrs Dalloway (1925): Set on a single day in post-WWI London. Clarissa Dalloway prepares for a party; Septimus Warren Smith, a shell-shocked veteran, moves towards suicide. The two characters never meet but their consciousness is woven together — Woolf uses time (Big Ben's chimes) and memory to blur past and present. Themes: trauma, social performance, the inner life vs. surface existence.

American literature in Italian licei:

Linguistics — speak before you see

🎙 Speak first
Distinguish phonetics from phonology. Give one example of a concept from each discipline that is relevant to teaching English pronunciation.
Phonetics vs phonology:

Phonetics is the physical study of speech sounds — how they are produced (articulatory), transmitted (acoustic), and perceived (auditory). It describes sounds without reference to meaning.

Phonology is the study of how sounds function within a particular language system — the rules governing sound patterns, syllable structure, stress, and intonation.

  • Phonetics example relevant to teaching: Knowing the difference between aspirated and unaspirated /p/ (as in pin vs spin) helps explain why Italian learners sometimes produce the sound incorrectly.
  • Phonology example relevant to teaching: Understanding English word stress patterns (noun/verb pairs: REcord vs reCORD) helps learners avoid miscommunication, since stress is phonemic in English.
🎙 Speak first
Explain Grice's Cooperative Principle and name all four maxims. For each maxim, give a classroom example of how a student might violate it.
Grice's Cooperative Principle:

Grice (1975) argued that speakers cooperate in conversation according to a general Cooperative Principle: "Make your contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange."

  • Maxim of Quantity: Be as informative as required, no more, no less. Violation: a student asked "What did you do at the weekend?" gives a 10-minute monologue, or answers only "Things."
  • Maxim of Quality: Do not say what you believe to be false. Violation: a student confidently provides a made-up definition during vocabulary work rather than admitting uncertainty.
  • Maxim of Relation (Relevance): Be relevant. Violation: a student answers a question about the text by talking about their own unrelated weekend experience.
  • Maxim of Manner: Be clear, brief, orderly; avoid ambiguity. Violation: a student gives a rambling, structureless spoken response that loses the thread of the argument.
🎙 Speak first
Explain the key concepts of morphology that might come up in the commission Q&A. Cover: morpheme, free vs bound morphemes, derivational vs inflectional morphology — with examples.
Morphology — key concepts:
  • Morpheme: the smallest unit of meaning in a language. Cats has two morphemes: cat + -s.
  • Free morpheme: can stand alone as a word (book, run, happy).
  • Bound morpheme: must attach to another morpheme (-ness, un-, -ed, pre-).
  • Derivational morphology: creates new words, often changing word class. happy → happiness (adj → noun); teach → teacher (verb → noun). Meaning changes.
  • Inflectional morphology: modifies a word for grammatical context without changing its class. walk → walked, walks, walking. English has relatively few inflections compared to Italian.
  • Relevance for EFL: Teaching prefixes/suffixes as a vocabulary strategy helps learners decode unknown words and expand productive vocabulary exponentially.

Key Terms — tap to flip

Stream of Consciousness
Narrative technique (Modernism) that renders the continuous, associative flow of a character's inner thoughts. Key authors: Woolf, Joyce. Key technique: free indirect discourse.
Free Indirect Discourse
Blends third-person narration with a character's inner voice. No "he thought that" tag. "She would never go back. Never." — whose view is this? Woolf and Austen use it extensively.
Phoneme
The smallest unit of sound that distinguishes meaning in a language. English has ~44 phonemes but only 26 letters — the source of many spelling/pronunciation difficulties.
Pragmatics
The study of how context shapes meaning beyond literal semantics. Includes speech acts (Austin, Searle), implicature (Grice), and politeness theory (Brown & Levinson).
Iceberg Theory
Hemingway: the dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water. Write with omission — the reader feels what is unsaid. Also called the theory of omission.
Metafiction
Postmodern technique: fiction that self-consciously draws attention to its own fictional nature. The text acknowledges its own artificiality. E.g., Fowles's The French Lieutenant's Woman.

Commission Q&A — speak first

🎙 Speak first
Commission question: "Why did you choose this particular method for this class? Would you have done anything differently with a weaker group?"
What a strong answer covers:
  • Name the method explicitly (PPP, TBL, CLT-based) and link it to the level and learning goal
  • Reference at least one theorist or principle (e.g., "This draws on Swain's Output Hypothesis — I wanted to push students to produce language, not just receive it")
  • For a weaker group: more scaffolding, shorter tasks, more controlled practice before production, visual supports, sentence frames, slower input
  • Show flexibility: "I'd keep the same task structure but pre-teach more vocabulary and provide a model text before asking them to produce their own"
🎙 Speak first
Commission question: "How does your lesson align with the national curriculum guidelines (Indicazioni Nazionali)?"
What a strong answer covers:
  • Reference the QCER/CEFR framework explicitly — the Indicazioni Nazionali are organised around CEFR levels
  • Mention the four macro-skills (reading, writing, listening, speaking) and explain which your lesson addressed
  • Reference competenze chiave di cittadinanza if relevant (critical thinking, collaboration, digital literacy)
  • For liceo linguistico: mention the international dimension, intercultural competence, and CLIL opportunities
  • Stay concrete: "The task I designed addresses the B2 descriptor for written production: 'can write clear, detailed text on a wide range of subjects'"
🎙 Speak first
Commission question: "If a student gave a completely wrong answer during your lesson, what would you do?"
What a strong answer covers:
  • Distinguish between a content error (factual misunderstanding) and a linguistic error (form/grammar) — the response differs
  • For content: use elicitation — ask the class, ask the student to reconsider, provide a hint, reframe the question
  • For language: use a recast during fluency work; use metalinguistic feedback or elicitation during accuracy work
  • Emphasise face-saving: never embarrass a student publicly; treat errors as learning opportunities, not failures
  • Reference Long's Focus on Form if appropriate: "If the error impedes communication, I'd address it briefly and return to the task"
