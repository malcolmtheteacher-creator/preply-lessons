Meet Arendt

Read one part at a time. After each part, close your eyes and answer the question before you check.

📖

A young philosopher

Hannah Arendt was born in 1906 near Hanover, Germany, into a Jewish family, and grew up in Königsberg. She studied philosophy with some of the most famous thinkers of the time, including Martin Heidegger and Karl Jaspers. In 1933, when the Nazis came to power, she was arrested by the Gestapo, the secret police, for collecting evidence of antisemitic propaganda. After eight days she was released, and she fled Germany.

Why did Arendt have to leave Germany in 1933?

The Gestapo arrested her for collecting evidence of antisemitic propaganda. After her release she fled the country.
🧳

A refugee with no country

In Paris she helped young Jewish refugees find new homes. In 1940 the French government held her, with other German women, in a camp in the south. She escaped in the confusion of France's defeat, and in 1941 she reached New York with almost nothing. Nazi Germany had taken away her citizenship, and for fourteen years she belonged to no country. In 1951 she published The Origins of Totalitarianism and became an American citizen.

How long was Arendt without any citizenship?

Fourteen years, from when Nazi Germany took it away until she became an American citizen in 1951.
⚖️

The trial in Jerusalem

In 1961 Arendt went to Jerusalem to report on the trial of Adolf Eichmann, a Nazi official who had organised the transport of millions of Jewish people to the death camps. She expected to see a monster. Instead she saw a dull, ordinary man who spoke in office phrases and said he was only doing his job. She called this "the banality of evil". Her book caused a huge argument, but it is still widely read today.

What surprised Arendt about Eichmann at his trial?

He wasn't a monster but a dull, ordinary man who used office language and said he was only doing his job.
Talk: Arendt's idea made many people angry, because it seemed to make Eichmann less of a monster. Why do you think people prefer to see great evil as the work of monsters?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. The dog's obedience was perfect: it sat down the moment she spoke.
obedience (n) — doing what you are told by a person, a law or a rule. Example: "The army trains soldiers in strict obedience."
2. He couldn't sleep; his conscience told him he should have spoken up.
conscience (n) — the inner sense that tells you what is right and wrong. Example: "My conscience wouldn't let me keep the money I found."
3. When the bridge fell down, people demanded that the engineers be held accountable.
accountable (adj) — expected to explain your actions and accept their results. Example: "Politicians must be accountable to the voters."
4. She didn't steal anything, but she kept the secret, so in a way she was complicit.
complicit (adj) — involved with others in something wrong, even if you only help or stay silent. Example: "By saying nothing, the staff became complicit in the fraud."
5. Choosing who to hire takes good judgement, not just a list of rules.
judgement (n) — the ability to make sensible decisions and form fair opinions. Example: "Trust your judgement: you know this situation better than anyone."
6. Everyone expected a dramatic speech, but his words were banal: the weather, the traffic, lunch.
banal (adj) — very ordinary and dull, with nothing special or interesting about it. Example: "The film had a banal ending: they got married and lived happily ever after."
7. When the officer asked to see his passport, he complied at once.
comply (v) — to obey a rule, a law or a request. Example: "All restaurants must comply with the new safety rules by June."

See it in action

Here is the idea in action, in an ordinary office. Tom's company tells him to process claims fast and to reject any form with one empty box. He does it perfectly. One day he rejects a sick woman's claim because a date is missing. He never thinks about her; she is just "case 4721". Tom isn't cruel. He simply isn't thinking. For Arendt, that is the danger. The answer is not to become a hero, but to stop and ask: What am I actually doing?

Hannah Arendt watched the trial of Adolf Eichmann, a Nazi official, in 1961. She expected a monster, but instead she saw an ordinary man who said he was only doing his job. She called this "the banality of evil": terrible harm done by people who simply stop thinking. For Arendt, the point is that we must always ask what we are really doing and judge it for ourselves.

Connect

Compare

Socrates and Arendt: Arendt admired Socrates, who examined everything and saw thinking as a silent conversation with yourself. How might that inner conversation stop someone from doing wrong?
If you keep talking with yourself about what you do, you must live with that inner partner afterwards. Arendt thought people who never have this conversation can do terrible things without noticing; Socrates' habit of examining is a kind of protection.
Kant and Arendt: At his trial, Eichmann claimed he had lived by Kant's idea of duty. Arendt said he had badly twisted it. What is the difference between doing your duty and simply obeying?
For Kant, duty means using your own reason to test whether a rule could be right for everyone. Eichmann turned it into obeying whatever the leader wanted. Kant asks you to think; Eichmann stopped thinking.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From Albert Camus

What did Camus mean by "the absurd"?

The clash between our need for meaning and a world that gives no answer.
From Ludwig Wittgenstein

What did Wittgenstein mean by "meaning is use"?

That a word gets its meaning from how people use it in real life, not from one fixed definition.
From Karl Marx

What did Marx say drives history?

Class struggle: the conflict between those who own things like factories and land, and those who must sell their labour for a wage.
From John Locke

What did Locke say about natural rights and government?

Everyone is born with rights to life, liberty and property. A government exists to protect them, rules only with the people's consent, and may be replaced if it abuses its power.
