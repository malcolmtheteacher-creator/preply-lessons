Use of English · Part 1

Multiple-choice cloze

For questions 1–4, read the text below and decide which answer (A, B, C or D) best fits each gap. There is an example at the beginning (0).

In 1976, a traditional double-hulled canoe called the Hōkūleʻa sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti without instruments of any kind. Its navigator, Mau Piailug, (0) relied entirely on the stars, the swell of the ocean, and the behaviour of seabirds. The voyage (1) ___ a system of wayfinding that European observers had long (2) ___ as a romantic myth. Show Answers

1. A (vindicated) — "to vindicate" = to prove right against opposition or doubt. "Settled" doesn't fit a "system." "Clarified" is too weak. "Pronounced" goes with judgement/sentence, not a system.

2. C (dismissed) — "to dismiss something as a myth" is the standard collocation: to wave it away as not worth taking seriously. "Condemned as" carries a moral weight that doesn't fit. "Ignored as" is grammatically wrong. "Mistaken" takes for, not as.

3. B (drawing) — "to draw on X" = to make use of X as a source. "Casting on" is a knitting term. "Carrying on" means continuing. "Applying" doesn't take "on" in this sense.

4. D (constituted) — "to constitute" = to be, to amount to. "Comprised" is the wrong direction (the whole comprises the parts). "Consisted" requires "of." "Contained" suggests inclusion, not identity.

Use of English · Part 2

Open cloze

For questions 5–8, read the text below and think of the word which best fits each gap. Use only one word in each gap. There is an example at the beginning (0).

Shortened practice — the real CAE Part 2 has 8 questions.

The skill of wayfinding has been with us (0) since the earliest human migrations. Long (5) the invention of any instrument, our species spread across the planet by reading the world it lived (6) — by paying close attention to what stayed the same and what changed. In a single generation, however, most of us have handed (7) that work to satellites. The question is no longer whether GPS can take us where we want to go; the question is what (8) being lost when navigating ceases to be something we do for ourselves.

Show Answers

5. before — "Long before the invention of any instrument" — fixed time phrase introducing what came before.

6. in — "the world it lived in" — a relative clause with the preposition stranded at the end (which world it lived in).

7. over — "to hand over X" = to surrender control of X. The phrasal verb captures the sense of giving up not just the task but the authority.

8. is — "what is being lost" — present continuous passive, describing what is happening to us right now.

Use of English · Part 3

Word formation

For questions 9–12, read the text below. Use the word given in capitals at the end of some of the lines to form a word that fits in the gap in the same line. There is an example at the beginning (0).

Shortened practice — the real CAE Part 3 has 8 questions.

Most modern (0) researchers agree that the skills of traditional wayfinders, far from being supernatural, can be precisely described. RESEARCH

Yet what looks (9) from the outside turns out to be a learnable craft. MYSTERY

Conventional navigation, by contrast, places (10) on a device rather than on the navigator. RELY

Studies of London taxi drivers, whose mental maps of the city are (11) detailed, show measurable changes in the hippocampus. ORDINARY

Where close (12) to the world around us once defined the practice of finding our way, a thinner, more passive habit has come to take its place. ATTEND

Show Answers

9. mysterious — MYSTERY → mysterious (noun → adjective).

10. reliance — RELY → reliance (verb → noun). The collocation "place reliance on" = to depend on.

11. extraordinarily — ORDINARY → extraordinarily (adjective + prefix extra- + adverb suffix -ily). Two transformations in one — a hallmark CAE Part 3 move.

12. attention — ATTEND → attention (verb → noun). The collocation "close attention to" = careful, sustained focus on.

Use of English · Part 4

Key word transformations

For questions 13–16, complete the second sentence so that it has a similar meaning to the first sentence, using the word given. Do not change the word given. You must use between three and six words, including the word given.

Shortened practice — the real CAE Part 4 has 6 questions.

13. "I regret that I did not learn to navigate by the stars when I was younger." WISH

I to navigate by the stars when I was younger.

Show Answer

wish I had learned (5 words inc. WISH). "Wish + past perfect" expresses regret about something that did not happen in the past.

14. "If satellites had not made navigation effortless, traditional skills might not have declined." NOT

made navigation effortless, traditional skills might not have declined.

Show Answer

Had satellites not (3 words inc. NOT). Inverted third conditional, formal register — drops "if" and inverts subject/auxiliary.

15. "It is no longer worth the effort of carrying paper maps now that phones do everything." POINT

There is paper maps now that phones do everything.

Show Answer

no point in carrying (5 words inc. POINT). The structure "there is no point in -ing" = there is no good purpose in doing something.

16. "Whatever else happens, I will not give up using paper maps." MATTER

happens, I will not give up using paper maps.

Show Answer

No matter what else (4 words inc. MATTER). "No matter what (else) X" = a concessive structure meaning "regardless of X."

Reading · Part 7

Gapped text

You are going to read an extract from an article about traditional navigation. Four paragraphs have been removed from the article. Choose from the paragraphs A–E the one which fits each gap (1–4). There is one extra paragraph which you do not need to use.

Shortened practice — the real CAE Part 7 has six gaps and seven options.

True North: An Old Skill, A New Loss

It is hard, today, to imagine sailing two and a half thousand miles across open ocean with no compass, no GPS and no chart — and arriving exactly where you intended. Yet that is precisely what Mau Piailug did in 1976, when the Hōkūleʻa, a traditionally built Polynesian canoe, made the journey from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti using the same techniques that had carried his ancestors across the Pacific a thousand years earlier.

Gap 1

Wayfinding, as anthropologists have come to call it, is not a single trick but a layered system of perception. The night sky provides a fixed framework; the sun by day gives bearings; the wind and the swell carry information about distant land. Even the behaviour of seabirds, the colour of the water, and the shape of clouds form part of the navigator's running calculation.

Gap 2

Compare this to what most of us now do. A device in our pocket tells us where we are; another in the car tells us where to go. Neither asks us to remember the route, attend to landmarks, or hold a sense of north in our heads. The skill has not died, exactly. It has been quietly handed over.

Gap 3

The cost of this handover is harder to see than the benefit. We arrive on time; we do not get lost; we are spared the discomfort of asking strangers for directions. What we lose is more abstract: the capacity to read a place, to form a mental map, to feel where we are within a landscape rather than merely on top of it.

Gap 4

None of this means that GPS is a mistake. It is, in most respects, an extraordinary tool. But like any tool, its costs are paid by the capacities it replaces. The thoughtful question, then, is not whether to use it, but how to remain capable, in its absence, of finding the way.

Missing paragraphs — pick which fits each gap. One is not needed.

A The voyage was, in one sense, a demonstration. Western scholars had long doubted that the great Pacific migrations could have happened without drift, accident, or instruments since lost. Piailug's success silenced that doubt — and recovered, in the process, a knowledge that had nearly disappeared in his own lifetime.
B What the wayfinder does, in short, is read the world as a single text written in many languages at once. Each element confirms, qualifies or contradicts the others. The skill is not memory, and not calculation, but a long-trained capacity to attend to everything together and to make a steady judgement from it.
C Recent research, much of it focused on the brains of London taxi drivers, has suggested that this displacement is not merely metaphorical. The hippocampus, the brain region responsible for spatial memory, appears to be measurably enlarged in those who use it heavily — and, conversely, to atrophy when its work is taken over by external aids.
D A small but stubborn movement has, for some years, been making the case for what its supporters call navigational fitness — the deliberate practice, in a GPS-saturated world, of finding one's way without help. Its proponents are not romantics. They argue that a faculty which is never exercised eventually cannot be relied on, even in emergencies.
E Critics of the new wave of long-distance walking and cycling have raised concerns about the safety of those who set off into unfamiliar terrain without proper preparation, particularly in mountain regions where conditions can change with little warning.

Show Answers

Gap 1: A — "The voyage was, in one sense, a demonstration" refers directly back to Piailug's voyage in the previous paragraph; "recovered… a knowledge that had nearly disappeared" sets up the next paragraph's explanation of what that knowledge actually is.

Gap 2: B — "What the wayfinder does, in short" summarises the layered list of perceptual clues in the previous paragraph; "the skill is not memory, and not calculation" then prepares the contrast with modern devices in the next paragraph.

Gap 3: C — "this displacement" refers back to the handover described in the previous paragraph; the cost of that displacement (the atrophying hippocampus) leads directly into the next paragraph's discussion of cost.

Gap 4: D — "A small but stubborn movement" introduces a counter-position to the picture of loss in the previous paragraph; "in its absence" in the next paragraph then echoes the movement's argument about exercise and capacity.

Extra paragraph: E — plausible-sounding but doesn't fit any gap. It introduces a new topic (safety of unprepared walkers) that the surrounding text never picks up.

Writing · Part 1

Essay (220–260 words)

You must answer this question. Write your answer in 220–260 words in an appropriate style.

Your class has just listened to a panel discussion on the question of traditional skills in a digital age. You have made the notes below.

Some opinions expressed in the discussion:

"Letting machines do tasks for us is progress; there is no need to mourn skills we no longer require."
"A society that loses its old practical skills also loses something of itself."
"The real danger is dependence — being unable to function when the technology fails."

Write an essay discussing two of the three opinions in your notes. You should explain which opinion you find more convincing, giving reasons in support of your answer.

You may, if you wish, make use of the opinions expressed in the discussion, but you should use your own words as far as possible.

Time remaining: 40:00

Planning frame — 5 minutes before you write

  • Paragraph 1 — Introduction: paraphrase the topic. Briefly introduce the two opinions you've chosen. Do not give your full opinion yet.
  • Paragraph 2 — Opinion A: explain the position, then evaluate it (concede what's strong, challenge what's weak).
  • Paragraph 3 — Opinion B: same shape — explain, then evaluate.
  • Paragraph 4 — Your conclusion: state clearly which of the two you find more convincing, and give your main reason.

Useful C1-register chunks for the essay

  • It is sometimes argued that… / There is a widespread view that…
  • Those who hold this position would point out that…
  • While there is some force to this view, it overlooks the fact that…
  • A more convincing argument is that…
  • What this objection underestimates is…
  • It is, of course, possible to take the opposite view, namely that…
  • On balance, I find the second view more persuasive, primarily because…
  • To conclude, …

Higher-band grammar to reach for

  • Inversion: Not only does GPS save us time, it also relieves us of the need to think.
  • Mixed conditional: Were the technology always reliable, the case against learning to navigate would carry more weight.
  • Concessive: However convenient the new tools, …
  • Cleft: What machines cannot replace is the judgement that comes from doing a thing yourself.
  • Mandative subjunctive: It is essential that such skills not be lost entirely.

Long turn (about 1 minute)

Examiner

"Here are your pictures. They show people in different situations involving finding their way. I'd like you to compare two of the pictures, and say what might be gained or lost in each way of navigating, and which is more useful in everyday life. You have about a minute."

Picture A — a hiker on a hilltop, reading a paper map and holding a compass, with mist filling the valleys below.

Picture B — a driver on a busy city motorway at night, following turn-by-turn directions on a phone mounted to the dashboard.

Picture C — a child in a back garden after dark, being shown how to find the Pole Star by an older relative; both are pointing up at the sky.

Time remaining: 1:00

Useful chunks for Part 2 (the long turn)

  • Comparing: "Both pictures show…" / "Whereas the first picture…, the second…" / "What both situations have in common is…"
  • Speculating: "He might be…" / "She could possibly be…" / "It looks as though…" / "I would imagine that…"
  • What is gained: "The obvious advantage here is…" / "What this approach offers is…"
  • What is lost: "The cost of this convenience is…" / "What seems to disappear in this kind of situation is…"

Speaking · Part 4

Discussion (about 4 minutes)

In Part 4, the examiner asks you more abstract questions related to the topic of Part 3. Your answers should be developed and analytical, not short.

Examiner questions
  1. Are there skills you think every adult should still have, even though technology can do them?
  2. What kinds of skill, in your view, simply cannot be replaced by a machine?
  3. Some people worry that we are becoming too dependent on our devices. Do you share that worry, or do you think it is overstated?
  4. Should schools teach traditional skills — map-reading, mental arithmetic, longhand writing — even if students will rarely use them in later life?
  5. If you had to keep just one of the old skills alive in your own household, which would it be, and why?

How to build a Part 4 answer (aim for > 4 sentences each)

  • Position: "Personally, I would argue that…"
  • Reasoning: "The main reason for this is that…"
  • Example or evidence: "Take, for instance, … / A good example would be…"
  • Counter and concession: "Of course, some would say… ; however, …"
  • Hedge: "It does seem reasonable to suggest, though, that…"
