I. Two Wars: Centuries Apart, Now United

Before we meet Nelson and Wellington, let's reach back into the shadows of our memory palace. You've already walked through Room 15 and Room 11. Today they connect.

Recall: Room 15 (Lesson 15) — Stuarts & Civil War

Who was Guy Fawkes and what year did he light his barrels of gunpowder? What happened to King Charles I? What civil war divided England in the 1640s, and what date marks the execution of a king?

1605: Guy Fawkes and the Gunpowder Plot — an attempt to blow up Parliament and King James I

1642–1651: The English Civil War — Parliament against King Charles I

1649: Execution of Charles I — the only English king to be formally tried and beheaded

This was the age of divine right challenged, of regicide and religious extremism.

1346: Battle of Crécy — English longbowmen defeat French cavalry in a devastating rain of arrows

This was the age of medieval knights, feudal honour, and war as personal combat.

The Bridge: War Transformed

Two wars separated by centuries. In 1346, warriors fought on horseback with swords and spears. By 1642, they fought with muskets and cannon. By 1805, Nelson commanded a navy of wooden walls driven by sail. By 1815, Wellington fought with infantry battalions in tight formations. And by 1830, steam locomotives were carrying soldiers and goods faster than any horse could gallop. The nature of war changed because the nature of technology changed. And that change — the Industrial Revolution — is where Marshall's story reaches its climax.

II. Nelson & Trafalgar: The One-Eyed Hero of the Sea

In 1789, France erupted into revolution. By 1803, the revolutionary general Napoleon Bonaparte had seized power and crowned himself Emperor. He wanted to invade England. To do so, he needed to defeat the British Navy. He gathered his fleet at Toulon. Britain sent one man to stop him: Admiral Horatio Nelson.

The Hero

Nelson was not a typical admiral. He had lost his right arm in battle off Corsica. He had lost the sight of his right eye at the siege of Calvi. Yet he was fearless, visionary, and beloved by his sailors. Marshall captures his spirit well:

Nelson was not tall, and he was spare and thin. He had lost an arm and an eye in the service of his country, yet he was the bravest and most daring of all the captains and admirals that England ever had. He loved his country and his duty more than his own life or comfort. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story (compiled from chapter on Nelson)

On 21 October 1805, Nelson's fleet spotted the French and Spanish fleets off Cape Trafalgar, near Spain. The enemy had 33 ships; Nelson had 27. But he had something they did not: absolute confidence in his captains and an unorthodox battle plan.

The Battle

Before the battle, Nelson issued an immortal signal. As the enemy drew near, his flagship HMS Victory hoisted the words:

England Expects Every Man to Do His Duty. — Nelson's signal at Trafalgar, 21 October 1805

The battle raged for hours. British ships broke the enemy line. Cannon-fire thundered across the water. Men screamed and fell. Blood ran on the decks. But one by one, the enemy ships surrendered or sank. By nightfall, the French and Spanish fleets were destroyed. Napoleon would never invade England. The Royal Navy was supreme.

The Cost

Below the deck of HMS Victory, in the cockpit, a wounded man lay dying. It was Nelson himself. A French musket-ball had struck him. As the battle continued above, his officers brought him word of victory.

As he lay dying, a message was brought to him that the enemy's fleet was entirely defeated. "Thank God," said Nelson, "I have done my duty." These were nearly the last words he spoke. But whether with his dying breath he murmured the tender name of the woman he loved, history does not tell us. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story (compiled from Nelson chapters)

Nelson was dead. But his victory would echo for two centuries. Britain ruled the waves. And the empire — for better and worse — was secure.

The historian's note: The famous dying words attributed to Nelson are disputed. Some say "Thank God I have done my duty," others claim "Kiss me, Hardy" (to his captain). But all accounts agree on his last coherent words: "Thank God I have done my duty." Marshall, writing in 1905, adds a romantic note about "the woman he loved" — a reference to Lady Hamilton, Nelson's mistress, with whom he had a daughter. The Victorian author was being tactful about scandal.

III. Wellington, Waterloo & the Industrial Age

The Iron Duke

While Nelson was dying in 1805, another officer was rising to fame: Arthur Wellesley, Duke of Wellington. He fought in India, in Portugal against the French, and then in Spain. Unlike Nelson, he was not brilliant in the flamboyant sense. He was systematic, cautious, and iron-willed. His soldiers called him "the Iron Duke." His enemies feared him.

Waterloo, 1815

By 1814, Napoleon had been defeated and sent into exile on the island of Elba. But in March 1815, he escaped and landed in France. His old soldiers flocked to his banner. A new army was raised. And for 100 days, Napoleon was Emperor again. On 18 June 1815, he met Wellington for the first and only time in direct combat. The place was Waterloo, near Brussels.

Marshall tells the story with dramatic power:

Now Napoleon said that at last he should measure swords with this Wellington. For many times the French and British armies had met in Spain, but Napoleon was fighting elsewhere and had never faced Wellington in the field. But now at last the moment had come. The armies met at Waterloo. It was a great and terrible battle. The ground was boggy and broken. The day was wet and misty. The armies fought from early morning until evening. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter on Waterloo (adapted)

Wellington had about 68,000 men. Napoleon had nearly 72,000. But Wellington also had an ally approaching from the east: the Prussian General Blücher with 30,000 fresh troops. As the day wore on and the British held their ground, Wellington waited for that relief. Late in the afternoon, Blücher's Prussians began to arrive on the French right flank. Napoleon's army began to crumble.

But Wellington was sad as he looked round on the dead and dying. Great was the victory, but great also was the cost. The British troops were worn out with the long day's fighting, but the Prussians were still fresh, and Blücher started off to chase the flying Frenchmen, who ran as fast as they were able. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Waterloo chapter

By evening, Napoleon's army was destroyed. He fled. Four days later, he surrendered to the British. This time he was sent to St. Helena, an island in the South Atlantic, thousands of miles from Europe. He would never return.

But Victory Comes With Questions

Waterloo was Britain's triumph. The threat from revolutionary France was ended. The old monarchy was restored. Order was preserved. But what kind of order? What kind of world had Britain now inherited?

The Industrial Revolution: A New Kind of Power

In the same years that Nelson and Wellington were fighting Napoleon, a different revolution was happening in England. It was quieter, less dramatic — but ultimately more transformative. Factories were rising. Steam engines were being perfected. Coal mines were going deeper and deeper into the earth. Railways were being built. And the population was pouring into cities to work in mills and mines.

Marshall knew this new world. She was writing in 1905, 90 years after Waterloo, and the Industrial Revolution was by then ancient history. But she also knew its human cost:

In the factories and mills, men, women, and even children worked from dawn till darkness for small wages. The conditions were dreadful. The air was thick with smoke and dust. Children as young as five or six were set to work, and many grew up weak and crippled from the labour. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story (from industrial chapters, paraphrased)
The historian's note: Marshall's description, while vivid, was actually restrained. The reality was often worse. In cotton mills, children aged 4–8 were employed for 12–16 hour days. In coal mines, they pulled carts of coal through tunnels so low a man could not stand. Many lost fingers, hands, or died of suffocation. The Factory Acts of the 1830s–1840s began to restrict child labour, but only after decades of campaigning. By Marshall's time, such horrors seemed almost unbelievable to a prosperous middle-class reader.

The Reform Act of 1832

The Industrial Revolution created a new class: the industrial working class. They had no vote. Parliament, still dominated by landowners, ignored their plight. In 1832, the Great Reform Act began to change the system of representation. It gave the vote to many middle-class men (though not to working men, and certainly not to women). It was a tiny crack in the door — but it was a beginning. Democracy, in its slow way, was beginning to dawn on Britain.

The Bridge Between Eras

Nelson died in 1805, believing he had saved Britain for glory. Wellington lived until 1852, watching as Britain became an industrial superpower — rich, powerful, and deeply troubled by the human cost of that power. The empire he had saved through military genius would be held together, in the next era, through the wealth generated by the factories he saw rising around him.

VI. Your Turn: Reflect & Discuss

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Nelson's final words

"Thank God I have done my duty." Does duty matter more than life? Can dying for your country in victory make death meaningful?

Why did Napoleon lose?

Was it Wellington's genius? Blücher's arrival? Napoleon's own arrogance? Or had the age of individual genius given way to the age of systems and logistics?

The Industrial Revolution's cost

Britain became the wealthiest nation on earth through industry. Thousands of children were crippled, many died, in the mines and mills. Was the progress worth the cost?

The Great Reform Act (1832)

It gave votes to some men, but not to workers, and not to women. Was this a step toward democracy or just a way for the middle class to seize power?

The clash of eras

Nelson and Wellington were the last of the great individual heroes. The Industrial Revolution created a world of faceless factories and mass production. Is something lost when heroism dies?

Britain's power, then and now

After Waterloo, Britain was supreme — militarily and soon economically. Does that power come with responsibility? Does winning wars give a nation the right to rule the world?

1. The Three Dates

2. Nelson and Wellington

What did each man do? What was the cost of their victories?

Nelson: Admiral, one-armed and one-eyed, defeated the French-Spanish fleet at Trafalgar (1805) and saved Britain from invasion. He died in the victory. His signal "England Expects Every Man to Do His Duty" became immortal.

Wellington: General, systematic and cautious, defeated Napoleon at Waterloo (1815). His victory ended the threat of French dominance and saved the old order.

The cost: Thousands of sailors and soldiers died. Britain's naval supremacy was guaranteed, but at what human price?

3. The Industrial Revolution

What was changing in Britain at this time? Who paid the price?

The Industrial Revolution: Factories, mills, coal mines, and railways transformed Britain. Steam engines replaced hand labour. Production increased enormously. Britain became the wealthiest nation in the world.

The cost: Men, women, and children worked 12–16 hours a day in dreadful conditions. Children as young as 4–6 worked in mines and mills. Many were crippled or died.

Reform: The Great Reform Act of 1832 began to give political voice to the new industrial middle class, though not yet to workers or women. It was a tiny crack in the door of democracy.
