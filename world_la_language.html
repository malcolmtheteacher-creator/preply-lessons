Everyone Speaks Spanish?

People often think "Latin America = Spanish". But the region's biggest country speaks Portuguese, and millions speak Quechua, Guaraní, Maya languages, French or English.

The stereotype: "Everyone in Latin America speaks Spanish." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A line on the map

In 1494, the Treaty of Tordesillas drew a line through the Atlantic: lands to the west were for Spain, and lands to the east for Portugal. The line gave Portugal the eastern bulge of South America, which became Brazil. As a result, today more than 200 million Brazilians speak Portuguese — making Portuguese one of the two biggest languages of the region, alongside Spanish.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the Treaty of Tordesillas do?

2. Why do Brazilians speak Portuguese?

1. Divided new lands between Spain and Portugal.

2. The treaty line gave Portugal the area that became Brazil.

Discuss:
  • How can a treaty shape a language for centuries?

Part 2 — Spanish and Portuguese

Spanish and Portuguese are closely related, and many speakers can read each other's languages quite well. Understanding speech is harder: a Brazilian might tell you that Spanish speakers are easy to understand, while many Spanish speakers say Brazilian Portuguese sounds fast and difficult. Near borders people often mix the two in "portuñol". Spanish also varies a lot: Argentines and Uruguayans usually say "vos" instead of "tú".

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is "portuñol"?

2. What do Argentines usually say instead of "tú"?

1. A mix of Portuguese and Spanish used near borders.

2. "Vos".

Discuss:
  • Is it easier to read or to listen to a related language?

Part 3 — Indigenous languages and more

Hundreds of Indigenous languages are still spoken. Quechua, the language of the Inca Empire, has millions of speakers in Peru, Bolivia and Ecuador. In Paraguay, Guaraní is co-official with Spanish and spoken by most of the population. Guatemala has more than twenty Mayan languages. Haiti speaks Haitian Creole and French, and English is official in Belize. Many Indigenous languages are endangered, but radio, schools and social media are helping some to revive.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where is Guaraní co-official?

2. Name a language of the Inca Empire still spoken.

1. In Paraguay.

2. Quechua.

Discuss:
  • Should Indigenous languages be taught in all schools?

Grammar — Reported Speech

Misunderstandings often begin with what someone said — and what we thought they meant. Reported speech moves the tense back and changes pronouns.

How it works:
• "It's quite good," she said → She said that it was quite good.
• "I will think about it," he said → He said he would think about it.
• Reporting verbs: claimed, suggested, admitted, insisted, explained.

Going deeper

The "everyone speaks Spanish" stereotype comes from the size of the Spanish-speaking world and from media that treat Spanish as "the" language of the region. It hides Brazil — the largest country — and the many Indigenous languages that colonial rule tried to replace. Language is closely tied to identity, so getting it wrong can feel like being erased.

Worth noticing: In reported speech tenses usually move back; "tell" needs a person, "say" doesn't.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Check the language first. Don't greet Brazilians in Spanish — try "Olá!" or "Bom dia!" — and don't assume every Latin American speaks Spanish. If you speak Spanish in Brazil, say so politely and ask whether it's OK; many Brazilians will understand.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Why do outsiders forget Brazil speaks Portuguese?
  • What is lost when a language disappears?
  • Which language of the region would you learn?
