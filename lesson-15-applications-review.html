Market day in Valdera

Ines runs a stall at the Saturday market of a small town. This week, she is also in charge of the market's opening ceremony, because the person who usually does it has fallen ill. At six in the morning she stands in the empty square with a head full of things to do and no time to write them down.

There are twelve stalls and each of them needs something. There are the names of the three people from the town council who will arrive early. There is the stall number for the mayor's table, which is 27. There is the price of the cheese, 3.40, that she must tell her helper. And at eleven there is a two-minute welcome speech that she will have to give without notes, because her hands will be full of flowers.

What does Ines have to remember?
Names of three council members, a stall number (27), a price (3.40), a shopping list and a two-minute speech.
Talk about it. Which of these jobs would you find hardest, and which easiest? Why?

In the old days she would have panicked. This time she does something calm. She walks slowly down the middle of the square and decides, for each job, which tool fits.

The shopping list is only six things, and she needs it quickly: a story chain. The stall number and the price are digits: she turns 27 and 340 into two quick scenes. The three names are for the council members' faces, as they come in, so she will use a name picture on each feature. And the speech is a talk: five points, five places along the market's central avenue, from the fountain to the church.

How does Ines choose her tools?
She matches each job to a tool: a story chain for the list, scenes for the numbers, name pictures for the faces, and a route for the speech.
Talk about it. Which tool would you choose for each of the jobs? Do you agree with Ines?

By nine, the work is done and the market is open. The council members arrive and she greets each of them by name. The mayor gets table 27. The helper hears the price and writes nothing. At eleven she walks down the avenue in her head, from picture to picture, and speaks to the crowd with her hands full of flowers.

Nobody notices that she used any method at all. That is the best sign it is working. This room is your own Saturday market. It asks you to do five small jobs, in a mixed order, using what you learned in the first three wings. And it ends with five habits that keep it all alive.

What is the sign that a method is working?
Nobody notices it. It becomes part of how you act.
Talk about it. Tell a short story about a day when your memory let you down. Which tool would have helped?

Five habits

  1. Pay attention. Most forgetting is not paying attention in the first place. Look, listen, repeat the name.
  2. Make a picture. Make it strange, moving and loud. Use two or three tools from the checklist.
  3. Give it a place. On a peg, in a room, in a chain. A picture with no place gets lost.
  4. Keep it organised. Fixed routes, fixed lists, fixed codes. Order is half the memory.
  5. Review it. Soon, then a little later, then later still.
attend, picture,
place, order, review.

The common mistakes

  • Reading instead of picturing. If you are not seeing, you are not encoding.
  • Pictures that are too tame. A calm picture is a forgettable picture.
  • Changing the route. The route must not change.
  • Learning and never reviewing. The curve is real: a first review the next day does most of the work.
  • Using the biggest tool for the smallest job. A short list does not need a palace.
Say it aloud. Say the five habits aloud and give one example of each from your own day.

Now you build

Have a go before you open any answer. A rough attempt teaches you more than a perfect model you only read.

The shopping list

Make one linked story for the list cheese, lemons, bread, flowers, honey, olives.
A giant block of cheese rolls into a crate of lemons, which fly up and bake a loaf of bread. The bread is being delivered by a bunch of flowers on a bicycle, which spills honey over the street. A herd of olives slides along in the honey.

Two numbers

Encode the stall number 27 and the price 3.40 (340). Use the Major System: 27 = n + k, 34 = m + r, 0 = s. Write both scenes.
27 = NECK or NOOK: a giraffe's neck with a sign saying 27. 340 = MARRY + S or MORES: a bride and groom are married in a heap of cheese rinds. Choose your own words as long as the sounds are right.

Three names

Maria (red scarf), Tomas (a very tall hat) and Elena (a gold necklace). Make a name picture and join it to each feature.
Maria: a marriage! A bride is wrapped tightly in her red scarf. Tomas: a tomato wearing a tall hat. Elena: an elephant with a gold necklace round its trunk.

The two-minute speech

Plan a welcome speech in five points (greeting, history of the market, thanks, today, close). Give each a picture and place it along an avenue you know.
1 greeting: a giant hand waves from the fountain. 2 history: a stone cart with a wheel as big as a house outside the bank. 3 thanks: a huge basket of letters on the bench. 4 today: a clock with a smiling face on the church. 5 close: a bell-ringing flock of doves.
