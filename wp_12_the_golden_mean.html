A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XII of L

The Golden Mean

In which Aristotle discovers that the good life is not about extremes but about finding the middle way — and that happiness is not a feeling but an activity

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We turn to Aristotle’s ethics. If philosophy is the love of wisdom, then ethics asks: what is wisdom for? What is the goal of human life? Aristotle’s answer is neither pleasure nor honor, but eudaimonia — flourishing, a state achieved by living well. You will learn the golden mean, meet a wise person who does not need rules, and discover why Aristotle’s beautiful ethics has a fatal flaw.

The Question: What Is the Good?

Plato said: The Good is a Form, eternal and perfect, known only through reason and ascent toward the divine.

The Sophists said: Goodness is whatever benefits you. Justice is the interest of the stronger.

Aristotle says: Something else. The good is eudaimonia — flourishing. And it is not a feeling or a place. It is an activity. An excellence of the soul.

Not “what should I feel?” but “what should I do?” And not as an isolated act, but as a whole life, a pattern, a way of being.

II. The Story — A Life of Excellence

Aristotle wrote his ethics for his son Nicomachus. So we call it the Nicomachean Ethics. But this is not a book of rules. It is a book of friendship, of seeing, of becoming someone who can see what is right in each situation. It begins with a question that every human being must answer: what is the highest good?

Eudaimonia: The Highest Good

What do we all want? Pleasure? Honor? Wealth? Aristotle says no. These are not the highest good. Why? Because if you got one of them, you would still ask: is this all there is? Is pleasure the purpose of a human life? Then a cow that eats well would be happy. Is honor the purpose? Then you are at the mercy of others’ opinions. Is wealth? Then you are enslaved to its acquisition.

Happiness depends upon ourselves. — Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book 1, Ch. 7

The highest good, Aristotle says, is eudaimonia — flourishing, living well, the realization of your potential. And it is not a feeling. It is an activity of the soul in accordance with excellence (arete). A human being flourishes when they are doing what a human being is built to do, and doing it excellently.

Virtue and the Golden Mean

So what is excellence? Virtue. But not in the modern sense of following rules. For Aristotle, a virtue is a habit, a state of character developed through practice. And every virtue is a mean between two extremes.

The Golden Mean

Courage is the mean between cowardice (too little fear) and recklessness (too much fear).

Generosity is the mean between stinginess (giving too little) and profligacy (giving too much).

Proper pride is the mean between humility (underestimating yourself) and arrogance (overestimating yourself).

Every virtue is a balance. Not a compromise, but a balance — the point where you give the right amount to the right person at the right time in the right way for the right reason.

The key word is the right amount. Too much anger is rage. Too little is apathy. The right amount, directed at the right target, for the right reason, is justifiable indignation. Aristotle does not give you a formula. He cannot. There is no formula for virtue. What he gives you is something harder: a picture of a virtuous person, and an invitation to become that picture.

Practical Wisdom (Phronesis)

But how do you know the right mean in each situation? This is where Aristotle introduces one of his most beautiful concepts: phronesis, often translated as practical wisdom. This is not theoretical knowledge. It is the ability to see what a situation requires, to judge it accurately, and to act rightly.

Phronesis is learned by doing: You cannot acquire it from a book. You acquire it by watching someone wise, by practicing, by failing, by learning from your failures. A young person cannot have practical wisdom, no matter how intelligent. A seventy-year-old who has paid attention can have it. The wise person (the phronimos) does not need rules. They see what is right, and they do it.

This is radically different from the Platonic ascent toward the eternal Forms. There is no stepping outside the world of particular situations. The wise person is deeply in the world, seeing clearly, acting rightly, embedded in relationships and circumstances.

The Requirements of Flourishing

But here is what makes Aristotle’s ethics beautiful and also troubling. He admits something that modern ethics often tries to hide:

You cannot flourish alone. You need friends. You need health. You need some basic level of wealth. You need luck. The human being who flourishes is not the hermit on a mountain or the martyr in chains. It is the person who lives well in community, with adequate resources, with good fortune.

This is honest. And it is devastating. Because it means that virtue is not equally available to all. A person born into poverty, ill-health, or a vicious society faces barriers that no amount of individual effort can overcome. Aristotle’s ethics works perfectly for the fortunate. For the broken, something else is needed.

III. The Ideas — The Heart of Aristotelian Ethics

There are three ideas at the core of Aristotle’s ethics. Together, they form a vision of the good life that has shaped Western thinking for 2,300 years. But each also reveals something it cannot reach.

Idea 1: Eudaimonia

Happiness is an activity of the soul, not a feeling.

This is revolutionary. It means you can be engaged in eudaimonia — in flourishing — even while suffering. A martyr who chooses to die for something true is flourishing, even in agony. A person pursuing a difficult goal is flourishing, even while struggling.

Eudaimonia is not pleasure. It is not comfort. It is the deep satisfaction that comes from living excellently, from realizing your potential, from becoming what you are capable of becoming.

Idea 2: The Golden Mean

Every virtue is a balance between two vices.

This is practical wisdom made visible. It tells us that virtue is not about following rules or maximizing some value. It is about finding the right measure in each situation. The mean is not the mathematical average; it is the amount that is right for you, in your circumstances, at this moment.

But the question remains: who decides? How do you know? Aristotle says: the phronimos. The wise person. But for the rest of us, still learning, still developing character, the path is unclear.

Idea 3: The Social Nature of Virtue

Virtue is lived in community and requires external goods.

Aristotle refuses the Stoic claim that virtue is sufficient for happiness. You need friends. You need health. You need some wealth. You need luck. A person born into slavery or extreme poverty, no matter how excellent their character, is hampered in their ability to flourish fully.

This is honest. It is also a problem. It suggests that the universe is not arranged justly. Some people have advantages, others do not. And those advantages matter. Virtue is not enough.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Unresolved Question

Think about this: Aristotle says everyone has the potential to flourish. But he also admits that flourishing requires health, wealth, friends, and luck. These are not equally distributed. Some are born sick. Some are born poor. Some are born into vicious communities. Does Aristotle’s ethics offer them any hope?

Aristotle’s answer is: not much. A person born into slavery can develop their character, but they cannot fully flourish because they are denied autonomy. A person born into poverty can be virtuous, but they are hampered by circumstances. A person born sick cannot engage in many excellent activities.

Later philosophers will wrestle with this. The Stoics will say virtue is all that matters. Christianity will say suffering has meaning beyond flourishing. Nietzsche will invert the whole thing. But Aristotle leaves the question open: if flourishing requires luck, and luck is beyond our control, what are we to do?

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Eudaimonia vs. Pleasure

Aristotle says happiness is not pleasure, but an activity of excellence. Do you agree? Can you be happy while suffering?

The Golden Mean

Is every virtue really a mean between two extremes? What about honesty? Or cruelty? Are there virtues that have no opposite?

Practical Wisdom

Aristotle says you cannot learn virtue from a book. You must practice, observe, learn from failure. Is this true? Can you learn ethics from philosophy?

Luck and Justice

Aristotle admits that flourishing requires luck — health, wealth, friends, good fortune. But luck is not fairly distributed. Is his ethics only for the privileged?

The Phronimos

The wise person needs no rules; they see what is right and do it. But what makes someone wise? How do you become a wise person?

Community

Aristotle says you cannot flourish alone. You need friends, community, society. What does it mean to need others to be fully human?

The Gap in Aristotle

Aristotle’s ethics is humane, practical, and beautiful. But it has a flaw: it works for the fortunate. A person born into slavery, poverty, or severe illness cannot fully flourish, no matter how virtuous they are. The medieval theologians tried to fix this by adding God’s grace. The Stoics tried to fix it by saying virtue is all that matters. But Aristotle left the gap open. Later philosophers will have to decide: is Aristotle’s ethics enough? Or is something more needed?
