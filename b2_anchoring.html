Anchoring

B2 Level · Why the first number you hear shapes everything that follows

The first number does all the work

A car salesman starts at €40,000. You negotiate hard, get him down to €34,000, and walk away pleased. The same car, in the next showroom, is openly listed at €31,000 — and you stroll past it thinking the price looks high. Nothing about the car has changed; the first number you heard did all the work. Psychologists have spent fifty years studying this strange effect, and they've given it a name: anchoring. It shapes more of our daily decisions than almost any other mental trap.

Speak · first reactions

Two or three full sentences per answer. Take your time.

  1. Think of the most recent thing you bought that cost more than €100. What was the first price you saw before you decided? How did that number shape what you felt was reasonable?
  2. In your country, how often is the "first price" the real price — and how often is it just an opening move?
  3. Salary conversations are full of anchoring. Have you ever been in one where the first number changed everything that came after? What happened?
  4. If you knew, with certainty, that every important price in your life was an anchor designed to manipulate you, would you behave differently — or would you carry on as before?

Eight chunks to put to work

Click each card; read it; close it; move on. You'll meet all eight again in the reading.

an anchor The first number you see or hear in a situation, which quietly shapes your sense of what's reasonable from that moment on.
to set the anchor To deliberately introduce the first number in a conversation — a price, a salary, an offer — in order to shape the rest.
a reference point The mental baseline you compare new information against — almost always shaped by the first number you encountered.
to adjust away from (an anchor) To move your estimate up or down from the starting figure. People typically don't adjust far enough.
a starting offer The first price proposed in a negotiation. Once it's on the table, it shapes everything that follows.
to lowball / to highball To open a negotiation with a deliberately low (or high) number, in order to anchor the other side.
gut feeling What "feels right" in the moment — almost always shaped by anchors you didn't consciously notice.
to recalibrate To deliberately reset your sense of what's reasonable, by asking what the number would feel like if you'd never heard the anchor.

One short reading. Then we talk.

Read this aloud, slowly. Two paragraphs — the first explains what the effect is; the second explains how to live with it.

The Israeli-American psychologist Daniel Kahneman, who won a Nobel Prize for his work on human decision-making, demonstrated something quite uncomfortable about how the brain handles numbers. In a famous experiment, he spun a roulette wheel that was secretly rigged to land on either 10 or 65. He then asked his subjects to estimate, in percentage terms, the proportion of African countries in the United Nations. The group that had just seen 10 on the wheel guessed, on average, 25%. The group that had just seen 65 guessed 45%. The wheel was obviously random — yet the random number had quietly become an anchor: a reference point that the brain used as a starting place and adjusted away from, never far enough.

The same effect runs through almost every negotiation, every price tag, every salary conversation. A skilled negotiator sets the anchor with their starting offer — deliberately lowballing or highballing to bias the entire exchange. Even people who know the trick fall for it, because gut feeling is shaped by whatever number landed first. The cure is not to ignore prices, but to recalibrate — to pause, before responding, and ask "what would this be worth if I had never heard the first number?" That single habit, practised over time, is one of the most useful pieces of mental hygiene a working adult can develop.

Phrasing · read aloud, with a small pause at each comma and dash. Chunk the long sentences so they breathe.

Two quick questions before we open it out:

1. What did the roulette-wheel experiment show about how random numbers can affect serious judgement?

Show Answer
It showed that even an obviously random number can shape people's estimates of a completely unrelated question. The group that saw "10" guessed 25%; the group that saw "65" guessed 45%. The number had become an anchor and biased the answer, even though the participants knew the wheel was random.

2. Why does the text say that even knowing about anchoring doesn't fully protect you?

Show Answer
Because gut feeling is shaped automatically, before conscious thought catches up. Even when you know an anchor has been set deliberately, the first number still shifts what feels reasonable. The defence isn't to ignore prices but to deliberately recalibrate — pause and ask what the number would feel like without the anchor.
Speak · open it out
  1. The text says we adjust away from an anchor "never far enough." Where in your own working life have you seen this play out? Be specific.
  2. Some shops always show a high "original price" crossed out, with a lower "sale price" beside it. What is the original price actually doing — and does knowing this make any difference to how you react?
  3. When you set a starting offer in a real conversation — pricing, a salary expectation, a contract — what factors actually decide the number you choose?
  4. If you had to teach someone — a younger colleague, a child — one habit to defend against anchoring in adult life, what would you teach them, and how?

Comparatives & "than" structures

Anchoring is fundamentally about comparison — this number against that one, the new price against the first one, what they offered against what you expected. The grammar of comparison is the natural language of anchor talk. Three structures do most of the work.

The forms: -er than for short adjectives (higher / lower / cheaper than), more / less… than for longer ones (more expensive than / less reasonable than), and (not) as… as for equality (twice as much as / not as cheap as). The auxiliary than is what carries the comparison — don't skip it.

Standard comparatives

-er than   /   more X than   /   less X than

The second offer was higher than the first.

It was much less reasonable than I had expected.

(not) as … as

Equality, or the lack of it.

The price wasn't as low as she'd hoped.

It felt twice as expensive as last year.

Multipliers

twice / three times / half as much as …

The starting offer was three times as much as the real value.

We countered with half as much as they asked.

Three speaking tasks

Time remaining: 3:00
Task A · an anchor in your own life (3 minutes)

Pick one recent decision in your own working life where you can clearly see, looking back, that the first number did most of the work — a price, a salary, an offer, a budget. Cover:

  1. What was the first number you saw, and where did it come from?
  2. What did you end up agreeing to, and how far did it sit from the anchor?
  3. If the first number had been half as low or twice as high, would you have decided differently?
  4. Was the anchor set at you by someone, or did you set it on yourself?

Use at least three chunks from Tab 2 and at least two comparative forms from Tab 4.

Task B · the negotiation role-play (3 minutes)

Your teacher is selling something — a service, a product, a piece of work — with a deliberately high starting offer. You are the buyer. Negotiate. The point isn't to "win"; the point is to notice the anchor in real time, and to name it out loud at least once during the conversation ("that's higher than I would expect because…"). After three minutes, debrief: where was the anchor doing the work? Then swap roles.

Task C · set the anchor (3 minutes)

Imagine you are presenting to an important counterparty — an investor, a client, a senior buyer — and you need to set the starting figure. Talk through:

  1. What number will you anchor with — and why that number, not a different one?
  2. How will you frame it so it doesn't sound like an opening trick?
  3. What's your walk-away — the lowest you'd accept, knowing the anchor you set?
  4. If they counter with a deliberate lowball, how do you avoid getting pulled toward their anchor instead of holding yours?

Final open discussion

  1. Anchoring works even on people who know about it. What does that mean for how we should design important decisions — salary structures, board negotiations, hiring offers?
  2. Should it be illegal to anchor in advertising or pricing — the "original price" crossed out, the "limited offer" sticker — or is that just commerce?
  3. Are there any decisions in life where you can actually escape anchoring — or is judgement always relative?
  4. If you imagine your own next significant negotiation, what anchor will you try to set — and what will you do if the other side has set one before you arrive?
