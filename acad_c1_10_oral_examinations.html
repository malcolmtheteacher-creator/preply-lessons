Six core techniques for performing well in oral examinations:

1The Precise Answer ▼

What it means: Answer the question asked, not a related question you prefer. Don't pad. Don't filibuster. Be direct.

Question: "What's the main limitation of your study?"

Bad answer: "Well, there are lots of limitations, and limitations are really important in research, and different studies have different limitations, and..."

Good answer: "The main limitation is that our sample was small—only 40 participants—which reduces generalisability. Future work would benefit from a larger sample."
2Buying Thinking Time ▼

What it means: You don't have to answer instantly. Use natural stalling phrases to buy thinking time without sounding evasive.

"That's an interesting question—let me think about that for a moment."

"There are a few angles to that. Let me start with the most important one..."

"That's a good question. What strikes me most is..."

Then pause. Breathe. Think. Then speak.
3Structuring Under Pressure ▼

What it means: When nervous, rambling happens. Combat it by signposting structure. Tell the examiner your plan, then execute it.

"I'd approach that in three parts: first, the theory behind it; second, the practical application; third, the limitations."

Then deliver those three things clearly and stop. You've answered, structured, and shown thinking.
4Showing Depth ▼

What it means: Don't just answer the surface question. Show that you've thought beyond it.

Surface answer: "Yes, X has been shown to increase productivity."

Depth answer: "Yes, but it's more nuanced than that. While productivity increases in knowledge work, the effect is weaker for collaborative tasks, and the mechanism appears to involve reduced distractions rather than any inherent superiority of the approach."

Phrases for depth: "What's interesting about that is..." / "Building on that..." / "A more nuanced reading might suggest..." / "There's a distinction worth making here..."

5Honest Uncertainty ▼

What it means: If you don't know, say so. But frame it honestly and move to what you do know.

Bad: Rambling or making something up.

Good: "I'm not certain of the exact figure, but what I can say is the general pattern is..." or "That's at the edge of my expertise, but my understanding is..."

This shows confidence and honesty at once.
6Recovery from Blanking ▼

What it means: Your mind goes blank. Don't panic. Use these recovery moves:

"I think I misunderstood the question—could you rephrase it?"

"Let me approach that differently..."

"That's a complex question. Let me break it into parts..."

Often, rephrasing the question gives your brain time to retrieve the answer. And examiners respect a candidate who asks for clarification.

Reflection

Oral examinations test more than knowledge. They test composure, clarity, depth of thinking, and the ability to speak precisely under pressure. These are the skills that matter in your career far more than memorisation.
