Today: spoken data — reading a transcript's symbols fluently and analysing spontaneous speech as identity, never as error.

The rule before anything else

Written down, everybody's natural speech looks chaotic: half-sentences, repeats, "er", grammar no textbook blesses. That chaos is not failure — it is what fluent, real-time talk IS. The exam's transcribers preserve every um and stumble because each one is evidence: of thinking, of feeling, of who the speaker is when nobody is polishing.

SPEAK Prove it on yourself: describe your breakfast for twenty seconds, naturally. Now — did you produce perfect written-style sentences? What DID you produce? That is the grammar of speech, and you are fluent in it.

And one concept to carry over: dialect and accent from Lesson 2 now appear as DATA — non-standard grammar in the words, regional sound in the slashes. Both are identity; neither is error.

SPEAK Warm-up decode: a speaker says /ˈwɔ:ʔə/ for water and /gəʊɪn/ for going. Describe both in exam voice — accent features, not mistakes.
A glottal stop replaces the medial /t/ in "water", and "going" ends /n/ rather than /ŋ/ (so-called g-dropping) — both widespread, socially meaningful accent features, often carrying covert prestige in informal talk.

The transcript

A Newcastle-born session musician, now living in London, interviewed on a music podcast about her first guitar. Key as above.

Host: so take us back (.) first guitar
M: right so it were me uncle's (.) it was (.) it was this knackered old thing with (.) with three strings and I loved it more than **anything** (1) me mam /mæm/ said you're not /nɒʔ/ having lessons pet we can't run to it (.) so I just sat in me room going /gəʊɪn/ round and round the same three chords (.) round and round (.) drove everybody **mental**
Host: and now you play arenas
M: aye which is (.) it's daft when you think (1) cos back then it was canny little clubs round Byker way (.) you'd get a tenner and a pie [laughter] (.) I say that to the young lads in the band now and they look at us /əs/ like I'm from the **moon** (.) they've all got their oat milk and their in-ear monitors (.) and fair play like (.) but part of us is still that bairn with the three strings (.) always will be

Clue hunt — find and name

a) Dialect grammar and dialect lexis

Grammar: "it were me uncle's", "me mam/me room" (me for my), "us" for me (/əs/ — "they look at us"). Lexis: "canny", "bairn", "aye", the endearment "pet". A dense North-East sociolect worn openly.

b) A self-repair that shows monitoring

"it were me uncle's (.) it was" — she half-corrects dialect "were" to standard "was", then the dialect flows on regardless. One tiny repair showing both codes live in her, and which one wins at home-topics.

c) Repetition doing emotional work

"round and round the same three chords (.) round and round" — the phrase performs the obsession it describes. Plus stressed "anything", "mental", "moon": the transcript's bold shows where feeling spikes.

d) The then-versus-now identity move

"a tenner and a pie" against "oat milk and in-ear monitors" — two eras of a music career in two lexical snapshots; then the explicit thesis: "part of us is still that bairn with the three strings". Speakers often SAY their identity claim outright — quote it when they do.

e) The audience shaping the talk

[laughter] rewards the pie line — she is performing, timing lines for a live listener; the host's prompts steer the story. Spoken identity is always co-produced, and saying so earns context marks.

Performed identity — the concept that lifts Section A

A speaker on a podcast is not caught talking — they KNOW the microphone is there. So what you analyse is a performance of self: which features they keep, which they play up, which they smooth away. Three questions to ask of any spoken text:

QuestionIn the musician's case
What could they have hidden but kept?The dialect: "bairn", "us", "it were" — kept, on national media. Covert prestige, chosen live.
Where does the identity intensify?On home topics — mam, Byker, the three strings — the dialect thickens. Topic pulls language.
Who are they performing FOR?Host + invisible listeners + "the young lads" — she converges toward none of them; the divergence IS the brand.

Which claim is the strongest analysis?

Exam voice: the spoken-text paragraph

Model, on the musician:

"The speaker's identity is carried as much by sound as by story. Her North-East forms — the dialect grammar of 'it were me uncle's', the lexis 'canny' and 'bairn', /əs/ for the first person — survive on national media where they could easily be smoothed away, a live investment in covert prestige. The transcript's spontaneity features are equally expressive: the self-repair 'it were (.) it was' shows both codes competing, and the repetition 'round and round (.) round and round' performs the childhood obsession it reports. Her lexical snapshots of two eras — 'a tenner and a pie' against 'oat milk and in-ear monitors' — compress a career's class travel into two noun phrases, before she states the identity thesis herself: 'part of us is still that bairn with the three strings'. The [laughter] confirms what the pauses suggest: this is timed, performed authenticity, co-produced with a listening audience."

Each spends a different currency for the same purchase: writing buys its voice with punctuation; speech buys its permanence with performance."

Recall zone

From Lesson 2: convergence and divergence — whose concept, and one example of each.

Giles, accommodation theory. Convergence: mirroring an interviewer's formality. Divergence: thickening your home accent to mark distance — or, as with the musician, holding your own code as a brand.

From Lesson 3: why are a text's allusions demographic data?

Each reference assumes a shared shelf — so the choices reveal generation, culture and intended audience.
