What Just Happened?

You've moved beyond the myth that the viva is about perfection. A successful viva isn't "defending perfection"—it's demonstrating intellectual ownership, acknowledging legitimate constraints, and showing why your contribution matters anyway.

The examiner who attacks your methodology is doing their job. Your job is to show: "I made deliberate choices, I know my work's boundaries, and I understand what this research contributes."

Your Moves

These are the specific language moves that separate candidates who pass with flying colors from those who squeak through.

1 Claiming Ownership +

You don't hide behind "the supervisor suggested" or "the literature shows." You own the choices. You say "I chose" or "I designed" or "I decided to focus on this because..."

When to use it:

When explaining any major decision. When an examiner challenges your approach.

Weak ownership

"The literature suggested using a qualitative approach, so we did interviews."

Strong ownership

"I chose qualitative interviews because my research question required understanding lived experience—not aggregate patterns. A survey wouldn't have accessed the interpretive depth I needed."

2 Defending Without Retreating +

You listen to the critique, acknowledge what's fair, then hold your ground on why the choice was still the right one given your constraints and research question.

When to use it:

When an examiner questions your methodology. When facing a challenge to your interpretation.

The move

Examiner: "Why didn't you use mixed methods?" You: "That's a fair question. I considered it. But given my 12-month window, mixed methods would have been: one—weakening both the qualitative and quantitative components, and two—diluting the exploratory depth the literature was actually asking for at this stage. I made a deliberate choice to do one thing well."

3 Acknowledging Limitations Honestly +

You don't apologize. You state the limitation clearly, explain what it means for your findings, and then explain why it doesn't invalidate your contribution.

When to use it:

When an examiner points out a gap. When you're presenting limitations proactively.

Weak acknowledgement

"I only had 20 participants, so the findings might not generalize."

Strong acknowledgement

"My sample of 20 was purposively selected for maximum variation in X. This is a small sample by generalizability standards—but that's not my claim. My claim is about the mechanisms at work in this context. A larger sample wouldn't tell us anything we don't know. What a future study should test is whether these mechanisms hold across different contexts."

4 Handling the "What Would You Do Differently?" Question +

You answer this at two levels: within-thesis changes (what you'd do if you had 6 more months) and beyond-thesis directions (what future research should explore).

When to use it:

When the examiner asks this direct question. It almost always comes.

The move

"Within the scope of this thesis, I would have deepened the comparison across the three contexts—that would have strengthened chapter 3. Beyond this thesis, the limitation that bothers me most is that I only looked at policy-level implementation. I'd want future work to follow this down to practice on the ground."

5 Handling the Hostile Examiner +

Hostility can come from genuine intellectual challenge or from an examiner's own agenda. Either way, you stay measured. You don't match their tone. You answer the substantive question, not the hostility.

When to use it:

When you sense aggression rather than genuine inquiry. Stay professional, curious, unmoved.

The move

Examiner (dismissively): "This approach is so outdated. Everyone's moved on to X." You: "I'm interested in your perspective. The dominant paradigm has shifted toward X in some areas, but the foundational question my thesis addresses is still in debate. I'm positioned within that debate deliberately. What's your read on where the literature is heading?"

6 Uncertainty With Confidence +

You admit what you don't know without undermining what you do know. The language of careful scholarship is not apologetic—it's precise.

When to use it:

When you're unsure of an answer. When you're stating what your data can and cannot show.

Weak uncertainty language

"I'm not really sure, but I think..." or "We can't really say anything definitive..."

Strong uncertainty language

"That's a question I can't answer from this data. What I can say is X. What would be required to answer your question is [method/data]. That feels like a natural next study."

Your Situation

You're 10 minutes into your viva. The internal examiner has finished introductory questions. The external (a well-respected scholar in your field) leans forward and says:

"I have a concern about your methodology. You chose a case-study approach, but the literature clearly moved toward comparative analysis five years ago. Why are you still defending a single case as sufficient? And how do you respond to the critique that case studies can't generate transferable knowledge?"

Your Task: Prepare Your Response

You have 2 minutes to prepare. Think through:

  • Is this a fair critique or a misreading of your work?
  • What's the assumption in the question that you need to challenge?
  • How do you defend your case-study choice without retreating?
  • What's the move that shows intellectual confidence without defensiveness?
2:00

Now Speak: Your Response (3 minutes)

Respond as you would in the viva. Acknowledge the critique, defend your choice, show that you understand the intellectual landscape you're working in. Make the examiner feel heard while standing your ground.

What Your Response Should Show

Strategic defense Are you defending the approach, or apologizing for it?
Measured confidence Do you sound like you know what you've done?

Testing Your Moves

Each challenge puts you in a specific viva moment. You'll have 60-90 seconds to respond. Press the button below to reveal challenges one at a time.

Challenge 1: The Direct Challenge

Examiner: "Your theoretical framework is interesting, but it's built on an assumption that I'm not convinced about. Specifically, you assume X, but there's equally strong evidence for not-X in the literature."

You have 90 seconds: How do you respond without being defensive?

1:30

Challenge 2: The Gotcha Question

Examiner: "In chapter 2, you claim X. But on page 43, you present data that seem to contradict that. How do you reconcile this?"

You have 60 seconds: You haven't looked at the thesis in weeks. You panic slightly. How do you recover?

1:00

Challenge 3: The Big Picture Question

Examiner: "Your work is technically sound, but so what? Why should the field care about this contribution? What changes because of what you've found?"

You have 90 seconds: This is asking you to articulate significance. You need to be confident here.

1:30

Challenge 4: The Limitation Probe

Examiner: "You mention this limitation in passing, but I think it's more serious than you're acknowledging. Your sample only includes cases where X was present. You can't make claims about Y because of that."

You have 90 seconds: Is the critique fair? Even if it is, how do you respond without caving?

1:30

Challenge 5: The "You're Wrong" Moment

Examiner (with some heat): "I don't think you understand the foundational literature here. You're misrepresenting what Smith argued. And that misreading undermines your whole chapter 1."

You have 60 seconds: You're nervous now. They might be right. They might be wrong. How do you stay grounded?

1:00

Closing Questions

No perfect answers. Reflect aloud on these.

Speak What's the difference between defending your thesis and defending your ego? How do you tell them apart in the moment?
Speak If you could go into your viva knowing one thing with certainty, what would it be? Not about your research—about how you want to show up as a scholar?
Speak After the viva is over, what matters more: that you convinced the examiners of your brilliance, or that you demonstrated intellectual maturity? Why?
