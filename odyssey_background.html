The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The Romans leave

For almost four hundred years Britain was part of the Roman Empire. Around 410, with the empire under attack at home, the Roman army left. Over the next two centuries, groups from northern Germany and Denmark — Angles, Saxons and Jutes — settled across the east and south. The Britons kept their language and kingdoms mostly in the west: in what became Wales, Cornwall and parts of the north. Historians now think there was mixing as well as fighting.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. When did the Roman army leave?

2. Which groups settled in Britain?

1. Around 410.

2. Angles, Saxons and Jutes.

Discuss:
  • What happens to a country when a powerful protector suddenly leaves?

Part 2 — Arthur and the Heptarchy

Later stories say a British leader called Arthur fought the newcomers. There may have been a real war leader behind the legend, but he probably wasn't a king, and the famous tales — Camelot, the Round Table — were added centuries later. By around 600 the Anglo-Saxon lands had become several kingdoms, traditionally counted as seven, the "Heptarchy": Wessex, Essex, Sussex, Kent, East Anglia, Mercia and Northumbria. In 597 a monk called Augustine, sent by Pope Gregory the Great, arrived in Kent, and over the next century Christianity spread from kingdom to kingdom.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Was Arthur definitely a king?

2. Who sent Augustine, and where did he land?

1. No — a real leader may lie behind the legend, but probably not a king.

2. Pope Gregory the Great; Kent, in 597.

Discuss:
  • Why do people keep telling stories about heroes like Arthur?

Part 3 — Offa, Egbert and Alfred

In the 8th century Mercia was strongest. Offa, king from 757 to 796, controlled much of southern England, traded with the Frankish emperor Charlemagne and was treated by the pope as an important king. He is remembered for Offa's Dyke along the Welsh border — though no one is sure whether it was a defence or simply a border line. Next, Wessex rose. Egbert, who had lived in exile at Charlemagne's court, became king in 802 and defeated Mercia at Ellendun in 825. His grandson Alfred the Great beat the Danish leader Guthrum at Edington in 878, agreed a division of the land that left the Danes the north and east (later called the Danelaw), and encouraged learning, including the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. Alfred's son, Edward the Elder, carried the fight on — and that is where our story begins.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is Offa's Dyke a mystery?

2. Who did Alfred defeat in 878?

1. We don't know if it was a defence or just a border.

2. Guthrum and the Danes, at Edington.

Discuss:
  • Is a leader remembered more for fighting or for building (laws, schools, records)?

Grammar — Past Modals of Deduction

When the evidence is thin, historians must deduce. Past modals show how certain we are about what happened.

How it works:
• must have + past participle — nearly certain: "He must have known."
• might / may / could have — possible: "The princes may have been killed."
• can't have — nearly impossible: "He can't have been in London that night."

Going deeper

"England" comes from "Angle-land", the land of the Angles. For centuries it was not one country but a set of rivals, and the Vikings were, strangely, part of what pushed them together: to fight the Danes, the other kingdoms gradually accepted the kings of Wessex. Much of what we know comes from sources written later, so historians always ask who wrote a story, and why.

Worth noticing: Older books often give "Ellandun" and "Ethandun"; historians today usually write Ellendun and Edington. Dates such as 410 and 597 are approximate or come from one source each.
Learning from the past — for today: Big things are built slowly, by many people. England was not founded on one day by one hero. Missionaries, traders, kings, scribes and ordinary farmers each added something. When you start a long project, remember: most of the work is unglamorous, and most of the builders are never famous.
