Switching into the present tense mid-story makes it feel like it's happening right now, in front of you — even though it's already over. Confident speakers do this constantly: for a punchline, for a habit that drives them mad, for breaking news, even for a plan that never worked out. The "normal" tense rule bends, and the bend itself carries meaning.
Today: five ways of bending the "normal" rules of tense — live storytelling, habits with attitude, breaking news properly, and a plan from the past that changed (or didn't) — so you can hear the difference in effect and produce it yourself.

Telling It Live

For anecdotes — and for a plan you had, seen looking back from a point in the past.

Spot the pattern

Someone telling a story about something that already finished:

  • "So I'm standing at the bus stop, and this woman just walks up and starts chatting to a dog that isn't even hers."
  • "He looks at me, dead serious, and goes, 'Is that mine?'"
  • "Next thing, the whole café is staring at us."

Every one of these already happened. What's odd about the verbs, and what effect does it have on you as the listener? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Every verb is in the present tense — simple or continuous — even though the story is finished. This is the historic present: a deliberate switch that puts the listener right there in the moment, mid-scene, instead of hearing a flat report. It usually kicks in once the story reaches the exciting part, often after "so" or "next thing."
(mid-story) present simple / present continuous — replacing the expected past tense

From report to live scene

"I was queuing for coffee when my old teacher tapped me on the shoulder." (report)
→
"So I'm queuing for coffee, and my old teacher taps me on the shoulder." (live)

Nothing about the facts changes — only how close the listener feels to the moment.

Spot the pattern — plans that changed

Three plans, told from a point in the past:

  • "I was going to ring you last night, but I completely lost track of time."
  • "She was going to hand in her notice — then they offered her a rise."
  • "He had no idea at the time, but he was to spend the next thirty years in that same house."

What's different about the last one compared with the first two? Have a think before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
was/were going to + base verb describes a plan someone actually made — usually one that got cancelled or overtaken by events. was/were to + base verb is more formal and narrative: it's the storyteller looking back and revealing what later turned out to happen, whether or not the person themselves ever planned it.
was / were going to + base verb — a plan someone made (often unfulfilled)
was / were to + base verb — formal narrative: what later happened, seen from an earlier point

Habits With Attitude

For habits that get on your nerves, or ones you secretly love — plus "will" for someone's true character.

Spot the pattern

Three habits, told with real feeling:

  • "He's always losing his phone — three times this month already."
  • "She's forever rearranging the furniture; I never know where anything is."
  • "They're constantly texting each other during dinner — it drives me mad."

Compare that with the flat statement "He loses his phone." What has changed, and what feeling does the change add? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
am/is/are + always/forever/constantly + verb-ing. The plain present simple ("he loses his phone") is a neutral fact. Swap in the continuous with "always/forever/constantly" and the habit suddenly feels endless and emotionally loaded — usually irritation, but context can flip it to affection just as easily.
am / is / are + always / forever / constantly + verb-ing

Your turn

1. My brother leaves the fridge open. It happens constantly and it genuinely annoys me.

Complain about it properly: "My brother the fridge open!"

2. My grandparents bring us homemade jam every time they visit. It's become a lovely tradition.

Describe it warmly: "My grandparents us homemade jam whenever they come."

Spot the pattern — "will" for character

  • "She will leave the window open, whatever I say about the cold."
  • "My dad will tell the same joke at every single family dinner."
  • "Ask him anything about football and he'll talk for an hour."

These aren't predictions about the future. What are they describing instead?

Check the pattern CLICK
Stressed will / 'll + base verb here describes a fixed, stubborn part of someone's character — "that's just what she does," not something about tomorrow. It's the same insistent flavour whether the habit is maddening or lovable.
subject + will / 'll + base verb — a fixed habit or character trait, not a future prediction

Your turn

3. No matter how many times I remind her, my flatmate forgets to lock the door. It's just what she does.

Complain about it: "My flatmate to lock the door, whatever I say."

4. Every single Sunday, without fail, my uncle rings just to talk about the weather. Honestly, it's rather sweet.

Describe him fondly: "My uncle every Sunday, just to chat about the weather."

5. Which one sounds most irritated, rather than neutral?

Breaking News Properly

The headline goes in one tense, the detail in another — get this the wrong way round and it sounds off.

Spot the pattern

Three pieces of news, told the way natives actually tell them:

  • "There's been an accident on the ring road — a lorry hit the central barrier around seven."
  • "I've had some news, actually — Priya's got the job. She starts in September."
  • "Something's gone wrong with the boiler; it started making a strange noise this morning."

Notice the two different tenses working together in each one. What job does each tense do? Have a think before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
The present perfect ("there's been," "I've had," "something's gone wrong") opens the news — it says "this is relevant right now" without pinning down exactly when. Once the story moves to the specific detail — what happened, or when — English switches to the past simple ("hit," "started"). Mixing a stated time with the perfect ("has hit this morning") is the mistake that gives learners away.
has / have + past participle — the headline (relevant now, no fixed time)
past simple — the detail, once a specific time or moment is named

Your turn

1. You just found out your street's bakery has closed down for good. It happened yesterday afternoon; you only heard this morning.

Break the news: "The bakery down!"

2. Now add the specific detail: it actually shut its doors at around three o'clock yesterday afternoon.

"It yesterday afternoon, apparently."

3. Your friend's car was broken into last night, and you're telling someone about it this morning. Which version breaks the news properly?

SPEAK

Think of a real piece of news — something that happened recently to you, your family, or somewhere near you. Break it to me: headline first with the perfect, then the detail with the past simple.

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: "lived" (past simple) suggests that year is closed — you don't live there now. "Have lived" (present perfect) leaves it open — you might still be living there, or the year still feels connected to now. Today's five forms all lean on this same "is it closed, or is it still relevant?" instinct.
