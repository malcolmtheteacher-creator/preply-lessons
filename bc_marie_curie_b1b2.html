Marie Curie

She believed that hard work and curiosity could change the world — and she proved it, as the first woman to do so much.

A scientist who never gave up

Today we will meet Marie Curie, one of the most famous scientists in history. First, let us talk about what you already know.

Talk to me first:
  • Have you heard the name Marie Curie before? What do you know about her?
  • Can you name any other famous scientists? What did they do?
  • Why do you think there have been fewer famous women scientists in history?
  • What makes a person a “genius”, in your opinion?

Her story

Marie Curie was born in 1867 in Warsaw, in Poland. At that time, Poland was ruled by Russia, and women were not allowed to study at university. But Marie loved learning, so she worked hard and saved money. Then she travelled to Paris to study at a famous university called the Sorbonne.

Life in Paris was difficult. She had very little money and often felt cold and hungry. But she studied science with all her energy. There she met another scientist, Pierre Curie. They fell in love, got married, and began to work together.

Marie and Pierre studied a strange new kind of energy that comes out of some special materials. Marie gave this energy a name: “radioactivity”. They discovered two new elements, which Marie called polonium (after her country, Poland) and radium. This work was new, exciting, and very important.

Her discoveries changed science forever, but they also slowly hurt her. She worked with dangerous materials for many years, and she almost certainly died because of them, in 1934. Even so, she never stopped believing in her work.

She believed there was nothing in life to fear, only things we need to understand better.

Over to you:
  • Marie left her country to follow her dream. Would you do the same? Why or why not?
  • She kept going even when life was hard. Where do you think people find that kind of strength?
  • Is it worth taking big risks for your work or your dream?

The big idea

Marie Curie's big idea is simple but powerful: curiosity, hard work, and refusing to give up can change the world. She was always asking questions about how things worked, and she was never afraid to spend years on a difficult problem.

What makes her story even more special is that she did all of this as a woman, at a time when many people believed women should not be scientists. Doors were closed to her again and again, but she opened them anyway. In 1903 she won the Nobel Prize in Physics, and in 1911 she won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, and she is still the only person ever to win it in two different sciences. She also became the first woman to teach as a professor at the Sorbonne.

She showed the world that a woman could be just as great a scientist as any man — and that no closed door has to stay closed.

Grammar focus — past simple and present perfect

When we tell Marie Curie's story, we use two important tenses. We use the past simple for her life and the things she did (her life is finished). We use the present perfect for things that are still true and still matter today.

1. Past simple — finished actions in the past

Use the past simple for actions that started and finished in the past. We often say when they happened.

subject + past form of the verb
She worked … / She discovered … / She was born …

She was born in 1867. (finished — a fact in the past)

She discovered radium with her husband. (a finished action)

She died in 1934. (finished)

2. Present perfect — the past that still matters now

Use the present perfect for things from the past that are still important today. We usually do not say exactly when they happened.

subject + have / has + past participle
She has become … / She has inspired …
Past simple (finished)Present perfect (still true now)
She won the Nobel Prize in 1903.She has won two Nobel Prizes. (the record still stands)
She was the first woman professor at the Sorbonne.She has become a symbol for women in science. (still true today)
Her work helped doctors during the war.Her work has changed science forever.

A simple test: if you can say the exact year, use the past simple. If it is about the lasting result or a “first” that still matters, use the present perfect.

Practice A — past simple or present perfect?

1. Marie Curie ____ (be) born in Poland in 1867.

2. She ____ (win) two Nobel Prizes, and no other person has done this in two sciences.

3. In 1891, she ____ (move) to Paris to study.

4. Her discoveries ____ (change) science, and they are still important today.

1. was (past simple — exact year)

2. has won (present perfect — still true now)

3. moved (past simple — exact year)

4. have changed (present perfect — still important)

Practice B — put the verb in the right tense

1. She ____ (become) the first woman to teach at the Sorbonne. (still a famous fact)

2. During the war, she ____ (build) special X-ray cars to help doctors. (finished, in the past)

3. Marie Curie ____ (inspire) millions of women, even now. (still happening)

4. Pierre and Marie ____ (get) married in 1895. (exact year)

1. has become (present perfect)

2. built (past simple)

3. has inspired (present perfect)

4. got (past simple — exact year)

A · Someone who never gave up (5 minutes)

B · A goal of your own (4 minutes)

What have you done so far to reach it? What do you still want to do? Use “I have…” for what you have already done, and the past simple for the moments along the way.

C · Curiosity and you (4 minutes)

Marie's curiosity led her to great discoveries. What are you curious about? What would you love to learn or understand better?

Tell me about something you have always wanted to learn, and something you tried to learn in the past. Did it go well?
