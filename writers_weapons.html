⚔️ Weapon 1: Simile
Make the reader SEE it.
⚔️
SIMILE
Comparing two things using "like" or "as...as"
❌ Boring
The armadillo sank quickly.
✅ With simile
The armadillo sank like a bowling ball dropped in a swimming pool.
Roald Dahl did this constantly. He'd compare things that have NO business being together. A person's face "like a shrunken walnut." That's what makes it funny — the surprise of the comparison.
Practice — Finish the simile (be weird!)

The termite chewed through the wire as fast as...

Cupid's radioactive glow was like...

The fish watched the angry numbers approach like...

💥 Weapon 2: Sound Effects
Make the reader HEAR it.
💥
ONOMATOPOEIA
Words that sound like the noise they describe
❌ Silent
The termite bit the wire and the bomb exploded.
✅ With sound
SNAP! The termite's jaws closed on the wire. BZZZZT! Sparks flew everywhere. Then — BOOOOOM!
Comic books are ALL onomatopoeia. POW! BAM! THWACK! But real writers use them too. They put SOUND into your brain while you read. Your story becomes a movie.

🎯 Weapon 3: Rule of Three
Three things together = funny, powerful, memorable.
🎯
RULE OF THREE
List three things — the third one hits hardest
❌ Flat
The armadillo was scared in the water.
✅ Rule of Three
The armadillo was wet, confused, and deeply offended.
Why three? Two feels incomplete. Four feels like a list. Three feels perfect. Comedians know this: "An Englishman, a Scotsman, and an Irishman walk into a bar..." The third one is ALWAYS the punchline.
Practice — Add the third thing (make it funny)

The gull was angry, hungry, and...

The fish tried to swim, tried to hide, and...

The bomb was big, shiny, and...

🥊 Weapon 4: The Short Hit
Long sentence... then a short one. PUNCH.
🥊
SHORT SENTENCE FOR IMPACT
Build up with a long sentence, then hit with a short one
❌ All same length
The termite crawled inside the bomb. It started eating the wires. The bomb went off and it was bad.
✅ Long then short
The termite crawled through the dark metal shell of the bomb, its tiny jaws working through wire after wire, sparks flying like tiny stars. Then it bit the red one.
This is how horror movies work too. Long slow build-up... then JUMP SCARE. In writing, the short sentence IS the jump scare. Four words can be more powerful than forty.
Practice — Write the short punch

I give you the long build-up. You write the 2-6 word hit.

The gull circled above the library, scanning the thousands of books below, choosing its target with the precision of a military bomber.

The armadillo paddled desperately, its little legs spinning, water flooding its armour, the shore getting further and further away.

🌋 Weapon 5: Hyperbole
Exaggeration so extreme it becomes comedy.
🌋
HYPERBOLE
Extreme exaggeration nobody takes literally
❌ Normal
The gull was very angry.
✅ Hyperbole
The gull was so angry it could have burned down every library on Earth with the heat of its rage alone.
You already do this in real life! "I'm SO hungry I could eat a horse." "I've told you a MILLION times." "This bag weighs a TON." Hyperbole is how humans make things funny. The trick is putting it on paper.
Practice — Go extreme

Make these SO exaggerated it's ridiculous.

The explosion was big.

The fish was scared.

The termite was hungry.

🪝 Weapon 6: The Cliffhanger
Stop at the WORST possible moment.
🪝
CLIFFHANGER
End at the most exciting moment — make the reader NEED more
❌ Everything resolved
The termite bit the wire and the bomb exploded and everything was destroyed. The end.
✅ Cliffhanger
The termite's tiny jaws closed around the last red wire. Deep inside the bomb, something clicked. And then—
Every Netflix episode does this. Screen goes black RIGHT when you need to know what happens. That feeling of "NO! TELL ME!" — that's POWER. Your writing can do the same thing.
Practice — Cut it off at the best moment

The armadillo is sinking. Something huge appears below.

The gull has found the most important book in the world.
