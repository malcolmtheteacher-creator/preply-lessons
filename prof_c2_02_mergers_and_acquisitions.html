Why this matters

Most M&A deals destroy value. The language you use as integration lead shapes whether this becomes one of them. You need to frame change as inevitable but structured, losses as tragic but necessary, and integration as a shared endeavor — not something being done "to" the acquired organization. At C2 level, you speak with complexity and candor.

Six M&A moves

1 Due diligence language +
Due diligence is the language of limitation. You're always working with incomplete information. Frame issues as "areas not yet fully assessed" rather than "problems we discovered." This leaves room for evolution without admission.
How it sounds

"Our diligence on talent retention has revealed areas where cultural alignment may require active management. We're developing comprehensive mitigation strategies."

2 Valuation framing +
The valuation is a starting point, not a ceiling. You justify it through synergies, but synergies are assumptions. Frame synergies as "value creation opportunities" that require execution. Execution risk is real.
The move

"We acquired at a 3.2x multiple. That multiple is justified by synergies we've identified. Our job now is to deliver those synergies — which means changing how we both operate."

3 Synergies — the honest version +
Synergies are almost always overstated. The honest framing acknowledges this. Revenue synergies are particularly risky. Cost synergies are more reliable but human. Name what "synergies" actually means: consolidation, elimination, structural change.
The move

"We've identified EUR 120m in cost synergies over three years. That means we'll consolidate seven regional centers into four. That means redundancies. We're modeling headcount reductions of roughly 200 roles in Year 1."

4 Integration milestones +
Integration is not a phase. It's a series of discrete, measurable decisions. Frame integration as milestones, not timelines. Timelines slip. Milestones are either achieved or not. The distinction matters for credibility.
The move

"By Month 3, we'll have aligned our IT platforms. By Month 6, our product lines will be consolidated. By Month 9, we'll have completed all headcount decisions. Each milestone is gated — we don't move to the next until we've completed the last."

5 Managing resistance to change +
Don't dismiss resistance as "fear of change." Resistance is rational. People will lose status, autonomy, or jobs. Acknowledge this. Then separate the legitimate concerns from those that reflect self-interest over organizational good.
The move

"I understand that integration feels like loss. For many of you, it is loss — loss of how things were. My job is to make sure that loss is necessary, not arbitrary. And I'll be transparent about which decisions are driven by strategic need and which are driven by cost."

6 Cultural integration language +
Culture isn't something you "fix." It's something you acknowledge, understand, and either preserve or deliberately change. Use language that respects what each organization brought, while being clear about what needs to change.
The move

"Both organizations have cultures of excellence. They're different — you value stability, they value speed. Our combined organization needs both. That means some of us will need to become more experimental. Some of them will need to become more rigorous."

High-stakes scenario

Three weeks into integration planning, the Head of Engineering from the acquired firm approaches you privately. He says: "I've got offers. Three of them. My team is panicking. Everyone thinks they're being replaced. We're losing people. You need to tell me — am I keeping my job or not?"

The truth: You don't know yet. The integration plan isn't final. You need his team, but you also need to consolidate engineering functions. You probably need him, but maybe not. You've got 100 engineers combined; you might need 75.
