Selling Your Sofa

You photographed it, priced it fairly, answered eleven messages and one 'is this still available?' at midnight. Now the buyer is actually here, walking around your sofa like a horse dealer. The listing said eighty. He has other ideas.

The Scene 🎬

Everyone knows how to haggle as a buyer. Then one day the sofa is yours, the doorbell rings, and you discover the other side of the game.

The sofa is good: comfortable, clean, one worn arm you photographed honestly and mentioned in the listing. Eighty is fair — you checked what others go for. You've already survived the marketplace gauntlet: the ghosts, the "would you take 30?" messages from three towns away, the man who asked fourteen questions and vanished.

Jan, at least, showed up. He's friendly, he has a van downstairs, and he has done this many times — because arriving with a van and a lowball is a strategy, not an accident. The van says: this could all be over in ten minutes if you'll just take less.

Your side of the game has different rules from buying. You don't chase; you hold. You declared the flaws first, so nothing can be "discovered". You know your floor — the number where you'd honestly rather keep it. And your best card is the one buyers forget sellers hold: you can simply say no, close the door, and sell it to Thursday's message instead.

  1. Why is it harder to defend a price than to attack one? What are we afraid the other person will think?
  2. Declaring the flaw before they find it — good ethics, good tactics, or both?
YouFair price, honest listing, a floor of sixty — and a mild, useful willingness to keep the sofa.
JanFriendly, experienced, van downstairs. Opens at half. Every move is practised; none of it is personal.
First: read the conversation and notice what You never does: never apologises for the price, never fills a silence with a discount, never argues with Jan's opinion of the sofa. The price just… stands there, like the sofa.

The Conversation 💬

Saturday, 10am. Jan has circled the sofa twice and pressed the cushions like a doctor.

  1. Jan tries three pressure moves before any real negotiation. What are they?
  2. The final deal is sixty-five. What did You add to make that number stick — and what did it cost You?
  3. Where is You's floor, and how does Jan learn about it?
1. "More worn than the photos" (discover a flaw), the soft cushion (find another), and the van on the meter (invent time pressure). Each is met calmly and none is argued with.

2. The two cushions and the fitted cover — "cost me thirty on their own". Sweetening the deal instead of cutting the price: the number holds, the value moves.

3. Around sixty: "At fifty-five I'd rather keep it, honestly." Said as a simple preference, not a threat — which is what makes it believable.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the seller's side of any deal — sofas, bikes, cars, your old phone. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The seller's voice is relaxed, because the seller's position is relaxed: you have the thing, they made the journey. Every phrase here works better slowly. The moment you sound eager, the price starts melting — so decide your floor before the doorbell, and let "I'd rather keep it" be true.

🔍 The inspection

Phrase 1

The job: answering "it's worse than the photos" without a flinch. Complete it:

"It's exactly as it was in the photos — have a ___ ___."

"It's exactly as it was in the photos — have a good look."
The opening lowball rarely starts with a number — it starts with doubt about the goods. This answer refuses the doubt and then does something bolder: invites more inspection. Only honest sellers say "have a good look", and buyers know it. Confidence about the goods is the foundation every later number stands on.

Take it anywhere: "It's exactly as described — have a good look." Works for anything you've listed honestly. Which is the catch: list honestly.
Phrase 2

The job: declaring the flaw before they can discover it. Complete it:

"I'll be ___ with you: it's got that worn patch on the arm, and that's the ___ of it."

"I'll be straight with you: it's got that worn patch, and that's the worst of it."
A discovered flaw costs twenty; a declared flaw costs nothing — it's already in the price. "That's the worst of it" is the powerful half: it draws a line under the inspection. There's nothing left to find, so there's nothing left to discount. Honesty, it turns out, is a negotiating position.

Take it anywhere: "I'll be straight with you: it's got ___, and that's the worst of it." Selling anything — including, in interviews, yourself.
Phrase 3

The job: defending the number itself. Which defence?

A. "Well, I suppose I could come down a bit if you're not happy…"
B. "I've priced it to sell — that's already the discount."
C. "Do you know what this cost new?!"
B — "I've priced it to sell — that's already the discount."
A surrenders before the battle — never discount because someone frowned. C is a feeling dressed as an argument; what it cost new isn't what it's worth now, and buyers know it. B reframes the whole listing: the haggling already happened, against yourself, before you posted it. Back it with a fact — "the same model's listed at ninety-five across town" — and the number grows roots.

Take it anywhere: "I've priced it to sell — that's already the discount." For every fair price you're tired of apologising for.

⚖️ Holding the line

Phrase 4

The job: gentle pressure — you have alternatives, and they should know. Complete it:

"I've had a fair bit of ___, so I won't ___ it long."

"I've had a fair bit of interest, so I won't hold it long."
The seller's mirror of the buyer's "I'll think about it". It reminds him that walking away cuts both ways — the sofa has a queue, and today's price is today's. It must be roughly true (there usually IS a Thursday woman in your messages somewhere), and it's said as information, not threat. Note Jan's scoff — "everyone has a Thursday woman" — and note that he raised his offer anyway.

Take it anywhere: "There's been a fair bit of interest, so I won't hold it long." Selling, subletting, even job offers.
Phrase 5

The job: refusing the number and countering in the same breath. Complete it:

"Fifty is a no — but I could ___ you at sixty-five."

"Fifty is a no — but I could meet you at sixty-five."
The clean counter has two halves and no cushioning: the flat refusal ("is a no" — not "I'm not sure…"), then the door left open ("meet you at"). Meet is the generous verb — it frames your counter as movement towards him, even though the number is yours. Never counter without refusing first; a counter alone reads as "keep pushing".

Take it anywhere: "___ is a no — but I could meet you at ___." Salaries, freelance rates, and every marketplace on earth.
Phrase 6

The job: revealing your floor as a simple preference. Put the pieces in order:

I'd ratherAt that pricehonestly.keep it,
"At that price I'd rather keep it, honestly."
The strongest sentence a seller owns — and it only works if it's true, which is why you decide your floor before the doorbell rings. It's not a threat and not a tactic: it's the honest point where the deal stops being better than no deal. Buyers can hear the difference between a bluff and a preference; a preference ends the descent immediately.

Take it anywhere: "At that price I'd rather keep it." Or its grown-up siblings: "At that salary I'd rather stay put." "At that rate I'd rather not take the project."

🎁 Closing without cutting

Phrase 7

The job: making your number easier to say yes to — without touching it. Complete it:

"If you take it today, I'll ___ ___ the cushions and the cover."

"If you take it today, I'll throw in the cushions and the cover."
To throw in — to add for free. The move that closed this deal: when the numbers deadlock, move value instead of price. The extras cost you little (they were leaving with the sofa anyway) but they let him say yes to sixty-five while feeling he won something. "If you take it today" attaches the sweetener to the decision, not to the sofa.

Take it anywhere: "If you ___ today, I'll throw in ___." Deadlocked negotiations everywhere: hold the number, sweeten the deal.
Phrase 8

The job: the terms, plainly, at the moment of yes. Complete it:

"Cash on ___, and it's yours."

"Cash on collection, and it's yours."
Marketplace deals die of vagueness: the "I'll transfer it later", the "hold it till Sunday, promise". Four words close every loophole — payment method, payment moment, and what triggers ownership. Said cheerfully, it's not distrust; it's the etiquette of dealing with strangers, and experienced buyers relax when they hear it.

Take it anywhere: "___ on ___, and it's yours." Payment on delivery, deposit on booking — name the trigger, close the loopholes.
Phrase 9

The job: giving them genuine permission to walk. Complete it:

"No hard ___ if it's not for you — it's a lot to ___ home."

"No hard feelings if it's not for you — it's a lot to lug home."
To lug — to carry something heavy, with effort. The paradox phrase: nothing signals a fair price like a seller relaxed about losing the sale. Pressure makes buyers suspicious; permission makes them decide. And when they do walk, the relationship survives — which matters, because walkers come back on Tuesday more often than you'd think.

Take it anywhere: "No hard feelings if it's not for you." The pressure-free close — for sales, invitations, and ideas at work.
Phrase 10

The job: sealing the buyer's happiness after the money moves. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Good decision — it's a great sofa. Mind the doorframe on the way out."
Buyer's remorse starts on the stairs. "Good decision" inoculates against it — the last thing he hears from you is confirmation he did well, which is what he'll repeat to whoever's holding the other end of the van. The doorframe line is the warmth: deal done, humans again. Five-star buyer review to follow.

Take it anywhere: "Good decision — you'll be happy with it." Close every sale by selling it one last time.

⚡ The twist

Round 1 again — and at the moment of handshake Jan opens his wallet, looks genuinely embarrassed, and says: "Ah. I've only brought fifty. That's honestly everything I've got on me — fifty, and we're done, or I've wasted your morning."

The oldest close in the book — and maybe it's even true. The van is downstairs. The Thursday woman is only mostly real. Your floor was sixty. What do you say?

The Echo 🕰️

In 1849, a hundred thousand people rushed to California to dig for gold. Almost none of them got rich. The fortunes went, famously, to the people standing still: the ones selling shovels, eggs, laundry and trousers to the diggers streaming past. A single egg could cost a dollar; a decent shovel, a week's wages. And a young trader called Levi Strauss did rather well in workwear.

The sellers' secret wasn't greed — it was knowing what things were worth when everyone around them was desperate to argue otherwise. Ten thousand diggers insisting eggs should be cheaper didn't move the price of eggs, because the merchants knew their floor: below this, I'd rather keep my stock. The diggers gambled; the sellers priced. History remembers who ate.

Your sofa is not a gold mine, and Jan is not a forty-niner. But the principle crossed two centuries intact: the person who knows what the thing is worth — and can say "I'd rather keep it" truthfully — sets the price. The person who only knows what they'd like to pay, pays it.
