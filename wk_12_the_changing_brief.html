The Changing Brief

Your favourite client is ringing about one more small change. It really is small. It's also the fifth one, and you've never mentioned the money once.

The Scene 🎬

Marisol is the client everyone wants. She's warm, she's enthusiastic about the work, she says thank you, and her invoices are paid before you've finished worrying about them. You would happily work with her for the next ten years.

She has also changed the brief four times. Not once did it feel like a change — each one arrived as "just a tiny tweak", each one took an afternoon rather than a morning, and each one you said yes to instantly, because saying yes is pleasant and doing sums out loud is not. Add them together, though, and the thing you're building isn't the thing you quoted for. You're doing roughly a third more work for exactly the same money, and the reason nobody has mentioned that is you. She doesn't know. She can't know, because you never said.

It's twenty past four on a Thursday and your phone is buzzing with her name on it. It'll be tweak number five. And here's the thing you need to be clear about before you pick up: you want the work. You are not going to say no, and this isn't a stand you're taking. It's much smaller and much harder than that — you're simply going to say the number out loud, in a friendly voice, so that tweak number six arrives with a price attached instead of an apology.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of a time you did extra work for someone and never mentioned it. What stopped you — was it the money itself, or something about how you'd sound if you brought it up?
  2. Is being easy to work with an advantage in your job, or has it quietly cost you something? Give a real example if you have one.

Who's who

You
The One Doing the Work 🛠️

Good at the job, allergic to the money conversation. You've said yes four times without blinking and now you have to say a number out loud to someone you genuinely like — which is somehow harder than any of the actual work.

The other voice
Marisol 📞

Your client. Enthusiastic, generous, pays on time, and has no idea she's been taking advantage — because from where she's standing, each thing she asked for really was tiny. She isn't a problem to be managed. She just hasn't been told.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's a phone call between two people who like each other and are both a bit embarrassed about money, so nobody is at their most eloquent. Keep the awkwardness in when you read it; that's where the lesson is.

MarisolHiya! Sorry, is this a bad — are you in the middle of something?
YouNo no, of course not. I mean, I am, but not — go on. Fire away.

YouMm. Right. Yeah. So — sorry, hang on. Yes — easily. Let me just tell you what it costs.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. You don't just hand Marisol a bill — there's an alternative offered alongside the price. What is it, and what does offering it do to the feeling of the conversation?

2. At one point You take a share of the blame. What exactly do You admit to, and what does that admission do for Marisol?

3. Marisol goes quiet more than once. What is she doing in those silences, and how does You fill them?

1. The FAQ page — it hasn't been built yet and it's about the same size, so she can swap one for the other instead of paying more. That turns the moment from a demand into a choice, and because the choice is hers, there's nothing for her to push back against.
2. That the silence was theirs: they should have said something at the second change, and they kept saying yes because it was easier than working out what it cost. It lets Marisol stop being the one at fault, which is the only reason she can stay in the conversation rather than get defensive.
3. She's doing the arithmetic and feeling rather bad about it — "Is it? Five?", then "Right. Right.", then just "Mm. Mm." You don't rush in to reassure her the first time; the pause is allowed to sit, and it does the work. Later, when she says she feels awful, You fill the space by handing some of the fault back and pointing out that half the changes improved the thing.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the moment you have to attach a number to something a nice person has asked you for. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Tone does nearly all of the work here. Every one of these phrases can be said as a complaint or said cheerfully, and the words don't change — only the voice does. So keep it light and keep it quick, because if you slow down and go serious, the other person hears an accusation and starts defending themselves, whereas if you say the number in the same tone you'd use for the weather, it's just information, and information is easy to say yes to.

✅ Saying yes first

Phrase 1

She's asked for a change. You want to do it — the last thing you want is for her to think you're reluctant. But the price has to arrive in the same breath. Which line does both at once?

A. "I'll have to look into whether that's even possible."
B. "Yes — easily. Let me just tell you what it costs."
C. "I'm afraid that falls outside what we agreed."
B — "Yes — easily. Let me just tell you what it costs."
The yes comes first and it comes fast, so she never has to wonder whether you're difficult, and the price then arrives as a detail rather than a refusal. "Easily" is doing a lot: it says the work isn't the problem, which quietly tells her that the money isn't a complaint either. A sounds like you're stalling, and C makes it a contract dispute before she's finished her sentence.
Phrase 2

You'd rather have the awkward bit now than let it turn up on the final invoice, where it would look like a trap. Put the chunks in order:

than surprise youI'd ratherat the endflag it now
"I'd rather flag it now than surprise you at the end."
To flag something is to raise it early so nobody can say later that they weren't told — it comes from putting a marker on a thing so it can be seen. The whole line works because it explains your motive before she has to guess at it: you're not asking for money, you're avoiding an ambush, and nobody argues with someone who is trying not to ambush them.

🔍 Making the invisible visible

Phrase 3

This particular change genuinely is small, and pretending otherwise would be a lie she'd see straight through. The problem isn't this one — it's the total. Complete the chunk: "___ ___ ___ it's nothing. It's the fifth one, though."

"On its own it's nothing. It's the fifth one, though."
You agree with her completely — the thing she asked for really is tiny — and then you move the subject from the request to the pile, which is where the actual problem has been living all along. "On its own" is the hinge: it concedes her point so thoroughly that she has nothing left to defend, and the number does the rest. Notice the "though" at the end rather than a "but" at the front; it lands softer that way.
Phrase 4

She's never once seen the changes added up, because nobody has ever shown her. You want to walk her through it — as a tour, not a charge sheet. Which line invites her to look rather than telling her off?

A. "You keep changing your mind, if I'm honest."
B. "Can I show you where we've drifted from the original?"
C. "The brief says four pages, so four pages is what you're getting."
B — "Can I show you where we've drifted from the original?"
To drift is to move slowly and without meaning to, the way a boat moves when nobody's steering — which is exactly what happened, and it's why the word is kind. And it's "we've drifted", not "you've changed it", so you're both in the boat. Asking permission with "can I show you" makes her a participant in the discovery instead of the accused. A blames her, and C is a door closing.

💷 Pricing it, cheerfully

Phrase 5

Now the actual number. The change will take you two extra days and your day rate is three hundred. Say it out loud, in one sentence, joining the work to the money — and say it casually, as if you were reading out a train time. Then reveal.

"That's about another two days, so it'd be an extra six hundred."
The days come before the money, so by the time she hears the figure she already knows what it's made of and it can't feel plucked from the air. "About" and "an extra" keep it light and a bit approximate, which is how people talk when they're relaxed, whereas an exact figure delivered precisely sounds like it has been rehearsed — and a rehearsed number sounds like a demand.
Phrase 6

There's a way to give her the change without either of you paying for it: trade it against something already in the plan. Offer her that, and hand the decision back. Put the chunks in order:

we've already agreedOr we swap it— your callfor something
"Or we swap it for something we've already agreed — your call."
This is the line that stops the conversation being about money at all, because now she isn't being charged, she's choosing, and the budget stops being a wall and becomes a shape she can rearrange. "Your call" means the decision is yours to make — it hands her the power back in two words, and people who have just been handed power very rarely fight you.

🤝 Not making it an accusation

Phrase 7

She's gone quiet and she's clearly mortified. You need her to understand that you're not cross — and that some of what she asked for genuinely improved the work. Which line does that without turning into a martyr?

A. "I don't want to make a fuss, but this is getting a bit silly now."
B. "I've been extremely patient about all of this, you know."
C. "This isn't a complaint. The project's better for half of these."
C — "This isn't a complaint. The project's better for half of these."
Saying what it isn't stops her filling in the blank herself, because in the silence she will assume the worst. The second half is what makes it believable: praise costs you nothing and it's true, and "half" is far more convincing than "all" would be, since if you'd said every change was an improvement she'd know you were being kind rather than honest. A and B are both complaints wearing a disguise, and she'd hear that instantly.
Phrase 8

Someone has to own the silence, and honestly it was yours — she asked, you said yes, you never mentioned the money. Take your share, lightly. Complete the chunk: "I should have said this at tweak two, honestly. That's ___ ___."

"I should have said this at tweak two, honestly. That's on me."
"That's on me" means I accept the fault for that, and it's short on purpose — a long apology would make it a scene, whereas four words let you take the blame and keep moving. A tweak is a very small adjustment, and using her own word for it here is generous, because you're agreeing that each one was minor even while you point at the pile. Taking a share early is the cheapest thing in this conversation and it buys the most: she can't be defensive about a mistake you've just said was partly yours.

🔮 Setting up the next one

Phrase 9

The real prize isn't the six hundred — it's that tweak number six arrives with a price already attached. Propose the routine, as a question, in three quick steps. Put the chunks in order:

you ask, I price it,Shall we do it this wayyou decide?from now on —
"Shall we do it this way from now on — you ask, I price it, you decide?"
Two of the three steps are hers, which is why it doesn't sound like a rule being imposed on her; she keeps the asking and she keeps the deciding, and all you've taken is the middle. "Shall we" makes it a suggestion she can accept rather than a policy she has to swallow, and the rhythm of the three short steps makes it sound like something obvious that you're both agreeing to at the same time.
Phrase 10

You've said the number, you've proposed the routine, and now you want to close it without her feeling cornered into a decision this afternoon. Which line lets her off the hook without taking any of it back?

A. "So can you confirm you're happy to pay the extra?"
B. "Nothing changes today. I just wanted it on the table."
C. "Anyway, forget I said anything — I'll just get on with it."
B — "Nothing changes today. I just wanted it on the table."
To put something on the table is to make it visible and available to be discussed, which is the entire aim — you're not chasing a decision, you're ending the state where the money was invisible. Removing the pressure is what makes the point stick, because she can now agree with you without agreeing to anything. A turns a conversation into an ultimatum, and C throws away everything you just did.

The Twist

🎯 "So what did the other four cost?"

It goes better than you expected. Marisol says it's completely fair, no argument at all — and then, because she's a reasonable person trying to do the right thing, she says: "Well, hang on. If this one's six hundred, what were the other four? Send me for those as well, I'd rather square it up properly." And you have no idea. You never tracked them. You didn't write anything down, because writing it down would have meant admitting there was something to write down. Start the timer and handle it live. The honest answer is "I don't know" — so how do you say that without either inventing a figure you can't defend or waving away money you've genuinely earned? What do you offer her instead of a number, and what does today cost you if you get it wrong?

The Bigger Picture 🔭

The person doing the work is nearly always the last one to mention what it's worth, and it isn't shyness exactly — it's that raising money feels like admitting you were counting, and counting feels like the opposite of caring about the work. So you say yes, and yes is lovely, and yes is free.

Except each small thing is genuinely small, which is what makes the arithmetic invisible. Nobody ever asks you for a third of a project. They ask you for an afternoon, four times, over five months, and each time you're only ever weighing up one afternoon against the awkwardness of saying so — which is a comparison you will lose every single time.

And being easy to work with is a genuine virtue, right up until it stops being a choice you're making and becomes a habit that makes the decision for you. The difference between the two is whether you could say the number if you needed to. Most people find out they can't only after they've spent a year not saying it.

Two bigger questions

  1. Why is it so much harder to say a number to someone you like than to someone you don't? And if that's true, does it mean your best clients are quietly the ones costing you the most?
  2. Where's the line between generosity and a habit you can't break? Think of someone in your working life who's easy, kind and always says yes — is that a strength you admire, or something you'd warn them about?
