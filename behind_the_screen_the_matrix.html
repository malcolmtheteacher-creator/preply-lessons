BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

The Matrix

Neo discovers that the world he has always trusted is a machine-built illusion — and the philosophers who first imagined this nightmare were arguing about it centuries before computers existed.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of The Matrix — a hacker who discovers that his entire reality is a simulation built to keep humanity docile while machines harvest its energy — trace that nightmare back to philosophers who imagined it long before computers existed, and master two structures for talking about worlds that aren't real: the second conditional (an unreal present) and the third conditional (an unreal past).
Before we begin — talk it through: How would you actually prove, right now, that what you're experiencing is real and not some kind of dream, illusion or simulation? Is there any test that could settle it for certain? And if you learned that your comfortable daily life was secretly controlled by something else entirely, would you want to know the truth — even if it was frightening — or would you rather stay comfortable and not know?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. Squinting across the burning desert, the travellers were sure they saw a lake glittering ahead — but it was only an illusion, a trick of the light with no water there at all.
an illusion (n) — a false appearance or belief; something that deceives the senses or the mind into seeing what isn't really there.
2. Colour-blind for years without knowing it, he could not perceive any difference between the red and green wires, though everyone else saw them as obviously different.
to perceive (v) — to become aware of something through the senses; to notice, recognise or understand it.
3. Outnumbered ten to one, the guards used tear gas to subdue the rioting crowd, until every last protester had been forced into quiet, exhausted submission.
to subdue (v) — to bring under control by force; to conquer, overpower or calm something that was resisting.
4. The old volcano had lain dormant for four hundred years, showing no sign of life at all, until one morning the ground began to shake again.
dormant (adj) — inactive, sleeping or showing no current activity, but capable of becoming active again.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. sentient is closest in meaning to: (a) unable to feel anything   (b) able to feel and be aware   (c) extremely fast
(b) able to feel and be aware — sentient (adj) means capable of sensing or feeling; conscious.
6. docile is closest in meaning to: (a) easily controlled   (b) violently angry   (c) highly intelligent
(a) easily controlled — docile (adj) means easy to control, teach or manage; calm and obedient.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. Each autumn the farmers harvest the wheat before the frost can ruin it, gathering in a single strained week the crop of a whole year's growth.
To harvest something is: (a) to plant it for the first time; (b) to gather it in once it's ready; (c) to destroy it completely.
(b) — to harvest (v) — to gather a crop or resource that has grown or built up, so that it can be used.
8. Mid-sentence, the news presenter's face froze and repeated the same half-second of speech three times before the picture corrected itself — just a glitch in the broadcast signal.
A glitch is: (a) a small, temporary technical fault; (b) a planned special effect; (c) a change of channel.
(a) — a glitch (n) — a small, usually brief malfunction or fault in a machine, system or plan.

D. Infer from context — two bigger ideas

9. Pull one loose thread and an entire sweater can unravel, the neat pattern coming apart stitch by stitch until nothing is left but a tangle of wool.
to unravel (v) — to come undone or fall apart, thread by thread; to investigate and expose something that was hidden or confused.
10. Some skills must be learned through years of practice, but a newborn's instinct to grip a finger placed in its palm is innate — present from birth, never taught.
innate (adj) — existing in someone from birth; natural rather than learned.

The film, in three parts

The Wachowskis' The Matrix (1999). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A question that won't leave him alone

Thomas Anderson lives two lives: by day a bored, ordinary programmer; by night the hacker "Neo," chasing a question that has haunted him for years — a nagging, unprovable sense that something about the world is wrong. A stranger named Trinity finds him first, then a mysterious figure called Morpheus, who claims that everything Neo has ever perceived — his job, his apartment, the sky over his head — is an elaborate illusion.

Morpheus offers a choice that cannot be taken back: a blue pill, and Neo wakes tomorrow believing none of this happened; or a red pill, and he learns the truth, whatever it costs. Neo swallows the red pill.

What he wakes into is nothing like his old life. His actual body has spent his entire remembered existence floating, dormant, in a pod of warm liquid, wired into a machine. Morpheus explains, gently and without flinching: the world Neo knew was "the Matrix," a simulated 1999 fed directly into human minds, built by machines that rose up after humans blocked out the sun. Billions of dormant human bodies are now kept as a living power source, their warmth and electrical activity harvested to keep the machines running — and the Matrix exists to keep a docile, distracted mind from ever asking why.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What choice does Morpheus give Neo, and what does each option mean?

2. According to Morpheus, what is the true purpose of the Matrix?

1. A blue pill, so he forgets everything and wakes in his old life; or a red pill, so he learns the whole truth, whatever it costs. He takes the red pill.

2. It is a simulation that keeps human minds docile and distracted while machines harvest their bodies' energy to power themselves.

Discuss:
  • Morpheus says he is only offering "the truth, nothing more." Is knowing an unbearable truth always better than staying inside a comfortable illusion? Would you take the red pill?

Part 2 — Learning to see the code

Freed but weak — his real muscles have never once been used — Neo must relearn how to live in a body that has spent its whole life lying still. Morpheus's crew trains him inside simulations of their own: in seconds, martial arts programs are uploaded directly into his mind, and Morpheus explains what Neo still can't quite accept — that the rules of the Matrix are only as fixed as a computer program's rules, and can be bent, or broken, by someone who truly understands that none of it is real.

A ship's mystic called the Oracle tells him he already suspects, in some part of himself, that he is not "the One" prophesied to end the war between humans and machines — though her tests seem to suggest otherwise. A small boy nearby, apparently bending a spoon with his mind, tells Neo the real trick: "there is no spoon." It is not the spoon that bends, only your own understanding of what's possible.

Not everyone on the crew has kept faith with this brutal, exhausting reality. Cypher, worn down by years of grim food and constant danger, secretly strikes a deal with the Matrix's enforcing programs, the Agents: betray Morpheus, and be reinserted into the simulation with his memory wiped, comfortable and unaware, forever. His betrayal turns a routine mission deadly, and Morpheus is captured.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. According to the training, and the boy with the spoon, what is the real key to bending the rules of the Matrix?

2. Why does Cypher betray the crew, and what does he ask for in return?

1. Understanding, completely, that it is only a program with rules — not a fixed physical reality — so those rules can be bent or broken.

2. He is exhausted by the hard, real world and wants to be put back into the Matrix with no memory, comfortable and unaware, forever — so he tells the Agents where to find Morpheus.

Discuss:
  • Cypher chooses a comfortable, false memory over a hard, real freedom. Is that a betrayal, or an understandable human choice? What would ignorance be worth to you?

Part 3 — Choosing to believe

To save Morpheus, Neo and Trinity return to the Matrix themselves, walking willingly back into the machine's control to rescue him before the Agents can erase his mind completely. The rescue succeeds, but escaping afterwards does not: cornered in a subway station, Neo is confronted by Agent Smith, the coldest and most relentless of the enforcing programs, and is shot.

Outside the simulation, on the ship, Neo's body goes still. Trinity, who has come to love him, refuses to accept it, and tells him — half plea, half prophecy — that he cannot be dead, because she was told she would fall in love with the One, and she has fallen in love with him. Inside the Matrix, Neo's heart restarts. He opens his eyes and, for the first time, actually sees the Matrix for what it is: not a solid world but a cascade of underlying code, patterns he can read and reshape at will.

Bullets that would have killed him a minute earlier now simply stop in the air. Neo defeats Agent Smith not through greater force but by finally understanding, completely, that the reality trying to kill him is only a program — and he is no longer bound by its rules. He ends the film promising the machines' controlled world that he will show every mind trapped inside it the truth, and a world "where anything is possible."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What finally happens to Neo when he is shot by Agent Smith, and why?

2. What changes about how Neo sees the Matrix once he "wakes" the second time?

1. His heart stops, but Trinity's declaration of love — and the prophecy behind it — revives him; he comes back believing, completely, that he is the One.

2. He starts to see it as streams of underlying code rather than a solid world, and can bend or ignore its rules — bullets no longer harm him.

Discuss:
  • Neo becomes unstoppable the moment he truly believes the Matrix isn't real. Is belief itself a kind of power — in the film, and maybe in real life too?

Grammar — the 2nd & 3rd conditionals

No film asks "what if none of this were real?" more insistently than The Matrix. English has two structures built exactly for imagining what isn't true: one for an unreal present or general situation, one for an unreal, unchangeable past.

The two structures:
• second conditional: If + past simple, would + base verb — an unreal or unlikely present/future: "If the Matrix collapsed tomorrow, nobody outside it would notice a thing." "If you swallowed the red pill, you would see the world completely differently."
• third conditional: If + past perfect, would have + past participle — an unreal, alternative past that cannot now be changed: "If Neo had chosen the blue pill, he would have forgotten this conversation by morning." "If Cypher hadn't betrayed the crew, Morpheus wouldn't have been captured."
Feel the difference:
• the 2nd conditional imagines a different present or future — the door is still, in theory, open.
• the 3rd conditional imagines a different past — the door already closed; nothing can now undo it.

1. Second or third? Choose the form the meaning needs

The Real Story — the philosophy behind the illusion

The Matrix isn't just science fiction borrowing scary ideas at random. Its central question — how do you know your reality is real? — is one of the oldest and most serious problems in philosophy, and the film knowingly quotes its sources.

🏛 Plato's Cave (Republic, Book VII, c. 375 BCE)

More than two thousand years before computers existed, the Greek philosopher Plato told a story of prisoners chained in a cave since childhood, facing a blank wall. Behind them a fire burns, and figures carry objects past it, casting shadows on the wall — the only reality the prisoners have ever known. Mistaking the shadows for the whole of existence, they have no reason to imagine anything more real behind them. Plato then imagines one prisoner freed and dragged out into the sunlight, dazzled and disbelieving, only slowly coming to understand that the shadows were never the real things at all — only the faintest trace of them. Morpheus's red pill, dragging Neo out of a comfortable, shadow-thin world into a painfully bright reality, retells this idea almost exactly.

Descartes' evil demon, and "I think, therefore I am"

In 1641, the French philosopher René Descartes pushed the same doubt even further, in his Meditations on First Philosophy. To find something he could be completely certain of, he imagined the worst possible case: what if not firelight and shadows, but a powerful, deceiving "evil demon," were feeding false experiences directly into his mind — making the sky, his own hands, even basic mathematics, an elaborate lie? Could anything at all survive that level of doubt?

Descartes found exactly one thing that could: the very act of doubting proves a doubter exists. Even a mind being completely deceived must exist in order to be deceived. He had already reached the same conclusion, more famously, in his 1637 Discourse on the Method: "cogito, ergo sum" — "I think, therefore I am." A brain fed false signals, or a mind wired into a machine, can still be certain of exactly one thing: that it is thinking.

The Simulation Argument — Nick Bostrom (2003)

The Matrix was released in 1999. Four years later, the Oxford philosopher Nick Bostrom gave the ancient question a strikingly modern, statistical form in a paper titled "Are You Living in a Computer Simulation?" His argument is a trilemma: either almost every civilisation like ours destroys itself before it can build the necessary technology; or advanced civilisations overwhelmingly choose not to run detailed simulations of their own history; or — if neither of those is true — nearly every mind like yours is, in fact, one of the simulated ones, hugely outnumbered by the original minds it was copied from. Bostrom's paper doesn't claim we ARE in a simulation — only that, logically, one of those three unsettling statements has to be true. The film couldn't have been drawing on Bostrom's paper (it came four years earlier) — but it dramatised, in advance, exactly the unease his argument later formalised.

📖 Baudrillard's Simulacra and Simulation — the book Neo actually holds

The film names its own source directly. In the opening scenes, Neo hollows out a copy of a real book — Simulacra and Simulation (1981), by the French theorist Jean Baudrillard — to hide his hacking disks. Later, Morpheus calls the ruined world outside the Matrix "the desert of the real," a phrase lifted straight from Baudrillard's opening pages. Baudrillard's real argument was subtler than the film's: he claimed modern life is saturated with copies, media images and signs so total that they have replaced any direct experience of "the real" — not one dramatic red pill, but a slow, ordinary drowning in simulation that we barely notice. Tellingly, Baudrillard himself said publicly that the Wachowskis had misunderstood his book, and that The Matrix was more a symptom of the simulated culture he described than any serious response to it.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Which of these four ideas — Plato's cave, Descartes' demon, Bostrom's trilemma, or Baudrillard's "desert of the real" — feels closest to what the film is actually about?
  • The film compresses over two thousand years of philosophy into one action movie. What does it gain from that, and what does it lose or oversimplify?
  • Baudrillard said the film missed his point entirely. Does it matter whether a film "gets the philosophy right," if it still gets people thinking about these questions?
