Plans That Fell Through: "I was going to..." / "I was about to..."

For an intention that existed at a point in the past — especially one that changed, or something that came within a second of happening.

Spot the pattern

Three plans, seen from the moment they were made:

  • "We were going to spend New Year in Lisbon, but the flights got cancelled."
  • "I was just about to send the email when I noticed the mistake."
  • "She was on the point of giving up when the results finally came through."

Look at what follows each one — does the plan actually happen? What tiny word usually shows up next, revealing that something got in the way? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Almost never as planned — and the word is usually "but" (or "when"). was/were going to names an intention that existed then; was/were about to and was/were on the point of + -ing describe something a second away from happening. All three describe the future as it looked from that earlier moment — not from now.
was/were going to + infinitive (an intention that existed then)
was/were about to + infinitive (a second away from happening)
was/were on the point of + -ing (the same idea, more formal)

From intention to interruption

"He intended to resign."
→
"He was going to resign — but he changed his mind."

The plan is named and, at the same time, marked as unfinished business — the reader expects a twist.

Looking Back at What Came Next: "...would become" / "...was to"

For telling a story and looking forward from a point in the past — revealing what, at that moment, still lay ahead.

Spot the pattern

  • "None of us guessed, sitting in that tiny kitchen, that we would still be friends thirty years later."
  • "The intern who barely spoke in meetings would go on to run the entire department."
  • "He was to take over the family business, but he chose to become a teacher instead."

In the first two sentences, does the speaker know all this at the moment the story is set, or only now, looking back? And in the third — did what was expected actually happen? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Only looking back. would lets a storyteller jump ahead and reveal the future from inside a past scene, as if it were already written. was/were to does something similar but sounds more formal and literary — and it often introduces a twist, showing that what was expected didn't actually happen (as in the third sentence, with "but").
would + infinitive (the future, seen from a point in the past)
was/were to + infinitive (the same idea, more formal — often with a twist)
Don't confuse this "would" with the everyday habit meaning ("When I was a child, I would spend hours reading"). Here, "would" always points forward in time to something that, at that moment, hadn't happened yet.

Official Instructions & Arrangements: "is/are to" / "was/were to"

For formal orders, rules and arrangements — not your own plan, but something decided by someone in authority: a notice, a schedule, a policy.

Spot the pattern

  • "Visitors are to report to reception before entering the building."
  • "The results are to be announced on the university website, not by phone."
  • "Staff are not to use the side entrance while it is being repaired."

Could you replace "are to" with "must" in each sentence without really changing the meaning? What kind of writing do these lines sound like they come from? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Yes, roughly — is/are to is a formal cousin of "must," typical of official notices, itineraries, contracts and instructions, not casual conversation. It reports a decision made by someone in authority, not the speaker's own wish.
am/is/are + to + infinitive (a formal instruction or arrangement — present)
was/were + to + infinitive (the same, reported from the past)

The same structure moves easily into the past, for reporting an arrangement that existed at the time:

"According to the original schedule, the delegation was to arrive at nine, but their flight was delayed by fog."

"Under the old rules, all applications were to be submitted in writing."
