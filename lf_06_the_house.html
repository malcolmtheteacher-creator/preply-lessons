The House

Your father's kitchen, six weeks after the funeral. Two of you, one house, and a conversation neither of you wants to be the first to start.

The Scene 🎬

The house smells of him still — the coats in the hall, the paper he never threw out, the drawer by the sink that has stuck since 1998. Your father died six weeks ago. He left the house to the two of you, equally, in a will written in 2004 that says nothing at all about what "equally" is supposed to feel like.

So there are two of you in the kitchen this afternoon. You, who rang on Sundays and drove up for birthdays. And Claire, your older sister, who did the appointments, the hospital car park, the shopping, the bins, the bad nights — for three years — and never once said so out loud, which somehow makes it louder.

And here is the ugly part, the part nobody says at a funeral: you need the money. Not in a greedy way. In a mortgage way. So does she, probably. Neither of you knows how to put that sentence in the same room as a dead man's coats.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. In your family, who would be the Claire — the one who does the driving, the ringing, the actual looking after? Does that person get thanked, or just relied on?
  2. Is it possible to talk about money and grief in the same conversation without one of them poisoning the other? Or should they always be kept apart — money first, feelings later?

Who's who

You
The One Who Rang on Sundays 📞

Grieving, guilty, and quietly skint. You want your half, you know you didn't earn the way she did, and you would rather lose the money than lose her — but you'd rather lose neither.

The other voice
Claire, Your Older Sister 🔑

Three years of nursing him, and she'd do it again. She's not asking for credit and she'd be embarrassed if you gave her any — but she's noticed, and she's exhausted, and she needs the money too.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how the hardest things get said — who says the awkward thing first, how the money gets separated from the feelings, and how every suggestion is put as a question rather than a decision. That's the skill here, not the outcome.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Both of them end up agreeing to sell, but neither of them ever actually proposes selling. So what made the decision?

2. One of them says something they clearly expect to be judged for. What was it — and what did the other one do with it?

3. They're getting on perfectly well, and yet they agree to bring in a stranger to price the house and to write things down. If they trust each other, why bother?

1. The arithmetic did. Neither of them can afford to take on the whole house — she'd already asked at the bank and been turned down, he'd done the sums repeatedly hoping for a different result — so selling is simply what's left once the impossible options have gone. Nobody has to argue for it, which means nobody has to be blamed for it later.
2. Admitting to needing the money, six weeks after the funeral, which he braces for by saying it quickly and calling it awful himself. She doesn't judge it — she agrees it's true and then admits the same thing about herself, so the shameful thing turns out to be shared rather than one-sided.
3. Not because either of them is suspicious now, but because neither wants to be suspicious later. A price from someone with no connection to either of them can't be doubted, and a written note protects them from the honest, tiring business of remembering the same afternoon in two different ways in six months' time.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the conversation where money and grief are in the same room. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Nothing here should sound prepared, because a smooth voice in this conversation sounds like someone who has been rehearsing their case. Go slowly, let yourself hesitate, and put every suggestion up as a genuine question with a rise at the end — you're thinking aloud together, rather than presenting a position. If you find your voice getting firm and fluent, you've stopped talking to your sister and started negotiating with her.

🚪 Opening it before it hardens

Phrase 1

You want the two of you to sort this between yourselves while it's still a conversation and not a case. Which one keeps the door open?

A. "I'm not having solicitors involved in this, and that's that."
B. "Shall we try and do this ourselves, before the solicitors get into it?"
C. "I suppose we should each get some legal advice, shouldn't we?"
B — "Shall we try and do this ourselves, before the solicitors get into it?"
A is a decision announced at her, which invites her to fight it, and C quietly sets the two of you up on opposite sides before anyone has said anything. B is a question, so she can shape the answer, and "get into it" describes people arriving and starting to pull something apart — which is what you're trying to be ahead of. "Try and" rather than "try to" is what makes it sound spoken rather than drafted.
Phrase 2

You want to settle the money while you're both calm, and keep his things for afterwards, so that the two conversations don't contaminate each other. Put the pieces in order:

and then do the rest?Can we getout of the way first,the money bit
"Can we get the money bit out of the way first, and then do the rest?"
To get something "out of the way" is to deal with it early so it stops looming over everything else, which is exactly what money does in a room full of a dead man's belongings. Calling it "the money bit" shrinks it down to one small item on a list, rather than The Money, and "the rest" quietly promises that the other things still matter and are still coming.

🕯️ Saying the unfair thing out loud

Phrase 3

She has spent three years doing what you didn't do, and she is braced for you to talk around it. Complete the chunk with one word — a stressed auxiliary verb that turns a flat admission into an insistent one: "You did far more for him than I did. I ___ know that."

"You did far more for him than I did. I do know that."
"I know that" on its own can sound like you're brushing her off, whereas the extra "do" carries the stress and means something closer to "you may think I haven't noticed, but I have" — which is the sentence she has been waiting three years for. Saying it first, before any numbers, means she never has to claim it herself, and nobody has to be told what they're owed.
Phrase 4

Money six weeks after a funeral feels shameful to mention, so you're going to name your own need before she has to guess at it. Put the pieces in order:

because I do.I don't need the money,I'm not going to pretend
"I'm not going to pretend I don't need the money, because I do."
The shape does the work: instead of confessing, you refuse to perform, which turns a shameful admission into a piece of plain honesty. It also disarms the accusation before she can make it, since nobody can catch you out over something you volunteered — and it gives her permission to say the same about herself, which is usually what happens.

🧮 Facing the arithmetic together

Phrase 5

You've both quietly worked out that neither of you can pay the other for their half. Complete the chunk — two gaps, two words: "Neither of us can afford to buy the other ___, ___ we?"

"Neither of us can afford to buy the other out, can we?"
To "buy someone out" is to pay them for their share of something you both own, so that it becomes wholly yours. The tag at the end is worth noticing: after a negative like "neither of us can", the tag turns positive — "can we?" — and it invites her to confirm the bad news with you rather than receive it from you, so the wall you're both facing is a fact rather than your argument.
Phrase 6

The house has to be priced, and whoever chooses the person doing the pricing will look like they've stacked the deck. Which line takes that suspicion off the table for good?

A. "Could we get it valued by someone neither of us knows?"
B. "You get a price, I'll get a price, and we'll see whose is nearer."
C. "Get whoever you like, I'll trust whatever they say."
A — "Could we get it valued by someone neither of us knows?"
B hands you two numbers and a competition, and C sounds generous but leaves you free to feel cheated later, which is worse than arguing now. A gives the decision to a stranger with nothing to gain from either of you, so no number can ever be traced back to one sister's choice — and "neither of us knows" is deliberately even-handed, since it disqualifies your friends as well as hers.

💛 Getting underneath the money

Phrase 7

Prices have taken over, and you suspect the thing she actually wants costs almost nothing. Put the pieces in order:

if the moneyWhat would you wantwasn't in it at all?
"What would you want if the money wasn't in it at all?"
If money is "in it", it's part of the situation and shaping what everyone says, so taking it out for a moment lets her answer as a daughter instead of an heir. It's a hypothetical question, which is precisely why it's safe — she isn't claiming anything by answering, so she can tell you the truth, and the truth is very often smaller and cheaper than either of you feared.
Phrase 8

A whole house has to be emptied, and the temptation on a long Saturday is to speed up and start throwing things out. Which line protects you both?

A. "Take anything you want, I'm honestly not bothered about the stuff."
B. "Don't throw anything away without checking with me first."
C. "Nothing goes to the tip until we've both been through it."
C — "Nothing goes to the tip until we've both been through it."
The tip is where you take rubbish to be dumped, and it's where an exhausting afternoon tends to end. A gives away a claim you may badly want back in a year, and B makes her the suspect and you the judge, whereas C is a rule that binds the two of you identically — "we've both" — so it protects her from you just as much as you from her.

🖊️ Making it last, and keeping her

Phrase 9

You'll both remember today slightly differently by the spring, and neither of you will be lying. Complete the word — the prefix mis- means "wrongly", so this verb means "remember it wrong": "Shall we write down what we've agreed, just so neither of us mis___?"

"Shall we write down what we've agreed, just so neither of us misremembers?"
The whole diplomacy of it sits in that one prefix: misremembering is an honest mistake that tired, grieving people make, whereas "so neither of us goes back on it" would accuse her of lying before she'd done anything. "Just" makes it a small piece of admin rather than a legal move, and "neither of us" means you're guarding against yourself too.
Phrase 10

Whatever the house is worth, she is worth more, and you want her to know that before the estate agents and the paperwork start. In your own words, tell her that she matters more than any of this — and say it so it sounds like a fact rather than a favour. What would you say?

e.g. "Whatever happens, I'm not falling out with you over a kitchen."
To "fall out with" someone is to quarrel and stop speaking, sometimes for years, which is exactly what happened to Sandra at work and her brother. Notice the deliberate smallness of "a kitchen": naming the huge thing — the house, the inheritance — would make it sound worth fighting over, whereas shrinking it to one room makes the fight sound as daft as it would look from the outside in ten years' time.

🔑 Make them yours

These phrases aren't only for houses. They work anywhere two people who love each other have to share something out. Pin three of them to your own life, so they're there when you need them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I do know that." — someone in your life has done more than you have, and has never once mentioned it. Who, and what did they do?

"I'm not going to pretend I don't need the money, because I do." — where are you currently being polite about something you actually need?

"What would you want if the money wasn't in it at all?" — a disagreement where the argument isn't really about the thing you're arguing over. What is it really about?

The Twist

😬 She's changed her mind

Four weeks on. The stranger's price came back, the envelope's on your fridge, there's a buyer, and everything is going exactly as the two of you agreed. Then Claire rings. She's been sleeping at the house most nights and she can't do it — she wants to keep it. Her plan: she stays, you get every penny of your half, just not yet. A bit each month, no bank, no paperwork, nothing signed. "You'd get it all in the end. You know I'm good for it." And you need your half this year, not in eleven. Start the timer. Everything you agreed has just gone, and there is no version of this where you both get what you want — so choose, out loud, and tell her. One rule: you may not say "let me think about it".

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Wills are written by people who imagine that "equally" is a simple word. It isn't. One of you gave three years and the other gave Sundays, and no solicitor has a column for that, so the arithmetic comes out clean and the feeling doesn't.

What breaks families isn't usually greed. It's the thing nobody said — the years that went unmentioned, the need that felt too shameful to admit in a dead man's kitchen. Silence, left alone, turns into a story, and the story is nearly always worse than the truth.

Which is why the sentences in this lesson are mostly just people saying the obvious thing before it can rot. It's not beautiful. It's an afternoon, and an envelope, and a sister you still ring at Christmas.

Two bigger questions

  1. Should the child who does the caring inherit more? Nobody agrees to it in advance, and almost everybody feels it afterwards — so is "equal shares" fair, or just easy?
  2. Think of a family you know that stopped speaking after a death. Was it really about the money — or was the money simply the first thing solid enough to argue about?
