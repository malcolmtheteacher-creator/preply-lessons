The Postman's Palace

A village postman tripped over a stone — and spent the next thirty-three years building a palace with it.

Today: you'll tell the true story of a postman who built a palace with his own hands — and talk about your own past with used to and would.

The story, in three parts

Read each part, or listen to it. Then answer the questions before you go on to the next part.

Part 1 — The stone

Ferdinand Cheval was a postman in Hauterives, a small village in the south-east of France. He had left school at thirteen. Every day he walked his round: about thirty kilometres, on foot, in sun and in snow.

He used to walk alone, and while he walked he used to dream. In his dreams he built a palace — strange, enormous, like nothing in France.

One day in April 1879, when he was forty-three, he tripped over a stone and nearly fell. He picked it up. It had such a strange shape that he put it in his pocket. The next day he went back to the same place and found more.

After that he began to collect stones on every round. At first he would carry them in his pockets. Then he took a basket. In the end he would leave small piles of stones beside the road and come back in the evening with a wheelbarrow.

True or false? Click your answer.

1. Cheval had studied to be a builder. No — he left school at thirteen and worked as a postman.

2. He found the first stone by accident. Yes — he tripped over it.

3. He carried all the stones home in his postbag. No — first his pockets, then a basket, then a wheelbarrow.

Talk about it
  • Thirty kilometres a day, alone. What do you think about when you walk or travel alone?
  • He went back the next day to look for more stones. What does that tell you about him?

Part 2 — Thirty-three years

He started to build in his garden. He had never learned to build anything, so he taught himself. His ideas came from the postcards and magazines that he delivered: temples from India, castles, caves, strange animals.

He worked after his round, often at night, by the light of an oil lamp. He would sleep for only a few hours and then walk thirty kilometres again.

His neighbours mocked him. They used to call him the village fool — a poor man who filled his garden with stones. But Cheval was stubborn. He did not answer them. He just went on building.

He worked like this for thirty-three years. When he finished, in 1912, he was seventy-six. The palace was about twenty-six metres long and, in places, twelve metres high, with giants, towers, stairs and animals — all made of stones, lime and cement.

On the walls he carved his own words. One line says: '10,000 days, 93,000 hours, 33 years of struggle.' Another says: 'The work of one man.'

True or false? Click your answer.

1. Cheval's ideas came from the postcards and magazines in his postbag. Yes — he copied the temples, castles and animals he saw in them.

2. His neighbours helped him with the heavy work. No — they laughed at him. He worked alone.

3. The palace took more than thirty years to build. Yes — thirty-three years, from 1879 to 1912.

Talk about it
  • The neighbours mocked him for years and he never answered. Could you do that?
  • He carved 'The work of one man' on the wall. Why was that important to him, do you think?

Part 3 — The second building

Cheval wanted to be buried inside his palace. French law did not allow it. So, at seventy-eight, he started again. He spent eight more years building a tomb for himself in the village cemetery. He finished it in 1922 and died two years later, at the age of eighty-eight.

For a long time, the experts did not know what to do with the palace. Was it art, or only a strange pile of stones? Some artists loved it — Pablo Picasso came to see it. Some officials called it ugly and wanted nothing to do with it.

In 1969 the French government decided. The palace was protected by law as a historic monument. Today more than a hundred thousand people visit it every year, and many of them call it a masterpiece.

The neighbours used to laugh at the postman and his wheelbarrow. Now the wheelbarrow is kept at the palace, and visitors stop to look at it.

True or false? Click your answer.

1. Cheval is buried inside his palace. No — the law did not allow it. He built a tomb in the cemetery.

2. Everybody agreed at once that the palace was great art. No — some officials called it ugly. It was protected only in 1969.

3. Today the palace is protected by law. Yes — it has been a historic monument since 1969.

Talk about it
  • He was seventy-eight when he started the tomb. What does that tell you about him?
  • Is the palace art? Who decides — experts, visitors, or the person who made it?
  • Tell the story in six sentences. Use: every day · one day · at first · for thirty-three years · but · in the end.

Talking about a past that was repeated: used to and would

This story has two kinds of past. Some things happened once: he tripped, he finished the palace in 1912. Other things happened again and again for years: he used to walk thirty kilometres, he would carry stones in his pockets.

FormUse it for…From the story
past simpleone finished eventIn 1879 he tripped over a stone.
used to + verbrepeated actions and situations that are not true nowHe used to walk alone. He used to be a postman.
would + verbrepeated actions only (not situations)At first he would carry them in his pockets.
didn't use to / Did you use to…?negative and question (no d)People didn't use to visit Hauterives.
The one thing to notice: you can say he used to be a postman, but not he would be a postman. Be, have, live, know, think, like describe a situation, not an action — so they take used to, never would.

1. Once, or again and again?

Write the verb in the past simple (one event) or with used to (repeated). Read the whole sentence first.

1. Every day Cheval (walk) thirty kilometres.

2. In April 1879 he (trip) over a stone.

3. For years his neighbours (call) him the village fool.

4. He (finish) the palace in 1912.

5. After his round he (work) at night, by the light of a lamp.

6. In 1969 the government (protect) the palace by law.

1. used to walk  ·  2. tripped  ·  3. used to call  ·  4. finished  ·  5. used to work  ·  6. protected

In 1, 3 and 5, would + verb is also correct: these are repeated actions.

2. Can you say it with would?

True = you can change 'used to' to 'would'. False = you cannot. Click your answer.

1. He used to be a postman. No — be is a situation, not an action.

2. He used to carry stones in his pockets. Yes — he would carry stones. A repeated action.

3. People used to think he was mad. No — think here is an opinion, a situation.

4. He used to sleep for only a few hours. Yes — he would sleep for only a few hours. A repeated action.
