Question 1: What is the difference between natural suffering and moral evil? Give one example of each.

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Natural suffering is caused by natural forces outside human control—earthquakes, diseases, famines, accidents, birth defects. Moral evil is suffering caused by human wrongdoing and cruelty—murder, theft, abuse, war, cruelty. Example of natural suffering: a child born with a disability, a famine from drought. Example of moral evil: a person deliberately harming another, genocide, theft causing poverty.

Question 2: State the logical problem of evil. If God is all-powerful, all-knowing, and wholly good, why does evil exist?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: If God is all-powerful (omnipotent), He can prevent all evil. If God is all-knowing (omniscient), He knows in advance that evil will happen. If God is wholly good (omnibenevolent), He would want to prevent evil and suffering. Therefore, logically, an all-powerful, all-knowing, wholly good God should prevent all evil. Yet evil clearly exists in the world. This appears to be a logical contradiction—either God cannot prevent evil (not omnipotent), or doesn't know about it (not omniscient), or doesn't care (not omnibenevolent).

Question 3: How did Augustine explain natural suffering? What role does sin play in his theodicy?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Augustine argued that all suffering—including natural disasters—is a direct consequence of original sin (Adam and Eve's disobedience to God). When humanity's first parents sinned, they corrupted not just human nature but the entire creation. Natural suffering is punishment for this primordial sin; it is not arbitrary or unjust but deserved. God is just to permit earthquakes, diseases, and deaths because these are the natural consequences of a corrupted world. Augustine's theodicy answers the logical problem by saying God is not unjust—suffering is punishment, and justice requires punishment for sin.

Question 4: What is the free will defence? Why do some Christians argue that evil is necessary for human freedom?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: The free will defence argues that genuine human freedom—the ability to make real moral choices—requires the genuine possibility of choosing evil. If God removed the option to do wrong, humans would not be truly free; they would be programmed robots forced to obey. A world in which humans can freely choose good or evil is better than a world of unfree puppets. Therefore, God permits moral evil to preserve human free will, which is a profound good. This explains moral evil (suffering caused by human wrongdoing) but not natural suffering.

Question 5: Name three different non-Christian perspectives on evil and suffering studied in this unit, and identify one key concept from each.

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Three non-Christian perspectives: (1) Islam: qadr (divine decree—everything is predetermined by Allah) and sabr (patient endurance as a virtue). (2) Buddhism: dukkha (suffering is inherent in all existence) without dependence on God. (3) Humanism: evil disproves God's existence; suffering must be addressed through reason and science. (Alternate: Irenaeus's soul-making theodicy—Christian but different from Augustine.)

The Foundation: Types of Suffering (Lesson 1)

Natural Suffering: Caused by natural forces—earthquakes, tsunamis, disease, accidents, famine. Not caused by human choice or wrongdoing.
Moral Evil: Caused by human wrongdoing and cruelty—murder, theft, abuse, war, injustice. The result of human free choices.

The Problem: The Logical Contradiction (Lesson 2)

The Problem of Evil is a fundamental philosophical challenge to belief in God. If God exists and is:

  • Omnipotent (all-powerful): God has the power to prevent all suffering
  • Omniscient (all-knowing): God knows about all suffering, past, present, future
  • Omnibenevolent (wholly good): God desires to prevent suffering

Then logically, God should prevent all suffering. But suffering exists. This is a logical contradiction. Philosophers like David Hume and J.L. Mackie have argued this problem cannot be solved—it's a genuine logical flaw in theism.

Christian Response 1: Augustine's Original Sin Theodicy (Lesson 3)

Core Claim: Suffering is Just Punishment

For natural suffering: When Adam and Eve disobeyed God (original sin), they corrupted the entire creation. Natural disasters, diseases, and death are the just consequences of this primal sin. God is not unjust in permitting them.

For moral evil: Humans misuse their free will and freely choose to do wrong. God does not cause evil but permits it to preserve human freedom.

Strength: Provides a unified explanation for both types of suffering—punishment for sin and respect for freedom. Addresses both natural and moral evil with a coherent theodicy.
Weakness: Why should we be punished for someone else's sin from thousands of years ago? Is it just to cause earthquakes that kill innocent children who have committed no sin?

Christian Response 2: Irenaeus's Soul-Making Theodicy (Lesson 3)

Core Claim: Suffering Develops Virtue

Evil and suffering are not punishments but are necessary for spiritual growth. Without hardship, challenge, and resistance, humans cannot develop virtues like courage, compassion, patience, perseverance, and wisdom. A world without suffering would be a world without moral development. God deliberately creates a world with suffering to allow humans to grow spiritually.

Humans are created "in God's image" but not perfectly—we must develop toward moral and spiritual maturity through facing difficulties and choosing the good despite opposition.
Strength: Makes sense of why a good God would allow suffering—it serves a higher purpose in human development. Explains the value of struggle and why easier lives might not be better for character formation.
Weakness: Does the amount and intensity of suffering in the world (child cancer, genocide, natural disasters killing thousands) seem necessary or proportionate for soul-making?

Islamic Response: Divine Decree and Patient Endurance (Lesson 4)

Core Concepts: Qadr, Sabr, Akhirah

Qadr (Divine Decree): Everything that happens—including suffering—is decreed and willed by Allah. Nothing happens by chance. This is not fatalism or determinism but trust in Allah's perfect wisdom and plan. Muslims believe submission to Allah's will brings peace.

Sabr (Patient Endurance): Bearing suffering with patience and faith is a virtue. How one responds to hardship reveals faith and character. Sabr is not passive acceptance but active virtue—maintaining faith, doing good, and seeking understanding even amid suffering.

Akhirah (Afterlife): Justice is not always visible in this world, but Allah will balance all suffering with reward in the next life. Earthly suffering may be recompensed eternally. Those who endure hardship with faith will be rewarded abundantly in paradise.

Strength: Offers a comprehensive framework combining divine trust (qadr), moral virtue (sabr), and ultimate cosmic justice (akhirah). Provides believers with meaning and comfort.
Weakness: The promise of afterlife justice doesn't solve suffering in the present moment. Some argue that trusting divine decree could excuse indifference to injustice.

Buddhist Response: Acceptance Without God (Lesson 4)

Core Concept: Dukkha and the Middle Way

Buddhism does not ask "Why does God allow suffering?" Instead, it takes a fundamentally different approach. It acknowledges that suffering (dukkha) is inherent in existence itself. Suffering arises from attachment, craving, and ignorance—not from divine punishment or design. The Buddha taught the Four Noble Truths: (1) suffering exists, (2) it has causes (attachment and craving), (3) it can end, and (4) the path to ending it is the Noble Eightfold Path.

The Noble Eightfold Path includes: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. These lead to liberation (nirvana) and freedom from suffering.
Strength: Avoids the logical problem of evil entirely by rejecting dependence on God. Offers practical, personal methods to reduce suffering here and now. Empowers individuals to address suffering themselves.
Weakness: For theists, simply rejecting God doesn't solve the problem of evil's existence in a theistic worldview. The problem of evil remains for those committed to monotheistic faith.

Humanist Response: Evil Disproves God (Lesson 5)

Core Argument: Atheistic Conclusion from the Problem of Evil

Humanists argue that the logical problem of evil has no adequate solution and that proposed theodicies fail when tested against real suffering. If a good, all-powerful God existed, evil would not exist. Since evil clearly does exist in vast quantities, either God does not exist, or He is not all-good and all-powerful (or both). Suffering is caused by natural forces (disease, accidents, natural selection) and human wrongdoing (injustice, cruelty, war, neglect). These problems must be addressed through reason, science, compassion, justice, and social action—not through prayer to a non-existent God.

Humanists advocate for practical solutions to suffering: medical research to cure disease, education to reduce ignorance and cruelty, social policies to reduce inequality and injustice, environmental protection to mitigate natural disasters.
Strength: Takes the logical problem of evil seriously and draws what they see as the only rational conclusion. Focuses energy on practical, evidence-based solutions rather than theological explanations.
Weakness: Religious believers argue that the theodicies (Augustine, Irenaeus, Islamic responses, soul-making) do provide coherent and satisfying answers to the logical problem. The debate continues.

Writing Framework (Genuine Scaffolding, Not a Template)

Introduction (1-2 sentences)

Argument 1: First Perspective (3-4 sentences)

Present evidence from your first chosen tradition. Use specific details and examples. Example prompts: "Augustine argues that... because..." or "Buddhism suggests that... which means..." Explain why this perspective is relevant and what it contributes to answering whether evil disproves God.

Argument 2: Second Perspective (3-4 sentences)

Present evidence from a different tradition. Show how it differs from the first and compare their approaches. Does this perspective strengthen the case that evil disproves God, or does it defend God's existence? Why do the two positions differ? What does each gain or lose?

Argument 3: Third Perspective or Counter-Argument (3-4 sentences)

Either present a third tradition, or address a challenge to your position. Examples: "However, a Humanist might object that..." or "Irenaeus's soul-making theodicy suggests..." Show intellectual honesty by acknowledging opposing views and explaining why you find your position stronger.

Conclusion (2-3 sentences)

Restate your position clearly. Which arguments were strongest in the debate? Why did some positions convince you more than others? Is evil truly a disproof of God, or can theodicies adequately explain it? What is your final reasoned conclusion based on the evidence?

Example Opening Sentences (Choose Your Position)

Assessing Strengths and Weaknesses: Key Questions

As you evaluate each position in your essay, ask yourself these questions about each theodicy:

  • Internal coherence: Does the position logically hold together? Are there contradictions within the theodicy itself?
  • Explanatory power: Does it adequately explain both natural suffering AND moral evil? Does it address the full scope of evil in the world?
  • Practical comfort: Does it provide comfort and meaning to people who suffer? Or does it seem cold and unsatisfying?
  • Proportionality: Does the explanation seem proportional to the amount of suffering we observe? Does extreme suffering seem necessary for the theodicy's purpose?
  • Assumptions: What does each theodicy assume about God, human nature, or the purpose of existence? Are those assumptions reasonable?
  • Logical gaps: Are there logical jumps or unsubstantiated claims in the theodicy?

Question 1: What is the logical problem of evil, and why do philosophers like David Hume think it may be unsolvable?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: The logical problem is that God's three core attributes (omnipotence, omniscience, omnibenevolence) seem logically incompatible with the existence of evil. If God is all-powerful, He can prevent evil; if all-knowing, He knows it will happen; if wholly good, He wants to prevent it. Therefore, such a God should prevent all evil. But evil exists. This is a genuine logical contradiction. Hume and others argued that theistic theodicies cannot resolve this fundamental logical flaw.

Question 2: Compare and contrast Augustine and Irenaeus. How do their theodicies differ in explaining suffering?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Augustine: Suffering is punishment for original sin; the world is corrupted because of human disobedience. Suffering is deserved. Irenaeus: Suffering is not punishment but a necessary tool for spiritual growth; the world is deliberately designed with challenges so humans can develop virtues. Augustine looks backward to sin as the cause of suffering; Irenaeus looks forward to human development as the purpose of suffering.

Question 3: Explain qadr and sabr and how they work together in Islamic theodicy.

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Qadr is divine decree—everything that happens, including suffering, is predetermined by Allah as part of His perfect plan. Sabr is patient endurance—the virtue of accepting Allah's will with trust and equanimity. Together, they create a framework: suffering is not arbitrary (qadr) but is a test of faith that should be borne with virtue (sabr). The afterlife (akhirah) provides ultimate justice beyond this world if earthly justice is delayed.

Question 4: What is dukkha in Buddhism, and how does Buddhism avoid the problem of evil?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Dukkha is suffering inherent in existence itself—not caused by God but arising from attachment, craving, and ignorance. Buddhism avoids the problem of evil by not claiming God exists. Instead, it offers practical methods (the Noble Eightfold Path) to reduce suffering through ethical living, mental discipline, and wisdom. Buddhism is agnostic on God's existence but focuses on ending suffering through personal practice.

Question 5: How do Humanists use the problem of evil as an argument against God's existence?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Humanists argue that the logical problem of evil has no adequate solution. If a good, all-powerful God existed, evil would not exist. Since evil does exist, either God does not exist, or He is not all-good and all-powerful. Therefore, evil is evidence that disproves God's existence. Humanists see suffering as resulting from natural forces and human choices, problems to be solved through reason, science, and compassion—not prayer to a non-existent God.

Key Terms Summary (16 Terms)

Theodicy
An attempt to justify God's existence and goodness despite the presence of evil and suffering in the world. The word literally means "justice of God."
Problem of Evil
The fundamental philosophical question: How can evil and suffering exist in a world created by an all-powerful, all-knowing, wholly good God?
Natural Suffering
Suffering caused by natural forces beyond human control: earthquakes, disease, accidents, famine, natural disasters.
Moral Evil
Suffering caused by human wrongdoing and cruelty: murder, theft, abuse, injustice, war, cruelty.
Omnipotent
All-powerful. The theological attribute that God has unlimited power over all creation.
Omniscient
All-knowing. The theological attribute that God knows everything—past, present, future, all possibilities.
Omnibenevolent
Wholly good. The theological attribute that God is perfectly good and desires only good outcomes.
Original Sin
In Christian theology, humanity's first sin (Adam and Eve's disobedience) that corrupted creation and made suffering inevitable.
Free Will Defence
The argument that true human freedom requires the genuine possibility of choosing evil. God permits moral evil to preserve human free will.
Soul-Making Theodicy
Irenaeus's theodicy: suffering and hardship are necessary for humans to develop virtue and spiritual maturity.
Qadr
Islamic concept of divine decree or predestination: the belief that everything that happens is willed by Allah as part of His perfect plan.
Sabr
Islamic virtue of patient endurance. Bearing hardship with faith, trust, and equanimity as a test of character.
Akhirah
The Islamic concept of the afterlife. Justice delayed in this world will be realised in the next life.
Dukkha
Buddhist concept of suffering inherent in all existence. Arises from attachment, craving, and ignorance. One of the Four Noble Truths.
Noble Eightfold Path
The Buddhist path to ending suffering: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.
Humanism
A non-religious worldview emphasising reason, science, and compassion. Argues that evil disproves God and suffering must be addressed through human effort and reason.
Unit Summary & Final Insight: This unit has explored one of the deepest questions in human thought: How can evil and suffering exist in a world created by a good God? Different religious and philosophical traditions answer this question very differently, revealing different assumptions about God, human nature, and the purpose of existence. Augustine and Irenaeus defend God's existence by appeal to punishment for sin and spiritual growth through hardship. Islamic theology emphasises divine trust (qadr), patient virtue (sabr), and ultimate justice in the afterlife (akhirah). Buddhism sidesteps the entire God question by rejecting the premise and offering practical solutions to suffering through the Noble Eightfold Path. Humanism denies God altogether, using the logical problem of evil as evidence of God's non-existence, and instead advocates reason and science as solutions to suffering. No single answer satisfies everyone. The question remains genuinely open and genuinely important. Philosophers, theologians, and ordinary people continue to grapple with it.
