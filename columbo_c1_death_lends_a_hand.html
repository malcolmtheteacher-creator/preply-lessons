THE COLUMBO CASEBOOK · B2/C1 · 50 min

Death Lends a Hand

A discreet private investigator sees an opportunity — and a rumpled lieutenant sees a detail.

Today: you'll tell the story of an investigator who crosses the line into blackmail, and you'll master the third conditional — the grammar of regret and “what if”.
Warm up — have a go, there are no wrong answers.
Talk about:
  • What do you think makes a person trustworthy with a secret?
  • Have you ever wished you could change one small decision? You don't have to say what it was — just say how it feels.
  • Would you rather be a detective who plays by every rule or one who takes shortcuts to catch a criminal?

The words for today's story

Work it out from the situation

Read each sentence aloud and say what you think the bold word means — then reveal.

People hired him because nothing they told him ever reached anyone else. He never discussed a client, not even with his own family. His reputation rested on his discretion.
discretion (n) — the quality of being careful about what you say, so that private matters stay private.
“Pay me two thousand a month,” he said, “or your husband will see these photographs.” It was blackmail, and she knew it.
blackmail (n/v) — threatening to reveal someone's secret unless they pay you or do what you want.
He had no interest in who got hurt: he fired his oldest friend, ruined a rival and never once looked back. He was a ruthless businessman.
ruthless (adj) — having no pity; willing to hurt others to get what you want.

Which word?

Read the meaning and try to find the word before you reveal. The first letter is given.

Which word means staying calm and in control of your feelings, especially under pressure?  c________ (n)
composure (n) — calmness and self-control. She kept her composure throughout the interview.
Which word means a sudden strong urge to do something without thinking about it?  i______ (n)
an impulse (n) — a sudden urge to act. On impulse, he bought the car.
Which word means a false reason you give to hide your real reason?  p______ (n)
a pretext (n) — an invented excuse. He visited her on the pretext of returning a book.

Choose the meaning

Pick a, b or c, then reveal.

bluff — “He had no proof, so he decided to bluff.”  bluff means:
a) speak very loudly   b) pretend to be more certain or powerful than you are   c) run away
(b) — to bluff (v) means to pretend you have something (proof, strength, a good hand of cards) that you do not have.
lure — “The advertisement was designed to lure customers into the shop.”  lure means:
a) tempt someone to go somewhere   b) frighten people away   c) lose track of
(a) — to lure (v) means to attract someone, often into a trap, by offering something attractive.
scrutinise — “The inspector began to scrutinise every inch of the carpet.”  scrutinise means:
a) read out loud   b) clean carefully   c) examine in great detail
(c) — to scrutinise (v) means to examine something very closely and carefully.

Odd one out

Three of these are kinds of error. One is not. Which is the odd one out?  
a slip  ·  a blunder  ·  a triumph  ·  a mistake
a triumph is the odd one out — it is a great success. a slip (n) — a small careless mistake, often one that reveals something you wanted to keep hidden.
Use them out loud:
  • Describe a time when someone kept their composure in a difficult situation.
  • What is the difference between a pretext and an honest reason?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, try the quick check from memory, then talk about the question.

Part 1 — A man who keeps secrets

Brimmer runs a private investigation agency, and his reputation is built on discretion. Clients trust him with their most private problems. One day a wealthy publisher hires him to follow his wife and report on where she goes.

Brimmer does his job, but he sees something else too: an opportunity. Instead of simply reporting what he has found, he goes to the wife and tries to blackmail her.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is Brimmer's reputation based on?

2. What does Brimmer do with what he discovers?

1. On discretion — he keeps his clients' secrets.

2. He tries to blackmail the wife.

Discuss:
  • Should a private detective's loyalty be to the client who pays him, or to the truth? Why?

Part 2 — A moment of violence

The wife refuses to be frightened. She threatens to expose him instead, and the argument ends in a moment of violence that Brimmer never planned. Suddenly he is left with a body and very little time.

But Brimmer is a professional. He knows exactly how a detective would examine a scene like this, and he sets out to stay one step ahead. What he has not prepared for is a detective who looks nothing like the ones he has met: Columbo.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does the quarrel turn violent?

2. What advantage does Brimmer believe he has?

1. She refuses to be blackmailed and threatens to expose him.

2. His professional knowledge of how detectives investigate.

Discuss:
  • Can expertise make a person overconfident? Think of an example, in crime or elsewhere.

Part 3 — The trap

Columbo notices a cut on the victim's cheek, and he sees that it matches a ring Brimmer wears on his hand.

He cannot prove it, so he invents a story about a lost contact lens. Brimmer, anxious about what might be found in his car boot, hurries to check it, and so walks straight into the trap. He confesses.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What small detail connects Brimmer to the crime?

2. How does Columbo make Brimmer give himself away?

1. A cut on the victim's cheek that matches Brimmer's ring.

2. He invents a story about a lost contact lens, so Brimmer rushes to check his car boot.

Discuss:
  • Is it fair for a detective to trick a suspect with an invented story? Where should the line be?

Grammar — the third conditional

Regret, blame and “what if”: the third conditional is how English talks about a past that did not happen. It is the language of detectives, historians and anyone who wishes they had acted differently.

The structure:
• If + past perfect, … would / wouldn't have + past participle — an imagined different past and its imagined result: “If the lights had gone out, nobody would have noticed.”
• I wish / If only + past perfect — regret about the past: “She wishes she had told someone.” “If only he hadn't panicked.”
• Mixed conditional — a different past action with a result now: If + past perfect, … would + base verb: “If he had listened, he would be free today.”
Watch out: never put would after if. Say “If she had known…”, not “If she would have known…”.

1. Complete the third conditional

Write the correct form of the verbs in brackets, one in each gap.

1. If the publisher (not / hire) a detective, none of this (happen).

2. If Brimmer (keep) his temper, he (not / become) a murderer.

3. Columbo (not / notice) the cut if he (not / look) so closely.

1. hadn't hired … would have happened

2. had kept … wouldn't have become

3. wouldn't have noticed … hadn't looked

2. Regrets: wish and if only

Complete with the correct form of the verb in brackets.

1. Brimmer wishes he (never / try) to blackmail her.

2. If only he (not / panic) about the car boot!

3. She wishes she (go) to the police straight away.

1. had never tried (wish + past perfect)

2. hadn't panicked (if only + past perfect)

3. had gone (wish + past perfect)

3. Past action, present result

These are mixed conditionals. Complete with the correct form: the result is about now or about the past — read carefully.

1. If Brimmer hadn't lost his temper, the woman (be) alive today.

2. If the publisher had checked, he (not / find out) the truth so late.

3. If Brimmer had been less greedy, he (still / run) his agency now.

1. would be (result is now)

2. wouldn't have found out (result is in the past)

3. would still be running (result is now)

Regret chain

Take turns. One person starts with “If Brimmer hadn't…”; the next person finishes and adds a new link with “…would have…, and if…”. How long can the chain go?

Start here: “If Brimmer hadn't tried to blackmail her, …”
A possible chain: …she wouldn't have threatened him; and if she hadn't threatened him, there wouldn't have been a quarrel; and if there hadn't been a quarrel, Columbo would never have come to the house…

Speak

Do all of this out loud. The timer keeps you going: fill the time with detail.

Useful language: for regrets — If only…, I wish…, If … had …, … would have …. To tell the story: “What he doesn't realise is…”, “It's only when… that…”, “That's what gives him away.” For opinions: “I'd argue that…”, “To my mind…”.

Task B · Is it fair to trick a suspect? (4 minutes)

The detective uses a made-up story to make the suspect give himself away. Police in many countries are allowed to bluff in some ways and not in others. Build an argument for where the line should be, using examples, and decide who is protected by that line.

Task C · What if…? (4 minutes)

Describe a moment in history, in a film or in everyday life when one small different decision would have changed everything. Use the third conditional and at least one mixed conditional.

Stuck for an idea? Choose one:

  • A travel plan that went differently because of a delay.
  • A friendship that began by chance.

To finish — the bigger questions
