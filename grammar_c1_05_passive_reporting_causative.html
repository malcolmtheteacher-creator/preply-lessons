Look again at what Anna and Priya say. Neither of them names exactly who "they" are, or who told Priya about the office. Why might English deliberately avoid naming the source when passing on something uncertain? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
Because neither speaker actually knows for certain — naming a source would claim more confidence than they have. English has a whole set of passive structures built exactly for this: reporting something without committing to its truth. The same instinct applies to arranging things: often you care about the result ("the roof's fixed"), not about who exactly did it.

Reporting Carefully: "It's said that..."

For passing on claims, rumours and reports without saying exactly who told you, or how sure you are.

Spot the pattern

Three claims you might overhear:

  • "It is thought that the old cinema will reopen as a bowling alley."
  • "It is rumoured that the couple next door are moving to Spain."
  • "It is reported that the motorway will be closed for resurfacing this weekend."

Before you check: what three ingredients appear in every bold phrase?

Check the pattern CLICK
it + is/are + a reporting verb in the past participle (thought, rumoured, reported, said, believed, understood) + that + clause. It puts the claim at the centre and quietly removes any named source.
It + is/are + said / thought / believed / reported / rumoured / understood + that + clause

From "people say" to a careful report

"There's a rumour that the bakery down the street bakes the best bread in town."
→
"It is rumoured that the bakery down the street bakes the best bread in town."

The specific source disappears completely — the claim stands on its own, and the speaker isn't personally vouching for it.

Your turn

1. Everyone says the new manager is very strict.

Report it carefully, without naming your source: "It that the new manager is very strict."

Naming the person directly: "She is thought to..."

You can also start with the person or thing itself instead of "it":

  • "It is believed that she is currently in Portugal." → "She is believed to be currently in Portugal."
  • "It is thought that the artist died in poverty." → "The artist is thought to have died in poverty."

Notice the difference between the two examples — one uses "to be", the other "to have died". What decides which one you need?

Check the pattern CLICK
Timing decides it. If the situation is happening now (she's in Portugal right now), use to be. If it happened before the report (the artist died in the past), use to have + past participle.
subject + is/are + believed / thought / said / reported / understood + to be ... (same time)
subject + is/are + believed / thought / said / reported / understood + to have + past participle (earlier time)

2. It is said that the shop on the corner is under new management.

Now name the shop directly: "The shop on the corner under new management."

3. It is thought that the letter arrived too late to be counted.

Name the letter directly: "The letter too late to be counted."

Getting Things Done

For arranging someone else to do a job for you — repairs, appointments, favours.

Spot the pattern

  • "We had the roof repaired after the storm."
  • "I'm getting my car serviced tomorrow."
  • "She's having her photos printed at the shop on the corner."

None of these people did the job themselves. What's the shared structure?

Check the pattern CLICK
have / get + the thing + past participle. Someone else does the job; you just arrange it. "Get" sounds a touch more informal, and sometimes hints at a bit more effort, than the neutral "have".
have / get + something + past participle

From "someone will do it" to "I'm having it done"

"A mechanic is going to fix my brakes."
→
"I'm having/getting my brakes fixed."

Arranging a specific person: "have someone do" vs. "get someone to do"

Two more everyday examples, with a small but important difference:

  • "We had the electrician check the wiring." — straightforward, no persuasion needed.
  • "I got the plumber to come back and finish the job properly." — took a bit of asking.

Compare the two bold phrases closely: what changes after the person's name, and why might that connect to how easy each favour was to arrange?

Check the pattern CLICK
With have + person, the verb stays in its plain base form — no "to": had the electrician check. With get + person, you need to before the verb: got the plumber to come back. "Get" often implies you had to ask, persuade or arrange it; "have" is more neutral, as if it were simply organised.
have + person + base verb (no "to")
get + person + to + base verb (persuasion / effort implied)

When It Happens To You: "I got locked out"

For something unlucky or unplanned that happens to you — no arranging involved this time.

Spot the pattern

  • "I got caught in a sudden downpour without an umbrella."
  • "My wallet got lost somewhere on the train."
  • "The fence got knocked down in the wind last night."

Nobody arranged any of this — it just happened. What's the structure?

Check the pattern CLICK
get + past participle. A plain passive like "was cancelled" just states the fact, neutrally. "Got cancelled" adds a more personal, informal feel — often a sense of bad luck or mild frustration, with nobody in particular to blame.
get + past participle (informal — something unwanted happens to the subject)

2. Say it out loud: what's the difference in meaning between "The window was broken" and "The window has been broken"?

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
Both are passive, but "was broken" just states a finished past event — it could have happened years ago and already be fixed. "Has been broken" is present perfect: it happened recently and the result (a broken window right now) still matters.
