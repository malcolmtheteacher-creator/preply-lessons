Asking for Directions

New city, dead phone, and a cathedral you swear was right here a minute ago. Time to stop a stranger and find your way.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

You've been in this city for exactly one afternoon, and you've already fallen in love with it — the narrow lanes, the washing strung between the balconies, the smell of coffee round every corner. There's just one problem: you have absolutely no idea where you are.

You wanted the old cathedral. The map on your phone said it was five minutes away, then it spun in a slow circle, then it said "no signal", then the battery gave a little sigh and died. Somewhere behind these rooftops is the thing you came to see — but the streets curl and double back, and every alley looks like the last one.

Coming towards you is a local — unhurried, at home here, the kind of person who'd know these streets blindfolded. You have about three seconds to stop them nicely. And when the directions come, they won't come slowly. The real skill today isn't asking — it's making sure you actually understood the answer.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last get properly lost — no signal, no idea which way was which? How did you get yourself out of it, and did you enjoy it more or less afterwards?
  2. Some people would sooner wander for an hour than stop a stranger and admit they're lost. Which are you — and what is it about asking for help that people find so hard?

Who's who

You
The Traveller 🧭

Curious, a little jet-lagged, phone stone dead. You'll happily admit you're lost — but you're determined to leave this corner knowing exactly where to go, not just nodding and hoping.

The other voice
The Local 🏘️

Knows every lane, bakery and dead end by heart. Friendly, quick, and gives directions at the speed of someone who's given them a thousand times — landmarks flying past faster than you can catch them.

When you're the traveller

🙋 Stop someone
"Sorry to bother you. You couldn't point me in the right direction, could you?"
"Excuse me, do you know the area at all?"
📍 Say where you want to go
"I'm trying to find the old market square. Am I anywhere near it?"
"Whereabouts is the nearest metro, roughly?"
🔁 Ask them to repeat
"Sorry, would you mind running that by me once more?"
"Sorry, could you take me through it a bit more slowly?"
✅ Check you understood
"So, let me get this straight: left at the lights, then second right?"
"…is that right?"
🚶 Ask about options
"Is it walkable, or am I better off jumping in a cab?"
"How long would that take on foot?"
🌟 Thank them
"Honestly, you've been a lifesaver. Thanks ever so much."
"That's so kind of you."

When you're the local

"Oh, that's easy. Go down here and take the second left."
"You'll see a bakery on the corner."
"It's about ten minutes on foot."
"I'm heading that way, so I'll show you."
"You can't miss it."

When something changes

"Is there a quicker way?"
"Is there a landmark I should look for?"
"Which side of the road is it on?"
"Could I follow you for a bit?"

Whenever you need a moment, or you missed something

"Sorry, I didn't quite catch that. Could you say it again?"
"Do you mean…?"
"Just so I've got this right, you're saying…"
"Could you go a bit slower, please?"
"Give me a second to think."
"How do you say that in English? I mean…"

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Listen for the moment the traveller stops nodding along and actually pins the answer down.

YouExcuse me — sorry to bother you. I don't suppose you know your way around here, do you? I'm completely turned around.
LocalHa, join the club — half the visitors end up on this exact corner with that exact look on their face. Where are you trying to get to?
YouThe old cathedral, ideally. I've been following the map on my phone for twenty minutes and it just kept spinning me round in circles — and now it's died on me completely.
LocalAh, the map's hopeless in the old town — too many alleys, no signal. Right, so… you go down past the fountain, take the second — no, the third left after the little bakery, and then it's more or less straight on. Can't miss it.
You…Sorry, would you mind running that by me once more? You lost me at the fountain, to be honest.
LocalCourse. Head straight down this street until you come to a little square with a stone fountain in the middle — you'll hear it before you see it. Cross the square, and there's a bakery on the far corner. Take the third turning on your left, just after the bakery.
YouSo — let me get this straight: down to the fountain, across the square, past the bakery, third left. Is that right?
LocalThat's it exactly. Third left, mind — not the second. The second one's a dead end full of restaurant bins; people get that wrong all the time and come back cursing.
YouNoted — avoid the bins. And roughly how far is it once I'm past the bakery? Is it walkable, or am I better off hopping on a tram?
LocalOh, it's a five-minute stroll — ten if you stop for a pastry, which honestly you should. Best bakery in the city, and you're walking straight past it.
YouTempting. And once I'm through those alleys — how will I actually know the cathedral when I see it? Everything round here looks about four hundred years old.
LocalOh, you'll know it — great big thing, green copper dome, bell tower you could spot from space. If you find yourself squinting at something small, you've gone wrong somewhere.
YouGreen dome, can't miss it. Honestly, you've been a lifesaver — I'd have been wandering these streets till nightfall.
LocalDon't mention it. Oh — one last thing: if the main door's shut for a service, don't give up. There's a little side entrance by the garden that nobody uses. Enjoy it.
YouSide door by the garden. I owe you a pastry. Thanks ever so much.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The local gives the route twice. What went wrong with the first version, and what made the second one so much easier to follow?

2. Two specific warnings will save the traveller from going wrong — one about a turning, one about a door. What are they?

3. The traveller never simply nods and walks off. Find the TWO things they do to make sure the directions have actually gone in.

1. The first version was rushed and vague — the local even corrected themselves mid-sentence ("the second — no, the third left") and gave no clear starting point. The second version was broken into ordered landmarks you can actually see or hear — fountain, then square, then bakery, then third left — so each step hands you to the next.
2. Take the third left, not the second — the second is a dead end full of restaurant bins. And if the cathedral's main door is shut for a service, don't give up: there's a little side entrance by the garden.
3. First, they ask the local to say it again — "would you mind running that by me once more?" Second, they repeat the whole route back in their own words and check it — "let me get this straight… is that right?" — so any mistake gets caught before they set off, not halfway down the wrong alley.

The Echo 🕰️

Around 1271, a teenage Venetian named Marco Polo set off east with his father and uncle — no phone, no map worth the name, just rumours, star charts and the kindness of strangers who spoke languages he'd never heard. He was lost, in the biggest possible sense, for the better part of twenty years.

He crossed deserts, mountains and cities no European had ever named, and every step depended on the same skill you just practised: stop someone, ask the way, and — the hard part — make sure you've actually understood the answer before you commit to it.

Your cathedral is five minutes away. His Cathay was three years and a couple of continents. Same instinct, different scale.

Two bigger questions

  1. Marco Polo had to trust strangers with his life, over and over, in places where he couldn't read the signs or the faces. What does it take to trust a stranger's directions — and when should you quietly not?
  2. We now have a glowing blue dot that always knows where we are. Have we gained something, or lost something, now that almost nobody has to stop and ask the way any more?
