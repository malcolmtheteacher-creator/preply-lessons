Asking for Directions

New city, dead phone, and a cathedral you swear was right here a minute ago. Time to stop a stranger and find your way.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

You've been in this city for exactly one afternoon, and you've already fallen in love with it — the narrow lanes, the washing strung between the balconies, the smell of coffee round every corner. There's just one problem: you have absolutely no idea where you are.

You wanted the old cathedral. The map on your phone said it was five minutes away, then it spun in a slow circle, then it said "no signal", then the battery gave a little sigh and died. Somewhere behind these rooftops is the thing you came to see — but the streets curl and double back, and every alley looks like the last one.

Coming towards you is a local — unhurried, at home here, the kind of person who'd know these streets blindfolded. You have about three seconds to stop them nicely. And when the directions come, they won't come slowly. The real skill today isn't asking — it's making sure you actually understood the answer.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last get properly lost — no signal, no idea which way was which? How did you get yourself out of it, and did you enjoy it more or less afterwards?
  2. Some people would sooner wander for an hour than stop a stranger and admit they're lost. Which are you — and what is it about asking for help that people find so hard?

Who's who

You
The Traveller 🧭

Curious, a little jet-lagged, phone stone dead. You'll happily admit you're lost — but you're determined to leave this corner knowing exactly where to go, not just nodding and hoping.

The other voice
The Local 🏘️

Knows every lane, bakery and dead end by heart. Friendly, quick, and gives directions at the speed of someone who's given them a thousand times — landmarks flying past faster than you can catch them.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the moment the traveller stops nodding along and actually pins the answer down.

YouExcuse me — sorry to bother you. I don't suppose you know your way around here, do you? I'm completely turned around.
LocalHa, join the club — half the visitors end up on this exact corner with that exact look on their face. Where are you trying to get to?
YouThe old cathedral, ideally. I've been following the map on my phone for twenty minutes and it just kept spinning me round in circles — and now it's died on me completely.
LocalAh, the map's hopeless in the old town — too many alleys, no signal. Right, so… you go down past the fountain, take the second — no, the third left after the little bakery, and then it's more or less straight on. Can't miss it.
You…Sorry, would you mind running that by me once more? You lost me at the fountain, to be honest.
LocalCourse. Head straight down this street until you come to a little square with a stone fountain in the middle — you'll hear it before you see it. Cross the square, and there's a bakery on the far corner. Take the third turning on your left, just after the bakery.
YouSo — let me get this straight: down to the fountain, across the square, past the bakery, third left. Is that right?
LocalThat's it exactly. Third left, mind — not the second. The second one's a dead end full of restaurant bins; people get that wrong all the time and come back cursing.
YouNoted — avoid the bins. And roughly how far is it once I'm past the bakery? Is it walkable, or am I better off hopping on a tram?
LocalOh, it's a five-minute stroll — ten if you stop for a pastry, which honestly you should. Best bakery in the city, and you're walking straight past it.
YouTempting. And once I'm through those alleys — how will I actually know the cathedral when I see it? Everything round here looks about four hundred years old.
LocalOh, you'll know it — great big thing, green copper dome, bell tower you could spot from space. If you find yourself squinting at something small, you've gone wrong somewhere.
YouGreen dome, can't miss it. Honestly, you've been a lifesaver — I'd have been wandering these streets till nightfall.
LocalDon't mention it. Oh — one last thing: if the main door's shut for a service, don't give up. There's a little side entrance by the garden that nobody uses. Enjoy it.
YouSide door by the garden. I owe you a pastry. Thanks ever so much.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The local gives the route twice. What went wrong with the first version, and what made the second one so much easier to follow?

2. Two specific warnings will save the traveller from going wrong — one about a turning, one about a door. What are they?

3. The traveller never simply nods and walks off. Find the TWO things they do to make sure the directions have actually gone in.

1. The first version was rushed and vague — the local even corrected themselves mid-sentence ("the second — no, the third left") and gave no clear starting point. The second version was broken into ordered landmarks you can actually see or hear — fountain, then square, then bakery, then third left — so each step hands you to the next.
2. Take the third left, not the second — the second is a dead end full of restaurant bins. And if the cathedral's main door is shut for a service, don't give up: there's a little side entrance by the garden.
3. First, they ask the local to say it again — "would you mind running that by me once more?" Second, they repeat the whole route back in their own words and check it — "let me get this straight… is that right?" — so any mistake gets caught before they set off, not halfway down the wrong alley.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the moment you're lost and have to trust a stranger's memory of the streets. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚶 Stopping a stranger

Phrase 1

You need to stop a passer-by without startling them or sounding like you're demanding a service. Which opener makes them want to help?

A. "Hey! Where's the cathedral?"
B. "Tell me how to get to the cathedral."
C. "Sorry to bother you — you couldn't point me in the right direction, could you?"
C — "Sorry to bother you — you couldn't point me in the right direction, could you?"
"Sorry to bother you" buys goodwill before you spend it, and "point me in the right direction" is a warm, low-pressure way to ask — a soft metaphor, not a blunt demand. The little "…could you?" tag makes it a request, not an order.
Phrase 2

Before you even ask for the place, you can gently check whether this stranger is a local at all. There's a two-word tag that turns your hopeful guess into a polite question: "I don't suppose you know your way around here, ___ ___?"

"…do you?" — "I don't suppose you know your way around here, do you?"
"I don't suppose…" politely expects a "no", which makes it easy to give one — and the tag "do you?" softens the whole thing into a question rather than an assumption. Perfect for a stranger who might just be another lost tourist.

🗺️ Asking clearly

Phrase 3

Instead of barking "Where's the market square?", you can name your goal AND check you're even close, all in one breath. Put the pieces in order:

the old market square —anywhere near it?I'm trying to findam I
"I'm trying to find the old market square — am I anywhere near it?"
"I'm trying to find…" is softer than "Where is…?", and "am I anywhere near it?" instantly tells the local whether they need to send you round the corner or across the whole city. It also lets them reassure you — "Oh, you're practically on top of it."
Phrase 4

You want the nearest metro, but you don't need pinpoint precision — just a rough idea and a sense of the distance. There's one word that means "approximately where" and sounds friendlier than a blunt "where", and another that quietly signals "a ballpark is fine". What would you ask?

"Whereabouts is the nearest metro, roughly?"
"Whereabouts" is a gentler, more conversational "where"; "roughly" tells the person you're not expecting a survey map, just a rough steer. Together they lower the stakes of answering — nobody's afraid of being slightly wrong.

🌀 When the answer's a blur

Phrase 5

The directions came out in a rush and you caught about half. You need them repeated — without sounding rude or slow. Which version would you rather say to a kind stranger?

A. "What? Say that again."
B. "Sorry, would you mind running that by me once more?"
B — "Sorry, would you mind running that by me once more?"
A sounds abrupt, even irritated. B takes the blame gently onto yourself. "Run that by me" is a lovely idiom for "repeat that so I can take it in", and "once more" implies you were listening the first time — you just need a second pass.
Phrase 6

It's still coming too fast — three turnings in one sentence and you're already lost. Rather than ask for the whole thing again, you can ask them to slow the pace. What might you say to get it delivered gently, step by step?

"Sorry — could you take me through it a bit more slowly?"
"Take me through it" asks for a guided, step-by-step walk-through rather than one breathless sentence, and "a bit more slowly" is a soft, face-saving nudge — you're not calling them a fast talker, just asking for a gentler pace.

✅ Checking you've really got it

Phrase 7

This is the move that saves you from the wrong alley: you say the whole route back in your own words and ask them to confirm it. Build the opener:

let meso —get this straight:left at the lights, then second right?
"So — let me get this straight: left at the lights, then second right?"
"Let me get this straight" signals you're about to repeat it back to check — the single most useful habit when someone's giving you directions. Say the route in your own words and let them catch any mistake before you walk off.
Phrase 8

You think you've spotted the building they mean, but you want to be sure before you commit. Tag a three-word check onto the end to make them confirm: "It's the one with the clock tower, ___ ___ ___?"

"…is that right?" — "It's the one with the clock tower, is that right?"
A tiny confirmation tag that turns a guess into a question and invites a clear yes or no. Cousins that do the same job: "…if I've got that right?" and "…have I understood you?"

🧭 Coping — and a proper thank-you

Phrase 9

You've got the route, but you're not sure it's worth walking. Ask, in one breath, whether to go on foot or give up and take a cab — sounding easy-going about either. What would you ask?

"Is it walkable, or am I better off jumping in a cab?"
"Walkable" is a neat one-word way to ask about distance, and "am I better off + -ing" asks for their honest recommendation between two options. "Jumping in a cab" is relaxed, everyday English — no drama, just weighing it up.
Phrase 10

The stranger has stopped, listened, repeated themselves patiently and handed you a shortcut you'd never have found. Which thank-you leaves them glad they stopped?

A. "Thanks."
B. "I am most grateful for your assistance."
C. "Honestly, you've been a lifesaver — thanks ever so much."
C — "Honestly, you've been a lifesaver — thanks ever so much."
A is bare; B sounds like a formal letter and kills the warmth. "You've been a lifesaver" is warm hyperbole that praises the person, and "ever so much" turns up the thanks without turning up the formality.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole encounter out loud using all ten, in order — stop the stranger → ask clearly → slow them down when it blurs → repeat it back to check → weigh up walking → thank them warmly. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

Your Turn 🎭

Now you're the one on the corner. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer — a real lost traveller doesn't get all day.

Round 1 — you are the traveller 🧭

The local speaks — you answer. No looking back at the phrases; trust your ear. Remember: soften first, and don't walk off until you've checked you understood.

Round 2 — now YOU are the local 🏘️

Swap chairs. It's your city now — your streets, your landmarks, your shortcuts. Invent the route. Give directions that someone lost could actually follow, and be patient when they lose you.

TravellerSorry, would you mind running that by me once more? I lost you just after the traffic lights.

The Twist

🌫️ The confident stranger

One more round — no script, no safety net. You stop someone and they answer instantly, brimming with confidence: "The museum? Easy — left, over the bridge, ten minutes." But something doesn't add up. The bridge is clearly behind you, and you're fairly sure the museum is the other way entirely. They're kind, and completely sure of themselves. Start the timer and talk it through: gently test their answer against what you can actually see, ask a second question to check ("is that past the river, or before it?"), and decide whether to trust them or thank them and quietly ask someone else. One rule: never make them feel stupid — and never walk off following directions you don't believe.

The Echo 🕰️

Around 1271, a teenage Venetian named Marco Polo set off east with his father and uncle — no phone, no map worth the name, just rumours, star charts and the kindness of strangers who spoke languages he'd never heard. He was lost, in the biggest possible sense, for the better part of twenty years.

He crossed deserts, mountains and cities no European had ever named, and every step depended on the same skill you just practised: stop someone, ask the way, and — the hard part — make sure you've actually understood the answer before you commit to it.

Your cathedral is five minutes away. His Cathay was three years and a couple of continents. Same instinct, different scale.
