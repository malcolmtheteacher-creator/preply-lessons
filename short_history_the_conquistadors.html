The Conquistadors

How a few hundred Spaniards toppled two of the greatest empires the Americas had ever known.

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. He stepped off the ship in a steel helmet and breastplate, a sword on his hip, dreaming of gold and glory; he was a conquistador, and he had crossed an ocean to take a new land for his king.
a conquistador (n) — a Spanish (or Portuguese) soldier-adventurer who conquered territory in the Americas in the 16th century.
2. The general spoke of the conquest with pride: a whole region taken by force in a single campaign, its rulers replaced and its land now flying his flag.
a conquest (n) — the act of taking control of a country or people by force, especially in war.
3. At its height the empire stretched across thousands of miles, dozens of peoples and cities all answering to a single ruler in the capital.
an empire (n) — a large group of states, peoples or lands ruled over by one supreme authority.
4. When the city fell, the soldiers spread through the temples and palaces to plunder them — carrying off gold, silver and anything of value they could lift.
to plunder (v) — to steal valuable things from a place by force, especially in war. (Also a noun: the stolen goods themselves.)
5. Before the soldiers ever attacked, an invisible enemy went ahead of them: smallpox swept from village to village, covering the sick in blisters and killing them in their thousands.
smallpox (n) — a deadly, highly contagious disease that covers the body in blisters; it killed enormous numbers of people before it was wiped out in the 20th century.
6. Outnumbered and far from home, the newcomers searched for local peoples who hated the ruling power, and offered them an alliance: fight beside us, and together we will bring it down.
an alliance (n) — an agreement between two sides to support or cooperate with each other, often against a common enemy.
7. The plotters' aim was simple but enormous: to overthrow the king — to pull him from his throne and seize his power for themselves.
to overthrow (v) — to remove a ruler or government from power, usually by force.
8. The defenders had never seen anything like it: men charging on huge animals, faster than any runner, smashing through their lines. Against such cavalry their foot soldiers had no answer.
cavalry (n) — soldiers who fight on horseback.
9. Neither side spoke the other's language, so everything passed through one woman, the interpreter, who listened in one tongue and repeated it in another.
an interpreter (n) — a person who translates speech from one language into another as people talk.
10. He showed no mercy to those who surrendered, broke every promise he made, and ordered the killings without a flicker of guilt; even his own men called him ruthless.
ruthless (adj) — willing to do cruel or harsh things to get what you want, without pity or mercy.

The story, in four parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A handful of men, a continent of empires

In the early 1500s, Spain had a foothold in the Caribbean and an appetite for more. Out from its islands sailed the conquistadors — adventurers, soldiers and minor noblemen, hungry for gold, land and a name. Few of them were sent by armies; most paid their own way, gambling their lives on the chance of sudden riches. Ahead of them, though they did not yet know it, lay two of the largest and most sophisticated empires on Earth.

To the north, in what is now Mexico, stood the Aztec empire, ruled from Tenochtitlan — a vast city built on a lake, with causeways, canals, markets and temples that astonished the first Europeans who saw it. Far to the south, along the spine of the Andes, the Inca empire ran for more than two thousand miles, linked by stone roads and governed with remarkable order from its capital, Cusco. Each empire ruled millions. Each could field armies the conquistadors could never hope to match in numbers.

And yet the Spanish carried advantages no local army had faced before: steel swords and armour, gunpowder weapons, war dogs, and horses — animals unknown in the Americas. They also carried something more terrible still, without even knowing it: the diseases of the Old World, against which the peoples of the Americas had no defence at all.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who were the conquistadors, and what were most of them hoping to gain?

2. Name the two empires and their capital cities.

1. Spanish soldier-adventurers — mostly self-funded — chasing gold, land and fame in the Americas.

2. The Aztec empire (capital Tenochtitlan) and the Inca empire (capital Cusco).

Discuss:
  • The Spanish were nowhere near as numerous as the peoples they met. What advantages can let a small group beat a much larger one — in war, in business, anywhere?

Part 2 — Cortés and the fall of the Aztecs

In 1519 Hernán Cortés landed on the Mexican coast with around 500 men, sixteen horses and a few cannon. Rather than retreat, he reportedly destroyed his own ships so that no one could turn back. As he marched inland he learned the secret that would decide everything: the Aztecs were not loved by everyone they ruled. Many peoples resented them, and the city-state of Tlaxcala, after first fighting the Spanish, became their most important ally. In the end tens of thousands of indigenous warriors would march with Cortés. He also gained an interpreter, an enslaved woman the Spanish called Doña Marina (Malintzin), whose skill made negotiation — and manipulation — possible.

Cortés entered Tenochtitlan in November 1519 and was received by the emperor Moctezuma, then seized him as a hostage. For months the Spanish held the city in an uneasy grip, until the Aztecs rose against them. On the night of 30 June 1520 — remembered as La Noche Triste, "the Night of Sorrows" — the Spanish were driven out, losing perhaps half their men. It looked like the end of the conquest. It was not.

While Cortés regrouped, an invisible ally went to work. Smallpox, brought unknowingly by the Spanish, broke out in Tenochtitlan and tore through a population with no immunity. It killed vast numbers, including the new emperor, Cuitláhuac, and shattered the city's ability to fight. In 1521 Cortés returned with his indigenous allies and besieged Tenochtitlan for some ninety days, cutting off food and water. On 13 August 1521 the city fell, and with it the Aztec empire.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Cortés turn the Aztecs' own subjects to his advantage?

2. What two blows broke Tenochtitlan after La Noche Triste?

1. He allied with peoples who resented Aztec rule — above all Tlaxcala — gaining tens of thousands of warriors, plus the interpreter Malintzin.

2. A smallpox epidemic that devastated the city (killing the emperor Cuitláhuac), followed by a roughly 90-day siege that starved it into surrender in August 1521.

Discuss:
  • Disease did at least as much as steel to bring down Tenochtitlan. Does that change how we should think about the "victory"? Why, or why not?

Part 3 — Pizarro and the fall of the Incas

News of Mexico's gold sent others looking for empires of their own. Francisco Pizarro, an older and harder man who had heard rumours of a rich kingdom in the south, sailed down the Pacific coast. By the time he marched inland in 1532 with fewer than 200 men, smallpox had already raced ahead of any Spaniard, killing the Inca emperor and many of his family. The empire had then torn itself apart in a civil war between two rival brothers, Atahualpa and Huáscar. Atahualpa had just won — and walked, victorious and unsuspecting, straight into Pizarro's path.

At Cajamarca in November 1532, Atahualpa came to meet the strangers almost unarmed, with thousands of attendants but no expectation of a fight. Pizarro sprang an ambush. Spanish guns, steel and cavalry caused panic and slaughter in the square, and Atahualpa was taken prisoner. To win his freedom, the emperor offered a staggering ransom: a room filled once with gold and twice with silver. The Spanish accepted, melted the treasure down — and then, having taken it all, accused him of crimes and executed him in 1533.

With the emperor dead and the empire leaderless, Pizarro marched on Cusco and took it later that year. Resistance went on for decades in the mountains, but the heart of the Inca empire had been seized by a few hundred men. Pizarro's own end came in his own coin: in 1541 he was assassinated in Lima by fellow Spaniards in a bitter quarrel over the spoils.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What weakened the Inca empire even before Pizarro reached it?

2. What happened to Atahualpa after he paid his ransom?

1. Smallpox had killed the emperor and many others, and a civil war between the rival brothers Atahualpa and Huáscar had split the empire.

2. Despite handing over a room of gold and silver, he was accused of crimes and executed by the Spanish in 1533.

Discuss:
  • Pizarro took the ransom and then killed the man who paid it. What does that one act tell you about how the conquest was carried out?

Part 4 — What the conquest left behind

For Spain, the rewards were almost beyond counting. Ships carried home gold and silver in quantities that reshaped the European economy and made Spain, for a time, the most powerful nation in the world. A new empire — "New Spain" — spread across Mexico, Central America and much of South America, bringing the Spanish language and the Catholic faith that still mark the region today.

For the peoples of the Americas, the same century was a catastrophe with few equals in human history. The wars were brutal, but the diseases were worse: smallpox, measles, influenza and others swept the continent again and again, against bodies that had never met them. Across the Americas, indigenous populations are estimated to have collapsed by anywhere from a half to as much as ninety per cent in the generations after contact. Whole peoples were plundered, enslaved and forced to labour in mines and fields. Empires that had built lake-cities and mountain roads were broken in a few short years.

It is tempting to tell this as a tale of a few daring men and their swords. The truth is harder and stranger. The conquistadors were ruthless and bold, but they did not win on courage alone. They won because they found enemies of the empires willing to fight beside them, because steel and horses gave them a battlefield edge — and, above all, because an invisible plague went ahead of them, emptying the land before they ever raised a sword.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the conquest bring Spain and Europe?

2. According to the story, what were the three real reasons the conquistadors won?

1. Enormous wealth in gold and silver, a huge new empire, and the lasting spread of the Spanish language and Catholic faith across the region.

2. Indigenous allies who hated the ruling empires; a military edge in steel, guns and horses; and — above all — disease, especially smallpox, which devastated the population in advance.

Discuss:
  • The story warns against the "daring men and their swords" version of events. Why do you think that simpler version has been so popular for so long?

Comparison: every bit as / nowhere near as

This whole story turns on uneven comparisons — a few hundred against many thousands, steel against stone, one weapon far deadlier than all the rest. English has a precise set of phrases for saying how equal or unequal two things are. Get them right and your comparison lands exactly where you want it.

Saying things are equal — or close to it:
• every bit as … as = fully, completely as much (emphatic "just as"): "The Spanish were every bit as ruthless as the empires they faced."
• no less … than = equally (often a touch formal): "Disease was no less deadly than the sword."
• the same … as = identical in that quality: "Atahualpa met the strangers with the same confidence as a man expecting tribute, not a trap."
Saying things are NOT equal — and by how much:
• nowhere near as … as = far less than (a big gap): "Cortés had nowhere near as many men as the Aztecs."
• not nearly as … as = also far less than (same idea): "The conquistadors were not nearly as numerous as their allies."
Note: "nowhere near as" and "not nearly as" mean the same — a large gap, not a small one.
Emphasisers — push the gap wider:
Add far or by far to make a comparison stronger.
• "Smallpox was far more destructive than the fighting."
• "Disease was by far the deadliest weapon of the conquest." (with a superlative — "by far the most/best/worst…")

1. Equal or unequal? Choose the phrase.

Complete each gap with one of: every bit as, no less, nowhere near as, not nearly as, the same … as.

1. The conquistadors were numerous as the armies they faced — yet just as bold.

2. Pizarro proved ruthless as Cortés before him.

3. To the empires, the invisible plague was dangerous than any sword.

4. Atahualpa walked in with confidence a man who feared nothing.

1. nowhere near as (or: not nearly as — a big gap in numbers)

2. every bit as (fully equal, emphatic)

3. no less (equally dangerous — "no less dangerous than")

4. the same … as ("the same confidence as")
