Just War Theory (7 Criteria)

🔒 Tap to reveal

Just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, last resort, proportionality, discrimination, probability of success. Framework for assessing moral permissibility of war.

Lesser Jihad vs. Greater Jihad

🔒 Tap to reveal

Greater Jihad: Internal spiritual struggle against evil. Lesser Jihad: External military struggle, only in self-defence with strict rules. Greater Jihad is primary.

Discrimination (in warfare)

🔒 Tap to reveal

Duty to distinguish combatants from civilians. WMDs violate discrimination because they kill indiscriminately.

The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37)

Parable showing that neighbourly love (and duty to help refugees) transcends ethnic and religious boundaries.

Surah 9:6 (Refugee Rights)

"If any one of the polytheists asks you for protection, grant it to him." Shows Islamic duty to provide asylum regardless of religion.

Ten Commandments (Exodus 20)

🔒 Tap to reveal

Foundation of Christian ethics. Covers worship, respect, life, truth. Crime violates God's law; all crime is sin (but not all sin is crime).

Shari'ah Law Framework

🔒 Tap to reveal

Hudud: Fixed crimes/punishments. Qisas: Retaliatory justice (eye for eye). Ta'zir: Discretionary punishment. Diyah: Compensation.

Qisas and Diyah

🔒 Tap to reveal

Qisas: Retaliatory punishment (eye for eye). Diyah: Compensation (blood money). Qur'an prefers diyah and forgiveness over qisas.

Four Purposes of Punishment

🔒 Tap to reveal

Retribution (punish equally), deterrence (warn others), reform (rehabilitate), protection (incapacitate/isolate). Modern justice blends all four.

Taubah (Islamic Repentance)

🔒 Tap to reveal

Sincere repentance for sin. Islamic theology: taubah is always possible and forgiven by Allah. Offenders deserve path to redemption.

Section B: Short Answer (3 marks each)

6. Explain how Christian teaching on forgiveness (e.g., Lord's Prayer) differs from a purely retributive approach to crime.

Model answer: The Lord's Prayer teaches "Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors." This prioritizes mercy and transformation over punishment equal to the crime. Retributive justice focuses on ensuring the offender suffers as much as their victim; Christian theology believes offenders can be redeemed through forgiveness and should be given opportunity for reform rather than permanent punishment.

7. Why do many Islamic scholars argue that capital punishment should be avoided, even though Qisas permits it?

Model answer: Surah 42:40 explicitly teaches that forgiveness is superior to retaliation. Victims' families can choose diyah (compensation) instead of qisas (execution). Taubah (sincere repentance) is central to Islamic theology—an offender who repents deserves mercy. Many scholars argue that modern prisons can achieve protection and justice without executions, making capital punishment unnecessary and incompatible with Islamic values of mercy.

Section C: Extended Response (10 marks)

8. Evaluate the extent to which Christian and Islamic teachings support pacifism.

Guidance:

  • Discuss Christian teaching: Jesus taught forgiveness and non-violence (Matthew 5:9, Luke 10:27); Quakers practice absolute pacifism
  • BUT: Just War Theory developed by Thomas Aquinas permits some wars under strict conditions
  • Discuss Islamic teaching: Surah 8:61 teaches peace should be pursued; Greater Jihad is primary
  • BUT: Lesser Jihad permits defensive military struggle under conditions
  • Conclusion: Both traditions value peace but allow defensive warfare; neither mandates absolute pacifism (though some groups within each do)

Extended Writing: Exemplary Response

Question: With reference to Christian and Islamic teachings, evaluate the extent to which religious perspectives support or oppose the use of military force.

Model Response (A-grade standard)

Both Christianity and Islam contain resources for both supporting and opposing military force, reflecting complexity within each tradition.

Supporting military force: Christian Just War Theory (Aquinas) permits war if it meets seven criteria: just cause, legitimate authority, right intention, last resort, proportionality, discrimination, probability of success. This framework allows self-defence and protection of innocent people. Similarly, Islamic law permits Lesser Jihad (military struggle) in defensive circumstances, when authorised by legitimate leadership, and subject to strict rules protecting non-combatants and requiring mercy.

Opposing military force: Christian tradition includes absolute pacifism (Quakers, Mennonites). Jesus taught forgiveness ("forgive seventy times seven") and non-violence ("put your sword back"). Modern Christianity increasingly opposes military force, especially WMDs, which violate Just War criteria on discrimination and proportionality. Islamic tradition emphasises Greater Jihad (spiritual struggle) as primary and requires all peaceful alternatives be exhausted before Lesser Jihad. Surah 8:61 mandates accepting peace offers.

Conclusion: Both traditions permit defensive military force under strict conditions, but prioritise peace and forgiveness. Modern contexts make it harder to meet these criteria; consequently, many religious leaders (Pope Francis, Muslim scholars) increasingly advocate pacifism or extreme caution in deploying force.

Why this is A-grade:

  • Shows nuance: acknowledges complexity within each tradition
  • Cites specific sources (Jesus, Aquinas, Qur'an verses)
  • Explains both sides fairly before evaluating
  • Makes a clear, supported judgement
  • Connects ancient teaching to modern context

Final Summary: Themes D & E

Theme D — Religion, Peace and Conflict:

  • Lesson 1: Christian peacemaking (Quakers, Just War, Matthew 5:9) and Muslim approaches (Surah 8:61, greater/lesser jihad). Forgiveness (Lord's Prayer, Surah 42:40) enables reconciliation.
  • Lesson 2: Just War criteria (7 principles) and Holy War/Lesser Jihad. WMDs violate Just War because they kill indiscriminately. Terrorism (targeting civilians) is condemned by both traditions.
  • Lesson 3: Religious duty to help refugees (Good Samaritan, Surah 9:6). Peace organisations (UN, Red Cross, Christian Aid, Islamic Relief) provide neutral humanitarian aid. Religious leaders broker peace.

Theme E — Religion, Crime and Punishment:

  • Lesson 4: Christian sin vs. crime distinction. Shari'ah framework (hudud, qisas, diyah, ta'zir). Four purposes of punishment: retribution, deterrence, reform, protection. Modern justice blends all four.
  • Lesson 5: Death penalty: Catholics/Protestants oppose it; Islam permits qisas but prefers forgiveness/diyah. Cases (Tutu, Wilson) show power of Christian forgiveness and taubah (Islamic repentance).
