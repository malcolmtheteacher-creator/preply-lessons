⚡ Boot Up — Fast Round

Quick-fire questions. No thinking — just answer. We'll check after.

1. When did the last non-avian dinosaurs go extinct?

About 66 million years ago, at the end of the Cretaceous period — caused by a massive asteroid impact (and possibly volcanic activity).

2. The woolly mammoth went extinct around 4,000 years ago. Where was the last known population?

Wrangel Island, an isolated island in the Arctic Ocean off the coast of Russia. The mainland population died out around 10,000 years ago, but this island group survived until about 1650 BCE — while the Egyptians were building pyramids.

3. What is CRISPR? (Take a guess — what do you think it means or does?)

CRISPR-Cas9 is a gene-editing tool — essentially molecular scissors that can cut DNA at precise locations and insert, remove, or rewrite genetic code. Scientists use it to edit the genomes of living organisms. It won the Nobel Prize in 2020.

4. In Jurassic Park, scientists use DNA from mosquitoes preserved in amber. Why doesn't that actually work?

DNA degrades over time. The theoretical maximum lifespan of DNA (even in perfect conditions) is about 1 million years — and 66-million-year-old dinosaur DNA would be completely unreadable. Scientists have recovered fragments of DNA from animals up to ~1 million years old (a horse frozen in permafrost), but nothing from the dinosaur era.

5. Which of these is NOT actually being targeted for de-extinction right now?

A) Woolly mammoth    B) Dodo    C) Triceratops    D) Thylacine (Tasmanian tiger)

C) Triceratops — it's 66 million years old; no usable DNA survives. The other three are all active projects by Colossal Biosciences, a US company founded in 2021. The mammoth, dodo, and thylacine all went extinct recently enough that genetic material survives.

🧬 Key Vocabulary — Click to Flip

de-extinction The process of reviving an extinct species — either as a true genetic copy or a functional equivalent
CRISPR-Cas9 A gene-editing technology that acts as molecular scissors — it cuts DNA at specific locations to insert or remove genetic code
proxy species A genetically engineered hybrid that performs the same ecological role as the extinct animal, even if not an exact copy
ecological niche The role a species plays in its ecosystem — what it eats, what eats it, and how it shapes the environment
rewilding The large-scale restoration of ecosystems by reintroducing species that were once part of them
keystone species A species whose presence has a disproportionately large effect on its ecosystem — removing or adding one reshapes everything
viable population A population large enough and genetically diverse enough to survive in the wild long-term without human intervention
Holocene extinction The ongoing mass extinction event caused by human activity — habitat destruction, overhunting, and climate change — over the past 10,000 years

📄 The Science File

In September 2021, a company called Colossal Biosciences launched with a bold promise: it would bring the woolly mammoth back from extinction within a decade. The announcement made headlines worldwide, igniting fierce debate among scientists, ethicists, and conservationists. But was it science fiction dressed up as a press release, or the opening chapter of a genuine biological revolution?

The science behind Colossal's approach relies on CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing. Because the Asian elephant and the woolly mammoth share approximately 99.6% of their DNA, scientists do not need to reconstruct the mammoth genome from scratch. Instead, they identify the key genes responsible for the mammoth's cold-weather adaptations — its dense fur, thick fat layer, and cold-tolerant blood — and splice those genes into an Asian elephant cell. The result would not be a true woolly mammoth but a proxy species: a cold-adapted elephant capable of surviving in Arctic conditions and, crucially, capable of doing the ecological job the mammoth once did.

That ecological job matters enormously. Mammoths were keystone species of the Arctic grasslands — the so-called "mammoth steppe" that once stretched from Spain to Canada. By grazing, trampling shrubs, and knocking over trees, they maintained an open grassland ecosystem. When mammoths disappeared, the grasslands were replaced by moss and scrubland, which insulates the permafrost less effectively. Scientists at the Pleistocene Park project in Siberia argue that restoring large grazers — including a mammoth proxy — could slow Arctic permafrost thaw, potentially reducing methane emissions and helping to brake global warming.

Colossal is not alone. The company is also pursuing the thylacine (Tasmanian tiger), which went extinct in 1936 when the last known individual died in Hobart Zoo, and the dodo, hunted to extinction in Mauritius by 1681. For both animals, the key advantage is recency: intact DNA can be extracted from museum specimens, and their habitats remain largely unchanged. The thylacine's genome has already been fully sequenced using a specimen preserved in alcohol for over 100 years.

However, the scientific community is divided. Critics argue that the creatures produced by CRISPR editing would be functional approximations, not true resurrected species — genetically engineered hybrids wearing the costume of something lost. Others raise the problem of viable populations: a handful of laboratory-born animals does not make a species. Reintroduction would require hundreds of individuals, decades of breeding programmes, and ecosystems prepared to receive them. And every dollar and hour spent on de-extinction, some argue, is a dollar and hour not spent on the 40,000 species currently threatened with extinction — species we still have.

Comprehension — Answer Before Revealing

1. Why don't scientists need to rebuild the complete mammoth genome?

Because the Asian elephant and the woolly mammoth share 99.6% of their DNA. Scientists only need to identify and edit the small number of genes responsible for cold-weather adaptations — they don't need to start from zero.

2. What is the ecological argument FOR bringing back the mammoth? (Give two details.)

Mammoths maintained open Arctic grasslands by grazing and trampling. When they disappeared, grasslands became moss and scrubland, which insulates permafrost less well. Restoring mammoth-like grazers could slow permafrost thaw and potentially reduce methane emissions — helping fight climate change.

3. Why are the thylacine and dodo better candidates for de-extinction than the mammoth in one key respect?

They went extinct much more recently (1936 and 1681), so intact, readable DNA can be extracted from museum specimens. Their habitats also still largely exist today, making reintroduction more realistic.

4. What does the word approximations (paragraph 5) suggest about the animals that CRISPR would produce?

It suggests the animals would be close-but-not-exact versions of the originals — functional copies rather than true resurrections. The word implies an element of imprecision; critics are saying these would be engineered approximations wearing the appearance of extinct species, not the real thing.

5. What is the "conservation distraction" argument? Explain it in your own words.

The argument is that resources — money, scientific talent, time — are limited. Every resource spent on de-extinction is a resource not spent protecting the 40,000 species currently threatened with extinction. The worry is that de-extinction is a glamorous distraction from the more urgent, less exciting work of saving species we still have.

⚔️ The Argument — Two Voices

✅ FOR: "We broke it — we should fix it"

The Holocene extinction is not a natural event. It is a human-caused catastrophe, and humanity has a moral obligation to undo the damage where it is scientifically possible. De-extinction is not hubris — it is reparation. The thylacine was deliberately hunted to extinction within living memory by a government that paid a bounty on its head. To say we should simply "accept" that loss is morally indefensible.

Furthermore, the ecological argument is compelling. Keystone species do not just occupy ecosystems — they construct them. The reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone in 1995 triggered a "trophic cascade" that reshaped rivers, restored forests, and increased biodiversity across the entire park. Imagine the scale of ecosystem restoration that a mammoth proxy could trigger across the Arctic — with the added benefit of potentially mitigating climate change in the process.

Critics call de-extinction a "distraction" from conservation. But this is a false dichotomy. Colossal Biosciences is not funded by conservation charities; it is venture capital. It would not otherwise be spent on protecting endangered orangutans. And the biotechnology developed for de-extinction — CRISPR techniques, genome sequencing, advanced breeding programmes — will directly benefit conservation of living species.

❌ AGAINST: "You can't press respawn in real life"

De-extinction fundamentally misunderstands what an extinct species is. A CRISPR-edited Asian elephant with some mammoth genes is not a woolly mammoth. It is a chimera — a laboratory construct that resembles the original the way a remake resembles a classic film. We would be creating a new organism and calling it something it isn't, then releasing it into an ecosystem that has spent 10,000 years adapting to its absence.

The Yellowstone wolf analogy is frequently misused. Wolves were absent from Yellowstone for decades, not millennia. The ecosystem had not fundamentally restructured itself. Reintroducing a mammoth proxy into an Arctic landscape that has been evolving without mammoths for 10,000 years is a categorically different intervention — one whose unintended consequences we cannot predict.

Perhaps most importantly: de-extinction sends a catastrophically wrong message about extinction. It implies that extinction is reversible — that we can destroy species and eventually press a biological undo button. This moral hazard is deeply dangerous. Conservation works because people believe extinction is final and irreversible. Undermine that belief, and we accelerate the very crisis we claim to be solving.

🔍 Language Analysis

Both writers use specific language techniques to persuade. Identify them below.

1. "You can't press respawn in real life" — what technique is this, and why is it effective for a general audience?

This is an analogy / cultural allusion — borrowing a concept from gaming ("respawn" = revival after death) to make an abstract scientific point vivid and immediate. It works for a general audience because it translates a complex idea into everyday language, and the gaming reference makes it feel current and accessible. It also subtly frames de-extinction as something trivial or childish.

2. The "FOR" argument calls the situation "morally indefensible." What rhetorical move is this?

This is moral framing / appeal to ethics (ethos). By using the phrase "morally indefensible," the writer is not just making a practical argument — they are positioning anyone who disagrees as ethically wrong. It raises the stakes from "this is a bad idea" to "this position is indefensible as a moral matter." It also pre-empts counter-arguments by making them seem immoral rather than just incorrect.

3. "This is a false dichotomy" — what does this mean, and what is the writer doing by using it?

A false dichotomy (or false dilemma) is when someone presents only two options as if no others exist. The "FOR" writer is pointing out that the AGAINST argument creates a fake either/or choice ("either de-extinction OR conservation funding"), when in reality the money comes from completely different sources. By naming the fallacy, the writer dismantles the AGAINST argument structurally, not just factually.

4. The AGAINST text uses the word "chimera." What does this word mean, and what connotations does it carry?

In mythology, a chimera is a fire-breathing monster made of parts from different animals. In science, it means an organism with cells from two genetically distinct sources. The AGAINST writer is using the word deliberately for both meanings — technically accurate (a hybrid organism) but with mythological connotations of something unnatural, monstrous, and wrong. The word choice does emotional work that a neutral term like "hybrid" would not.

📝 Writing Focus — Sentence Structures for Argument

C1 writers vary their sentence structures. Try using at least two of these in your pitch:

Inversion for emphasis:

"Not only does the thylacine's genome survive intact, but its original habitat remains largely unchanged."
(Standard order: "The thylacine's genome not only survives intact, but...") — Inversion makes it more emphatic and formal.

Fronted adverbials:

"For the first time in human history, we have the technology to repair what we destroyed."
Moving the time phrase to the front gives it weight.

Cleft sentences:

"It is not the science that is lacking — it is the political will."
(Standard: "The political will is lacking, not the science.") — Much more pointed.

Rhetorical questions into assertions:

"Can we afford not to act? The answer — given what we now know — is no."
