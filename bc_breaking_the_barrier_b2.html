Breaking the Barrier

Some limits are real. Many are only in our heads — until one person proves the whole world wrong.

When “impossible” turns out to be wrong

History is full of things that “could never be done” — until someone did them, and then everyone else followed. Let us start with you.

Let's talk:
  • Can you think of something people once said was impossible, but which is completely normal today?
  • Have you ever surprised yourself by doing something you thought you couldn't? What was it?
  • When someone says “that's impossible”, are they usually talking about the world — or about themselves?
  • Do you think most of our limits are real, or are they in the mind?

Two kinds of wall

Some walls are made of stone: they are real, and you cannot walk through them. Other walls only look solid. People stand in front of them for years, certain they cannot pass — and then one day, someone simply walks through, and everyone realises the wall was never really there.

Over to you:
  • How do you tell the difference between a “stone wall” and a wall that is only in the mind?
  • Is it useful to believe something is possible, even before there is any proof?

The wall that everyone could see

Here is a true story about a barrier that turned out to be imaginary. Read it, then we will talk about it.

For many years, people believed that no human being could ever run a mile in under four minutes. It was not just a difficult goal — many experts and scientists said it was physically impossible. Some even warned that a person who tried might do serious harm to their body. The world record stayed just above four minutes for years. The barrier seemed unbreakable.

On 6 May 1954, a young man named Roger Bannister decided to try. He was not a full-time athlete — he was a busy university student who trained in his spare time, often during his lunch break. On a cold, windy day in Oxford, with two friends helping to set the pace, he ran the mile in 3 minutes and 59.4 seconds. He crossed the line and collapsed, completely exhausted. But he had done the “impossible”.

Then something strange happened. Just forty-six days later, another runner also broke four minutes. Within a few years, dozens of athletes had done the same thing. Today, even many talented school runners can do it.

Nothing about the human body had changed in those few years. What had changed was belief. For a long time, the four-minute mile had been a wall inside people's minds. The moment Bannister showed it could be done, everyone else began to believe it too — and the wall fell.

The barrier was never in the legs. It was in the mind — and one man's example was enough to break it for everybody.

Let's talk about it:
  • Why do you think so many people were sure it was impossible? Were they wrong about the body, or about belief?
  • After Bannister, the record fell again and again, very quickly. What does that tell us?
  • Is there a “four-minute mile” in your own life — something you would love to do but quietly believe you cannot?
  • How much does seeing other people succeed change what we think is possible for ourselves?

a barrier
noun

"For her, the biggest barrier was not skill, but fear."
to persevere / perseverance
verb / noun
To keep trying hard, even when something is very difficult.
"Success often comes down to quiet perseverance, not talent."
a setback
noun
A problem that stops or slows down your progress.
"He saw every setback as a lesson, not a defeat."
determined / determination
adjective / noun
Wanting to do something so much that you will not let anything stop you.
"She was determined to finish, whatever it took."
to give up
phrasal verb
To stop trying to do something.
"Most people gave up; a few refused to."
to push yourself
phrase
To make yourself work very hard, beyond what feels comfortable.
"You only find your real limits when you push yourself."
a turning point
noun
A moment when an important change begins.
"That day was a turning point — after it, everything felt possible."
mindset
noun
The way you think about something; your general attitude.
"A small change in mindset can change what you attempt."

Quick grammar check — talking about firsts and changes

We often use the present perfect for achievements and firsts, and the past perfect for what was true before a key moment. Put the verb in the best form.

1. Before 1954, no one ____ (run) a mile in under four minutes.

2. Since that day, thousands of athletes ____ (break) the four-minute barrier.

3. People ____ (believe) it was impossible — until Bannister proved them wrong.

4. It is one of the most famous races that ____ (ever / take place).

1. had run (past perfect — before that point in the past)

2. have broken (present perfect — from then until now)

3. had believed (past perfect — true before the key moment)

4. has ever taken place (present perfect with “ever”)

A · Something you thought you couldn't do (5 minutes)

Tell me about a time you did something you — or other people — thought you couldn't.

Tell it like a story: what the challenge was, why it felt impossible, what made you keep going, and how it ended. Use phrases like “at first I didn't think I could, but…” and “the turning point was when…”.

B · The power of one example (4 minutes)

Bannister proved that a barrier was only in the mind, and suddenly others believed too.

Talk about a person who showed you that something was possible — by doing it themselves. It could be someone famous, or someone you know. What changed in you after you saw them do it?

C · If you knew you couldn't fail (4 minutes)

Imagine that success was guaranteed — you simply could not fail.

What would you try to do? Explain why it matters to you, and why you think people don't attempt it now. Is the real barrier money, time, skill — or just belief?
