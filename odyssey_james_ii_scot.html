The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. He had a red birthmark on his face.
a birthmark (n) — a coloured mark on the skin from birth.
2. The "Black Dinner" was a deadly banquet.
a banquet (n) — a big formal meal.
3. The young earl was beheaded.
to behead (v) — to cut off the head.
4. Douglas came under a safe-conduct.
a safe-conduct (n) — a promise of safe travel.
5. James loved artillery.
artillery (n) — large guns, cannons.
6. He died at a siege.
a siege (n) — surrounding a castle to capture it.
7. A cannon exploded.
to explode (v) — to burst violently.
8. The Douglases were overmighty.
overmighty (adj) — too powerful for safety.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The boy and the Black Dinner

James became king at six after his father's murder in 1437. He had a red birthmark, so he was called "Fiery Face". In 1440 the young Earl of Douglas and his brother were invited to dinner at Edinburgh Castle, then seized and beheaded. James was only ten; his guardians were responsible. Legend says a black bull's head was served as a sign of death.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why "Fiery Face"?

2. Who was responsible for the Black Dinner?

1. Because of a red birthmark.

2. His guardians — James was only ten.

Discuss:
  • Is a child king responsible for what is done in his name?

Part 2 — Stirling

Grown up, James struggled with the powerful Douglas family. In February 1452 William, Earl of Douglas, came to Stirling Castle under a safe-conduct. When he refused to break an alliance, James stabbed him, and courtiers finished him off. Parliament later declared Douglas guilty.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened at Stirling in 1452?

2. Why was it shocking?

1. James stabbed the Earl of Douglas.

2. Douglas had a promise of safe travel.

Discuss:
  • Can breaking a promise ever be justified?

Part 3 — Roxburgh

James was fascinated by cannons. In 1460 he besieged Roxburgh, held by the English. On 3 August, as a cannon was fired in his honour, it exploded. James was killed. His widow, Mary of Guelders, pressed on, and the castle was taken.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did James die?

2. What did his widow do?

1. A cannon exploded beside him at Roxburgh.

2. Continued the siege until the castle was taken.

Discuss:
  • What does his death say about new technology?

Grammar — The Passive Voice

Historians often care more about what was done than who did it — or they simply do not know who did it. That is the job of the passive.

How it works:
• Form: be + past participle: "The king was crowned at Westminster."
• Add by only when the doer matters: "The castle was built by Norman masons."
• Reporting passives hedge a legend: "He is said to have…", "It was believed that…"

1. Make it passive

Complete each sentence about James II. Use the word in brackets.

1. The earl (behead) at the Black Dinner.

2. Douglas (stab) at Stirling.

3. James (call) "Fiery Face".

1. was beheaded

2. was stabbed

3. was called

2. Choose the right passive form

1. The cannon (fire) in his honour.

2. He (kill) by the explosion.

3. The castle (take) after his death.

1. was fired

2. was killed

3. was taken

Going deeper

James II broke the "Black Douglases" and strengthened the crown, but by violence. His interest in artillery was modern; it cost him his life.

Worth noticing: The giant cannon Mons Meg, given to James II in 1457, can still be seen at Edinburgh Castle.
Learning from the past — for today: Respect new tools. James loved the newest technology and stood too close. Be excited about new tools — AI, machines, systems — but learn the safety rules first.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Was James justified at Stirling?
  • Which modern technology do we "stand too close" to?
  • Should leaders take personal risks?
