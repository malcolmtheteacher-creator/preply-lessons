The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The original study

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, the psychologist Walter Mischel and his colleagues at Stanford University tested preschool children, aged between three and five, in what became known as the marshmallow test. A child was left alone with one treat and told that, if they could wait until the researcher returned, they would receive a second. Some gave in within seconds; roughly a third held out for around fifteen minutes.

What made the study famous was the follow-up. Mischel and his team contacted many of the children years later. Those who had waited longer were reported to have higher exam scores, better coping skills and, in some analyses, a lower body mass index as adults. The headline practically wrote itself: self-control at four predicts success at forty.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the test work?

2. Why did it become famous?

1. A child was left with one treat and told they would get a second if they waited until the researcher returned.

2. Because of the follow-up: children who waited longer seemed to do better in later life.

Discuss:
  • Why do simple, memorable experiments tend to spread further than careful, complicated ones?

Part 2 — The cracks appear

There were problems from the start. The original sample was small and unrepresentative: fewer than a hundred children in the key follow-ups, all from one university nursery. A single strong correlation from a small group is exactly the kind of result that often disappears when others attempt to replicate it.

In 2018, Tyler Watts and his colleagues did just that, using a sample of around nine hundred children from far more varied backgrounds. They found that the link between waiting and later achievement was roughly half as large as before, and that it shrank further once they controlled for family background, early cognitive ability and home environment. The effect did not vanish entirely, but it was modest.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was wrong with the original sample?

2. What did the 2018 team find?

1. It was small and unrepresentative: fewer than a hundred children in the key follow-ups, all from one university nursery.

2. A link about half the size of the original that shrank further after controlling for family background and early ability.

Discuss:
  • What would you want to know about a study before you based a decision on it?

Part 3 — Waiting as a rational choice

A further twist came from a study at the University of Rochester in 2012. Before the test, children were divided into two groups. One group met an adult who kept their promises, bringing the art supplies they had said they would bring. The other group met an adult who broke them. The children who had been let down waited, on average, far less time than those who had not.

That suggests waiting is not simply a matter of willpower. If your experience tells you that promises are often broken, taking the sweet now is not a failure of self-control but a sensible bet. On this view, the test may be measuring something about a child's environment as much as their character.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was different about the two groups in the 2012 study?

2. What does this suggest?

1. One group met an adult who kept promises; the other, an adult who broke them.

2. That waiting may depend on trust and experience, not only willpower; taking the sweet may be a rational choice.

Discuss:
  • If waiting depends on trust, what does that say about how we should interpret the behaviour of children, or adults, in unstable situations?

Grammar — conditionals with unless, provided that and as long as

Beyond if, English has precise linkers that show exactly what condition a result depends on. They are essential for describing experiments, rules and promises.

Three linkers:
• unless = if … not: "The child gets two sweets unless they eat the first." (= if they don't eat it). Do not add another negative: unless they don't is wrong.
• provided (that) / providing (that) = only if: "The reward is real provided that the adult returns." It stresses the condition.
• as long as / so long as = on condition that: "Children wait as long as they trust the adult."
Feel the difference:
• if is neutral.
• unless introduces the one exception: Nothing changes unless something is different.
• provided that and as long as are slightly stronger, and put the condition first in the reader's mind. Use present tenses after all of them even for the future: "as long as she waits", not "as long as she will wait".

What does it really show?

Five claims about the marshmallow test. Say whether you accept each and what evidence you would want. Then reveal.

1. "Children who wait are more successful as adults."
Overstated. The original link was reported, but the 2018 replication found it was about half as strong and shrank further with background controls.
2. "Waiting is purely a matter of willpower."
Doubtful. The Rochester study suggests trust and experience of reliability matter, so waiting may be a rational response to circumstances.
3. "The test is useless, so we should ignore it."
Too strong. The effect shrank but did not disappear; it can still tell us something, provided we do not treat it as destiny.
4. "If we teach self-control early, we will fix inequality."
Uncertain. It assumes the trait causes the outcome; if family circumstances are the deeper cause, training one child may not change the bigger picture.
5. "A good experiment is one that is easy to explain."
A trap. Easy-to-explain studies spread widely, but being memorable is not the same as being reliable.
