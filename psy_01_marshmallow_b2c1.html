The marshmallow test: true or false?

In the late 1960s, a psychologist left preschool children alone in a room with one marshmallow. Here are six claims about the test. Decide which are true and which are false.

1. Each child was told they could eat one marshmallow now, or have two if they could wait until the researcher came back.
True. That was the whole design.
2. The children were left alone for about an hour.
False. The wait was about fifteen minutes, which is already a very long time for a four-year-old.
3. Some children covered their eyes, sang, or turned away to resist the temptation.
True. The way they distracted themselves is one of the things researchers recorded.
4. A large replication has since shown that the link is just as strong as the original study claimed.
False. A big replication found the link was about half as strong, and it shrank further once family background was taken into account.
5. Most of the children managed to wait the full time.
False. Only a minority managed it; many gave in within the first few minutes.
6. Researchers followed up the children later and reported that those who had waited tended to do better on school tests.
True, and this follow-up is why the study became so famous.
Talk about it:
  • Why might the original result have been so popular with newspapers and schools?
  • Name a reason a child might not wait that has nothing to do with willpower.

The evidence, in three steps

Part 1 — The original study

Before you read: Before you read: what would you expect the original study to claim about waiting, and what would you want to know about the children it studied?

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, the psychologist Walter Mischel and his colleagues at Stanford University tested preschool children, aged between three and five, in what became known as the marshmallow test. A child was left alone with one treat and told that, if they could wait until the researcher returned, they would receive a second. Some gave in within seconds; roughly a third held out for around fifteen minutes.

What made the study famous was the follow-up. Mischel and his team contacted many of the children years later. Those who had waited longer were reported to have higher exam scores, better coping skills and, in some analyses, a lower body mass index as adults. The headline practically wrote itself: self-control at four predicts success at forty.

Economists call it delayed gratification: choosing a larger reward later over a smaller one now.

Part 2 — The cracks appear

Before you read: A single strong link between two things is found. Name two reasons why it might be misleading.

There were problems from the start. The original sample was small and unrepresentative: fewer than a hundred children in the key follow-ups, all from one university nursery. A single strong correlation from a small group is exactly the kind of result that often disappears when others attempt to replicate it.

In 2018, Tyler Watts and his colleagues did just that, using a sample of around nine hundred children from far more varied backgrounds. They found that the link between waiting and later achievement was roughly half as large as before, and that it shrank further once they controlled for family background, early cognitive ability and home environment. The effect did not vanish entirely, but it was modest.

New words in this part — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

The study was widely cited, but its sample was small and unrepresentative: almost all the children came from one university nursery.
unrepresentative (adj) — not typical of the larger group you want to draw conclusions about.
Which word means "a hidden factor that affects two things and makes them seem connected"? (c________)
confounding (adj) — a confounding variable is a third factor, e.g. family income, that muddies the conclusion.

Part 3 — Waiting as a rational choice

Before you read: Suppose the adult in the room had already broken a promise to the child. What would you expect the child to do?

A further twist came from a study at the University of Rochester in 2012. Before the test, children were divided into two groups. One group met an adult who kept their promises, bringing the art supplies they had said they would bring. The other group met an adult who broke them. The children who had been let down waited, on average, far less time than those who had not.

That suggests waiting is not simply a matter of willpower. If your experience tells you that promises are often broken, taking the sweet now is not a failure of self-control but a sensible bet. On this view, the test may be measuring something about a child's environment as much as their character.

Conditions: unless, provided that, as long as

These three give conditions for a result. They are useful for explaining when a finding holds and when it does not.

unless = if … not. You won't get a second marshmallow unless you wait.
provided (that) / providing (that) = only if. The result is worth trusting provided that it can be replicated.
as long as = on condition that. As long as the child trusts the adult, waiting is sensible.
After all of these, use a present tense for the future: unless they wait, not unless they will wait.

Take a side

Five claims people make about the test. Choose I agree, I disagree or It depends, then defend your choice in one sentence using the linker shown. Check the evidence last.

1. "Children who wait are more successful as adults."unless
Overstated. The original link was reported, but the 2018 replication found it was about half as strong and shrank further with background controls.
2. "Waiting is purely a matter of willpower."provided that
Doubtful. The Rochester study suggests trust and experience of reliability matter, so waiting may be a rational response to circumstances.
3. "The test is useless, so we should ignore it."as long as
Too strong. The effect shrank but did not disappear; it can still tell us something, provided we do not treat it as destiny.
4. "If we teach self-control early, we will fix inequality."unless
Uncertain. It assumes the trait causes the outcome; if family circumstances are the deeper cause, training one child may not change the bigger picture.
5. "A good experiment is one that is easy to explain."as long as
A trap. Easy-to-explain studies spread widely, but being memorable is not the same as being reliable.

Used to can describe habits and states (I used to live there). Would only describes repeated actions (We would walk to school), not states.
