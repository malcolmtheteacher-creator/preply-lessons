Reading & Writing Literature Reviews

B2 Level · Academic Writing

Understanding Literature Reviews

A literature review is a comprehensive summary and critical analysis of existing research on a topic. It's not just listing papers; it's synthesizing ideas and showing relationships between studies.

Why Literature Reviews Matter

  • Establish what's known about a topic
  • Identify gaps in existing research
  • Show how your research will fill those gaps
  • Demonstrate comprehensive knowledge of your field
  • Synthesize conflicting findings into coherent understanding

Lit Reviews as a Standalone Assignment

Your course may ask you to write a literature review essay. This is different from:

  • Summary: Just describing what papers say (too simple)
  • Annotated bibliography: Brief summaries of each source (not synthesized)
  • Literature review: Integrated analysis showing relationships, themes, and gaps

Structure of a Literature Review

Introduction: Define the topic, explain why it's important, state the scope of the review
Organized body: Group sources by theme, methodology, or chronology (not just one source per paragraph!)
Thematic sections: Each section explores a different aspect or debate in the literature
Gaps and limitations: Discuss what's missing or underdeveloped in current research
Conclusion: Synthesize main themes, identify research directions, lead to your research question
Synthesise To combine information from multiple sources into a coherent whole
Thematic Organized by themes or topics rather than chronologically
Debate An ongoing disagreement among researchers about something
Consensu General agreement among researchers on a point
Trajectory The path or development of ideas over time
Reconcile To resolve or harmonize conflicting ideas
Seminal Foundational; a key work influencing later research
Divergence Difference or separation in direction or findings

Language for Literature Reviews

Introducing Sources (Not Just "Smith Says...")

Showing agreement: "Multiple researchers have found..." / "There is broad consensus that..." / "Studies consistently show..."
Showing disagreement: "However, some researchers argue..." / "By contrast, Jones (2020) suggests..." / "This contrasts with earlier findings by..."
Showing development: "Early research focused on... More recent studies have examined..." / "The field has evolved from... to understanding..."

Synthesizing Information

Combining sources: "While Smith (2020) found that X, Brown (2021) and Johnson (2022) both suggest Y, indicating a shift in understanding."
Identifying patterns: "Several studies have explored this question, with mixed results. Three found support for X, while two found contradictory evidence."
Reconciling conflict: "These seemingly contradictory findings can be explained by differences in methodology. Smith used quantitative surveys, while Brown employed interviews, which may account for the divergence."

Evaluating Sources

Strength: "The study's large sample size (N=500) and rigorous design strengthen these conclusions."
Limitation: "However, the research was limited to a single geographical region, limiting generalizability."
Significance: "This seminal study established the foundation for subsequent research in the field."

Key principle: A literature review is not a list. It's an argument about what the research shows, where gaps exist, and why your research matters. Use transitions and analytical language to show relationships between sources.

Summarising and Synthesising

Summarising a Source

Goal: Capture the essence in your own words, briefly.

Example: Summarising

Original study: A quantitative study of 300 undergraduate students examining whether active recall techniques (self-testing) produce better long-term retention than passive rereading. Students were randomly assigned to one of two conditions and tested immediately and two weeks later. Results showed the active recall group scored 25% higher on the delayed test.

Summary: Smith (2021) found that active recall techniques significantly improved long-term retention compared to passive reading in undergraduate students.

Synthesising Multiple Sources

Goal: Show how different sources relate to each other and to a larger theme.

Example: Synthesising

Three related studies:

  • Smith (2021): Active recall improves retention
  • Brown (2020): Spacing out study sessions improves retention
  • Jones (2022): Students don't naturally use these techniques despite benefits

Synthesis: Research has established that specific study techniques—particularly active recall and spacing—significantly enhance long-term retention (Smith 2021; Brown 2020). However, a persistent gap exists between evidence and practice: students rarely adopt these techniques despite their documented benefits (Jones 2022), suggesting that knowledge alone doesn't change behavior.

Identifying Themes and Gaps

As you read, look for:

  • Common findings: What do multiple studies agree on?
  • Methodological approaches: Do studies use similar or different methods?
  • Conflicting results: Where do studies disagree? Why?
  • Gaps: What questions remain unanswered? What populations haven't been studied?
  • Evolution: How has thinking changed over time?

Writing Strategy

  1. Read and take notes on each source
  2. Identify 3-5 major themes in the literature
  3. Create a theme-based outline (not source-based)
  4. Write by theme, integrating multiple sources within each theme
  5. Show how sources relate to each other
  6. Conclude with gaps and how future research could address them

Writing Your Literature Review

Before Writing

  • Read at least 10-15 peer-reviewed sources (more for longer reviews)
  • Take notes on each: title, authors, year, main findings, methodology, relevance
  • Identify major themes across sources
  • Create a thematic outline

During Writing

Paragraph structure: Each paragraph should focus on one theme/idea, integrating multiple sources.

Bad paragraph: Smith (2021) found that active recall improves retention. Brown (2020) found that spacing improves retention. Jones (2022) found that students don"t use these techniques.

Good paragraph: Research has established multiple evidence-based study techniques. Active recall—testing oneself on material—significantly enhances long-term retention (Smith 2021), as does spacing study sessions across time rather than massing them (Brown 2020). However, a notable gap exists between evidence and practice: despite documented benefits, students rarely employ these techniques (Jones 2022), suggesting that awareness alone doesn"t change study behavior.

Common Mistakes

  • Summary mode: Describing each source separately instead of synthesizing
  • Too much detail: Including specifics that don't support your main themes
  • Missing analysis: Presenting sources without evaluating or comparing them
  • Unclear structure: Sources don't clearly connect to any organizing principle

Revision Checklist

☐ Introduction clearly states topic and scope

☐ Body organized by themes, not sources

☐ Multiple sources integrated within paragraphs

☐ Sources compared and evaluated, not just described

☐ Gaps and limitations in literature clearly identified

☐ Conclusion synthesizes main themes and points forward

☐ All sources cited correctly
