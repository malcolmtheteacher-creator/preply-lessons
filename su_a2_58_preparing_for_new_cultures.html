Preparing for New Intercultural Situations

A2 Level English • Intercultural Communication Skills

What you'll learn

Learn to predict cultural preferences and prepare for intercultural interactions. Understand the difference between generalisations and stereotypes, analyse context, and develop strategies to communicate effectively across cultures.

Let's Get Started

Understand the Difference

Look at these two statements about British people. Which one is a generalisation and which one is a stereotype? What's the difference between them?

Statement A:

Some British men tend to express their feelings indirectly.

Statement B:

All British men are cold and unemotional.

Discussion Questions

Think about these questions:

1. What words make Statement A more flexible than Statement B?

"Some" and "tend to" make it a generalisation. "All" and "are" in Statement B make it a rigid stereotype.

2. When can generalisations be useful in preparing for an intercultural meeting?

Generalisations help you anticipate expected norms of behaviour and prepare appropriately. They're flexible and open to new information.

3. Why can generalisations be dangerous?

They can lead to stereotypes if you apply them too rigidly or don't stay open to individual differences.

Generalisations and Stereotypes

Click on each card to reveal what makes generalisations different from stereotypes. Understanding this distinction is key to preparing for intercultural interactions respectfully.

What are they?

Generalisations are:
  • Statements about a group based on observations
  • Flexible and open to new information
  • Useful in new cultural situations — they help you anticipate expected norms of behaviour
  • Can lead to stereotypes if not careful
Stereotypes are:
  • Statements based on assumptions, not observations
  • Resistant to new information — applied rigidly to all group members
  • Often inaccurate and can lead to prejudice
  • Prevent genuine connection between people

Avoiding Stereotypes

Learn practical strategies to avoid rigid stereotyping and keep your thinking flexible when preparing for intercultural interactions.

Strategy 1: Adapt Your Vocabulary

The words you choose make your statement a stereotype or a generalisation. Compare:

Rigid Stereotype:

IT staff are always introverted!

Flexible Generalisation:
  • Some IT staff may be introverted.
  • In my experience, IT staff can be introverted.
  • IT staff tend to be introverted.

Strategy 2: Question Your Generalisations

Before you apply a generalisation, ask yourself these questions:

REFLECTION QUESTION
Is this based on facts I've observed or on assumptions?
Make sure you have real evidence, not just assumptions
REFLECTION QUESTION
Have I met individuals who don't fit this pattern?
Individual differences are always more important than group patterns
REFLECTION QUESTION
Am I applying this to everyone in the group, or just some?
Generalisations only work if they apply to many — not all

Strategy 3: View Generalisations in Wider Context

Always consider what other factors might explain a pattern you've noticed:

Example:

If "people from Culture X tend to avoid eye contact," ask yourself:

  • Is this cultural or personal?
  • Does the person feel comfortable with me?
  • Are they nervous or just different?
  • What's my own cultural background about eye contact?

Your Practice

Rewrite these stereotypes as flexible generalisations:

Stereotype: "Germans are always on time."

Rewrite as:

Many Germans tend to value punctuality. / In my experience, German colleagues often arrive on time.

Stereotype: "Americans are loud and outgoing."

Rewrite as:

Some Americans may be outgoing. / In certain contexts, American communication styles can be direct.

Analysing the Context of an Intercultural Interaction

Before any intercultural interaction, analyse four types of context. Understanding each one helps you predict how people will behave and prepare appropriately.

The Four Contexts

CULTURAL
The cultural identities of the people involved. What languages do they speak? What values and beliefs are important to them?
INDIVIDUAL
The individuals and their relationship with each other. How well do you know them? Do you have a history together? What's their personality?
ORGANISATIONAL
What kind of meeting is it? One-to-one? All staff? Formal presentation or casual chat? What's the purpose?
ENVIRONMENTAL
Where, when and how the meeting takes place. Is it online or face-to-face? Morning or evening? Formal office or relaxed café?

Understanding How Context Works

Click to reveal how context affects intercultural interaction:

KEY PRINCIPLE
Context impacts the way we perceive things and the way we behave.
The same person will communicate differently in different contexts — formal vs. informal, with a stranger vs. a friend, in their home country vs. abroad.

Individual context: What was your relationship with this person? First meeting or ongoing?

Organisational context: Was it formal or informal? One-to-one or group?

Environmental context: Where and when did it happen?

Preparation Strategy: LAWS

When preparing for an intercultural interaction, research and think about the other person's LAWS: Language, cultural values, Norms, and Signs (customs).

What Information Should You Gather?

L = LANGUAGE

Their first language and proficiency in other languages. How do they prefer to communicate? Formally or casually? Written or spoken?

A = VALUES

Their beliefs, behaviour and preferences. What's important to them? Family? Individual achievement? Harmony? Respect?

W = NORMS

Agreed ways of behaving in their culture. How do they greet people? Give gifts? Show respect? Make decisions?

S = SIGNS (Customs)

Their rules, regulations, systems and processes. What's formal? What's appropriate dress? How do they structure meetings?

How to Find This Information

METHOD
Research online — look for cultural guides, country information, or articles about business culture.
Websites, cultural guides, and business blogs often have useful information about communication styles and expectations.
METHOD
Ask someone from that culture — a colleague, friend, or mentor who knows the culture well.
Personal insights are often more accurate and current than general information.
METHOD
Look at the person's LinkedIn profile or social media — what languages do they use? How do they present themselves?
Professional profiles often give clues about their communication style and values.
METHOD
Ask the person directly before the meeting — "What should I know to prepare for our conversation?"
Direct, respectful questions often get honest answers and show you care about the interaction.

Preparing for Your Interaction

Imagine you are preparing to meet a new colleague from a different country. Use the LAWS framework to organise what you know:

Who is this person? Where are they from?

L — Language: What languages do they speak? How might this affect the meeting?

A — Values: What's important in their culture? (Family, respect, relationships, success, harmony?)

W — Norms: How do people in their culture greet, communicate, make decisions?

S — Signs/Customs: What formal or informal rules might apply?

How will you adapt? What will you do differently to accommodate this colleague?

Preparing for an Intercultural Interaction

In this task, you'll prepare to meet a new colleague from a different country. Use everything you've learned about generalisations, context, and the LAWS framework.

Your Scenario:

You are about to start working with a new colleague. You've just learned their name and which country they're from. You have one day to prepare for your first meeting. What will you do?

What You Need to Do:

  • Choose a country and imagine a colleague from that culture.
  • Research and share information about some laws, values, norms and language in that country.
  • Decide how you could adapt your behaviour to accommodate this colleague.
  • Explain your preparation strategy to show you understand generalisations vs. stereotypes.

Remember to:

  • Use flexible language: "Some people may..." / "In many cases..." / "They might tend to..."
  • Show you understand context: Mention cultural, individual, organisational and environmental factors.
  • Avoid stereotypes: Say "may" instead of "always" — keep your thinking open.
  • Explain your thinking: "I researched this because..." / "I asked someone because..."
