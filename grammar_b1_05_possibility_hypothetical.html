Look again at what Jonas says. First he talks about Saturday — a plan he isn't sure about yet. Then he says something completely different: "I wish I had more time off." Is that about the future, or about right now? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
It's about right now — his week already happened, and he can't change it. "I wish I had more time" isn't a guess about tomorrow; it's a daydream about how things are today. English has one set of tools for guessing ("might", "probably") and a completely different one for daydreaming about the present ("I wish" + a past-shaped verb). Today you'll get both.
Today: guessing about things you're not sure of — might / may / could and words like probably, definitely, perhaps — and daydreaming about things you'd like to be different right now with I wish / if only — so you can talk naturally about an uncertain future and an imperfect present.

How Sure Are You? "It'll probably rain"

For guesses about now or later, when you're not 100% sure.

Spot the pattern

Three guesses you might hear from a friend:

  • "She's probably still at work — it's only five."
  • "Perhaps we could try that new place on the corner."
  • "He might not come — he wasn't feeling great this morning."

Before you check: where does the "guessing word" sit in each sentence — right at the start, or tucked in the middle?

Check the pattern CLICK
Probably and definitely sit in the middle — right after "be" (she's probably) or right after a modal (might not). Perhaps (and its twin maybe) usually opens the sentence instead: "Perhaps we could...", "Maybe he's busy." They almost never sit in the middle — English speakers don't say "we could perhaps try" as their first choice.
subject + be/modal + probably / definitely + verb
Perhaps / Maybe + subject + rest of sentence

From certain to uncertain

"It will rain." (sure)
→
"It'll probably rain." (not sure)

"Probably" slots in between "will" and the main verb — it never jumps to the end or the start when you're using "will".

Wishing Things Were Different: "I wish I had more time"

For daydreaming about how things are right now — not a guess, not the future, just wishing today were different.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday daydreams:

  • "I wish I had a bigger kitchen — this one's tiny."
  • "If only it wasn't so expensive, I'd buy it right now."
  • "I wish I knew more people here — I've only just moved."

Look at "had", "wasn't", "knew". Are these past events, or something else? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
They look like the past simple, but nothing happened in the past — the kitchen is small right now, the price is high right now. English borrows the past-tense shape to signal "this isn't real" — a trick, not a time reference. I wish / If only + subject + past simple = a wish about the present.
I wish / If only + subject + past simple (not real, about now)

From complaint to wish

"I don't have a car — it's annoying."
→
"I wish I had a car."

Same complaint, softer, more natural — and notice: no "don't" survives inside the wish. "I wish I had" (not "I wish I didn't have not" or any negative copy of the original).

This "past simple for something unreal right now" is the same trick English uses in sentences like "If I had more time, I'd relax more" — that's a close cousin of today's grammar, but a topic for another lesson.
