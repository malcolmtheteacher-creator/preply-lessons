The big idea

You now have four lenses and many techniques. The risk is a list: “There is a metaphor. There is alliteration. There is a rhyme.” A strong paragraph chooses two pieces of evidence and uses them to argue one idea.

Read the poem aloud once. It is short, and very dense. Accent marks are omitted here.

Spring and Fall: to a young child · Gerard Manley Hopkins (1918)

1Margaret, are you grieving
Over Goldengrove unleaving?
Leaves, like the things of man, you
With your fresh thoughts care for, can you?
5Ah! as the heart grows older
It will come to such sights colder
By and by, nor spare a sigh
Though worlds of wanwood leafmeal lie;
And yet you will weep and know why.
10Now no matter, child, the name:
Sorrow's springs are the same.
Nor mouth had, no nor mind, expressed
What heart heard of, ghost guessed:
It is the blight man was born for,
15It is Margaret you mourn for.

From a guiding question to a Position

In some exams you are given a guiding question. Turn it into an arguable claim.

Guiding question: How does the poet present loss?
Weak Position: The poet writes about loss.
Strong Position: Hopkins presents loss not as a single event but as a permanent condition of being human, so that the child’s grief over falling leaves becomes grief for herself.

SPEAK: What makes the strong Position arguable? How could someone disagree?

It makes an interpretive claim (loss as a condition, not an event) and gives an idea the paragraph can prove. Someone could argue that the poem is mainly about growing up, or about empathy, not about loss.

A model paragraph

P Hopkins presents childhood grief as the first lesson in an adult truth: that mourning for small things is mourning for oneself. A The speaker’s direct question, “Margaret, are you grieving / Over Goldengrove unleaving?”, begins gently, and the coined word “unleaving” turns the falling of leaves into an active, almost cruel process. C This tenderness is then undercut by the bluntness of the final lines, “It is the blight man was born for, / It is Margaret you mourn for”, where the short, closing couplet and the repeated “for” make grief inevitable. E Together, the soft opening and the hard closing show an adult voice moving from comfort to unavoidable truth. R The reader is positioned to feel the tenderness of the speaker and the weight of what he cannot spare her.

SPEAK: Label each sentence P, A, C, E or R, then underline the effect verbs.

Labelled above. Effect verbs: turns, undercut, make, show, positioned. Notice that “Connect” adds a second, different technique that contrasts with the first.

Build your own

1. Choose your evidence

Choose two pieces of evidence from the poem for a paragraph on how Hopkins moves from tenderness to hard truth. Write each as quotation + technique.

Opening: the direct, gentle question and the coined word “unleaving”. Middle: the list of future coldness (“It will come to such sights colder / … nor spare a sigh”). Close: the final two lines with short, flat phrasing and the repeated “for”. Any two that contrast well work.

3. Fix this paragraph

Hopkins uses a question at the start. He also uses rhyme and alliteration. This shows the speaker is sad and the poem is about growing up. The last line is sad.

SPEAK: List four problems.

No arguable Position; no quotations; techniques listed, not analysed; “shows” and “sad” are vague; no Connect or Evaluate; no effect on the reader. A repair would choose two quotations, name their effects, and link them in one argument.

Looking back

SPEAK: Which slot was hardest for you in poetry? What would help?

I can: write a PACER paragraph on a poem that argues one idea with two pieces of evidence and ends on the effect on the reader.
guiding question (noun) — a question that directs your analysis of a text. Example: "The guiding question focuses on loss."
thesis (noun) — the central argument of an essay or paragraph. Example: "Your Position is the thesis of the paragraph."
coinage (noun) — a new word invented by a writer. Example: "Unleaving is a coinage by the poet."
inevitable (adjective) — certain to happen and impossible to avoid. Example: "The poem presents grief as inevitable."
undercut (verb) — to weaken something by contradicting it. Example: "The blunt ending undercuts the earlier tenderness."
evaluate (verb) — to judge the overall effect or value of something. Example: "Evaluate the combined effect of the two choices."
blunt (adjective) — direct and unsoftened. Example: "The final lines are blunt."
elegiac (adjective) — sad and reflective, like a poem of mourning. Example: "The tone is elegiac."
