When "Yes" Doesn't Mean Yes

Pragmatic Strategies for Cross-Cultural European Business

The Hook

A Dutch project manager says: "That timeline won't work."
A British project manager says: "That's quite an ambitious timeline."

They mean exactly the same thing.
But one will offend a Spanish stakeholder. The other will be completely missed by a German one.

The linguist Erin Meyer calls this the "communication scale" - the spectrum from low-context (say what you mean) to high-context (read between the lines). And in European business, you're constantly code-switching between positions on that scale, often within the same meeting.

The Pragmatic Challenge

In linguistics, pragmatics is the study of how context shapes meaning - what's communicated beyond the literal words. Today's lesson focuses on pragmatic competence: your ability to modulate directness, manage face-threat, and decode implied meaning across cultural contexts.

What Makes This Hard

The challenge isn't learning to be more direct OR more indirect. It's developing the pragmatic range to calibrate your communication to the context - and the interpretive skill to decode others' calibrations correctly.

Consider this...

When you need to disagree with a senior stakeholder from a different European culture, what factors influence how direct you are? How do you read whether your message has actually landed?

Today we'll work with five expressions that give you calibrated control over your directness - language that lets you modulate force, manage face-threat, and signal intent clearly across the European communication spectrum.

Language Focus A

These expressions help you disagree without damaging relationships and probe for real positions when you suspect someone is being diplomatically evasive.

"I see where you're coming from, but I'd push back slightly on..."

Click to reveal
Meaning

Signals genuine disagreement while acknowledging the other person's reasoning has merit. The phrase "push back slightly" is doing significant work - it frames your objection as moderate even when it may not be.

Form

I see where you're coming from, but I'd push back (slightly/a bit) on + [noun phrase / the idea that...]

Note: "slightly" and "a bit" are downtoners - they soften without necessarily reducing your actual disagreement.

Pragmatic Force

This expression works across the European spectrum because it separates the person from the position. "Where you're coming from" validates their reasoning process; "push back on" targets the conclusion. This distinction matters enormously in face-sensitive cultures.

Too weak (may be missed)

"That's an interesting point, although I wonder if..."

Appropriately calibrated

"I see where you're coming from, but I'd push back slightly on the assumption that..."

In a steering committee:
"I see where you're coming from on the vendor lock-in concern, but I'd push back slightly on the idea that building in-house eliminates that risk - we'd just be trading one dependency for another."
NOW YOU TRY

Scenario: A colleague argues that you should delay the launch until testing reaches 100% coverage. You think this is unrealistic and unnecessary.

Construct your response using the full expression. Pay attention to what specifically you're "pushing back on" - the coverage target? The delay? The underlying assumption?

"Help me understand the thinking behind..."

Click to reveal
Meaning

Ostensibly a request for explanation, but pragmatically functions as a challenge with plausible deniability. Forces the other person to articulate (and potentially expose weaknesses in) their reasoning.

Form

Help me understand the thinking behind + [noun phrase / decision / approach]

Variations: "Walk me through the logic of..." / "Can you take me through how you arrived at..."

Pragmatic Force

This is a face-saving challenge. By framing your scepticism as a comprehension gap ("help me understand"), you give the other person an opportunity to either strengthen their case or gracefully revise their position. It works particularly well when questioning decisions made by those senior to you.

Direct challenge (higher face-threat)

"I don't understand why we're doing it this way."

Indirect challenge (lower face-threat)

"Help me understand the thinking behind this approach."

Challenging a technical decision:
"Help me understand the thinking behind going with the microservices architecture at this stage - I want to make sure I'm not missing something about the scalability requirements."
NOW YOU TRY

Scenario: Your manager has decided to remove a key developer from your project mid-sprint to help another team. You think this is a mistake but need to challenge carefully.

Use "Help me understand the thinking behind..." to probe the decision without directly opposing it.

"Just to make sure we're not talking at cross-purposes..."

Click to reveal
Meaning

Checks for alignment while framing any misunderstanding as mutual rather than attributing error to either party. Creates space to surface hidden disagreement without accusation.

Form

Just to make sure we're not talking at cross-purposes, + [clarifying question / restatement]

Often followed by: "When you say X, do you mean...?" or "Are we agreed that...?"

Pragmatic Force

The idiom "at cross-purposes" implies shared responsibility for any confusion. This is crucial: it avoids the face-threat of "I think you've misunderstood" or the subordination of "I might have misunderstood." It's particularly valuable when you suspect "false agreement" - everyone said yes but meant different things.

After an apparently successful meeting:
"Just to make sure we're not talking at cross-purposes - when we agreed to 'phase the rollout,' are we talking about geographical phasing or functional phasing? I want to make sure Paris and Berlin have the same picture."
Exposing hidden disagreement:
"Just to make sure we're not talking at cross-purposes here - I'm hearing broad support, but I'm also sensing some reservations about the timeline. Can we surface those before we commit?"
NOW YOU TRY

Scenario: Everyone in the meeting said "yes" to the proposal, but body language suggested some people had concerns. You need to check whether the agreement is real.

Use "Just to make sure we're not talking at cross-purposes..." to create space for people to voice doubts.

Language Focus B

These expressions allow you to introduce alternatives without threatening the status quo and raise objections from a position of apparent neutrality.

"At the risk of complicating things..."

Click to reveal
Meaning

Introduces an alternative or concern while acknowledging the social cost of doing so. Signals awareness that you're potentially disrupting consensus or adding complexity - and doing it anyway because you believe it matters.

Form

At the risk of complicating things, + [alternative view / concern / question]

Variations: "At the risk of overcomplicating this..." / "At the risk of throwing a spanner in the works..."

Pragmatic Force

This is a pre-emptive face-saving device. By acknowledging upfront that your contribution might be unwelcome ("at the risk of"), you signal self-awareness and make it harder for others to dismiss you as obstructive. The phrase also elevates what follows - if you're willing to risk social cost, it must be important.

No acknowledgment of disruption

"Actually, I think we should consider a completely different approach."

Acknowledges social cost

"At the risk of complicating things, have we fully stress-tested this against the regulatory changes coming in Q3?"

Introducing an uncomfortable alternative:
"At the risk of complicating things at this stage, I wonder whether we've fully considered the build-vs-buy question. I know we've been assuming we'll build, but the landscape has changed."
NOW YOU TRY

Scenario: The team has nearly finalised a decision to use Vendor A. You've just learned that Vendor B has released a product that might be significantly better, but raising this now will delay everything.

Use "At the risk of complicating things..." to introduce this new information appropriately.

"I'm conscious that I may be the only one in the room who sees it this way, but..."

Click to reveal
Meaning

Allows you to voice dissent while creating space for others to join. Frames your position as potentially isolated (reducing threat to group consensus) while often having the opposite effect - giving others permission to agree.

Form

I'm conscious (that) I may/might be the only one (in the room) who + [verb phrase], but...

Variations: "I may be alone in thinking this, but..." / "It's possible I'm an outlier here, but..."

Pragmatic Force

This is sophisticated coalition-building through apparent isolation. By claiming potential solitude, you: (1) reduce face-threat to the group, (2) signal that you've genuinely considered you might be wrong, and (3) often prompt others who secretly agree to voice support. It's particularly effective in consensus-driven cultures (Nordic, Dutch) where explicit dissent feels uncomfortable.

Voicing an unpopular concern:
"I'm conscious I may be the only one in the room who sees it this way, but I'm not convinced we've adequately stress-tested the security implications. I'd feel more comfortable with an external audit before we proceed."
Challenging direction:
"I may be alone in thinking this, but I wonder if we're optimising for the wrong metric here. We keep talking about velocity, but the client's actual pain point seems to be quality."
NOW YOU TRY

Scenario: Your project team is enthusiastic about an AI tool that promises to automate a significant portion of the testing process. You have serious concerns about over-reliance on it, but the momentum is strong and you don't want to be seen as resistant to innovation.

Use "I'm conscious I may be the only one..." to voice your concerns effectively.

The Calibration Principle

Notice that all five expressions share a common structure: acknowledgment + position. The acknowledgment does pragmatic work - validating the other person's view, accepting social cost, or admitting potential isolation. This isn't weakness; it's strategic face-work that makes your actual position more likely to be heard.

The skill is calibrating how much softening you need for the context. With a direct Dutch colleague, heavy hedging may seem evasive. With a British stakeholder, insufficient hedging may seem aggressive. Your job is to read the room and adjust.

The Directness Paradox

Dutch directness is often perceived as rude by high-context cultures - but the Dutch see it as a form of respect. Meanwhile, British indirectness is often perceived as dishonest or passive-aggressive.

Analyse: What are the underlying cultural values that produce these different styles? And is there a "right" amount of directness, or is this purely contextual?
2

Decoding "That's interesting"

In British English, "That's interesting" often signals scepticism or disagreement. In other contexts, it's genuine curiosity. The same words, completely different pragmatic force.

Analyse: How do you currently decode such ambiguous signals? What contextual cues do you rely on? Have you ever got it seriously wrong?
3

The False Agreement Problem

Everyone said "yes" in the meeting. Three weeks later, half the team reveals they had serious reservations. This is endemic in multinational teams.

Analyse: What structural or linguistic interventions might prevent false agreement? Is it purely a facilitation problem, or are there cultural dynamics that make explicit disagreement costly?
4

Code-Switching Authenticity

Should you become more direct with Dutch teams and more indirect with British ones? Or does constant adaptation make you seem inauthentic - or worse, patronising?

Analyse: Where's the line between pragmatic adaptation and losing your authentic voice? Is there a risk that excessive code-switching is itself a form of stereotyping?
5

Remote Strips Context

Video calls eliminate many contextual cues: body language is harder to read, there's no informal coffee-chat afterward, and chat messages are easily misread.

Analyse: Has remote work made cross-cultural communication harder, easier, or just different? What specific strategies have you developed to compensate for lost context?
6

When Hierarchy Intersects Culture

Cultural communication norms interact with hierarchical dynamics in complex ways. Disagreeing with a peer is different from disagreeing with a senior stakeholder - and both vary by culture.

Analyse: How do you calibrate your communication when both cultural and hierarchical factors are in play? Which takes precedence when they conflict?
