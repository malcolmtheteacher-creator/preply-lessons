Coaching Conversations

C2 Level · Leadership Communication

The Coaching Mindset

Coaching is fundamentally different from mentoring, training, or managing. A coach doesn't solve problems for the coachee — instead, they create the conditions for the coachee to discover their own solutions. This shift from directive to non-directive is what separates coaching from other forms of professional development.

Coaching vs. Mentoring vs. Managing

Mentoring is typically based on the mentor's experience and expertise. A mentor might say, "When I faced that issue, here's what I did."

Coaching assumes the coachee has the answers within themselves. A coach asks, "What do you think might work?" and "What would success look like for you?"

Managing involves authority, accountability, and evaluation. Managers assess performance; coaches build capability.

The best leaders move fluidly between all three modes, depending on context and urgency.

The GROW Model

GROW is a simple but powerful framework:

  • Goal: What does the coachee want to achieve?
  • Reality: Where are they now? What's the actual situation?
  • Options: What choices do they have? What's possible?
  • Will/Way Forward: What specific steps will they take? By when?

The power of GROW is that it's linear and logical. It prevents coaching from becoming aimless conversation. Each stage builds toward commitment.

Reflection: Think about a time someone coached you effectively. How did they help you discover your own solution rather than giving you advice?

Powerful Coaching Questions

Awareness Questions:

  • "What's really going on here?"
  • "What patterns do you notice?"
  • "What are you assuming that might not be true?"

Future-Focused Questions:

  • "If you achieved this, what would be different?"
  • "What does success look like for you?"
  • "What's the first step?"

Accountability Questions:

  • "What commitment are you willing to make?"
  • "By when will you do this?"
  • "How will you know you've succeeded?"

Active Listening Techniques

Listen for what isn't said: Gaps, hesitations, and contradictions often reveal deeper concerns than words alone.

Reflect back what you hear: "So you're saying that you feel overlooked because your contributions aren't recognised?" This builds rapport and ensures understanding.

Use silence strategically: Pause after a question. Resist the urge to fill silence. The coachee often finds their deepest insights in that space.

Notice tone and body language: A coach's presence is as important as their words. Show genuine interest through your full attention.

Coaching Role-Play: The Performance Conversation

Your task: Coach a talented but frustrated team member who has missed deadlines on their last two projects. They're underperforming and disengaged. Your goal is to help them identify the root cause and create their own action plan using the GROW model.

Your Opening

You've asked them to meet with you. You've blocked off 30 minutes. You want to understand what's happening. Craft your opening statement that creates psychological safety while being direct about the issue.

They say: "I don't know. I've just been struggling to focus. There's a lot going on."

This is vague. Your next question should help them get specific. What do you ask?

Effective approach: Move from "What's going on?" to specific exploration. You might ask: "Walk me through the last two weeks. When did you notice your focus dropping?" or "What does 'a lot going on' mean? Is this work-related or personal or both?" The goal is reality clarity—understanding the actual situation, not assumptions.

They reveal: They're overwhelmed, unclear about project priorities, and haven't asked for help because they thought they should be able to handle it.

You've diagnosed the reality. Now guide them to options. What do you ask?

Effective approach: "What would it look like if you reached out for help on this project? Who could you involve? What would you need to ask them?" Or: "If you had to prioritise the three things on your plate, which would you focus on first and why?" The coach doesn't tell them what to do—the coachee discovers their own options.

Close the coaching conversation.

They've identified: ask their manager (you) for clarity on priorities, delegate one task to a peer, and schedule a mid-week check-in. How do you lock in their commitment and end the session?

Effective closing: "So by Thursday, you'll have clarified priorities with me, delegated the report to Sarah, and we'll touch base Wednesday to see how it's going. Does that feel right to you? What could stop you from making this happen? What support do you need from me?" This confirms commitment, anticipates obstacles, and builds accountability—not through threat, but through clear expectations.

Coaching Culture & Context

Coaching is increasingly seen as a core leadership competency, but it thrives only in certain organisational conditions.

Building a Coaching Culture

In a coaching culture, managers see development as part of their role, not a distraction from it. Mistakes are treated as learning opportunities. Questions are valued over answers. People feel safe being vulnerable about what they don't know.

This requires:

  • Time: Coaching can't happen in rushed conversations. It requires protected space.
  • Trust: People must believe that their manager is coaching them for their benefit, not to set them up for failure.
  • Patience: Coaching is slower than telling. It takes time for people to own their solutions.
  • Consistent modelling: Senior leaders must visibly coach, not just mandate it.

When Coaching Is Inappropriate

Crisis situations: If someone is about to make a decision that will cause serious harm, direct them immediately rather than asking them to explore options.

Performance management: If someone is being underperforming and not responding to coaching, at some point you move from coaching to managing. Set clear expectations and consequences.

Lacking capability: If someone doesn't have the foundational skills or knowledge, you need to teach or train before coaching will be effective.

Someone who doesn't want it: Coaching requires a willing participant. You can't coach someone who's defensive or not ready to reflect.

Self-Coaching Questions: Coaching skills apply to yourself too. When facing a challenge: "What's my real goal here?" "What am I assuming?" "What would my best self do in this situation?" "What's one small step I could take this week?" Self-coaching builds the emotional resilience that leaders need.

The Paradox of Coaching

The irony of great coaching is that it often looks like you're doing nothing. You're not giving advice, solving problems, or showing expertise. You're asking questions and listening. To others, it might look passive. But it's some of the most powerful leadership work you can do. You're not building dependence on yourself—you're building capability in others.
