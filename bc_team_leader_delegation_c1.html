Expressions with "get"

These informal, energetic expressions using get are ideal for conveying urgency in professional emails. Click each card to see the meaning.

get in touch with Contact someone (e.g. "Please get in touch with the supplier by Friday.")
get back to me Respond to me / send a reply (e.g. "Please get back to me by end of day.")
get our heads down Work hard and focus (e.g. "We need to get our heads down if we're going to finish this.")
get something done Complete a task, often with urgency (e.g. "We need to get the report done before Monday.")
get the ball rolling Make a start on something (e.g. "Can you get the ball rolling on the venue bookings?")
get cracking Start working immediately, without delay (e.g. "We'd better get cracking — there's a lot to do.")

Using by, till, and until for deadlines

What's the difference between "by Friday" and "till Friday"?

By = no later than a deadline (e.g. "Submit by Friday" — the action must be complete before or on Friday).
Till / Until = up to a point in time (e.g. "I'll be in the office till Friday" — continuing up to that point).
In delegation emails, by is usually more appropriate for setting deadlines.

What's wrong with this email?

Read the email below. What's the problem? What tone does it create? How could it be improved?

Hi everyone,

We're off on our interrail soon and we haven't done everything yet, so we should start thinking about doing things.

John, I was wondering if it would be at all possible to find out about train times from London to Paris for the first leg of our journey. When you have that information, it would be great if you could let me know.

Pearl, could you maybe look into hotels for the second week? I know you're busy, so no pressure, but it would be really helpful if you got round to it at some point.

Thanks,
Sam

What are the key problems with this email?

The email is vague ("doing things"), has no urgency or deadlines, uses overly hedged language ("I was wondering if it would be at all possible"), and undermines the urgency with phrases like "no pressure" and "at some point". It sounds apologetic rather than authoritative.

Now compare with the improved version:

Hi everyone,

Our interrail is looming and we've still got loads to do, so we'd better get our heads down if we're going to get everything done in time.

John, please get in touch with National Rail to find out London to Paris train times and get back to me ASAP — by Wednesday at the latest.

Pearl, we really need to get some hotel options sorted for the second week. Can you have a shortlist of three options to me by Thursday?

Let's make sure we get the ball rolling on this — I'll follow up with everyone on Friday.

Thanks,
Sam

Analyse the improvement:

How many "get" expressions can you identify in the improved version?

How are deadlines conveyed differently? Which words create urgency?

Does the tone feel bossy, professional, or something else? How would you describe it?

Write a Delegation Email

You are a team leader. You're going to write an email to your team, delegating responsibilities for an upcoming event or project you're in charge of.

First, decide what the event or project is. Some ideas:

Choose your scenario (or invent your own):

• The launch of a new product or service

• Organising a company away day or team event

• A client visit or trade show

• A major deadline — a report, a pitch, or a proposal

• A process that needs to change quickly across the team

Plan your email structure:

Opening: Set the scene — what's the situation and why is it urgent?

Middle paragraphs: Delegate specific tasks to specific people, with clear deadlines.

Closing: Motivate the team and confirm follow-up.
