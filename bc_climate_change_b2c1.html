The Future is Already Here

Consider this sentence about future commitments:

Click to explore → "We will all be driving electric cars because we will not be using fossil fuels."

What does this tell us?

This sentence uses a simple future prediction. It assumes a cause (stopping fossil fuels) and a result (electric cars). But is this realistic? What would need to happen first? What barriers exist? This is the kind of thinking that drives environmental solutions.

Active & Passive Voice with Infinitives

One way scientists and environmentalists discuss solutions is by shifting focus between who does the action (active) and what happens (passive). This is especially useful when we're not sure who will do something, or when the action itself is more important than the agent.

Compare These Two Approaches

Version 1: Active Focus → "This would involve rocketing a giant sunshade into space."

Active Infinitive (after 'involve')

Focus: the action and who does it

Structure: involve + -ing form (active gerund)

Why use it: When we want to emphasize the concrete action and the agent doing it.

Version 2: Passive Focus → "A giant sunshade would be rocketed into space."

Passive Infinitive (be + past participle)

Focus: the action and what happens to the object

Structure: would be + past participle (passive infinitive)

Why use it: When we want to emphasize the result or when the agent is unknown or obvious.

Grammar Summary: Active vs. Passive Infinitives

Passive voice with infinitive: [subject] + [modal/auxiliary] + be + past participle

  • "The ice caps could be melted by rising temperatures."
  • "Artificial trees would be planted across deserts."
  • "Ships need to be fitted with solar panels."

More Examples from Our Topic

ACTIVE VERSION

"You could do this by constructing more wind farms."

PASSIVE VERSION

"This could be done by constructing more wind farms."

Notice the difference: The active version focuses on "you" (the agent). The passive version focuses on the action itself. Both are correct—it depends on what you want to emphasize.

Top tip: Passive voice is especially useful in academic and professional discussions because it sounds more objective. When you're arguing for a climate proposal, using passive constructions makes your argument sound more credible and less personal.

Expressing and Debating Opinions

Climate change is contentious. People disagree passionately about solutions. To participate in these debates effectively, you need sophisticated language for giving opinions, justifying your stance, agreeing and disagreeing respectfully.

Giving Opinions

Use these to introduce your viewpoint or position.

Justifying

Use these to support or explain your opinion.

Agreeing

Use these to align with someone else's view.

Disagreeing

Use these to politely or firmly reject someone's view.

Giving Opinions: "If you think about it...", "I'm absolutely convinced that...", "I reckon that...", "As far as I'm concerned..."

The Green Panel: Allocate the Funding

You are part of an international panel of environmental experts. A government has allocated €1 million to fund the top 3 climate solutions. Your task: evaluate the four proposals below, choose your top three, and present your verdict with evidence and opinion language.

What you'll evaluate: readiness level (how soon can it work?), estimated cost, and perceived flaws (what could go wrong?).

The Four Proposals

1. Giant Sunshade in Space

Click to reveal details

Description: Deploy a massive sunshade in orbit to reflect sunlight and cool the planet.
Readiness: Theoretical—technology doesn't yet exist at scale. 15–20 years away.
Estimated Cost: €500 million to €2 billion (very expensive).
Perceived Flaws: Unintended side effects on weather, solar power generation, unequal benefit distribution, space debris, political control issues.

2. Plankton Blooms

Click to reveal details

Description: Fertilise ocean waters to encourage plankton growth, which absorbs CO₂ from the atmosphere.
Readiness: Tested on a small scale. Could be deployed within 3–5 years.
Estimated Cost: €50–200 million (moderate).
Perceived Flaws: Unpredictable ecological effects, nutrient runoff, potential harm to fisheries, questions about carbon storage permanence.

3. Artificial Trees (Carbon Capture)

Click to reveal details

Description: Deploy mechanical devices that absorb CO₂ directly from the air and store it underground or use it.
Readiness: Prototypes exist. Scaling up is the challenge. Could expand within 5–10 years.
Estimated Cost: €300–600 million (high).
Perceived Flaws: Energy-intensive, expensive per ton of CO₂ removed, uncertain long-term storage, doesn't address emissions reduction.

4. Cloud Ships (Marine Clouds)

Click to reveal details

Description: Ships spray salt water into clouds over oceans to make clouds whiter and more reflective, cooling the planet.
Readiness: Early testing phase. Could be piloted within 2–3 years.
Estimated Cost: €20–50 million for pilot (low).
Perceived Flaws: Localized effects, unproven at scale, potential impacts on precipitation patterns, limited duration.
Your panel's priorities: You want solutions that are safe, have a clear timeline, make the best use of €1 million, and address real climate risks.

Make Your Evaluation

Step 1: Which proposal seems most ready to implement?

Justify your choice using passive voice: "This would be..." or "This could be done by..."

Step 2: Which proposal offers the best value for €1 million?

Use opinion language: "I reckon that...", "As far as I'm concerned..."

Step 3: Which proposal has the fewest risks?

Justify: "There may be some truth in that, but...", "After all..."

Step 4: Choose your top 3 proposals and rank them (1 = first choice)
1. My first choice: __________________
2. My second choice: __________________
3. My third choice: __________________

Present Your Verdict

You have 3 minutes to present your final recommendation to the panel. Use the template below. Practice speaking this aloud.

Your verdict (write or practise speaking):

"As far as I'm concerned, the top three proposals that should be funded are [your three choices]. My first choice is [proposal name] because [reason involving readiness/cost/risk]. This would involve [passive infinitive describing implementation]. Second, I'd recommend [proposal name]. There may be some truth in the concerns about [flaw], but [your justification]. Finally, [your third choice] because [reason].

If you think about it, [explanation of why these three matter]. I'm absolutely convinced that this allocation offers the best value and the fastest results. These are the investments we need now."
