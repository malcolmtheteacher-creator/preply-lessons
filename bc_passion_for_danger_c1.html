Adrenaline and Accountability

Starting Questions

Consider these questions before we dive into the lesson. What draws people toward activities where the margin for error is negligible?

1. What constitutes an "extreme" sport? How would you distinguish it from competitive sport?

An extreme sport typically involves higher risk of serious injury or death, an element of environmental hazard, and individual autonomy in decision-making. It's less about standardised competition and more about pushing personal boundaries.

2. Have you encountered situations where pursuing your goals meant accepting considerable risk?

This is personal reflection. Consider situations from sport, career, education, or travel where the potential reward warranted accepting potential negative consequences.

3. To what extent should governments regulate activities like adventure sports?

This is debatable. Consider: individual liberty vs. public cost (rescue operations, healthcare); informed consent; protection of minors; environmental impact; and precedent for other risky activities.

Extreme Sports: A Spectrum of Risk

Read about ten extreme sports. Consider how danger varies. Which elements distinguish merely adventurous activity from genuinely extreme sport? How might hazard level influence your own willingness to participate?

Base Jumping

Parachuting from fixed objects: buildings, cliffs, bridges. The leap of faith with seconds to deploy the chute.

Risk factor: Equipment failure at low altitude offers minimal recovery time.

Big Wave Surfing

Riding waves exceeding 60+ feet. The ocean becomes a cathedral of raw power.

Risk factor: Drowning, shallow water impact, hypothermia, and the raw force of tonnes of water.

Free Solo Climbing

Scaling sheer rock faces without ropes, harnesses, or safety equipment. Pure skill and focus.

Risk factor: One mistake is terminal. No margin for error whatsoever.

Mountaineering (High Altitude)

Summiting peaks above 8,000 metres, including Everest. The "death zone" where oxygen is lethal.

Risk factor: Altitude sickness, avalanche, exposure, frostbite, and permanent disability.

Heli-Skiing

Descending unmarked mountain slopes accessed by helicopter, often in avalanche-prone terrain.

Risk factor: Avalanche burial, trauma, and environmental unpredictability.

Wingsuit Flying

Leaping from aircraft or cliffs wearing a specially designed suit to glide, then deploying a parachute.

Risk factor: Collision with terrain, equipment failure, loss of altitude awareness.

Motorcycle Racing (MotoGP)

High-speed racing on purpose-built circuits. Pushing physics and human reflex to limits.

Risk factor: Crashes at 200+ mph, spinal injury, death from impact or fire.

Cave Diving

Underwater exploration in confined spaces with limited exit points and zero natural light.

Risk factor: Nitrogen narcosis, equipment failure, disorientation, drowning.

Parkour in Urban Environments

Moving fluidly through city landscapes via vaults, leaps, and precision balance work across obstacles.

Risk factor: Falls from height, traffic collision, environmental instability.

Iceclimbing

Ascending frozen waterfalls and ice-covered rock using specialised equipment and technique.

Risk factor: Ice collapse, falling equipment, altitude exposure, frostbite.

The Public Cost of Private Risk

"There are many people who are getting fed up with adventurers who get themselves into trouble and then have to be rescued. That can mean complicated, time-consuming missions involving the coast guard and even the navy, which can put others' lives at risk and end up costing governments — and therefore tax-payers — millions of pounds."

Unpacking the Argument

This statement raises several distinct claims. Which are empirical? Which are ethical? Which are economic?

EMPIRICAL CLAIM
Rescue operations do occur and incur financial cost
Factual statement. We can verify this with data on coast guard budgets and rescue mission frequencies. Not inherently controversial, but the interpretation of what constitutes "waste" is.
ETHICAL CLAIM
Individuals who voluntarily assume risk should bear the cost of their own rescue
This invokes a principle of personal responsibility. But consider: Should we apply this principle consistently? Do we charge firefighters' families for residential fire rescue? Where lies the boundary?
PRACTICAL QUESTION
How can we effectively deter high-risk behaviour through financial penalty?
If someone charges £50,000 for a rescue operation, would adventurers modify their behaviour? Or would they simply obtain rescue insurance, shifting cost to insurance companies (and thus all their clients)?
COUNTERPOINT
Society funds rescue operations for many activities — not just extreme sports
We pay for coast guard operations that rescue pleasure boaters, mountain rescue for lost hikers, and air ambulances for accident victims. Are adventurers uniquely culpable?

Discussion Prompts

Invert the perspective:

If governments banned dangerous sports, what would be lost? Is the cultural and personal value of risk-taking worth the public cost?

Examine the precedent:

We allow motorcycles, skiing, mountaineering, and skydiving. What principle distinguishes permissible from impermissible risk?

Consider autonomy:

Does the principle of informed consent override public interest concerns? Can someone truly consent to an outcome they believe won't happen?

Language Structures at C1 Level

Notice how sophisticated argument deploys particular grammatical and rhetorical strategies. Study these patterns; they recur across academic discourse.

Cleft Sentences (for Emphasis)

Standard: "The real problem is that we cannot predict all outcomes."

Cleft (Emphatic): "What we cannot do is predict all outcomes."

Effect: Fronts the limitation, intensifies the point.

Standard: "Rescue operations cost money."

Cleft (Emphatic): "It is the financial burden of rescue operations that troubles critics."

Effect: Isolates the specific grievance for emphasis.

Inversion (for Rhetorical Force)

Standard: "Rarely have we witnessed such disregard for collective welfare."

Inverted: "Rarely have we witnessed such disregard for collective welfare."

The inverted word order (fronting of adverb + inversion of subject/auxiliary) creates formality and emphasis. Common in academic and polemic writing.

Narrative Tenses in Complex Sequences

Past Perfect for Earlier Action: "By the time rescuers arrived, the climber had exhausted her oxygen supply."

Establishes causal chain: depletion occurred before arrival, thus rescue failed.

Past Continuous for Background Context: "While the team was climbing, weather conditions were deteriorating rapidly."

Simultaneous actions; sets stage for disaster narrative.

Hedging & Epistemic Modality

Notice how cautious language signals nuance:

Certain: "Extreme sports cause serious injury."

Hedged: "Extreme sports may well entail considerable risk of serious injury."

may well = likelihood acknowledged but not absolute

Assertion: "Risk-takers are selfish."

Hedged: "One might argue that certain risk-takers prioritise personal fulfilment over collective fiscal responsibility."

One might argue = frames claim as interpretation, not fact

Planning Your Adventure

You will design a detailed proposal for an extreme sport adventure — then defend it against the public cost argument. Structure matters. Sophistication matters. Evidence matters.

Your Specific Adventure Must Include:

  • Location & Sport: Precise detail. Example: "Ice climbing the Séracs on the Mer de Glace, Chamonix" rather than merely "mountaineering"
  • Preparation: Training regimen, equipment, professional guidance, insurance, contingency plans
  • Danger Analysis: Specific hazards you'd encounter. What could go wrong? How would you mitigate?
  • Ethical Argument: Why is your adventure defensible? How do you address public cost concerns?

Language Reminders

  • Use multi-word verbs: "I would get into rock climbing by…", "This deals with the cost concern because…"
  • Deploy complex narrative structures to sequence events: "Having completed…", "Once I reach…"
  • Employ cleft sentences for emphasis: "What critics overlook is…", "It is training that would mitigate…"
  • Hedge appropriately: Avoid absolute claims; use modal language ("may", "could arguably")
