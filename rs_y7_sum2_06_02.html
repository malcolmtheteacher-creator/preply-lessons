The Ten Commandments and Jewish Ethics

Exploring how Judaism's moral law shapes life and practice

For Jews, Torah and Halakhah (Jewish law) are exactly this—a comprehensive guide to living. But it's not seen as oppressive. Let's explore why.

Key Terms: Jewish Law and Practice

Torah: The first five books of the Hebrew Bible; also called the Pentateuch; contains the Law given by God to Moses
Halakhah: Jewish law; the binding rules and practices that guide how Jews live
Mitzvot (plural of Mitzvah): A commandment or religious obligation; often refers to the 613 commandments in the Torah
Pikuach Nefesh: The principle that saving a life overrides almost every other commandment in Jewish law
Tikkun Olam: Meaning "repairing the world"; the Jewish obligation to work for justice and improve society
Tzedakah: Charitable giving; but more than charity—it's a religious duty and obligation to those in need
Bal Tashchit: The commandment not to waste or destroy unnecessarily; environmental stewardship

The Ten Commandments (Exodus 20)

Commandments 1–4: Duties to God

1. You shall have no other gods before Me.

Monotheism: God is the one and only authority. Loyalty is to the God of Israel.

2. You shall not make idols or graven images.

Do not worship carved statues or objects. God cannot be represented as an object.

3. You shall not take the name of the Lord your God in vain.

Do not misuse God's name through false oaths, curses, or irreverent speech. God's name is sacred.

4. Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy.

Set aside the seventh day (Saturday) as sacred; rest from work; focus on God and family.

Commandments 5–10: Duties to People

5. Honour your father and mother.

Respect for parents and elders; the foundation of family and community order.

6. You shall not murder.

Sanctity of human life is absolute. Unjustified killing is forbidden.

7. You shall not commit adultery.

Faithfulness in marriage; betrayal of trust is wrong.

8. You shall not steal.

Respect for others' property; honesty in dealings with others.

9. You shall not bear false witness against your neighbour.

Do not lie in court or spread falsehoods. Truth and justice matter.

10. You shall not covet your neighbour's house, wife, servant, ox, or donkey.

Don't desire what belongs to others. Contentment and respect are virtues.

613 Commandments: The Ten Commandments are the foundation, but the Torah contains 613 mitzvot (commandments) that govern EVERYTHING: what to eat (kashrut/kosher laws), what to wear, how to lend money, how to treat animals, how to harvest crops, laws of Sabbath, rules about honesty in business, and much more. Every area of life is sanctified by law.

Beyond Rules: The Spirit of Jewish Law

Pikuach Nefesh: Life Overrides Everything

This principle shows that Jewish ethics is NOT just blind rule-following. If keeping a commandment would result in harm or death, you must BREAK that commandment to save life. If someone is drowning on Sabbath (when work is forbidden), you must rescue them—violating the Sabbath to save a life is not just permitted, it's required. Life is sacred above all else.

Tikkun Olam: Repairing the World

Jewish ethics demands active engagement in justice and improvement of society. This goes beyond personal morality—it's about making the world a better place. Fighting poverty, injustice, and suffering is a religious duty. Jews see themselves as partners with God in creation, continuing the work of building a just society.

Tzedakah: Duty, Not Charity

When non-Jews give charity, they choose to help. When Jews give tzedakah, they fulfil an obligation. It's not optional generosity—it's a commandment. Poor people have a RIGHT to receive; wealthy people have a DUTY to give. Eight levels of tzedakah exist, with the highest being helping someone become self-sufficient so they don't need charity.

Bal Tashchit: Don't Waste

This commandment forbids unnecessary destruction or waste. It evolved into a principle of environmental stewardship: don't cut down fruit trees, don't pollute water, don't waste food or resources. The earth is not ours to destroy; we're stewards of God's creation.

Jewish Ethics in Practice: Real-Life Examples

How Jewish Law Guides Decisions

Example 1: Business Ethics

Kashrut teaches honesty in weights and measures. You can't cheat customers by using false scales. More broadly: Jewish law forbids "ona'at devarim" (hurting through words). You can't lie to customers, misrepresent products, or take advantage of people's ignorance. Business must be honest.

Example 2: Treating Animals

The Torah says you must help an enemy's donkey that has fallen under its load (Exodus 23:5). This shows that even an enemy's animal matters—cruelty is forbidden. Many Jewish laws protect animal welfare: no causing unnecessary pain, animals must rest on Sabbath, feeding animals before yourself.

Example 3: Sabbath and Rest

Sabbath is not punishment or boring restriction—it's a gift. One day a week, no work, no commerce, no creation. Time with family, prayer, study, and rest. Even slaves rest on Sabbath. It's a law that protects the vulnerable by ensuring everyone, rich or poor, gets a day of rest.

Example 4: Helping the Poor

Jewish law requires leaving the corners of fields unharvested (pe'ah) so the poor can gather food. It requires lending money without interest to fellow Jews. Tithes go to support Levites and the poor. The system is designed so that poverty is minimized and dignity is preserved.

How Jewish Ethics Differ from "Just Following Rules"

You might think: "If there are 613 commandments, isn't Judaism just about following rules?" But that's a misunderstanding.

The Difference

Rules (without spirit): Do this because it's the law. Don't think; just obey. Fear punishment if you break them.

Jewish Law (with spirit): Each mitzvah teaches something about how to live righteously and sanctify the world. The law trains your character. Over time, following the law reshapes how you think and what you value. The goal is not obedience—it's transformation into a more ethical, holy person.

Example: The commandment to remember the Sabbath is not "do no work on Saturday." It's about learning what's important: time, relationships, rest, creation, and remembering that you're not meant to dominate everything. Following the law shapes your soul.

The rabbis debated for centuries what the laws mean. They didn't just follow blindly—they interpreted, questioned, and argued about the right application. This shows that Jewish ethics is a living tradition, constantly wrestling with how to live ethically in a changing world.

Discussion Questions

Choose at least TWO and write 3-4 sentences for each.

Question 1: Is a law that covers every part of life limiting or liberating?

The Torah has rules about food, clothing, business, and rest. Does knowing the "right way" to do everything feel freeing (because you know exactly what's expected) or oppressive (because you have no freedom)? What's your view?

Question 2: Is pikuach nefesh a good principle for ethics?

Saving a life overrides almost any other law. Does this mean morality is NOT absolute (relative)? Or does it mean the highest absolute principle is "preserve life"? How would you decide if two values conflict?

Question 3: Should tzedakah be a duty or a choice?

Jewish law makes giving to the poor a duty, not optional charity. Is that fair? Does it force people to give, or does it protect the dignity of poor people by making support a right, not a favour?

Question 4: How do the commandments about God relate to ethics?

The first four commandments are about God (monotheism, no idols, respect God's name, Sabbath). The last six are about people (family, life, marriage, property, honesty, contentment). Does loving God make you more ethical? Or are they separate?

The Big Question

"A moral life requires following a detailed set of rules, not just following your conscience."

Do you agree or disagree? Explore both sides.

Arguments FOR (Rules matter):

  • Conscience can be wrong. People's feelings lie to them or are influenced by bias and culture. Rules provide objective standards.
  • Without clear rules, people rationalize bad behaviour. "I felt like it was okay" is not a good ethical standard.
  • Rules protect people. Laws against theft, murder, and fraud keep society functioning and vulnerable people safe.
  • The Jewish example shows that a framework of law actually enables moral growth and sanctifies daily life.

Arguments AGAINST (Conscience matters more):

  • Rules can be oppressive or unjust. Slavery was legal. Apartheid was law. Conscience must sometimes say "this rule is wrong."
  • Rules are made by someone. Whose values do they express? Rules can be used to control people unjustly.
  • Rules can't cover every situation. You need wisdom and empathy to apply them rightly. The spirit of the law matters more than the letter.
  • Jewish ethics itself (pikuach nefesh) shows that sometimes you MUST break the rules to be ethical.
Your turn: Write 4-5 sentences explaining your view. Can you hold both ideas in tension? (Some rules necessary, but conscience and wisdom also necessary?)
