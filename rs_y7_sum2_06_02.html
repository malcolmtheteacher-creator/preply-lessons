For Jews, Torah and Halakhah (Jewish law) are exactly this—a comprehensive guide to living. But it's not seen as oppressive. Let's explore why.

Key Terms: Jewish Law and Practice

Torah: The first five books of the Hebrew Bible; also called the Pentateuch; contains the Law given by God to Moses
Halakhah: Jewish law; the binding rules and practices that guide how Jews live
Mitzvot (plural of Mitzvah): A commandment or religious obligation; often refers to the 613 commandments in the Torah
Pikuach Nefesh: The principle that saving a life overrides almost every other commandment in Jewish law
Tikkun Olam: Meaning "repairing the world"; the Jewish obligation to work for justice and improve society
Tzedakah: Charitable giving; but more than charity—it's a religious duty and obligation to those in need
Bal Tashchit: The commandment not to waste or destroy unnecessarily; environmental stewardship

The Ten Commandments (Exodus 20)

Commandments 1–4: Duties to God

1. No other gods before God - Monotheism is fundamental. God alone is the focus of worship and devotion.
2. No idols - Do not make or worship carved images. God cannot be represented by physical objects.
3. Do not take God's name in vain - Treat God's name with utmost respect. Do not swear falsely or misuse it.
4. Remember the Sabbath - Set apart the seventh day as holy. Rest and refrain from work to honour God's creation.

Commandments 5–10: Duties to Others

5. Honour your father and mother - Respect and care for parents. This extends to all elders.
6. Do not murder - Protect human life; it is sacred.
7. Do not commit adultery - Remain faithful in marriage and respect the sanctity of families.
8. Do not steal - Respect others' property and livelihood.
9. Do not bear false witness - Tell the truth, especially in legal proceedings. Honesty and integrity matter.
10. Do not covet - Do not desire what others have. Cultivate contentment and gratitude instead.

Why Two Categories?

The first four commandments focus on humanity's relationship with God (vertical). The final six focus on relationships between people (horizontal). Both are essential to Jewish ethics.

Beyond the Ten: The Larger Picture of Jewish Law

The Ten Commandments are the foundation, but Judaism teaches that there are 613 commandments (mitzvot) in total. These cover:

Dietary Laws (Kashrut)

What Jews can eat; animals, preparation methods, and combinations of foods are regulated.

Sabbath Observance

How to sanctify the seventh day—39 categories of work are forbidden from Friday evening to Saturday evening.

Prayer and Worship

Specific prayers, blessings, and services structure daily Jewish life.

Family Purity

Laws governing marital relations and purification rituals, rooted in respect for the body.

Justice and Courts

Laws for settling disputes, protecting the vulnerable, and maintaining social order.

Charity and Community

Obligations to help the poor, sick, and vulnerable; supporting communal institutions.

Key Ethical Principles in Judaism

Pikuach Nefesh (Saving a Life)

This is one of the most important principles in Jewish ethics. It states that saving a human life overrides almost every other commandment—even the most sacred laws like Sabbath observance.

Example:

If someone needs immediate medical help on the Sabbath, a Jew must break Sabbath laws to save their life. Healing and preserving life are considered holy acts that take precedence over other religious duties.

Tikkun Olam (Repairing the World)

Jews have a responsibility to work for justice and improve the world. This includes:

  • Advocating for the poor and oppressed
  • Working against injustice and inequality
  • Protecting the environment
  • Building a more just society

Modern Example:

A Jew might volunteer at a homeless shelter, work for environmental protection, or campaign against discrimination—all as expressions of Tikkun Olam.

Tzedakah (Charitable Obligation)

Often translated as "charity," but tzedakah is not optional generosity—it's a binding obligation. The word comes from "tzedek" (justice), meaning it's about ensuring justice by supporting those in need.

Levels of Tzedakah:

  • Highest: Help someone become self-sufficient so they don't need charity
  • Give anonymously
  • Give to someone you know, who doesn't know you're giving
  • Give publicly to someone you know
  • Give before being asked
  • Give after being asked
  • Give grudgingly
  • Lowest: Give less than you should, with a sad face

Bal Tashchit (Do Not Waste)

This principle forbids unnecessary destruction or waste. It extends beyond physical objects to include:

  • Environmental conservation
  • Not wasting food, water, or natural resources
  • Not destroying property out of anger
  • Using resources wisely and sustainably

Are These Laws Oppressive or Liberating?

From the outside, 613 commandments might seem restrictive. But many Jews describe Halakhah differently:

A Jewish Perspective:

"The mitzvot give structure, meaning, and purpose to life. They connect us to God, to community, to history, and to ethical living. Rather than limiting freedom, they provide a framework within which we can thrive. Choosing to follow them is not burden—it's blessing."

The key difference is motivation: Jewish law is not about fear of punishment, but about:

  • Love of God and commitment to relationship
  • Belonging to a community with shared values
  • Creating a life of meaning and purpose
  • Improving yourself and the world

Applying Jewish Ethics to Modern Situations

Scenario 1: Sabbath vs. Emergency

The Situation:

Your neighbour has collapsed and needs to be taken to hospital. It's the Sabbath. Jewish law forbids driving on the Sabbath, but Pikuach Nefesh says saving a life overrides this.

The Ethical Principle:

Pikuach Nefesh wins. Drive them to hospital. You are not just permitted—you are obligated—to break Sabbath laws to save a life.

Scenario 2: Tzedakah and Personal Need

The Situation:

You have limited money. You need new shoes, but someone asks for help paying their utility bill.

The Ethical Challenge:

Tzedakah is an obligation, but Judaism also teaches you must care for yourself (you cannot help others if you cannot survive). The ethical path involves balancing personal need with community responsibility—and often sharing more than feels comfortable.

Scenario 3: Tikkun Olam in Action

The Situation:

Your school's uniform supplier uses child labour. No one else seems to care. What should you do?

The Ethical Principle:

Tikkun Olam. You have a responsibility to advocate for justice, even if it's uncomfortable. This might mean raising concerns with school leadership, researching ethical suppliers, or joining campaigns for fair labour practices.

Scenario 4: Bal Tashchit and Consumption

The Situation:

Your parents want to throw away a coat because it's "out of fashion," though it's still wearable. You know a younger sibling of a friend who needs warm clothing.

The Ethical Principle:

Bal Tashchit. Don't waste. Give the coat to someone who needs it. Extend its useful life. Treat resources as sacred, not disposable.

Comparing Jewish Ethics with Other Traditions

Commandment vs. Principle

Different traditions have different approaches to ethics:

Judaism: Law and Relationship

Ethics are expressed through commandments (mitzvot) within a legal framework. Following these laws is an act of covenant—a binding relationship with God and community.

Christianity: Love and Grace

While Christianity inherits the Ten Commandments, the emphasis shifts to love: "Love God with all your heart, and love your neighbour as yourself." Grace (unearned forgiveness) is central, not just rule-keeping.

Islam: Submission and Sharia

Islamic law (Sharia) is similarly comprehensive, covering all aspects of life. Like Halakhah, it's seen not as restriction but as guidance toward living in harmony with God's will (submission = Islam).

Buddhism: The Noble Eightfold Path

Rather than divine commandments, Buddhism teaches ethical principles (the Eightfold Path) aimed at reducing suffering and achieving enlightenment. Ethics are about training the mind.

Humanism: Reason and Compassion

Humanists base ethics on reason, empathy, and concern for human wellbeing—not on religious law or divine command. Ethics are about creating a just society based on shared human values.

Key Differences

Source of Authority

Judaism: Divine revelation (Torah) and ongoing interpretation (Talmud, Halakhah)
Christianity: Scripture + teachings of Jesus + Church tradition
Islam: Quran + Hadith (sayings of Prophet Muhammad)
Buddhism: Buddha's teachings (Dharma); no supreme creator
Humanism: Human reason and shared values

Purpose of Ethics

Judaism: Fulfil covenant with God; sanctify life; repair the world
Christianity: Express love of God and neighbour; receive salvation through grace
Islam: Submit to God's will and create a just society
Buddhism: End suffering; achieve enlightenment
Humanism: Create a just, flourishing society for all

What About Disagreement?

Jewish Interpretation and Debate

Judaism is not monolithic. Different branches (Orthodox, Conservative, Reform) interpret Halakhah differently. Key questions include:

  • How strictly should ancient laws be observed in modern contexts?
  • Can ethical principles (like equality) override traditional practices?
  • What role does individual conscience play in following law?

Example: Women's Roles

Orthodox Judaism traditionally restricts certain roles to men, seeing this as part of divine law. Conservative and Reform Judaism have reinterpreted these laws to allow women equal participation in all aspects of religious life, prioritising the ethical principle of equality.

Summary and Key Takeaways

The Ten Commandments

Ten core principles revealed by God to Moses. The first four focus on duty to God (monotheism, no idols, respect God's name, Sabbath). The final six focus on duty to others (honour parents, no murder, no adultery, no theft, no false witness, no coveting).

Halakhah and the 613 Mitzvot

Jewish law governs every aspect of life: diet, prayer, family, business, justice, charity. These aren't seen as oppressive restrictions but as a framework for meaningful, purposeful living connected to God and community.

Pikuach Nefesh

Saving a human life overrides almost every other commandment. Human life is sacred, and preserving it is a supreme value in Judaism.

Tikkun Olam

The Jewish obligation to repair the world through justice, advocacy, and working to improve society. It's not optional—it's a core religious duty.

Tzedakah

Charitable giving, but more than charity—it's an obligation rooted in justice. Supporting the vulnerable and ensuring dignity for all is a fundamental requirement.

Bal Tashchit

Do not waste. Resources—whether food, water, or materials—should be treated with respect and used sustainably. This is both an ethical and environmental principle.

Final Reflection

Jewish ethics demonstrate that "following rules" is not the same as "living ethically." The mitzvot are a framework for a life of meaning, connection, and purpose. The principles you've explored—Pikuach Nefesh, Tikkun Olam, Tzedakah, and Bal Tashchit—show that Jewish law is ultimately about:

  • Honouring the sacred in all life
  • Caring for community and the vulnerable
  • Working for justice and a better world
  • Using our resources and choices wisely

Check Your Understanding

1. What are the Ten Commandments, and how are they divided?
Two sections: 1–4 are duties to God (monotheism, no idols, respect God's name, Sabbath); 5–10 are duties to others (honour parents, no murder, no adultery, no theft, no false witness, no coveting).
2. What is Halakhah, and why is it important in Judaism?
Halakhah is Jewish law—the binding rules and practices that guide daily Jewish life. It's important because it provides structure, meaning, and purpose; it connects Jews to God, community, and history; and it's a framework for ethical living.
3. What does Pikuach Nefesh mean, and why is it significant?
Pikuach Nefesh means that saving a human life overrides almost every other commandment. It's significant because it shows that preserving life is the highest Jewish value—even higher than ritual observance.
4. How is Tzedakah different from ordinary charity?
Tzedakah is not optional generosity but a binding obligation rooted in justice (tzedek). It's a religious duty to ensure the dignity and survival of those in need, not just a voluntary act of kindness.
5. What is Tikkun Olam, and how might a young person practice it?
Tikkun Olam means "repairing the world"—the obligation to work for justice and improve society. A young person might practice it by volunteering, advocating against injustice, protecting the environment, or supporting community causes.
