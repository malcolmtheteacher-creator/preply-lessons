The Café Poster

Your photograph is in a shop window, blown up to A2, advertising somebody else's winter menu. Nobody asked you. Now you have to walk in and say so — over a flat white, to a neighbour you rather like.

The Scene 🎬

You stop outside the café at the end of the road, because there's a poster in the window and something about it is very familiar. Frost on the grass. The bench by the war memorial. That flat white winter light you got up at half six to catch, back in January, and then posted on the village Facebook page because someone asked what the park looked like in the snow.

It's your photo. Two feet tall, in a window, with WINTER MENU — SOUPS FROM £4.50 across the bottom of it in a font you would not have chosen.

You're not a professional, exactly. You've sold a few. And you like Elena — everyone likes Elena, she runs the place more or less single-handed and remembers what everyone drinks. So this is not a row you want. It's just that you took that photo, nobody asked you, and if you say nothing now you'll be thinking about it every time you walk past for the next six months.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Has anyone ever used something of yours — a photo, an idea, a recipe, a bit of work — without asking? What did you do, and would you do the same again?
  2. When you put something online, who do you think owns it after that? Be honest about what you actually believe, not what the rules say.

Who's who

You
The One Who Took It 📷

You want your name on it, you'd like something for it, and you want to still be able to buy a coffee here on Thursday. All three, ideally, and in that order.

The other voice
Elena, from the Café ☕

Runs the café at the end of the road, and your photograph is on every poster in the window. She didn't take it off anyone — her nephew made the posters, and neither of them thought about it for a second. She'll be horrified. That's its own kind of problem.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how the claim gets made before the apology arrives — and how, once Elena starts panicking, the speaker keeps taking the temperature down without ever letting go of what they came in for. Warmth and firmness at the same time. That's the skill.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the moments where it would have been easiest to say "oh, never mind, forget it" — and notice that nobody does.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The speaker proves the photo is theirs without showing Elena anything on a phone. How?

2. Elena tries to solve the problem instantly, twice — first by feeling terrible, then by going to fetch a chair. Why does the speaker stop her the second time, and what do they ask instead?

3. The speaker names a hundred and settles for fifty. Given what they'd already said about what actually mattered to them, was the hundred a bluff?

1. They give a detail only the person who was standing there would carry around: the corner of the bin in the bottom left, and being irritated by it at the time. It isn't proof exactly, but it's the kind of thing nobody invents, so Elena believes it immediately.
2. If the poster comes down that minute, the conversation is over and the speaker still has nothing — no name, no money, and the photo is still on the website and the cards and possibly Instagram. So they slow her down and ask where else it has been used, because you can't ask for anything until you know the size of what you're asking about.
3. Not really. A hundred is what the work is worth, and saying it out loud sets the scale, so that fifty sounds like a discount rather than a cheek. Since they'd already said the money mattered less than being asked, they had room to come down — and they spent that room on getting their name on the website instead.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for saying "that's mine" to someone you'd rather not fall out with. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Everything here depends on being mild and immovable at the same time, which is mostly a matter of speed. If you rush, you sound like you've been rehearsing it in the street outside, and Elena will hear an accusation. If you slow down and let the sentences be a bit ordinary — the same voice you'd use to mention that the milk's off — the words do the work and the tone stays friendly. Firm is not the same as loud, and it is certainly not the same as fast.

👀 Naming it

Phrase 1

You're about to tell someone they've taken something of yours, and you'd like them to be able to hear it. Which opener gets the fact across while leaving them somewhere to stand?

A. "You've used my photo without asking."
B. "I think that might be my photo you've got on the poster."
C. "Where did you get that picture from, out of interest?"
B — "I think that might be my photo you've got on the poster."
A is true but it arrives as a charge, so she'll defend herself before she's even understood you. C is a trap dressed up as a question, and she'll feel it. B says the whole thing — it's mine, it's on your poster — while "I think" and "might be" leave her room to reply without either of you having started an argument. The hedges aren't weakness here; they're what makes it possible to say at all.
Phrase 2

She's not sure you're right, and you need to settle it with the one detail only the photographer would have. Put the pieces in order:

that frosty morningI'm fairly sureit's the one fromin January.it's mine —
"I'm fairly sure it's mine — it's the one from that frosty morning in January."
"Fairly sure" is doing something clever: it sounds modest, but it's followed immediately by a memory so specific that nobody could have it second-hand. You claim quietly and then prove it by knowing when you were standing there — which is far more convincing than insisting.

🌡️ Taking the temperature down

Phrase 3

Her face has fallen. She thinks she's about to be shouted at, or sued, and in about four seconds she'll be too flustered to have a useful conversation. Which line stops that and keeps your claim alive?

A. "Honestly, it doesn't matter, forget I said anything."
B. "I'm not cross about it, before you panic."
C. "Well, you should have asked, shouldn't you."
B — "I'm not cross about it, before you panic."
A throws away the thing you came in for, which is the most common way this conversation fails. C is a telling-off. B separates two things people always weld together — being annoyed, and wanting something sorted — so she can hear that you're not angry without hearing that you've dropped it. "Before you panic" is light, almost teasing, and it tells her what's coming next isn't a fight.
Phrase 4

You want her to know that the whole thing was avoidable, and that you'd have been pleased to be asked — said ruefully, not as a rebuke. Put the pieces in order:

the funny thing.I'd have said yesthat'sif you'd asked,
"I'd have said yes if you'd asked, that's the funny thing."
The third conditional does the work: it puts you both in the version of events where she asked and you were delighted, which is a much warmer room to stand in than this one. "That's the funny thing" means "that's the odd part of it" rather than anything actually amusing, and it keeps the whole sentence rueful instead of accusing.
Phrase 5

She's assuming you're here about cash. You're not, mainly — you're here because a thing you made got used and nobody came to you first. Complete it: "It's less about the money and more about ___ ___."

"It's less about the money and more about being asked."
The passive is the point. "More about asking" would put the work on you; "being asked" names the thing that should have happened to you and didn't. It's a useful shape for any complaint where the offence isn't really the loss — less about the plate, more about being told; less about the hour, more about being kept waiting.

🔍 Finding out how big it is

Phrase 6

Before you can ask for anything, you need to know what you're asking about — a poster is one thing, a poster plus a website plus a year of Instagram is another. Put the pieces in order:

or the websiteWhere elseas well?is it just the poster,has it gone —
"Where else has it gone — is it just the poster, or the website as well?"
"Where else has it gone" assumes there's more, so she doesn't have to confess to anything; she just fills in the list. And offering her two options is what makes it easy to answer — an open "where have you used it?" gets a shrug, while "the poster, or the website as well?" gets you the loyalty cards and the Instagram too.

💷 Asking for something

Phrase 7

Time to say what you want. She has to feel there's a real choice here, and she has to be able to see which one is less trouble for her. Which line does both?

A. "Two options, really: you credit me and pay something, or it comes down."
B. "I'd like some money for it, please, and my name on it."
C. "Take it down or I'll have to speak to someone about it."
A — "Two options, really: you credit me and pay something, or it comes down."
C is a threat, and threats make people dig in. B is a request, which she can simply decline. A hands her a choice where both branches are acceptable to you, and where the one you want happens to be the easier one for her — the poster is already printed and in the window. "Really" softens the whole thing into something you're thinking aloud rather than a demand you arrived with.
Phrase 8

Say it yourself, out loud, before you look. You need to name your price — and it has to sound like the price, the one you'd quote anybody, not a number you've just made up while standing at the counter. How would you say it?

e.g. "I'd normally charge a hundred for something like that."
Two things are carrying it. "I'd normally" turns a figure into your standard practice, so she's not being singled out, she's just being told the rate. And "for something like that" makes it a category rather than a personal transaction — this is what a photo of that sort costs. Say the number and then stop talking. The urge to explain, apologise for it, or start bargaining with yourself is very strong and it will cost you the fifty.
Phrase 9

She's gone quiet at the hundred, and you can see the café's books in her face. You'd rather have this settled today than be right. Which counter-offer is most likely to get a yes you can both live with?

A. "Just give me whatever you think is fair."
B. "Could we say fifty and my name on the website?"
C. "Fine, don't worry about the money, just credit me."
B — "Could we say fifty and my name on the website?"
A hands her a decision she doesn't want and can't get right. C gives away half of what you came for the moment she looks uncomfortable, which is generous but leaves you resentful by Thursday. B moves on the number and holds the credit — you drop the part you already said mattered less, and keep the part you said mattered more. "Could we say" is the ordinary British way of proposing a figure: it sounds like arriving somewhere together rather than one of you winning.

🤝 Leaving the door open

Phrase 10

It's sorted, she's mortified, and you'd like her to feel able to look at you in the queue next week — and to actually come to you next time instead of quietly avoiding it. Put the pieces in order:

just sendand it's yours.me a messageNext time
"Next time just send me a message and it's yours."
"Next time" quietly settles that there will be one, which is the kindest thing in the sentence. "And it's yours" gives the yes in advance, so the only thing you're actually asking her to do is ask — which is exactly what the whole conversation has been about, and now she knows it costs her nothing.

🔑 Make them yours

These work anywhere somebody has helped themselves to something of yours and probably didn't mean any harm by it — a colleague, a neighbour, a cousin with a printer. Pin three of them to your own life so you'll reach for them when it happens. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I think that might be my ___ you've got on the ___." — something of yours that someone else is using, or has used. What is it, and who has it?

"It's less about the money and more about being asked." — a time you minded far less about the loss than about the way it happened. What was really bothering you?

"I'd normally charge a hundred for something like that." — what do you do that you undercharge for, or do free because asking felt awkward? Name the real rate.

Your Turn 🎭

Now you're the one at the counter with a coffee going cold. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer — in a real café you get about four minutes before someone queues up behind you.

The Twist

😬 There is no fifty

Run it once more, no script. This time Elena doesn't argue and doesn't apologise her way out of it — she just opens the till, turns it slightly towards you, and says the honest thing: she can't pay you fifty. She can't pay you twenty. The rent went up in March, she's had one member of staff since Easter, and if you want the poster down she'll take it down, tonight, and eat the £300 the print run cost her. What she can do is your name on everything, and coffee for a year, and she'll say your name to every person who asks about that photo. And she means every word of it. Start the timer. You cannot have the fifty — that option is gone. Decide, out loud, in front of her: what do you take? One rule: you have to actually say yes to something. "Never mind, forget it" is not an answer, and you're not allowed to leave without settling it.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said it was mine in the first minute — I didn't hint at it and hope she'd guess.
  • When she panicked, I calmed her down without quietly dropping what I came in for.
  • I found out how far the photo had spread before I agreed to anything.
  • I named a real number out loud and didn't start apologising for it.
  • I got something — money, credit, or both — and I can still walk in there on Thursday.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Nobody in this story is a thief. A nephew with a laptop found a nice picture of the park where a nice picture of the park was, and put it on a poster, and it did not occur to anyone that the picture had a person attached to it. That's most of what happens now — not theft, just a sort of enormous absent-mindedness.

The awkward part is that the person attached to it has to say so, out loud, to a neighbour, over a coffee. Which is a much harder thing than being angry on the internet, and much rarer.

Fifty quid isn't the point and never was. The point is that somebody now knows the photo was taken by someone who got up at half six in January, and next time they'll ask. That's a small repair, and small repairs are more or less all anybody gets.

Two bigger questions

  1. When something you made is online and easy to copy, does asking permission still mean anything — or is it just good manners that the technology has quietly made optional? Which is it in your own behaviour?
  2. People who make things are told constantly that exposure, credit and goodwill are worth having. When is that true, and when is it what you're offered instead of money? How would you tell the difference standing at a counter?
