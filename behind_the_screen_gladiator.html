BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Gladiator

A betrayed general fights his way back from slavery to face the emperor who destroyed his family. Almost none of it happened — but the emperor really was that mad.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Gladiator — a general betrayed by the emperor he served, and his long road back through slavery and the arena to revenge — discover how much of ancient Rome's maddest true story the film left out, and master the way English guesses at the past: must have, might have and can't have + past participle.
Before we begin — talk it through: If someone in power destroyed everything you loved, would you want personal revenge, or would you rather see them face justice through a proper system? And do you think ordinary people are ever treated as pure entertainment by those with power?

The film, in three parts

Ridley Scott's Gladiator (2000). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A general betrayed

Maximus Decimus Meridius is Rome's most trusted general, fresh from a brutal victory in Germania, and the ageing emperor Marcus Aurelius tells him something extraordinary: rather than pass power to his own son Commodus, he wants Maximus to become a temporary regent who will hand power back to the Senate and the people of Rome. Commodus, listening at the door, is devastated at being passed over. That same night, he murders his father with his own hands and seizes the throne.

Commodus orders Maximus and his family executed to remove any threat. Maximus escapes his own execution and rides desperately for home — but arrives too late. His wife and young son have already been murdered, their bodies left hanging. Grief-stricken and gravely wounded, Maximus collapses and is found by slave traders, who sell him into slavery in the North African provinces.

There, under a shrewd gladiator trainer named Proximo, Maximus is forced to fight for his life in provincial arenas. He refuses to give his name, fighting only as "the Spaniard," and wins bout after bout with a controlled, terrifying skill — even as something in him has gone cold and hollow, driven only by the promise of finding the men who killed his family.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Marcus Aurelius ask Maximus to do, and how does Commodus respond?

2. What happens to Maximus's family, and how does he end up a gladiator?

1. Marcus Aurelius asks Maximus to become regent and eventually return power to the Senate instead of Commodus; Commodus murders his own father that night and takes the throne.

2. Commodus has them executed; Maximus escapes but arrives home to find his wife and son already killed. Badly wounded, he is captured and sold into slavery, where he becomes a gladiator.

Discuss:
  • Marcus Aurelius chose "the right man" over his own son to rule Rome. Was that fair to Commodus — or was it exactly what a good ruler should do?

Part 2 — The Spaniard comes to Rome

Proximo brings his gladiators to Rome to fight in the great games Commodus is staging to celebrate his own reign — spectacles designed to win the love of a restless crowd. Fighting in the Colosseum itself, Maximus and his fellow gladiators are meant to lose to re-enact a famous Roman battle. Instead, Maximus organises them into a fighting unit and wins, delighting the crowd and infuriating Commodus, who did not expect the underdogs to survive.

Commodus descends to congratulate the unknown champion and asks his name. Maximus removes his helmet and delivers the line that defines the film: he is Maximus Decimus Meridius, loyal servant of the true emperor, father to a murdered son and husband to a murdered wife — and he will have his vengeance, in this life or the next. The crowd, sensing a hero, begins to chant for his life to be spared. Trapped by his own need for their approval, Commodus cannot have him killed on the spot.

Maximus is drawn into a dangerous alliance: Lucilla, Commodus's sister, and Senator Gracchus both want Commodus removed and the Senate's power restored, in keeping with what Marcus Aurelius had wanted. Maximus, still a slave in the emperor's own arena, agrees to help — while Commodus, growing ever more paranoid, begins to suspect exactly who "the Spaniard" really is.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Maximus win over the Colosseum crowd, and how does he reveal himself to Commodus?

2. Why can Commodus not simply have Maximus killed after this?

1. He organises his fellow gladiators to win a fight they were meant to lose; he then takes off his helmet and announces his real name and his intention to have vengeance.

2. The crowd, thrilled by Maximus, chants for his life to be spared, and Commodus depends on the crowd's love too much to defy them.

Discuss:
  • Commodus rules through spectacle and crowd approval rather than law. How much power should "public opinion" really have over a ruler's decisions — then, or now?

Part 3 — The final duel

Commodus, unwilling to risk losing to Maximus in fair combat and desperate to end the threat before it costs him his throne, has Maximus stabbed and his lung punctured just before their duel is due to begin — then has the wound tightly bound so it will not show. He intends to fight a dying man in front of the entire city and be seen to win honestly.

In the arena, gravely wounded and fighting through the pain, Maximus finds a strength that outlasts Commodus's cowardice. He disarms and kills the emperor before collapsing himself. With his last breaths, he asks that the political prisoners be freed, that his fellow gladiators be given their liberty, and that Senator Gracchus be allowed to restore the Senate's old power — everything Marcus Aurelius had once wanted. Maximus dies in the sand of the Colosseum, and is carried out by the crowd not as a slave, but as a hero returning home to the family that waits for him beyond death.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Commodus do before the final fight, and why?

2. What does Maximus ask for with his dying breaths?

1. He secretly stabs Maximus and has the wound hidden, so he can fight and defeat an already-dying man while appearing to win honestly.

2. He asks for the political prisoners to be freed, his fellow gladiators to be freed, and for Senator Gracchus to restore power to the Senate.

Discuss:
  • Maximus gets his revenge but dies getting it. Is that a victory, a tragedy, or both? Would the ending feel different if he had lived?

Grammar — modals of deduction about the past

Gladiator is the perfect film for this: almost nothing in it is provably true, so we're constantly guessing at what could, might or must have really happened. English has a precise toolkit for exactly that kind of guessing about the past.

The three structures — modal + have + past participle:
• must have + past participle — a confident, logical conclusion: "Commodus must have felt deeply threatened by Maximus's popularity." (the evidence makes it almost certain)
• might/could have + past participle — a possibility, not a certainty: "Maximus might have survived if the wound had been treated." (it's possible, but we don't know)
• can't have + past participle — near-certainty that something did NOT happen: "A real gladiator called Maximus can't have existed exactly as the film shows him — no historian records him."
Feel the difference:
• must have = "I'm almost sure this happened" (strong positive deduction).
• might/could have = "it's possible, but I'm not sure" (open possibility).
• can't have = "I'm almost sure this did NOT happen" (strong negative deduction).

The Real Story — myth vs history

This is the heart of the lesson. Gladiator feels like history, but its hero is invented from top to bottom — while the emperor everyone assumes is exaggerated for drama turns out to be, if anything, even stranger than the film shows.

⚔️ Maximus never existed

There was no Roman general called Maximus Decimus Meridius, no secret plan by Marcus Aurelius to make him regent, and no gladiator who killed an emperor in the Colosseum. Maximus is a fictional character, most likely stitched together from fragments of several real figures: Narcissus, the wrestler who really did kill Commodus (see below); Tiberius Claudius Pompeianus, a general of humble background who was a trusted son-in-law of Marcus Aurelius; and Tarruttienus Paternus, a real Roman commander from the same wars in Germania the film opens with. None of them lived Maximus's story.

Marcus Aurelius was real — but he never planned to restore the Republic

Marcus Aurelius (reigned AD 161–180) really was one of Rome's most respected emperors, and really was a serious Stoic philosopher: his private notebook of reflections, written mostly on military campaign and never intended for publication, survives today as the Meditations, still read for its advice on duty, mortality and self-control. He really did die on campaign in AD 180, near modern Vienna — but of illness, quietly, not murdered by his son.

The film's whole plot rests on one invention: that Marcus Aurelius wanted to abolish the succession and hand Rome back to the Senate as a republic. There is no evidence for this anywhere, in the Meditations or elsewhere — the Republic had ended two centuries earlier, and nothing suggests Marcus Aurelius wanted it back. In fact, he had already made Commodus his co-emperor three years before his death. It's a powerful dramatic idea, but a historical invention.

Commodus was real — and arguably madder than the film dares show

Commodus really did become sole emperor in AD 180 and really did rule for about twelve years — not the short, feverish reign the film implies. He really did fight in the Colosseum as a gladiator, though far more safely than any professional: he speared animals from a raised platform and fought wounded or disarmed opponents from the crowd, always guaranteed to win. His self-belief curdled into open megalomania — he declared himself the reincarnation of Hercules, and at the height of his delusion renamed the twelve months of the year, the city of Rome itself, and even the Senate and the Roman people, all after his own names and titles.

He was not killed in the arena. On 31 December AD 192, after twelve years of increasingly erratic rule, a conspiracy involving his mistress Marcia and the Praetorian prefect Laetus tried first to poison him; when that failed, his own wrestling trainer, a man named Narcissus, strangled him in his bath. No crowd, no duel, no dramatic last words — just a quiet, brutal assassination behind palace walls.

Gladiators, slavery and the arena

The film gets the broad picture right: most gladiators were slaves, prisoners of war or condemned criminals, forced to fight for others' entertainment, though free volunteers also existed and made up a real share of the profession. What the film exaggerates is the killing: trained gladiators were valuable investments, and most professional bouts did not end in death — a defeated fighter who had fought well was often spared to fight again. It was the unarmed condemned criminals thrown into the arena for public executions, not the trained gladiators, who were never expected to survive.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, which parts did you assume were historical fact — and which turn out to be invented for drama?
  • Does it change how you feel about the film to learn the "villain" was, if anything, stranger and more excessive than shown — but the "hero" never existed at all?
  • Why do you think filmmakers so often invent a fictional hero rather than tell the story of a real historical figure? Does it matter, for a story like this?
