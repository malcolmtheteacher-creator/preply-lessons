II. The Story — Fear and Hobbes Were Born Twins

Thomas Hobbes was born on April 5, 1588. His mother, terrified by news of the Spanish Armada approaching England, went into premature labour. Hobbes would later write: Fear and I were born twins. This is not merely poetic. Fear shaped every moment of his life and thought. He lived through the English Civil War, fled his homeland in terror, and wrote a political philosophy so dark that even contemporary monarchs found it excessive.

A Life Lived in Motion

Hobbes spent his early adulthood in service to the Earl of Devonshire, where he enjoyed access to books and learning. He was a polyglot: Greek, Latin, French, Italian. But in 1642, everything changed. England tore itself apart. The English Civil War (1642–1651) was not a distant military campaign. It was neighbour against neighbour. Hobbes, a royalist, was terrified not just for his life but for civilization itself. In 1640, he fled to France. He would spend eleven years in exile, watching his country destroy itself.

In Paris, Hobbes moved among the greatest minds of the age. He knew Galileo and Descartes. He studied mathematics, seeking the rational order beneath chaos. But his mind was consumed with one question: What makes humans willing to slaughter each other? And what could possibly force them into peace?

The State of Nature

After Cromwell established military rule and a kind of peace returned, Hobbes came home. He was in his sixties now. He wrote Leviathan, published in 1651. It is the most terrifying vision of human nature ever constructed.

Whatsoever therefore is consequent to a time of war, where every man is enemy to every man; the same is consequent to the time, wherein men live without other security, than what their own strength, and their own invention shall furnish them withal. In such condition, there is no place for industry; because the fruit thereof is uncertain: and consequently no culture of the earth; no navigation, nor use of the commodities that may be imported by sea; no commodious building; no arts; no letters; no society; and which is worst of all, continual fear, and danger of violent death; and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short. — Hobbes, Leviathan, Part 1, Chapter 13

This is Hobbes' vision of the state of nature. Not a few bad people creating danger, but all people, fundamentally at war with one another. Life without government is war. War without end. War without mercy.

The Equality of Fear

Why is this inevitable? Hobbes claims something more troubling than that humans are naturally evil: humans are naturally equal in their capacity to kill each other. A strong man can be poisoned in his sleep. A clever weak man can outwit a fool. Everyone is vulnerable to everyone else. In this condition, there is no security. Without security, there is no property, no law, no morality. Only fear. Only the pursuit of safety.

Is Hobbes right? Examine the collapse of state authority in modern times. Somalia after government dissolved. Syria during civil war. Libya after Gaddafi. When central power vanishes, something disturbingly like Hobbes' war emerges. Warlords replace law. Tribes replace citizenship. Violence becomes currency. Hobbes seems to have identified something true about human nature under extreme stress.

The Social Contract and the Sovereign

Given this nightmare, how can humans escape? Hobbes' answer is shocking: they must surrender their freedom entirely to a sovereign with unlimited power.

This is the generation of that great Leviathan, or rather, to speak more reverently, of that mortal god, to which we owe under the immortal God, our peace and defence. For by this authority, given him by every particular man in the commonwealth, he hath the use of so much power and strength conferred on him, that by terror thereof, he is enabled to form the wills of them all, to peace, at home, and mutual aid against their enemies abroad. — Hobbes, Leviathan, Part 2, Chapter 17

The sovereign is the Leviathan — a mortal god. Every individual contracts to surrender their natural right to all things to this one ruler. In exchange, the sovereign provides peace. The sovereign's power must be absolute and indivisible, or humans will question its legitimacy and fall back into war. Hobbes prefers monarchy because a single person has a personal interest in the kingdom's prosperity. A parliament is always divided.

Most shockingly, Hobbes allows no right of revolution. Once the covenant is made, it cannot be unmade. To resist the sovereign is to choose the state of nature again. And that is worse than any tyranny.

The Terrible Peace

What freedom remains? Hobbes is brutally honest:

Liberty, or freedom, signifieth, properly, the absence of opposition; and by opposition, I mean external impediments of motion; and may be applied no less to irrational, and inanimate creatures, than to rational. For whatsoever is so tied, or environed, as it cannot move, but within a certain space, which space is determined by the opposition of some external body, we say it hath not liberty to go further. — Hobbes, Leviathan, Part 2, Chapter 21

Freedom is simply the absence of external obstacle. You are free to do whatever the sovereign does not forbid. That is the only freedom available. But it is better than the freedom of the state of nature, which is the freedom to starve, to be murdered, to live in terror.

The Core of Hobbes' Vision

Fear and I were born twins. This is where Hobbes begins. All of his political philosophy flows from fear — fear of death, fear of others, fear of chaos. The sovereign rules through terror. Subjects obey through fear of anarchy. Peace is maintained by the equilibrium of fear. It is a system of genius and of absolute despair.

III. The Three Revolutionary Ideas

Hobbes shattered the medieval worldview with three radical claims.

Idea 1: The State of Nature Is War

Without government, humans naturally war against each other.

Medieval thinkers inherited from Aristotle and Augustine the idea that humans are naturally social. We have an inclination toward community, toward God. Hobbes turns this upside down. In nature, humans are solitary, seeking power at the expense of others. There is no natural sympathy. Community is imposed through fear. There is no natural order, only the order we artificially construct.

Idea 2: The Social Contract Is Absolute Surrender

We trade all freedom to a sovereign for peace and security.

Humans are not naturally social, so they must be forced into society. All humans contract to surrender their unlimited right to all things to a single sovereign. The sovereign is not bound by the contract — it is the enforcer. The contract is irrevocable. You cannot withdraw consent. This idea will echo through Enlightenment philosophy, but Hobbes uses it to justify absolute monarchy.

Idea 3: Absolute Sovereignty Cannot Be Divided

Any division of sovereignty leads back to war.

Hobbes is merciless toward constitutional limitations and checks. These things create endless disputes over who has real power. If subjects wonder whether they truly owe obedience to anyone, they will resist. And resistance begins the war of all against all. Only absolute, indivisible sovereignty maintains peace. A known tyrant is preferable to chaos.

The Unresolved Problem: Fear or Love?

Hobbes has constructed a logically rigorous system. But he leaves unanswered a question that will haunt all subsequent philosophy:

Think: Hobbes says the sovereign must rule through terror, and subjects must obey through fear. This maintains peace. But is it really peace? Or is it merely the absence of open violence?

Hobbes has built a society on fear alone. But human beings need more than mere survival. We need forgiveness. We need love. We need grace. In Hobbes' Leviathan, none of these exist. The sovereign cannot forgive, only punish. Subjects cannot trust, only obey. A human being formed by fear alone is not truly free. This is where Jesus enters the conversation. Where Hobbes says humans need fear to behave, Jesus says humans need love to flourish. Forgiveness becomes the key to liberation.

The Thesis Seed

Hobbes teaches that humans are naturally dangerous. Without absolute power and fear, we will war against each other. But this leaves the deepest question unanswered: What kind of person is created by a society built on fear alone? A being who obeys only from terror is not free. Jesus teaches forgiveness—the release from guilt and debt. In Jesus' vision, humans are freed not by fear but by love. By the cancellation of the debt that keeps us enslaved. Which foundation liberates the soul: fear or grace?

The Milesians asked what everything is made of. Hobbes asks what prevents humans from destroying each other. These seem like different questions. But they converge. Because how we should live depends on what we are made of, what we are capable of, what we truly need. The philosophers keep asking. Each answer opens a new question.
