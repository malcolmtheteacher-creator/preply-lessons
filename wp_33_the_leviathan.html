A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XXXIII of L

The Leviathan

Thomas Hobbes: The state of nature, the social contract, absolute sovereignty

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet a man who watched a kingdom tear itself apart. Thomas Hobbes saw the English Civil War and concluded that humans are naturally selfish and brutish. Without a strong authority, life is "nasty, brutish, and short." The solution is the Leviathan: an absolute sovereign to whom we surrender our natural freedom in exchange for safety. This is the dark side of the social contract. But it opens a question that will define modernity: what is the proper relationship between individual freedom and state power?

II. The Story — The War of All Against All

Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) lived through the English Civil War (1642–1651). He watched a kingdom tear itself apart. The result was simple: if we are not constrained by an absolute power, we destroy each other.

The State of Nature

Hobbes asked: What is humanity like without law, without government, without civilization? His answer:

In such condition, there is no place for Industry; because the fruit thereof is uncertain: and consequently no Culture of the Earth; no Navigation, nor use of the commodities that may be imported by Sea; no commodious Building... no Arts; no Letters; no Society; and which is worst of all, continual fear, and danger of violent death; And the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short. — Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, Part I, Chapter 13

In nature, Hobbes says, everyone has a right to everything. This produces perpetual conflict. Every person is at war with every other person. And the only way to escape this war is to surrender our natural freedom to an absolute sovereign.

The Social Contract

Humans make an agreement: we each renounce our natural right to do whatever we want. In exchange, we grant absolute power to a sovereign — a king, a parliament, a dictator. This sovereign enforces order through fear.

Hobbes's bargain: You lose freedom. But you gain safety. It is a trade, not a liberation. The Leviathan is not benevolent. It does not exist to help you flourish. It exists to prevent war. And that is enough.

The Sovereign

The Leviathan — Hobbes's image for the absolute state — rules through force. It cannot be judged by the citizens. It cannot be overthrown. It is absolute.

The Sovereign... is not subject to the Civil Law... The Sovereign cannot be punished by his own authority... no Subject can be free from the Sovereign Power. — Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan, Part II, Chapter 26
♦ ♦ ♦

The Horror and the Realism

Hobbes is terrifying. But he is honest about something: people will sacrifice freedom for safety. We do this all the time. We follow laws, submit to authority, constrain our desires. And we do this not out of moral duty but out of fear. Hobbes removed the moral language and showed the naked mechanism. Is he cynical? Or realistic?

III. The Ideas — Fear as Foundation

Idea 1: Natural Egoism

Humans are naturally selfish. We seek power, pleasure, and security for ourselves.

Hobbes did not invent this view. But he gave it the clearest voice. We are not naturally moral. We are naturally self-interested. Morality is a constraint we accept because we fear something worse. This contradicts Augustine (we are naturally broken but redeemable), and Jesus (the kingdom is within, a kingdom of love). Hobbes says the kingdom is nowhere. There is only appetite.

Idea 2: The Social Contract as Surrender

Government exists not to help us flourish but to prevent us from destroying each other.

Later social contract theorists (Locke, Rousseau) will argue that government protects natural rights. Hobbes says there are no natural rights. There is only natural power. And government exists to suppress it. This is a dark view. But it explains why people tolerate harsh rule.

Idea 3: Absolute Sovereignty

Power cannot be divided. The sovereign must be absolute, or the war returns.

If the sovereign can be questioned, challenged, resisted, then the social contract breaks. The Leviathan must be all-powerful, otherwise people will appeal to different authorities and the state of nature reemerges. This is Hobbes's case for absolute monarchy (or absolute government of any kind).

Place Hobbes's Dark Vision

The 3 Images for Location 33

1. A vast, swirling battlefield: soldiers against soldiers, no order, only chaos and blood — The state of nature. War of all against all. Nasty, brutish, short.

2. A great beast rising from the sea, made of thousands of tiny human bodies fused together, with the sovereign's face at the top — The Leviathan. The state as monstrous necessity.

3. Chains of gold binding all the people to the beast's feet: they are enslaved, but they chose this, because the alternative was worse — The social contract. Freedom surrendered for safety. The bargain Hobbes sees as the only rational choice.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 questions to reflect on.

Is Hobbes right about human nature?

Are we naturally selfish and brutal? Or is Hobbes projecting his experience of civil war onto universal human nature?

The price of safety

Would you trade your freedom for complete safety? Is this bargain worth it? Or is freedom more important than security?

Can the sovereign be resisted?

Hobbes says the sovereign is absolute. But what if the sovereign becomes tyrannical? Do we not have the right to rebel?

Jesus vs. Hobbes

Jesus taught that the kingdom is within, and it is a kingdom of love and forgiveness. Hobbes says there is no kingdom within, only appetite. Who understood humans better?

Hobbes without God

Medieval thinkers believed God guaranteed moral order. Hobbes removes God. What remains? Just power. Is this more honest, or more dangerous?

The Leviathan today

Which is more true to our world: Hobbes's vision of power, or the visions of philosophers who believe in freedom, reason, and human dignity?

VII. Final Retrieval

1. The State of Nature

How did Hobbes describe life without government?

"Solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." There is no culture, no arts, no learning. Only fear and violence. A war of all against all.

2. The Social Contract

What do humans do to escape the state of nature?

We surrender our natural rights and freedom to an absolute sovereign. In exchange, we gain safety. The sovereign enforces order through fear.
