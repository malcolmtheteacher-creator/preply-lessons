The Placebo Effect

The Lie That Heals: Cause, Effect, and What Counts as Real

💬 Cause & Effect, Logic, Definitions in Context

The Experiment

Start with a thought experiment.

A doctor gives you a pill and tells you it will cure your headache. The pill is made of sugar. Nothing else. But your headache goes away. Did the pill work?

Talk to your partner for 1–2 minutes. What would you say? Why?

Now imagine two scenarios:

Scenario A

The patient doesn't know it's a sugar pill. The doctor tells her it's real medicine.

Scenario B

The patient knows it's a sugar pill. The doctor is honest: "This is sugar, but studies show placebos work." The headache still goes away.

Which scenario presents a more interesting problem? In A, we might say it's "just psychology." In B, the lie is already revealed — yet it still works. Something deeper is happening.

Placebos work in 30–40% of cases, even when patients know it's a placebo. The effect is real. The question is: what counts as "real"?

Take a position: Should doctors be allowed to prescribe placebos if they work — even if it means bending the truth? Explain your reasoning.

You'll return to this question at the end.

Today's lesson explores the strange science of placebos — and practises four powerful phrases for discussing cause and effect, correlation and coincidence, logical arguments, and what counts as 'real' in different contexts.

❌ Vague

"It's just in their head. The effect isn't real because it's psychological."

Assumes psychology = not real; oversimplifies the debate

✓ Precise & sophisticated

Language for Cause & Effect

These four phrases help you discuss what causes what, distinguish correlation from causation, build logical arguments, and question definitions. Click each one to explore:

"The question isn't whether... it's whether..." Click to expand
MEANING

You reframe a false debate. There are two ways to view something; you dismiss the wrong framing and push toward the real issue.

FORM

The question isn't whether [false framing], it's whether [real issue].

PRONUNCIATION

Stress: "The QUEStion isn't WHETHER... it's WHETHer..." Pause between the two parts. Sounds like you're correcting a misunderstanding.

Example in context

"The question isn't whether the placebo effect is 'real' because the patient feels better. The question is whether the physical mechanism matters if the outcome is the same."

Now you try:

Think of a debate where people are arguing the wrong thing. Use this phrase to reframe it toward the real question.

"Correlation doesn't necessarily imply..." Click to expand
MEANING

Two things happen together, but that doesn't mean one caused the other. A sophisticated way to challenge false cause-and-effect thinking.

FORM

Correlation doesn't necessarily imply [assumed cause]. It could be [alternative explanation].

PRONUNCIATION
/ˌkɔr.əˈleɪ.ʃən/ — stress on second syllable. "Correlation doesn't NEcessarily imply..." Slight pause after "imply" before you offer the alternative.

Example in context

"The patient got better after taking the pill, but correlation doesn't necessarily imply the pill caused the improvement. The body heals itself; the patient's expectations might have reduced stress, which aided healing."

Now you try:

Find two things that often happen together but might not be causally linked. Use the phrase to separate correlation from cause.

"Even if we accept that..., it doesn't follow that..." Click to expand
MEANING

You accept one fact, but you show that a conclusion drawn from it is wrong. The ultimate phrase for logical argument: concede a point, then dismantle the conclusion.

FORM

Even if we accept that [fact], it doesn't follow that [false conclusion].

PRONUNCIATION

"Even if we ACcept that..., it DOESN'T follow that..." Stress on "accept" and "doesn't." Slight pause before "follow" — this is where the argument turns.

Example in context

"Even if we accept that the placebo effect is psychological, it doesn't follow that it's not real. Psychological changes are real changes — the body responds to expectation."

Now you try:

Grant something your partner might believe, then show why a conclusion they draw from it is wrong.

"What counts as [X] in this context?" Click to expand
MEANING

You question the definition itself. What seems like a concrete word depends on context. Definitions are negotiable — and that matters.

FORM

What counts as [thing] in this context? Is it [criterion 1] or [criterion 2] or something else?

PRONUNCIATION

"What COUNTS as...?" Emphasise "counts." Sounds like you're asking a genuine question — because you are. Rising intonation throughout.

Example in context

"What counts as a 'side effect' in this context? If the patient experiences the symptom because they expect it — even on a sugar pill — is it a real side effect or an artefact of expectation?"

Now you try:

Take a word that seems obvious — "success," "help," "real," "fair." Question what it counts as in a specific context you care about.

The Evidence

Read each research finding. Then use the target phrase to discuss it — don't just summarise, question what it tells us about cause, effect, and reality.

🧪 Open-Label Placebos: Harvard, 2014

Patients with irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) received sugar pills. But they were told the truth: "These are placebos — inactive pills with no direct medical effect." Despite full transparency, 60% reported significant symptom improvement. Their symptoms reduced. The placebo worked — even knowing it was a placebo.

Use "The question isn't whether... it's whether..." — The question isn't whether the improvement is real. So what is the real question here?

🏥 Placebo Surgery: Moseley 2002

Patients with osteoarthritis of the knee participated in a randomised trial: some received real arthroscopic surgery; others received sham surgery — the surgeon made cuts and went through the motions, but did nothing. For two years, both groups improved equally. Real surgery and fake surgery produced identical outcomes. The surgery wasn't what healed the knee — something else was.

Use "Even if we accept that..., it doesn't follow that..." — Even if we accept that surgery is more invasive than placebo, what doesn't follow about why surgery works?

💊 Pill Colour Matters

Red placebos work better as stimulants; blue placebos work better as sedatives. Bigger pills work better than smaller pills — even though size has no chemical effect. The colour and size trigger different expectations, which produce different physiological effects. Context, appearance, and ritual all shape the outcome. The placebo is working, but not through chemistry.

Use "Correlation doesn't necessarily imply..." — Red colour is correlated with stimulation effect. But what doesn't it necessarily imply about why the effect occurs?

😨 Nocebo Effect: Expectation Creates Harm

Patients told a drug has severe side effects are more likely to experience those side effects — even on sugar pills. Women told a fragrance would cause headaches reported headaches. Men told the same fragrance wouldn't cause headaches reported no headaches. The substance was identical. Expectation alone determined the outcome. Negative expectations create negative outcomes as powerfully as positive ones.

Use "What counts as [X] in this context?" — What counts as a "real" side effect when the body is producing a genuine symptom response to expectation?

Alternative Medicine

Sport & Performance

Athletes wear "lucky" items and often perform better. Is this placebo? Should coaches encourage it? Is a psychological edge a real advantage?

Try: "The question isn't whether the advantage is real, it's whether..." / "What counts as fair competition?"

Marketing & Perception

Expensive wine tastes "better" in brain scans than cheap wine with a fancy label swapped on. Brain activity is identical to real pleasure. Is the pleasure fake? If your brain registers pleasure, is it real?

Try: "The question isn't whether..., it's whether..." / "What counts as authentic pleasure?"
