Communicating Change

C2 Level · Organisational Communication

Why 70% of Change Initiatives Fail

The statistic is cited so often it's become cliché, but it reflects a genuine reality: most ambitious organisational changes don't achieve their stated objectives. The reasons are rarely technical. They're almost always human.

People resist change not because they're obstinate but because change threatens identity, competence, and security. A new system means mastering unfamiliar tools. A restructuring means uncertain career prospects. A cultural shift means questioning beliefs held for years. These are legitimate concerns. Leaders who dismiss them as "resistance to change" rather than addressing them directly virtually guarantee failure.

Kotter's Eight-Step Change Model emphasises that change requires: creating urgency (why now?), building a coalition (who supports this?), crafting a vision (where are we going?), communicating relentlessly, empowering action (removing barriers), celebrating progress (showing early wins), consolidating gains, and anchoring in culture (making change stick).

Communication is essential throughout. Yet many organisations communicate change once—the announcement—then assume implementation will follow. Effective change requires repeated, varied communication: from leadership, from peers, through story and data, acknowledging difficulty alongside necessity.

Consider: Recall a failed change initiative you've witnessed. Where did it break down? Where did communication fail? What would have needed to happen differently?

Change Communication Language & Strategy

The language you use shapes how people experience change. Some framing creates fear; other framing creates possibility.

Vocabulary Cards

Transformation Fundamental, systemic change; not incremental improvement but reimagining the organisation.
Restructuring Reorganisation of reporting lines, divisions, or functions; often perceived as threatening by those affected.
Redundancy Position elimination; the employment termination that results from restructuring. Communicate this clearly and compassionately.
Morale Emotional state and confidence of the workforce; change typically depresses morale before it rebuilds.
Transition The period between the old state and the new; acknowledging transition time legitimises difficulty and complexity.
Rollout Phased implementation; effective rollouts build confidence through early wins and iterative adjustment.
Cascade Communication flowing from leadership through organisational layers; cascading change vision ensures alignment.
Resistance Opposition to change; often a signal of unaddressed concerns rather than obstinacy.

Deliver an All-Hands Address: Company Restructuring

Scenario: Your company is undergoing significant restructuring involving 15% headcount reduction. Reporting lines are changing. Some divisions are consolidating. You must address the entire organisation at an all-hands meeting. Your challenge: acknowledge the difficulty and disruption whilst maintaining confidence in the future and demonstrating respect for those affected.

Your All-Hands Checklist

Open by acknowledging this is difficult news, not merely sharing new direction
Explain the business rationale clearly: why now? What market or financial pressures necessitate this?
Address the human impact directly: be clear about who is affected and when they'll learn
Communicate support: severance, outplacement, transition time, career development for those remaining
Articulate the future state: what will the organisation look like post-restructuring?
Explain how restructuring enables strategy: this isn't cost-cutting; it's strategic repositioning
Acknowledge uncertainty: you won't have all answers; commit to transparency as information becomes available
Invite questions and commit to follow-up conversations, especially with affected teams
10:00

Execution

Prepare a 5–7 minute address. The tone must balance honesty about difficulty with confidence in direction. You're not celebrating the restructuring, but you're demonstrating that leadership is thoughtful, has a plan, and respects the impact on people. Practise delivering this aloud; this is one of the most emotionally charged communications a leader makes.

Reflection: Empathy in Leadership & Transparency vs Protection

Change communication presents an ethical dilemma: transparency demands honesty about difficulty and disruption, but leaders also want to project confidence and calm. How do you balance these?

Ethical Questions: Is it protective or deceptive to downplay negative impacts? When is reassurance appropriate, and when is it dishonest? What do employees really need to hear versus what leadership is comfortable saying?

The most effective leaders are those who say: this change is necessary and difficult. I understand the impact on you. Here's what I don't yet know, and here's what I'm committed to doing. Intellectual honesty about trade-offs and challenges builds trust far more than false reassurance.

Empathy in leadership isn't about making pain go away; it's about acknowledging it's real and demonstrating you care about people's wellbeing throughout change. This might mean: slower rollout to allow training, generous transition support, career development for those displaced, or honest conversations about uncertainty.

The cruel paradox: leaders who communicate change poorly—downplaying difficulty, appearing disconnected from impact—often face greater backlash than those who are transparent about the human cost. Employees distrust spin; they respect honesty combined with action to minimise harm.
