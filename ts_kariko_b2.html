The Scientist Nobody Would Fund

She was demoted, rejected and told her idea had no future. Then her idea helped save the world — and she got a phone call from Stockholm.

Today: you'll follow the most improbable career in modern science — forty years from a teddy bear full of banknotes to the Nobel Prize — and you'll master the tenses that tell a career: she worked on it for decades; she has changed medicine forever.
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • How long would you keep working on an idea that nobody would pay for? What would make you stop?
  • What does a career "going well" actually mean — titles, money, or getting to do the work?
  • Who takes the bigger risk: the person who quits a bad system, or the person who stays inside it and endures?

1. DNA holds the instructions; a working copy carries them to the cell's factories. That messenger molecule is mRNA.
mRNA (messenger RNA) — the molecule that tells a cell which protein to build. Her forty-year obsession: if you could deliver your own message, you could make the body its own medicine factory.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — The teddy bear full of money

Katalin Karikó grew up in a small Hungarian town, in a two-room house without running water, the daughter of a butcher. She fell in love with biology, earned her doctorate, and went to work at the Biological Research Centre in Szeged — until 1985, when the underfunded lab let her go. She decided to try America. Hungary's currency rules allowed emigrants to take only a trivial sum abroad, so the family sold their car on the black market, exchanged the money for about nine hundred pounds sterling, and sewed the notes into their two-year-old daughter's teddy bear. She has told the story many times: everything they had crossed the border inside a child's toy.

Her obsession travelled with her. Messenger RNA — the molecule that carries DNA's instructions to a cell's protein factories — struck her as the most beautiful tool imaginable: if you could write your own message and deliver it, you could instruct the body to manufacture any protein you wanted. Any medicine. The body as its own factory. Almost nobody agreed. mRNA was notoriously unstable, absurd to work with, and worst of all it triggered violent inflammation when injected — the immune system attacked it as an invader. The field's verdict through the 1990s was settled: a dead end. DNA and gene therapy were the future; mRNA was a curiosity for the stubborn.

The system delivered its verdict on her accordingly. At the University of Pennsylvania, her grant applications were rejected one after another — she has said she never once won a major US federal grant for the mRNA work in those years. In 1995, instead of the expected promotion, the university demoted her off the professional track: no grants, no professorship, adjunct wages. In the same period, she has recounted, came a cancer scare and her husband stranded abroad by visa trouble. It was the moment careers end. She considered leaving; she stayed — for the simple, unreasonable reason that she believed the molecule worked and wanted to be at the bench.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the family reach America, and what does the detail tell you?

2. What was the field's verdict on mRNA — and the system's verdict on her?

1. They sold their car on the black market and smuggled the proceeds — about £900, everything they had — sewn inside their daughter's teddy bear. The stake was total; there was no plan B.

2. That mRNA was an unstable, inflammation-triggering dead end. Accordingly: rejected grants throughout, and in 1995 a demotion off the career track instead of promotion — the standard signal to quit. She stayed.

Discuss:
  • The demotion was the system working as designed: no results the system valued, no funding, no post. Where exactly is the design wrong — or is it?

Part 2 — The photocopier and the fix

The turning point arrived at a photocopier. In the late 1990s, queueing to copy journal articles, she fell into conversation with Drew Weissman, a newly arrived immunologist who had trained under Anthony Fauci and wanted a vaccine against HIV. She said — as she told it ever after — that she could make any mRNA; he studied the immune cells that mRNA enraged. Neither had money or attention to spare, which freed them to work on what mattered. Their question became the field's oldest objection: why does synthetic mRNA drive the immune system wild, when the body's own mRNA passes unmolested?

The answer, published in 2005, was almost insultingly elegant. Natural mRNA is not raw: its letters carry small chemical modifications, and those modifications are the password. Swap one letter — replace uridine with a naturally occurring variant, pseudouridine — and the immune system reads the molecule as "self" and lets it pass. Inflammation collapsed; protein production soared. The dead end had been a locked door, and they had found the key. The paper appeared — and the world shrugged. Little press, little funding, few citations at first. Karikó and Weissman patented the discovery and founded a small company; the university later sold the patent rights on terms that meant the discoverers never controlled their invention. For years, the key sat in the lock while almost nobody pushed the door.

Almost nobody — except two young companies betting everything on the molecule: Moderna in America, and BioNTech in Germany, founded by Uğur Şahin and Özlem Türeci, a Turkish-German couple as obsessed with mRNA as Karikó herself. In 2013, aged fifty-eight, permanently unpromotable at Penn, she made her choice: she left the university that had demoted her and joined BioNTech as a senior vice-president — a company then so obscure, she later said, that it did not even have a website. Colleagues told her she was committing career suicide. It was, at last, the right laboratory.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the 2005 discovery, in one sentence — and what happened next?

2. What did the 2013 move mean, and how was it received?

1. Swapping one mRNA letter (uridine → pseudouridine) makes the molecule read as "self" — no inflammation, high protein output: the key that unlocked mRNA medicine. The world largely ignored it for years, and the university sold the patent rights away.

2. At 58 she left Penn for BioNTech — then a tiny, website-less German company betting on mRNA. Colleagues called it career suicide; it was the first institution fully committed to her molecule.

Discuss:
  • The decisive collaboration began in a photocopier queue — two underfunded people with adjacent obsessions. What does that say about how institutions could engineer luck, and why they mostly don't?

Part 3 — Eleven months

In January 2020, a new coronavirus's genetic sequence was posted online by Chinese scientists. What followed compressed her forty years into eleven months. mRNA's great weakness had always been commercial — but its great strength was speed: to make a vaccine, you do not need to grow the virus at all, only to print its spike protein's instructions in mRNA and wrap them in fat droplets for delivery. BioNTech designed vaccine candidates within days and partnered with Pfizer; Moderna moved in parallel. The trials that autumn returned numbers nobody had dared forecast — roughly ninety-five per cent efficacy — and in December 2020, less than a year after the sequence appeared, the first mRNA vaccines in history went into arms. Billions of doses followed. Estimates published in medical journals credit the global vaccination effort with preventing deaths in the tens of millions in its first year; the mRNA platform, built on the 2005 modification, carried much of it.

The recognition arrived like a dam breaking — prize after prize, and in October 2023 the call from Stockholm: the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, to Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman. The announcement's timing supplied the story's most quoted irony: the laureate had been demoted by her university, which now issued proud press releases; she has remarked that the same institution once considered her not of faculty quality. She heard the news, by her own account, exactly as she had spent the previous forty years — without ceremony. Her memoir's title states her whole method: Breaking Through.

She has spent her fame carefully — on one argument. The lesson of her life, she insists, is not that perseverance triumphs; for every vindicated Karikó there are thousands the system simply lost, and she nearly was one. The lesson is that the machinery of science — grants judged by fashion, careers judged by funding, prestige journals judged by trend — filters out precisely the unfashionable work from which platforms grow. The scientist survived the system; her point, made from every stage in the world, is that the system should not require survivors. What the next Karikó is working on right now, unfunded, in the wrong department, nobody knows — which is, she says, exactly the problem.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was mRNA uniquely suited to the 2020 emergency?

2. What does Karikó herself say the lesson of her story is — and is not?

1. Speed: no need to grow the virus — just print the spike protein's instructions in modified mRNA. Vaccines were designed within days of the sequence appearing, trialled at ~95% efficacy, and delivered in under a year, with tens of millions of deaths estimated prevented.

2. Not "perseverance wins" — she calls that survivorship. The lesson is that science's funding and career machinery filters out unfashionable foundational work, loses most of its Karikós, and should be redesigned so it doesn't require survivors.

Discuss:
  • She rejects her own story as an inspiration and offers it as an indictment. Which reading do institutions prefer to hear, and why?

Grammar — present perfect vs past simple

A career story lives in two tenses: the finished chapters (she moved in 1985) and the results that stand today (she has changed medicine). Choosing between them is choosing what is over and what still counts.

Past simple — closed chapters: a finished action at a finished time, usually dated.
• "She emigrated in 1985." · "Penn demoted her in 1995." · "They published the key paper in 2005."
Signals: in 1995, twenty years ago, that year, when she was 58.
Present perfect — open accounts: have/has + past participle, when the time is open or the result is now.
• Result standing: "Her discovery has saved millions of lives."
• Life-so-far experience: "She has told the teddy-bear story many times."
• Unfinished period: "mRNA medicine has grown into a whole industry (and is still growing)."
Signals: ever, never, so far, since, for + period reaching now, over her career.

The rule that solves 90% of errors: a dated event takes the past simple, always. ✗ "She has moved to America in 1985." ✓ "She moved to America in 1985."

1. Closed or open?

Put each verb into the past simple or present perfect.

3. Since 2020, mRNA technology (become) one of medicine's central platforms.

3. The award citation — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the right tense, then read it aloud as a formal citation.

Going deeper

A note on the record: this story is recent and largely told by its living participants — the teddy bear, the photocopier and the demotion come from Karikó's own accounts and interviews, consistent over decades but single-sourced by nature. The 2005 science and the 2020 results are documented beyond dispute.

What the story actually teaches. First, take the laureate's own warning seriously: beware the survivorship halo. The system did not nurture this discovery; it failed to prevent it. Between "the cream rises" and "we lost the rest" lies the entire policy question, and only one of those sentences appears in university press releases.

Second, the economics of unfashionable work. Grant systems fund what a majority of reviewers already believe promising — an averaging machine, superb at incremental science and structurally blind to platform shifts, which by definition begin as minority opinions. The fixes people propose (golden tickets, lotteries for borderline grants, funding people not projects) all share one idea: deliberately spend a slice of the budget against the consensus. Almost no funder dares — every one of those grants looks wasted right up until one of them is mRNA.

Third, the migration thread. This story runs Hungary → America → Germany, and its Nobel-winning partnership was Hungarian-American; the companies that carried it were founded by, among others, a Turkish-German couple and a Lebanese-Armenian-American. The platform that answered a global pandemic was built almost entirely by people working far from where they were born — worth remembering in any decade, including this one, when countries debate how welcoming to be to other people's stubborn scientists.

Talk it through:
  • Design the "against-the-consensus" fund: what share of a national science budget, judged how, and how do you face the parliament asking about the 99 failures?
  • The demotion followed the rules; the rules measured funding, not promise. What does your own field's career machinery measure — and what does that metric systematically lose?
  • Fleming's discovery waited a decade; Karikó's fifteen years. Is the delay between breakthrough and recognition shrinking, growing, or just moving — and what sets its length?
  • Series thread: Semmelweis raged at the system and broke; Karikó outlasted it quietly and won. Temperament, era, or luck — what actually separates the two endings?
