Genghis Khan

An abandoned boy from the steppe who built the largest land empire the world has ever known.

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The family was nomadic: they owned no house and no fixed land, but moved their tents across the open grassland with the seasons, following the grass and the water their animals needed.
nomadic (adj) — moving from place to place rather than living in one fixed home. A nomad (n) is a person who lives this way.
2. Each tribe had its own chief, its own herds and its own name, and its members were bound together by blood and loyalty — and they often fought the tribe in the next valley.
a tribe (n) — a group of people, often related by family, who share a leader, customs and a way of life.
3. To stop the two families fighting, the leaders met, shook hands and agreed to forge an alliance: from that day they would defend each other as if they were one.
to forge an alliance (v phrase) — to create a strong partnership or agreement, usually to face a shared enemy. To forge here means to build something with effort.
4. He was a ruthless commander: he felt no pity, gave his enemies no second chance, and did whatever it took to win, however cruel.
ruthless (adj) — hard and without mercy; willing to be cruel to get what you want.
5. Through years of conquest, the king took city after city by force, until lands that had never bowed to anyone were all under his command.
conquest (n) — the act of taking control of a country or people by military force. To conquer (v) is to defeat and take over.
6. The ruler sent an envoy to the neighbouring court — not an army, but a single trusted messenger, carrying his words and his terms to the rival king.
an envoy (n) — an official messenger or representative sent by one ruler or government to another.
7. From the hilltop the land stretched away, vast and empty — grassland running on and on to the horizon, further than a horse could ride in many days.
vast (adj) — extremely large in size or extent; enormous.
8. One by one he subdued the rebellious villages: those that resisted were crushed, and those that surrendered were forced to obey and pay him tribute.
to subdue (v) — to bring a person, group or place under control, usually by force.
9. The army surrounded the walled city and settled in for a long siege: no food could get in, no one could get out, and they waited for hunger to do their work.
a siege (n) — a military operation in which an army surrounds a town or fortress and cuts it off to force it to surrender.
10. Long after he was gone, his legacy lived on — the roads he built, the laws he wrote and the borders he drew still shaped the lives of people who had never seen his face.
a legacy (n) — what is left behind by a person or event and continues to have an effect long afterwards.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The boy on the steppe

He was not born a conqueror. He was born Temüjin, around 1162, on the cold, open grassland between modern Mongolia and Siberia. His people were nomadic herders — tribes of riders who moved their tents across a vast sea of grass, following their animals and, often, raiding one another. His father, Yesügei, was a clan chief, and for a few early years the boy's life was secure.

Then it collapsed. When Temüjin was about nine, a rival tribe, the Tatars, poisoned his father at a feast. With Yesügei dead, his own clan abandoned the family, judging a widow and her small children too weak to be worth feeding. Temüjin's mother kept them alive on roots, fish and small game while the boy grew up hard, fast and unforgiving. As a teenager he was captured by a former ally and held as a slave with a wooden collar around his neck — and he escaped, slipping away at night and hiding in a river until his captors gave up the search.

What he understood, earlier than most, was that on the steppe a lone man was nothing. Survival meant loyalty, and loyalty had to be built. Slowly, shrewdly, he began to forge alliances — with his father's old sworn friends, with a powerful neighbouring chief — and to draw warriors to him not by blood but by reward and trust. Men who followed him and fought well were promoted, whatever family they came from. Men who betrayed him did not get a second chance.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What two disasters struck Temüjin's family when he was about nine?

2. How did Temüjin choose the men he promoted, and how was this unusual?

1. A rival tribe poisoned his father, and then his own clan abandoned the family, leaving them to survive alone on the steppe.

2. He rewarded loyalty and ability rather than birth, promoting men whatever family or tribe they came from — unusual in a world ruled by blood ties.

Discuss:
  • He was left to die as a child and built his strength on loyalty rather than blood. How far do you think a hard early life shapes the kind of leader someone becomes?

Part 2 — One ruler of all the tribes

For over twenty years Temüjin fought the other peoples of the steppe — the Tatars, the Kereyids, the Naimans, the Merkids — sometimes as an ally, often as an enemy. He won not only with courage but with intelligence. He reorganised his fighters into neat units of tens, hundreds and thousands, so that orders could pass quickly and cleanly. He used spies, surprise and, again and again, the famous feigned retreat: his horsemen would pretend to flee in panic, drawing the enemy into a wild chase, then suddenly turn and destroy them on open ground of his own choosing.

By 1206 he had done what no one before him had managed: he had united the warring tribes of Mongolia under a single command. At a great assembly by the river, the chiefs proclaimed him their supreme leader and gave him a new name — Genghis Khan, meaning something like "universal ruler." To hold this fragile new nation together he set down a code of laws known as the Yassa. It demanded absolute loyalty, banned the kidnapping of women and the stealing of animals, protected envoys and merchants, and — strikingly for the age — granted freedom of religion across his lands.

Then he looked outward. His cavalry — tough, fast, able to live for days on dried meat and mare's milk — could cover ground no settled army could match. He turned south against the rich kingdoms of China, and west toward central Asia, and the conquest began in earnest.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name two ways Genghis Khan made his army more effective than his rivals' armies.

2. What was the Yassa, and name one thing it required or protected.

1. Any two: he organised his fighters into units of tens, hundreds and thousands; he used spies and surprise; he used the feigned retreat to lure enemies into a trap; his cavalry was fast and could live off the land.

2. The Yassa was his code of laws. It demanded loyalty, banned the kidnapping of women and theft of animals, protected envoys and merchants, and granted freedom of religion (any one).

Discuss:
  • His law protected merchants and allowed people to follow any religion, yet he was ruthless in war. Can a leader be both a lawmaker and a destroyer? Does one cancel out the other?

Part 3 — The world he left behind

One moment, more than any other, shows what he became. In 1218 Genghis Khan sent a peaceful trade caravan and, later, envoys to the wealthy Khwarazmian Empire in central Asia. The local governor had the merchants killed as spies; the Shah then beheaded the chief envoy and sent his head back. Genghis Khan's reply was total. His armies fell on the empire and, within a few years, erased it — sacking its great cities one after another in long, brutal sieges, and killing on a scale that is still hard to imagine. Whole cities that resisted were destroyed so completely that they never recovered.

And yet that is only half the story. By the time he died in 1227 — falling ill, most accounts say, on campaign — he ruled the largest land empire ever assembled, stretching from the Pacific coast toward the Caspian Sea. His sons and grandsons pushed it further still. Across this vast space the Mongols enforced a fierce peace, sometimes called the Pax Mongolica. For the first time, a merchant could carry goods the whole length of the Silk Road in relative safety, guarded by Mongol patrols and supplied by a network of relay stations. Ideas, goods, inventions and even diseases travelled between East and West as never before.

His personal legacy is just as vast. He left descendants beyond counting — by one famous genetic study, millions of men alive today may carry his line, though scholars now argue over the true number. To some he is a national hero and founding father; to others, the author of one of history's great catastrophes. He was almost certainly both: the abandoned boy who became the most ruthless and the most consequential conqueror the world has known.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What set off the war against the Khwarazmian Empire?

2. What was the Pax Mongolica, and what did it make possible?

1. The Khwarazmians killed his trade caravan as spies and then beheaded his chief envoy; in revenge, Genghis Khan's armies destroyed the entire empire.

2. The "Mongol peace" — a period of order across the huge empire that made the Silk Road safe to travel, so goods, people and ideas could move between East and West as never before.

Discuss:
  • The same empire brought mass slaughter and safe trade, open roads and shared ideas. When we judge a historical figure, how should we weigh what they destroyed against what they built?

The grammar this story runs on: ability — could, was able to, managed to

This is a story about what people could do — and, more dramatically, about what one man managed to do against impossible odds. English has a small family of forms for talking about ability, and choosing the right one makes the difference between a flat sentence and a vivid one.

The system in one place:
• could — for a general ability in the past, something you were able to do any time: "He could ride a horse before he could walk." "Their cavalry could cover huge distances."
• was / were able to and managed to — for a specific success, one particular thing achieved at one particular moment, often with difficulty: "Against all odds, he was able to unite the tribes." "As a slave, he managed to escape." (managed to stresses the effort or difficulty.)
• couldn't / wasn't able to — for inability. Here both work, even for one specific event: "No settled army could stop the Mongol cavalry." "The Shah wasn't able to save his cities."
• be able to — the form you use for the tenses where could won't reach: future ("he will be able to…") and perfect ("they have been able to…").
The one trap to remember:
For a single, successful action in the past, use was able to or managed to, not could.
✗ "After years of war, he could finally unite the tribes."   ✓ "After years of war, he was able to / managed to finally unite the tribes."
But could is fine for general ability ("He could ride all day") and for the negative ("No one could stop him").

1. Which form fits — general ability or a one-off success?

Decide whether the sentence needs could (general ability) or was/were able to / managed to (a single success). Write your choice in the gap.

1. As a boy on the steppe, Temüjin ride and shoot from a young age. (general ability)

2. Although he was chained and watched, one night he escape from his captors. (one difficult success)

3. The Mongol horsemen live for days on dried meat and mare's milk. (general ability)

4. After twenty years of war, he finally unite every tribe under one command. (one hard-won success)

1. could (a general past ability)

2. was able to / managed to (a single, difficult success — not "could")

3. could (a general ability)

4. was able to / managed to (one specific achievement against the odds)

2. Ability or inability?

Complete each gap with a positive or negative form of ability, following the meaning in brackets.

1. No settled army stop the Mongol cavalry on open ground. (general inability)

2. The Shah (not) save his great cities once the sieges began. (a single failure)

3. Thanks to the Yassa, merchants travel the Silk Road in relative safety. (general ability)

1. could (general inability works with the negative: "no army could stop…")

2. wasn't able to (a single failure; "couldn't" is also acceptable here)

3. could / were able to (general ability after the law was made)

3. Reach the other tenses with be able to

Here could can't be used — put be able to into the right form (future or present perfect).

1. Once the tribes were united, the Mongols (future) turn their full strength outward.

2. Few rulers in history (present perfect) build an empire so quickly.

1. would be able to (future-in-the-past; "could" would also be understood, but "be able to" is the safe form here)

2. have been able to (present perfect — "could" is impossible in this tense)
