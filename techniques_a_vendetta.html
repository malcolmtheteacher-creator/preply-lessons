SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B2/C1 · 50 min

A Vendetta

Guy de Maupassant (1883) — a widow has no man left to avenge her murdered son, so she makes a weapon of the only living thing she has

Read in three parts

On the cliffs of Corsica, where an insult is still paid for in blood, an old woman is left with a duty she has no strength to perform. Maupassant tells the whole thing plainly, almost without comment. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. Notice how calm the prose stays even as the plan grows monstrous — and do not skip the last line.

Part 1 — The murder and the vow

Paolo Saverini's widow dwelt alone with her son in a poor little house on the outskirts of Bonifacio. The town, built on an out-jutting part of the mountain, in places even overhanging the sea, looks across the straits, over a ground bristling with reefs, to the lower shores of Sardinia. Beneath it, on the other side, girding it almost completely, is a cleft in the cliff, like an immense corridor, which serves as a harbour.

In this little house, on the very edge of the cliff, Paolo Saverini's widow lived with her son Antoine and their dog Semillante, a big, thin beast, with a long rough coat, of the sheep-dog breed. The young man took her with him when out shooting.

One night, after some quarrel, Antoine Saverini was treacherously stabbed by Nicolas Ravolati, who escaped the same evening to Sardinia. When the old mother received the body of her child, which the neighbours had brought back to her, she did not weep, but she remained for a long time motionless, watching it. Then, stretching her wrinkled hand over the body, she promised him a vendetta. She did not wish anyone near her, and she shut herself up beside the body with the dog, which howled continuously, standing at the foot of the bed, her head stretched towards her master, her tail between her legs. She did not move any more than did the mother, who, now leaning over the body with a blank stare, was weeping great silent tears and watching it.

The young man, lying on his back, dressed in his jacket of coarse cloth, torn at the chest, seemed asleep; but he had blood all over him: on his shirt, that had been torn off to attend to the wound; on his beard, on his hands. Clots of blood had hardened in his beard, in his hair. His old mother began to talk to him. At the sound of her voice the dog was quiet.

"Never fear, never fear, you shall be avenged, my son, my little son, my poor child. You may sleep in peace; you shall be avenged, I tell you. Your mother has promised, and your mother never breaks her word; you know she does not."

Slowly she leaned over him, pressing her cold lips to the dead lips. Then Semillante began to howl again with a long, monotonous, penetrating, horrible sound. And now the old woman remained motionless; and the dog, standing at the edge of the bed, its head stretched towards her master, and its tail between its legs, howled again.

Pause and discuss:
  • The mother "did not weep" when the body was brought in — she watched it. What does this refusal to break down tell you about her, and about the world she lives in?
  • She promises a vendetta by "stretching her wrinkled hand over the body." She is old and alone, with no man left in the family. Why does that make the vow feel both hopeless and dangerous?
  • Maupassant gives almost as many lines to the dog Semillante as to the mother. Why introduce the dog so carefully, so early?

Part 2 — The training of the dog

The next morning Antoine was buried, and soon his name ceased to be mentioned in Bonifacio. He had neither brothers nor cousins. No man was there to carry on the vendetta. Only his mother, an old woman, thought of it. On the other side of the straits she saw, from morning until night, a little white spot on the coast. It was the little Sardinian village of Longosardo, where the Corsican bandits took refuge when too closely pursued. They were almost the only inhabitants of this hamlet, opposite the shores of their native country, and they awaited there in safety the moment to return home, to the "maquis." She knew that Nicolas Ravolati had sought refuge in this very village.

All alone, all day long, seated at her window, she looked over there and thought of revenge. How could she do it without help — she, an invalid and so near death? But she had promised, she had sworn on the body. She could not forget, she could not wait. What could she do? She could no longer sleep at night; she had no more rest nor peace of mind; she thought persistently. The dog, dozing at her feet, would sometimes lift her head and howl. Since her master's death she often howled thus, as though she were calling him, as though her beast's soul, inconsolable, too, had also retained a recollection that nothing could wipe out.

One night, as Semillante began to howl, the mother had a sudden idea, an idea of vengeance, ferocious and fierce. She thought it over until morning. Then, having risen at daybreak, she went to church. She prayed, prostrate on the floor, begging the Lord to help her, to support her, to give to her poor, broken-down body the strength which she needed in order to avenge her son.

She returned home. She had in her yard an old barrel, which acted as a cistern. She turned it over, emptied it, made it fast to the ground with sticks and stones; then she chained Semillante to this kennel and went into the house. She walked ceaselessly now, her eyes always fixed on the distant coast of Sardinia. He was over there, the murderer. The dog howled all day and all night. In the morning the old woman brought her some water in a bowl, but nothing more: no soup, no bread.

Another day went by. Semillante, exhausted, was sleeping. The next day her eyes were shining, her hair on end, and she pulled wildly at her chain. And the old woman gave her nothing to eat. The beast, furious, barked hoarsely. Another night went by. Then, at daybreak, Mother Saverini asked a neighbour for some straw. She took the old clothes which had been worn formerly by her husband and stuffed them so as to make them look like a human body. Having planted a post in the ground, in front of Semillante's kennel, she fastened to it this dummy, which seemed to be standing up. Then she made a head out of some old linen.

The dog, surprised, looked at this straw man, and was quiet, although famished. Then the old woman went to the pork butcher's and bought a long piece of black pudding. When she got home she lighted a wood fire in the yard, near the kennel, and cooked the black pudding. Semillante, maddened, jumped about, frothed at the mouth, her eyes fixed on the food, the smell of which went to her very entrails. Then the mother made of the smoking sausage a necktie for the straw man. She tied it very tight around his neck, as if to strangle him. When she had finished she unchained the dog.

With one ferocious bound the beast leaped at the straw man's throat, and with her paws on his shoulders she began to tear at it. She fell back with a piece of the food in her mouth, then dashed at it again, sinking her teeth into the string, snatched a few pieces of meat, fell back again, and rushed forward anew. She tore away the face with her teeth and tore the whole neck to pieces. The old woman, motionless and silent, was watching eagerly. Then she chained the beast up again, made her fast, and starved her for two days. Then she repeated the strange exercise. For three months she taught her, by a series of daily exercises, this savage attack, this way of tearing to pieces and devouring by using her teeth. She no longer chained her up, but urged her on to attack the straw man with a word.

She had taught the dog to tear and devour it without even leaving any traces in the yard. Then, as a reward, she gave her a piece of black pudding, cooked expressly for her.

Pause and discuss:
  • The whole plan is built through hunger — the mother withholds food, dresses the dummy in her dead husband's clothes, ties meat at its throat. Trace the steps. What is chilling about how methodical and patient it is?
  • "For three months she taught her" — the revenge takes a season to build. How does the slow, workmanlike pace change the way we feel about what is coming?
  • The mother has, in effect, had the murder committed for her — she cannot strike the blow, so she conditions the dog to strike it. Is the dog a weapon, an accomplice, or a version of the mother herself?

Part 3 — The revenge and the calm

Then, disguising herself as a poor old beggar, she asked a Sardinian fisherman to take her over to the other side. She had a leather bag, which she carried under her cloak. Semillante followed her. When they were in the boat, she called the dog to her and fed her, so that no one would notice how starved she was. When she reached the town, she prowled about the streets. She stopped in front of a poor house and peered through the windows into the suspicious-looking den. It was empty. Nicolas Ravolati lived alone; he was a cabinet-maker. She waited until he should return, and then she loosened the dog and cried:

"At him, at him! Tear him to bits!"

The famished animal sprang for his throat. The man stretched out his arms, clasped the dog and rolled to the ground. For a few seconds he squirmed, beating the ground with his feet; then he stopped moving, while Semillante dug her fangs into his throat and tore it to ribbons. Two neighbours, seated before their door, remembered perfectly to have seen an old beggar come out with a thin, black dog, which was eating, as it went along, something dark that its master was giving it.

At nightfall the old woman was at home again. She slept well that night.

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • Read the last line again: "She slept well that night." After months of sleeplessness, this is the only rest she gets. What does that single sentence do to everything that came before?
  • Maupassant reports the killing itself in a few flat sentences and adds no comment — no horror, no judgement, no relief. Why does refusing to react make the moment stronger, not weaker?
  • The neighbours see only "an old beggar" and a dog eating "something dark." The witnesses understand nothing. What does their blindness tell us about how perfectly she has planned this?
  • Your turn: The mother is at peace because a code of honour has been satisfied. When, if ever, does "settling the score" bring real peace — and when does it just deepen the wound?

How Maupassant controls the reader — three devices to listen for

The power of this story is not in what is described; it is in how little the voice reacts to it. Notice three things at work throughout.

Grammar — The Causative: have / get something done

This whole story turns on one grammatical idea. The mother cannot kill Nicolas Ravolati herself, so she arranges for it to be done through the dog. In English we have a special structure for when we do not do something ourselves but cause someone (or something) else to do it. It is called the causative: she does not attack the killer — she has him attacked. Get this right and you can talk about everything you delegate, arrange, or make others do.

1. have / get + object + past participle — arranging for a thing to be done

Use this when you do not do the action yourself; someone else does it for you (or something is done to you). The pattern is have + object + past participle, or, more informally, get + object + past participle.
"She had the dog trained to kill." (she arranged the training; she did not do it directly)
"I had the barrel emptied and chained to the ground."
"You should get that wound seen to."
The same pattern also describes something unwanted done to you: "He had his son murdered by Ravolati" would mean he arranged it — but "She had her son taken from her" means it was done to her.

1. She could not strike the blow, so she the killer by the dog. (auxiliary of arranging + past participle of "to kill")

2. Before she began, she the old barrel and chained to the ground. (have, past tense + past participle of "to empty")

3. "You really ought to that cough at," said the neighbour. (informal causative + past participle of "to see")

1. had … killed (have + object + past participle: she arranged the killing)

2. had … emptied (have + object + past participle, past tense)

3. get … looked ("get that cough looked at" — informal causative + past participle)

2. have / make someone do  ·  get someone to do — causing a person to act

When you name the person who does the action, the verb form changes.
have someone do (bare infinitive) — arrange/instruct, fairly neutral: "She had the fisherman row her across."
make someone do (bare infinitive) — force, no choice: "Hunger made the dog attack."
get someone to do (to-infinitive) — persuade, arrange with effort: "She got a fisherman to take her over."
Notice: after have and make you drop the "to"; after get you keep it.

2. got … to take (get + someone + to-infinitive: persuade/arrange)

3. had … attack (have + someone/something + bare infinitive: instruct, neutral)

3. let / help someone (to) do — permitting and assisting

Two more causative-family verbs complete the picture.
let someone do (bare infinitive) — allow/permit: "She let the dog loose and cried, 'At him!'"

What Maupassant does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are the reason a story this short and this quiet still lands like a blow.

1. Cold, methodical structure — a weapon built in plain sight

Most of the story is not the killing but the preparation for it: the barrel, the chain, the straw dummy in her dead husband's clothes, the black pudding tied at its throat, three months of daily drills. Maupassant structures the tale as a slow, patient construction — the grammar of a project, not a passion. He accumulates procedural detail so that the revenge feels engineered rather than felt, and this cold method chills us far more than any outburst of rage could.

2. Irony and the final calm — "she slept well"

The story ends not on the horror but on the mother's rest: "At nightfall the old woman was at home again. She slept well that night." For months she could not sleep; only the killing restores her peace. Maupassant undercuts our expectation of remorse with a single line of contentment, and this closing calm ironises everything — the reader is left uneasy precisely because the murderer is not. The peace is real, and that is the point.

3. The restrained, visceral ending — horror shown, not commented on

The killing itself takes only a few sentences: the dog springs, the man squirms, the throat is torn "to ribbons," and the narrator moves straight on to what two neighbours "remembered perfectly to have seen." Maupassant restrains the prose exactly where a lesser writer would linger; he reports the violence flatly, adds no adjective of horror, and this refusal to react implicates the reader, who must supply the feeling the text withholds.

4. Setting as character — harsh Corsica and the code of honour

Bonifacio is introduced before any human being: cliffs "overhanging the sea," "a ground bristling with reefs," the killer's refuge a "little white spot" always visible across the straits. Maupassant grounds the vendetta in a specific, unforgiving landscape and lets the code of honour do the work of motive — "No man was there to carry on the vendetta. Only his mother." The place and its law compel the action; the setting does not decorate the story, it drives it.

Spot-the-technique — quick MCQ

Speaking tasks

Task A · Retell the story in your own words (3 minutes)

Before we discuss it, tell the story back to me as if I had never read it — in your own words, not line by line. Aim for about eight to ten sentences.

As you go, use at least five words from the Vocabulary tab and at least three causative structures from the Grammar tab (for example: "she had the dog trained", "hunger made it attack", "she got a fisherman to take her across"). These beats will help you: a widow's only son is treacherously killed and the murderer flees across the straits · alone and old, she swears a vendetta she has no man to carry out · over three months she conditions her dog through hunger, training it on a straw dummy with meat tied at its throat · she takes the dog to the killer's town and sets it on him · she returns home and sleeps well.

Task B · Getting things done through other people (4 minutes)

The mother could not act herself, so she had the deed done through the dog. Most of adult life works the same way — we get results through others. Tell me about a time you achieved something important not by doing it yourself but by arranging for someone else to do it: a repair, a project at work, a favour, a piece of professional help.

  1. what you needed done, and why you could not simply do it yourself;
  2. how you got the right person to do it — did you have them do it, make them, or get them to;
  3. whether the result was as good as if you had done it with your own hands.

Use at least three causative structures ("I had the roof repaired", "I got a lawyer to draft it", "I couldn't make them hurry") and two effect verbs from the Technique tab.

Task C · A long, patient goal (3 minutes)

The revenge takes a whole season to build — barrel, straw, drills, three months of discipline. Tell me about a goal of your own that could only be reached slowly, by patient daily work: learning a language, training for something physical, building a skill, raising a child, growing a business.

Talk me through the boring middle — the repetition, the days when nothing seemed to change — and how you kept going. What did you have to get done along the way, and who did you get to help you? Was the patience itself the hardest part?

Build the account around causative structures ("I had a coach set my plan", "I got a friend to test me every week").

Task D · Honour codes versus the law (4 minutes)

The mother acts entirely within the code of the vendetta — and entirely outside the law. To her town, she has done the right thing; to a court, she has committed murder. Tell me about a situation, real or that you have read about, where a private code of honour clashed with the official rules or the law.

Where does your sympathy sit, and why? When a community feels the law has failed it, is taking matters into its own hands ever justified — or does it always just make people have more blood spilled? At the end, ask me where I would draw the line.

Use at least one result-style causative ("the failure of the courts made people take…") and one let / help structure ("the code let them justify…").

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The mother sleeps well after the killing. Does that peace make her more sympathetic to you, or more frightening — and why?
  2. She never strikes a blow herself; the dog does everything. Is she any less responsible for the death because she had it done through another creature?
  3. Maupassant tells the murder in flat, unshocked sentences and passes no judgement. Should a writer take a side, or is it more powerful to show and stay silent?
  4. If the mother could speak one sentence to a court that would never understand her world, what do you think she would say — and would it change how you judge her?
