Two Hours and Two Minutes

In November 1863 the star speaker talked for two hours and the president for two minutes. Only one of them had decided where he would land.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — A field full of graves

In the first days of July 1863, two armies fought for three days around a small Pennsylvania town called Gettysburg. By the end, the number of dead, wounded and missing was in the tens of thousands. Many of the Union dead were buried quickly, in shallow graves, in the fields where they had fallen.

The state decided to create a proper cemetery and to hold a ceremony to dedicate it. The organisers wanted the finest speaker in America to give the main address: Edward Everett, a former senator, Secretary of State and president of Harvard, known for speaking for hours without notes. He said he needed time to prepare, so the date was moved to 19 November.

Only then, in early November, did the organisers think to invite the president. Abraham Lincoln was asked to follow the main speaker with "a few appropriate remarks". It was an afterthought, and it sounded like one.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was chosen as the main speaker, and what was he known for?

2. Why was the date of the ceremony moved?

3. What was Lincoln asked to do — and when?

1. Edward Everett — a former senator, Secretary of State and president of Harvard, famous for long speeches given from memory.

2. Everett said he needed more time to prepare.

3. To make "a few appropriate remarks" after the main speaker; the invitation came in early November, only a couple of weeks before the ceremony.

Discuss:
  • Everett was given extra time and Lincoln was given a few weeks and a few minutes. Who had the harder task — the person who must fill two hours, or the person who must say something that matters in two minutes?

Part 2 — Two hours, then two minutes

On 19 November, Everett stood up and spoke for about two hours. His speech ran to roughly thirteen thousand words. It described the battle day by day, compared the dead to the heroes of ancient Greece and explained the causes of the war. Many people who were there considered it a magnificent performance.

Then Lincoln rose. His text had about 272 words and took around two minutes to deliver. It did not describe the battle at all. It said that the nation had been "conceived in liberty" and "dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal", that the living must finish the work the dead had begun, and that government "of the people, by the people, for the people" should not disappear from the earth.

Lincoln had worked on those words for days beforehand, and there are several handwritten copies in which he changed small details. Reactions at the time were mixed: some newspapers praised the remarks, while others, divided by politics, hardly mentioned them or dismissed them.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Roughly how long, and how many words, was Everett's speech?

2. What did Lincoln's speech leave out — and what did it say instead?

3. What is the evidence that Lincoln prepared carefully?

1. About two hours and roughly thirteen thousand words.

2. It left out the details of the battle. Instead it said what the nation stood for and what the living now had to do.

3. He worked on the text for days, and several handwritten copies survive in which he changed small details.

Discuss:
  • Lincoln did not describe the battle. What does a speaker gain by leaving out most of what they know — and what is the risk?

Part 3 — The note

The next morning, 20 November, Everett wrote to Lincoln. He congratulated him and added one sentence that has been quoted ever since: he would be glad if he could flatter himself that he had come as near to the central idea of the occasion in two hours as Lincoln had in two minutes.

Lincoln replied the same day. He said that neither of them could have been excused from his part, Everett from making a long address or himself from making a short one, and that he was pleased to learn the little he had said was not entirely a failure.

Was Everett simply wrong to speak for two hours? Not necessarily. The crowd had come expecting a long oration, and the full story of the battle was worth telling. But the man who gave the longer speech was the one who admitted which of the two had reached the point. Today your listeners rarely have two hours. The question is whether you know your central idea — and whether you say it first.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. When did Everett write to Lincoln, and what did he say?

2. How did Lincoln reply?

3. Why is it unfair to say that Everett was simply wrong?

1. The next day, 20 November. He said he would be glad to have come as near to the central idea in two hours as Lincoln had in two minutes.

2. He said that neither man could have been excused from his own part, and that he was pleased the little he said was not entirely a failure.

3. The crowd expected a long oration and the full account of the battle had value. The occasion allowed for it — most of our conversations do not.

Discuss:
  • Everett said himself that Lincoln had reached the central idea faster. When has someone's short answer taught you more than a long one?

How the tool works

The oldest advice about speaking is to know your ending before you begin. Stephen Covey made it the second of his seven habits in 1989: begin with the end in mind. Barbara Minto, who worked at McKinsey, taught consultants to open with the answer and then support it — her Pyramid Principle. The tool below turns that idea into three moves you can make in one breath: a point, one reason, and a stop.

Using it, step by step

  1. Decide your landing first. Ask: what do I want them to know, decide or do when I finish? Write it as one sentence.
  2. Say it first. Open with the answer itself: The short answer is…, My recommendation is…, What I need from you is…
  3. Add one reason — the strongest, not all of them. The rest of the detail waits until someone asks for it.
  4. Stop. End on your last word and let the silence do its work. If more is wanted, they will ask: Happy to go into detail.

Try it yourself

These are sentences from rambling answers. What job does each one do in a landing plan? Choose, then check.

1. Well, I've been looking at this for quite a while now, and there's honestly a lot to say about it.
2. We should move the launch to March.
3. The supplier cannot deliver the components before the end of February.
4. It reminds me of something that happened on a completely different project last year.
5. So that's my recommendation — I'd like a decision by Friday.
6. Yes, we can finish by Friday.
7. Two of the three tests have already passed.

1. Cut it — it announces that you will speak but says nothing; delete it.

2. Point — it is the answer itself, in the first sentence.

3. Reason — one concrete reason that supports the point.

4. Cut it — it is a detour; keep it for later if anyone asks.

5. Stop — it closes the answer and says exactly what you need next.

6. Point — a direct answer to a direct question.

7. Reason — a fact that supports the point, short enough to say in one breath.

Where the tool breaks

Delicate news. A blunt opening can sound cold. Keep the order but soften the delivery: I'm afraid the short answer is no.

You don't have a view yet. A forced point is worse than an honest one. Land on that instead: I don't have an answer yet. What I can tell you is…

Occasions and stories. Everett's audience had come for a long oration, and some stories need their journey. The plan is for answers, not for ceremonies.

Talk it through:
  • Which would you find harder: saying the point first, or stopping after one reason? Why?
  • When is it wiser to give the background before the point?

Language — Opening with the verdict

Point-first speaking depends on a small set of openers that commit you to an answer in the first few words. Learn them as chunks: they are what you reach for when your mind is still planning the rest.

Direct openers: The short answer is… · The bottom line is… · The upshot is… — followed by the answer. The short answer is no. The bottom line is that we cannot deliver by Friday.
Wh-cleft (focus on what matters): What I need from you is a decision. What worries me is the timeline. The first part names the topic; the verb is then delivers the point.
Recommendations: I recommend that + subject + base verb — I recommend that we postpone the launch. After recommend, suggest, insist and propose, the verb stays in the base form, even with he, she or it: I suggest that she attend, not attends.

1. Base form after recommend

Write the verb in brackets in the correct form.

1. I recommend that the committee (postpone) the decision until the figures arrive.

2. She insisted that every new employee (be) given a written plan.

3. We suggest that he (send) the revised budget before Thursday.

4. I'd propose that the two teams (meet) once a week from now on.

Task 1 — Thirty seconds

Pick one question: Should people finish what they start, even when it is no longer worth it? Is a meeting ever better than an email? Which matters more in a leader — being decisive or being right? Plan for ten seconds: your point, one reason, how you will stop. Then answer in thirty.

Task 2 — Say it again, shorter

Think of a time you gave a long, unfocused answer. Tell me the question and then give the answer you wish you had given: point, one reason, stop.

Task 3 — The story in sixty seconds

Tell the story of Everett and Lincoln, but start with the point: what does it show about speaking? Then add one fact as the reason, and stop.

To finish — the harder questions

  1. Is it more important to know your point before you speak, or to be able to find it while you speak?
  2. Everett's audience expected a two-hour oration. How do you decide how long a particular listener will really give you?
  3. Is a speaker who always opens with the answer less interesting than one who builds up to it? When would you choose to build up?
  4. From memory: explain the landing plan in four sentences, using the short answer is, recommend that, what I need and the bottom line.

Practice

One page of questions for using the Landing Plan again and again. Pick a question, take ten seconds to decide your point and one reason, then answer in thirty seconds or less. Say your point in the first sentence. Give one reason. Stop.

Each time:
  1. Ten seconds: what is my point, in one sentence? What is the one best reason?
  2. Thirty seconds: point first, then the reason. Do not add a second reason.
  3. Stop on your last word. Count one second of silence before you say anything else.

The questions

Updates and meetings

Someone wants the position quickly.

  1. How is the project going?
  2. Why are we behind schedule?
  3. Where do we stand with the budget?
  4. What is the biggest risk this month?
  5. Can you give me an update on the client? You have one minute.
  6. What happened at the meeting I missed?

Asked for a recommendation

The answer comes first. Reasons come second.

  1. Should we hire someone or use a freelancer?
  2. Which of these two suppliers would you choose?
  3. Should we launch now or wait until the new year?
  4. Is it worth paying for this training course?
  5. Would you move the deadline or reduce the scope?
  6. Which task should we do first?

Clients and customers

They are busy and they want the point.

  1. Why is your price higher than your competitor's?
  2. Why should we choose you?
  3. Why has the delivery been delayed?
  4. What exactly will I get for this money?
  5. Can you do it cheaper?
  6. What would you do in my position?

Interviews

Short, clear answers sound confident.

  1. Tell me about yourself. Give it in thirty seconds.
  2. Why do you want this job?
  3. What is your biggest strength?
  4. Tell me about a time something went wrong.
  5. Why should we hire you?
  6. Where do you want to be in five years?

After a presentation

The question comes from the audience, and you have no notes.

  1. What is the main thing I should take away from this?
  2. What would you do differently next time?
  3. How long will this take?
  4. Who is responsible if it fails?
  5. What is the evidence for that?
  6. What do you need from us?

Opinions about work and life

Take a side. A balanced answer can come later.

  1. Is working from home better than working in an office?
  2. Is a four-day week a good idea?
  3. Should people change jobs every few years?
  4. Is a degree still worth the money?
  5. Is it better to specialise or to know a little about everything?
  6. Is it ever right to say no to your manager?

Everyday choices

Low stakes, so you can practise the shape.

  1. Where should we go for dinner?
  2. Which is better for a holiday, a city or the countryside?
  3. Should I buy a car or use public transport?
  4. Should I tell my friend the truth about her new haircut?
  5. Is it better to rent or to buy a home?
  6. What is the best way to learn a language?

Bigger questions

The reason has to carry more weight.

  1. Should schools ban phones?
  2. Is social media good for society?
  3. Should companies be allowed to use AI to write their reports?
  4. Should cities ban cars from the centre?
  5. Is it fair that some jobs are paid so much more than others?
  6. Do we trust experts too much, or too little?

How it sounds

Six examples of the shape. Try the question first, then open the answer.

Why is your price higher than your competitor's?

Point: Because the price includes support after the project, which most of them charge for separately.

Reason: Our clients tell us that a problem in month three costs them more than the difference.

Stop: That is the difference.

Should we launch now or wait until the new year?

Point: My recommendation is to launch now.

Reason: Our main competitor has announced for January, and we want to be in the market first.

Stop: That's my view.

Tell me about yourself. Give it in thirty seconds.

Point: I am a logistics manager who likes solving problems that other people avoid.

Reason: In my last job I cut delivery times by a fifth by changing how we plan each week.

Stop: That is what I would bring here.

Is working from home better than working in an office?

Point: For most tasks, yes.

Reason: I can concentrate for three hours without interruption, which I cannot do in an open office.

Stop: The exception is the first month in a new job.

Where do we stand with the budget?

Point: We are ten per cent over.

Reason: The extra cost is the longer testing phase, and it is already finished.

Stop: I do not expect any more.

Should schools ban phones?

Point: Yes, during lessons.

Reason: A phone on the desk pulls attention away from the lesson, even when it is silent.

Stop: At break time, it is the students' choice.

Make it harder

  • Shorter: answer in twenty seconds, then in ten.
  • Interrupted: your partner says Why? after your point. Give the reason, then stop.
  • Opposite: answer the same question again, taking the opposite side. Use the same shape.
  • For a different listener: answer to your manager, then to a new colleague, then to a child. The point stays the same. Only the reason changes.
  • Chain: after your stop, your partner asks one follow-up question. Answer that in the same shape.
