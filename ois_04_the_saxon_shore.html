Now: The Romans Leave

In Lesson 3 you learned that Rome withdrew from Britain around 410 AD. The last legions sailed home. The province of Britannia was left to fend for itself. But Britain had grown soft under Roman rule, and without the legions, it was defenceless.

A British leader named Vortigern faced the same problem the Romans had always faced: invaders from the north. The Picts and Scots crossed the borders, raiding and burning. Desperate, Vortigern made a decision that would change Britain forever.

II. The Coming of the Saxons (410–500 AD)

Marshall tells this story with all the detail of a great drama. A desperate king. Two foreign brothers. A beautiful daughter. A terrible treachery.

Vortigern's Invitation

With the Romans gone, Vortigern needed warriors. Marshall writes:

The Romans will not help us. We must ask some one else. Across the sea, called the North Sea, there is a great country called Germany. The people who live in this country are Saxons. They are very brave and valiant fighters. Let us send over to Germany and ask the Saxons to come and help us. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter IX

Three ships arrived carrying two Saxon leaders: brothers named Hengist and Horsa. At first, they came as hired mercenaries, paid to defend against the Picts and Scots. But Marshall tells what happened next:

Then Hengist and Horsa, seeing what a fine country Britain was, resolved never to go away again. They resolved rather to stay and conquer it for themselves. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter IX

The Cunning of Hengist

Hengist was a clever man. He had a beautiful daughter named Rowena. He decided to use her in a bold strategy:

Rowena went up to Vortigern, and kneeling before him held out the cup, speaking in the Saxon language. 'She calls you "Lord, King," and offers to drink your health. You must say, "Drinc heil," ' he answered.

They could not talk to each other because he could only speak British and she could only speak Saxon. But they looked at each other all the more. Vortigern loved Rowena. He loved her so much that he wanted to marry her. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter IX

Vortigern asked Hengist for permission to marry Rowena. And Hengist was ready with his price:

This was just what Hengist had hoped would happen. He knew he would have a great deal of power in Britain when his daughter was queen. But at first he pretended to object, and only consented at last as if it were a great favour. He made Vortigern give him the whole of Kent, too, in return for allowing him to marry Rowena. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter IX

The Treachery

Now Hengist owned land and had a British king as his son-in-law. He used his position to invite more Saxons. Ship after ship landed on the shore. Soon the Saxons were not a garrison — they were an army of conquest.

When Vortigern finally realized what was happening and tried to resist, it was too late. The Saxons defeated the Britons in battle after battle. Within a generation, the entire eastern coast and much of the interior had fallen to Saxon rule.

The historian's note: Marshall's account comes from Geoffrey of Monmouth and the Anglo-Saxon Chronicles, which were written centuries after these events. The real history is murkier. What probably happened: in the late 5th century, Britain's economic collapse and continued warfare created instability. Germanic peoples may have been invited as soldiers, but the boundary between "settlement" and "invasion" is blurred. Genetic evidence shows that Anglo-Saxon immigration was real and substantial, but it was gradual, not a sudden conquest. Vortigern may have been a real person, or a name for a type of ruler ("Vor-tigern" means "overlord"). The name Hengist and Horsa appear in the Anglo-Saxon genealogies, but whether they were real individuals or legendary is unknown. What is certain: by 500 AD, the map of Britain had changed dramatically.

III. The Making of England (500–600 AD)

The Seven Kingdoms (The Heptarchy)

By 500 AD, the Saxon, Angle, and Jute invaders had carved Britain into separate kingdoms. Each had its own king, its own laws, its own culture. The learned called this system the Heptarchy — the rule of seven:

The Seven Kingdoms of Anglo-Saxon England

1. Kent (southeast) — settled by Jutes; ruled by the descendants of Hengist

2. Sussex (south) — the kingdom of the South Saxons

3. Wessex (west) — the kingdom of the West Saxons; would later become dominant

4. Essex (east) — the kingdom of the East Saxons

5. East Anglia (east) — the kingdom of the Angles; rich and powerful

6. Mercia (midlands) — the kingdom of the Mercians; the largest and mightiest

7. Northumbria (north) — the kingdom of the Angles in the north; powerful and learned

These kingdoms warred with each other constantly. But they shared a language, beliefs, and a culture that would become distinctly English.

Augustine Arrives (597 AD)

One hundred years after the Saxons arrived, the Christian world turned its attention to this distant pagan island. Pope Gregory the Great in Rome sent a missionary named Augustine with about forty monks to convert the Saxons to Christianity.

Augustine landed in Kent and brought his message to King Ethelbert. Marshall tells us:

Ethelbert was a heathen, but he had married a Christian lady, and so had already heard something of the story of Christ. Augustine and his followers preached to the people. Soon Ethelbert and all his people were baptized. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XI

From Kent, Augustine's monks spread throughout the kingdoms. Each conversion of a king meant the conversion of his people. By the early 600s, Christianity had become the religion of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms.

The historian's note: Augustine's mission is well documented in both English and Continental sources. Pope Gregory's letters to Augustine survive, giving us insight into how the missionary work was planned and managed. Ethelbert of Kent is an historical figure, and his conversion in 597 AD is one of the few dates in early Anglo-Saxon history that we can verify. The Gregorian mission (as it's called) didn't instantly convert everyone — pagan practices survived for centuries alongside Christianity. But it marked the moment when the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms rejoined the Christian world after the collapse of Roman Britain.

Language, Law, and Culture

The Saxons and Angles brought their own languages — Germanic dialects that would eventually merge into what we call Old English. These languages shaped the island's culture in ways that still echo today. Where the Celts had used one root for something, the Saxons used another. The language we speak now is Saxon, not Celtic.

They also brought their own legal systems. The concept of the shire (an administrative division) comes from Saxon law. The idea of local assemblies making decisions — the folk-moot — influenced how Britain would eventually govern itself.

And they brought their own gods: Woden, Thunor, Tiw, Frige. The days of the week still bear their names: Wednesday (Woden's day), Thursday (Thunor's day), Tuesday (Tiw's day), Friday (Frige's day).

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Was Vortigern a fool?

He invited mercenaries to protect his kingdom, and they conquered it. But was there a better choice? With Rome gone and the Picts attacking, what else could he have done?

Hengist's cunning

Hengist married his daughter to Vortigern, extracted Kent as a bride-price, then used his power to conquer. Was this genius or treachery? Both?

Language and conquest

The Saxon languages didn't replace the Celtic languages because the Saxons killed everyone. They replaced them because the Saxons controlled the power structures. How does language follow conquest?

Arthur's mystery

The Battle of Badon Hill appears in the chronicles as a great British victory. Was Arthur real, or is he a legend built to explain a British victory that happened to slow (but not stop) the Saxon advance?

Augustine's mission

Augustine arrived in 597 with no army, no weapons, just monks and faith. Yet the kings converted. Was Christianity so compelling, or did the new religion offer something politically useful?

The Heptarchy puzzle

Why did the Anglo-Saxons split into seven kingdoms instead of staying united? And why did one kingdom (Wessex) eventually rise to dominate all the others?
