Saying No to Your Boss

Five o'clock on a Thursday and he's at your desk with something urgent. You're already full. A flat no isn't available to you — so you don't refuse the work. You make the trade visible and hand the decision back to him.

The Scene 🎬

You are going to say yes. You can feel it coming and you have about two seconds.

Dele has appeared at your desk with a pricing deck that's suddenly needed for Monday. It's real, it matters, and he's not being unreasonable — he's just been handed it himself and hasn't done the arithmetic either.

You are already full. Not dramatically, not martyr-ishly — genuinely full. If you take this, something else slips quietly and nobody finds out until it's a problem, and then it will be your problem.

The move here isn't refusal. It's making the cost visible and giving him the choice, because the choice is legitimately his. Done well, you never say the word no and the work still doesn't get done.

  1. When did you last say yes to something you didn't have time for? What happened to the thing that slipped?
  2. Is it your job to protect your own capacity, or your manager's job to know what you're carrying?
YouFull. Tired. About to agree reflexively, which is how the last three weeks happened.
DeleYour manager. Half out of the door, not a bully, and genuinely unaware of what's already on your plate.
First: read the conversation and find the moment You starts to say yes and stops. Then notice that the word "no" never appears — and the work still doesn't happen this week.

The Conversation 💬

Your desk, five past five.

DeleOh — you're still here. Good. Well, not good, but — look, have you got a second? It's nothing, it's just — Marchetti's moved the meeting. To Monday. So the pricing deck's — yeah. Could you pick it up?
YouYeah, sure, I can— …sorry. Hang on. Can I look at what I've got on and come back to you in ten?
DeleCourse. It's only a small one, honestly.
YouIt might be. I just don't want to say yes and then let you down quietly on Friday.
Dele…Fair enough. Yeah. Go on then, what have you got?
YouRealistically I've got about a day and a half spare between now and Monday. The deck's two days if it's done properly. So I can do it — but something has to give. Which?
DeleGive? Nothing has to — well. Hm. What's on?
YouThe Renner report and Alina's handover pack. If I take the deck on, the Renner thing lands late. Is that alright?
DeleHow late?
YouWednesday instead of Monday. And I promised Alina Thursday for the handover — I'd rather not be the reason that slips, she's got people waiting on it.
DeleNo, that can't move, she's — no. Right.
YouYou'd know better than me what matters most this week. I'm not saying no. I'm asking you to pick.
Dele…Yeah. Okay. What are my options, realistically?
YouI could do a rough version of the deck by Monday — numbers right, presentation basic — or a proper one by Friday. Or Priya takes the Renner report and I do the deck properly. Those are the three.
DeleRough by Monday's fine. Honestly. Marchetti won't look past the numbers.
YouRough by Monday. That works. Can we say that out loud so we both remember it — rough version Monday, Renner still lands Monday, Alina gets Thursday?
DeleYes. Yeah, that's it. And I'll tell Marchetti it's a first cut.

  2. What three options does You eventually put in front of Dele?
  3. Why does You ask to say the agreement out loud at the end?
1. "Yeah, sure, I can—" cut off by "sorry. Hang on." Buying ten minutes is what stops it; the reflexive yes happens in about two seconds and the only defence is a delay.

2. A rough deck by Monday; a proper deck by Friday; or Priya takes the Renner report so the deck can be done properly. Three real options, all of which You could actually deliver.

3. So that the trade is remembered as a trade. Otherwise next week the deck exists, the Renner report is late, and nobody recalls that a decision was made about it.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for declining upward without ever refusing. Each names a job, and each works on any manager, client or colleague who's handing you something. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Brisk and businesslike, not apologetic. The temptation is to sound sorry, and sorry invites him to reassure you rather than decide — which is how you end up with the work anyway. You're doing capacity planning out loud. Say it the way you'd read out a train timetable.

⏸️ Not saying yes

Phrase 1

The job: stopping the reflexive yes without refusing. Which one buys you the time?

A. "I'm not sure I can, to be honest, I'm pretty snowed under."
B. "Can I look at what I've got on and come back to you in ten?"
C. "Yeah, no problem, leave it with me."
B — "Can I look at what I've got on and come back to you in ten?"
C is the two-second yes that costs you the week. A refuses immediately on a feeling, and "snowed under" is vague enough to be argued with. B refuses nothing — it just moves the decision ten minutes down the road, which is long enough to look at your actual commitments and come back with numbers instead of a mood. Almost every over-commitment happens in the first two seconds; this is the defence.

Take it anywhere: "Can I look at what I've got on and come back to you in ten?"
Phrase 2

The job: framing your caution as loyalty rather than reluctance. Complete it:

"I don't want to say yes and then ___ you down quietly."

"I don't want to say yes and then let you down quietly."
"Quietly" is the word that lands. Every manager has been let down loudly — someone missing a deadline and saying so — and it's survivable. What they actually fear is finding out on Friday afternoon that something they assumed was in hand isn't. Naming that fear puts you visibly on their side while you decline.

Take it anywhere: "I don't want to say yes and then let you down."

⚖️ Making the trade visible

Phrase 3

The job: stating your actual capacity as a fact. One word makes it information rather than complaint. Complete it:

"___ I've got about a day and a half spare."

"Realistically I've got about a day and a half spare."
"Realistically" converts optimism into planning — it signals this is the honest number rather than the hopeful one, and managers hear it as professionalism rather than resistance. Give a quantity in time, not a feeling. "I'm very busy" can be argued with; "a day and a half" can only be worked with.

Take it anywhere: "Realistically I've got about ___."
Phrase 4

The job: the central move — accept the work, name the cost, hand back the choice. Put the pieces in order:

but somethingI can do it —Which?has to give.
"I can do it — but something has to give. Which?"
The whole lesson in eight words. It opens with a yes, so you're not obstructing. Something has to give means something must be sacrificed — a standard, gentle idiom for a trade-off. And the single-word question at the end makes it his decision, which it properly is. He has the information about priorities that you don't.

Take it anywhere: "I can do it — but something has to give. Which?"
Phrase 5

The job: naming the specific casualty, because vague overload gets ignored. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "If I take that on, the Renner thing lands late. Is that alright?"
A named victim gets a decision; general busyness gets sympathy and no change. To land late is everyday English for arriving after its deadline. Ending on "Is that alright?" is deliberate — it's a real question, and it makes him actively accept the consequence rather than passively not notice it.

Take it anywhere: "If I take that on, ___ lands late. Is that alright?"
Phrase 6

The job: protecting a promise you've already made to someone else. Complete it:

"I promised Alina Thursday, and I'd rather not be the ___ that slips."

"I promised Alina Thursday, and I'd rather not be the reason that slips."
Invoking a third party is strong because it isn't about your comfort — someone else is depending on it, and letting them down has a cost your manager can see. It also demonstrates that you keep commitments, which is the same quality he's relying on right now.

Take it anywhere: "I promised ___, and I'd rather not be the reason that slips."

🙌 Handing the decision back

Phrase 7

The job: deferring to his knowledge, genuinely — because he really does know the priorities. Complete it:

"You'd know ___ than me what matters most this week."

"You'd know better than me what matters most this week."
Not flattery — a true statement about who holds which information. You know how long things take; he knows what the business needs. Saying it explicitly makes the handover of the decision feel natural rather than like you're passing him a problem, and it's much harder to resent than "well, you decide then".

Take it anywhere: "You'd know better than me what matters most."
Phrase 8

The job: saying exactly what you're doing, so it can't be reported upstairs as a refusal. Put the pieces in order:

I'm asking youI'm not saying no.to pick.
"I'm not saying no. I'm asking you to pick."
Says the quiet part out loud, and that's what protects you. If he later describes this conversation to anyone, the sentence he'll remember is that you didn't refuse — you asked him to prioritise, which is his job. Two short sentences; the contrast is what makes it stick.

Take it anywhere: "I'm not saying no. I'm asking you to pick."

📐 Offering real options

Phrase 9

The job: giving him a choice between two deliverables rather than yes/no. Complete it:

"I could do a ___ version by Tuesday or a ___ one by Friday."

"I could do a rough version by Tuesday or a proper one by Friday."
Two options beat one apology, and both must be things you can actually deliver. It also quietly teaches him that quality and speed trade against each other, which most people know in theory and forget when they're asking. In the conversation he takes the rough version immediately — because he never needed the polished one.

Take it anywhere: "I could do a rough ___ by ___ or a proper one by ___."
Phrase 10

The job: nailing down what was agreed while you're both still standing there. Which version?

A. "I'll email you to confirm what we've agreed."
B. "Can we say that out loud so we both remember it?"
C. "So that's agreed then, yes?"
B — "Can we say that out loud so we both remember it?"
A is fine and slower, and an email can go unread. C invites a yes without checking you both mean the same thing. B makes one of you restate the whole trade — including the parts that didn't change — while the other can correct it. It's framed as shared memory rather than as getting it on record, so nobody feels they're being pinned down.

Take it anywhere: "Can we say that out loud so we both remember it?"

🔑 Make them yours

These work on anyone handing you work — a manager, a client, a colleague, a committee. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"Realistically I've got about ___." — what's your honest spare capacity this week?

"If I take that on, ___ lands late. Is that alright?" — what's currently at risk of slipping quietly?

"I could do a rough ___ by ___ or a proper one by ___." — where could you offer two versions instead of one?

⚡ The twist

You lay out the trade clearly. Dele listens, nods — and says "do both", then walks off before you can answer.

He hasn't chosen, which means the choice is now yours by default and whatever you drop will be your fault. He's four steps away and moving. You get one sentence.

Before you close the tab…

  • Can you offer two deliverable options instead of one apology?

The Echo 🕰️

Fletcher Christian did not set out to seize a ship. For months he absorbed what Captain Bligh handed down — more sail, more speed, more work, and a running commentary on his failings — and each time he said nothing, because saying something to Bligh was not an available move.

On 28 April 1789 he stopped absorbing it, all at once, with a cutlass. Bligh and eighteen loyal men were put into a seven-metre open boat with a sextant and no charts, and sailed six and a half thousand kilometres to Timor.

Nobody in your office is going over the side. But the pattern is worth noticing: yes, yes, yes, and then something that isn't a no so much as an explosion. The trade said out loud on a Thursday is what prevents it.
