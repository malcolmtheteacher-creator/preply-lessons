Saying No to Your Boss

Thursday, five o'clock, coat half on. Your manager appears at your desk with one more urgent thing and asks if you can just pick it up. You're already full — and "no" isn't a word you can use.

The Scene 🎬

It's Thursday, just gone five. Half the floor has already gone. You've got your bag on your shoulder and one hand on the light switch when Dele, your manager, appears at the end of your desk — not sitting down, which is always a bad sign, because a manager who doesn't sit down is about to ask for something quick.

A client has moved a meeting to Monday morning, and the pricing deck that was due in three weeks is now due in three days. Dele has just been handed this himself, ten minutes ago, by someone above him. He's not being unreasonable and he isn't a bully; he simply hasn't done the arithmetic, and he's hoping you'll say yes so that he doesn't have to.

And here's the honest position you're in. You're already full. Not dramatically full, not I-can't-cope full — just genuinely, ordinarily full, with the Renner report due Thursday and Alina waiting on it. If you say yes to Dele, nothing explodes. Something else quietly slips instead, and nobody finds out until it's a problem, and by then it's your problem. A flat "no, I can't" isn't really available to you — not to your own manager, not on a Thursday, not about a real client. So the move today isn't refusal. It's arithmetic, out loud, in front of him: here's what I've got, here's what stops if I take this, you choose which. You don't refuse the work. You make the trade visible and hand the decision back to the person whose decision it actually is.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of the last time you said yes at work when you knew, somewhere at the back of your mind, that you shouldn't have. What made you say it — and what actually happened to the thing you'd already promised?
  2. In your workplace, if you told your manager "I can do that, but the other job will be late" — what would happen? Would it be heard as useful information or as a complaint? And does that change in English?

Who's who

You
Already Full 🗂️

Reliable, which is exactly the problem — you're the person things get given to because you deliver. Today's job is to stay that person without quietly taking on a fourth thing you can't finish.

The other voice
Dele, Your Manager 🚪

Decent, busy, and halfway out the door. He's just been handed this himself and hasn't worked out what it costs. Show him the sum and he'll do the right thing — but somebody has to show him.

Watch for one thing: in the whole conversation coming up, You never once refuses — there's no "I can't", no "sorry, not this time", nothing Dele could repeat to anyone as a no. And yet Dele walks away having been told no, and the extra work has gone. Read it and see if you can find the exact line where the refusal happened.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's five o'clock and neither of them is at their sharpest, so let the tired bits sound tired. Watch what happens to the sentence You starts and doesn't finish.

DeleOh — you're still here. Right. Good. Well, not good, but — look, have you got a second? It's nothing, it's just — Marchetti's moved the meeting. To Monday. So the pricing deck's — yeah. Could you pick it up?
YouYeah, sure, I can— …sorry. Hang on. Say that again, the deck for when?
DeleMonday. Monday morning. Well — Sunday night, realistically, if I'm honest with you.
YouMm. Right. Can I look at what I've got on and come back to you in ten?
DeleIt's only a small one, honestly. It's basically the deck we did in — was it March? February? You'd be topping and tailing it, that's all. It's not a — it's not a big job.
YouYeah — no, I know. I'm not — it's not the work that worries me. It's just that I don't want to say yes and then let you down quietly. That's the bit I'd rather avoid, for both of us.
Dele…No. No, fair enough. God, it's gone five, hasn't it. Sorry. Go on, what's — where were we?
YouI can do it — but something has to give. Which?
DeleGive? Nothing has to — well. Hm. What have you actually got on?
YouRealistically I've got about a day and a half spare between now and Monday, and the Renner thing's sitting right in the middle of it. So if I take that on, the Renner thing lands late. Is that alright?
DeleAh. Renner's — when's Renner due, Thursday?
YouThursday. And I promised Alina Thursday, and I'd rather not be the reason that slips. You'd know better than me what matters most this week, though — I'm not saying no. I'm asking you to pick.
DeleRight. Hm. That's — yeah, that's not brilliant either way, is it. Hang on. Is there a — is there something in between? Sort of?
YouThere is, yeah. I could do a rough version by Tuesday or a proper one by Friday. If it's the rough one, Renner still lands Thursday and nobody's cross with anybody.
DeleRough one. Rough one's fine. Honestly, Marchetti just wants numbers on a page, he doesn't — yeah. Rough, Tuesday. That'll do.
YouThat works. Can we say that out loud so we both remember it? Rough deck, Tuesday. Renner still Thursday.
DeleRough deck Tuesday, Renner Thursday. Yeah. Right. Go home, it's five o'clock.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Dele never actually chose between the two jobs. He got out of choosing — how? And who put that escape route in front of him?

2. You said you had "about a day and a half" spare. Why is a slightly vague number like that stronger than simply telling your manager you're very busy?

3. At the very end, You asks for something a bit odd — not more time, not help, not a thank you. What is it, and what's it actually for?

1. He found a middle option — a rough deck on Tuesday instead of a finished one — but he only groped towards it ("is there something in between?"), and it was You who supplied the two real shapes it could take, so that Dele's choice was between two actual things rather than a vague wish. Notice the order, because it matters: You made the trade visible first, and only then offered the middle. Offer the middle too early and it just becomes the new full job.
2. Because "I'm very busy" is an opinion, and your manager can disagree with an opinion. A day and a half is a quantity, which sits in the conversation and has to be dealt with. It also sounds like someone who has looked, rather than someone who is protesting — and the "about" is doing real work, since a suspiciously exact figure would sound like a defence you'd prepared.
3. They ask Dele to say the agreement out loud with them. It's not really about memory, whatever the phrase says; it's that a decision only one person heard tends to turn back into your fault later, so repeating it together makes it something you both agreed to rather than something you claimed afterwards.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the moment when you're full and someone senior wants one more thing. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Tone matters more than words here. Every one of these lines can be said in two completely different ways, and the words won't save you if the delivery is wrong. Say them flat, unhurried and slightly bored — the tone of someone reading a train timetable, not someone defending themselves. The moment your voice rises or speeds up, you've turned useful information into a complaint, and your manager will hear a person with a problem rather than a person with a plan. Warm, slow, and a little dull is exactly right.

⏸️ Buying a second instead of saying yes reflexively

Phrase 1

He's asked. Your mouth is already forming "yes". You need a small, unremarkable way to leave the desk with the question still open — nothing dramatic, just a pause with a deadline on it. Put it in order:

and come back to youCan I look atin ten?what I've got on
"Can I look at what I've got on and come back to you in ten?"
The reflexive yes is the enemy, because it happens before you've done any arithmetic, and once it's out you're committed. This buys you the ten minutes you need to work out what the job actually costs. It works because it's so small — you're not refusing anything, you're just asking for a few minutes, and almost nobody says no to that. "What I've got on" is simply the work already in your diary. Say it as you're picking your bag up, not as a stand-off. Similar: "Give me till the morning and I'll tell you properly."
Phrase 2

He's pressing, gently. You want to explain why you're hesitating — and the honest reason isn't that you don't want the job, it's that you're worried about what an easy yes would do to him later. Which line says that?

A. "I'm too busy, sorry. You'll have to find someone else."
B. "I don't want to say yes and then let you down quietly."
C. "Fine — I'll squeeze it in somewhere, don't worry about it."
B — "I don't want to say yes and then let you down quietly."
This is the line that changes the temperature, because it puts you and your manager on the same side of the problem rather than opposite sides of it. To let someone down quietly is to fail them without telling them — the work simply arrives late one day and it's too late to do anything about it, which is the worst outcome for both of you and the one your easy yes was heading towards. A is a refusal, and it invites an argument about how busy you really are. C is precisely the quiet letting-down, said out loud in a cheerful voice.

⚖️ Making the trade visible

Phrase 3

Now the core move, in eleven words. You accept that the work is doable, you state that it costs something, and you stop talking. Complete the chunk: "I can do it — but something has to ___. Which?"

"I can do it — but something has to give. Which?"
If something has to give, it means the situation can't hold as it is and one part of it must bend or break — it's the image of a shelf with too much on it. The phrase is doing three jobs at once: "I can do it" removes any argument about your willingness, "something has to give" states the physics of the week, and the one-word question hands him the choice. And then you stop, which is the hard part — the silence after "Which?" belongs to him, so don't fill it by helpfully suggesting what could give. That's his sentence to finish.
Phrase 4

Your manager wants you to take on an extra audit review. There is one specific thing in your week that will arrive late as a result, and he doesn't know that yet. Name it, out loud, and then ask him to accept it — in one sentence, in your own words. Say your version first.

"If I take that on, the Renner thing lands late. Is that alright?"
To take something on is to accept it as your responsibility, and when work lands late it simply arrives after the day it was promised — "lands" is worth having, because it's what deadlines do in British offices. The whole strength of the sentence is the naming: not "something will slip" but the Renner thing, a real job with a real person waiting for it, which is much harder to wave away than a general shortage of time. Then "Is that alright?" — friendly, almost casual, and impossible to answer without making a decision. If he says yes, you're covered. If he says no, he's just chosen something else.

🔄 Handing the decision back up

Phrase 5

You genuinely don't know which of the two jobs matters more to the business this week — and he genuinely does. Say so, in a way that flatters him slightly and puts the problem in his hands. Put it in order:

this week.You'd knowwhat matters mostbetter than me
"You'd know better than me what matters most this week."
It's true, which is why it works — he sees more of the business than you do, and prioritising between two client jobs is genuinely his job rather than yours. But it also quietly moves the whole problem across the desk, because once you've agreed that he's the one who knows, he can hardly leave the ranking to you. Said warmly it sounds like respect. Said with a raised eyebrow it sounds sarcastic, so don't.
Phrase 6

He's gone quiet and he's hoping you'll rescue him by just agreeing. You need to make it clear you haven't refused anything — and that the decision is still sitting there, waiting for him. Which line does that?

A. "I can't do it, sorry — you'll have to ask someone else."
B. "Whatever you think is best. I'll manage somehow."
C. "I'm not saying no. I'm asking you to pick."
C — "I'm not saying no. I'm asking you to pick."
This is the sentence the whole lesson is built on, and it's worth learning word for word. The first half takes the refusal off the table, so he can't hear you as difficult or unwilling; the second half puts the choice back where it belongs and, crucially, doesn't let him leave without making it. A is the flat no you can't really use with your own manager. B sounds generous but is the most expensive line in the room — "I'll manage somehow" means you have accepted the whole thing, including whatever silently breaks.

📏 Being specific about capacity

Phrase 7

He asks what you've actually got on. Which answer sounds like a professional who has looked at their week, rather than someone complaining or someone hedging?

A. "I'm absolutely swamped. I've got no time at all, honestly."
B. "I might have a bit of time. Maybe. I'm not really sure."
C. "Realistically I've got about a day and a half spare."
C — "Realistically I've got about a day and a half spare."
A quantity is much harder to argue with than a feeling, which is why this works: your manager can disagree with "swamped" but he can't really disagree with a day and a half, he can only decide what to spend it on. "Realistically" signals that you've allowed for the week going slightly wrong, as weeks do, and "about" keeps it human — an exact figure would sound like a case you'd prepared in advance. A invites the reply "everyone's busy". B leaves him free to assume the answer is yes.
Phrase 8

He's stuck between two bad options and would take a third if one existed. Offer him the same job in two different sizes — one quick and imperfect, one slower and finished — with a real day attached to each. One sentence, your own words. Say it out loud first.

"I could do a rough version by Tuesday or a proper one by Friday."
Most requests arrive with only one imagined shape — the finished thing, by the impossible date — and simply pointing out that quality and speed are two separate dials is often enough to solve the whole problem, because a rough version on Tuesday is frequently all anyone needed. A rough version is unpolished but usable; a proper one is the real thing. Give both a specific day, because two options without dates is just a conversation, whereas two options with dates is a decision. And offer this after you've made the trade visible, never before — if you lead with the middle option, it quietly becomes the new full job and you've traded nothing.

🛡️ Protecting the promise you've already made

Phrase 9

The job at risk isn't just a task — there's a colleague at the other end of it who is expecting it on a particular day. Say whose promise it is and that you'd rather it survived. Put it in order:

that slips.and I'd rather not beI promised Alina Thursday,the reason
"I promised Alina Thursday, and I'd rather not be the reason that slips."
When a date slips, it quietly moves later without anyone deciding that it should. The power of this line is that it isn't about you at all — it puts a named person into the room, and a promise to Alina is a much more solid object than "I'm busy", because your manager now has to override an actual commitment to an actual colleague rather than simply disagree with your sense of your own workload. "I'd rather not be the reason" is deliberately mild; you're not refusing, you're just declining to be the person everyone blames on Friday.
Phrase 10

He's picked. It's fine. But the agreement exists only in the air between two tired people at ten past five. Which line locks it in without sounding like you don't trust him?

A. "That works. Can we say that out loud so we both remember it?"
B. "Great. I'll email you a summary of this conversation for the record."
C. "Okay. Let's hope we both remember what we've just said."
A — "That works. Can we say that out loud so we both remember it?"
An unrepeated decision has a way of turning back into your decision by Friday, so you want it said twice, by both of you, while you're standing there. "So we both remember it" is the polite fiction that makes it possible — it blames the fog of a long week rather than his memory, and nobody can object to it. B does roughly the same job but arrives as a document, which reads as building a case against him. C hopes, which is not a plan.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your desk. Speak your answers out loud FIRST — this is speaking practice, so keep going even when the sentence comes out untidy. Use the timer: your manager is standing up and he wants to go home too.

Role-play timer: 4:00

Round 1 — you're the one being asked 🗂️

New job, same Thursday. Dele wants the supplier audit re-run before Monday. You've already got the Renner report due Thursday and Alina waiting on it. Don't look back at the phrases — reach for them. And don't refuse: nothing you say should be repeatable as a no.

DeleIt's only a small one, honestly. It's the same spreadsheet, you'd just be updating the numbers. Half a day, tops.
DeleRight. Okay. So — what, you can't do it?
DeleWell — what have you actually got on between now and Monday?
DeleHmm. Renner's due Thursday though, isn't it. Alina's been on at me about it.
DeleGod. Is there not some way of doing a bit of both?

Round 2 — now you're Dele 🚪

Swap seats. You're the manager, and this is harder than it looks — because your colleague isn't refusing, they're asking you to choose, and there's no answer that doesn't cost you something. You may not say "do both", you may not say "I'll leave it with you", and you may not walk away without a decision. Somebody's work is going to be late. Decide whose, and say it.

ColleagueYeah — no, I've got it, I understand. Can I look at what I've got on and come back to you in ten?

The Twist

🎯 "Do both."

You've done everything right. You've laid out the day and a half, you've named the job that lands late, you've asked him to pick. And Dele looks at his phone, says "Ah — do both, you'll be fine", and walks off towards the lifts.

Look at what's just happened. He hasn't chosen — he's declined to choose, which means the choice is now yours by default, and whatever you end up dropping on Sunday night will be a thing you dropped. He didn't say no to you. He just left, and left the arithmetic on your desk.

He's four steps away and about to turn the corner. You get one sentence, to his back. It has to stop him, it can't sound like a whine, and it can't be "no". Start the timer, say it out loud — then say three more versions, because the first one is never the right one. Then decide which of the four you'd actually have the nerve to use.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I caught my own reflexive "yes" before it got out, and bought myself a minute instead.
  • I gave a real number for what I had spare, rather than saying I was busy.
  • I named the exact piece of work that would land late, and who was waiting for it.
  • I asked my manager to choose — and then I stopped talking and let the silence sit.
  • I got the agreement said out loud, by both of us, before he walked away.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that'll cost you on Sunday night. Run the twist again and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

"Yes" is the most expensive word at work, and it's expensive precisely because it's free at the moment you say it — the bill doesn't arrive until Sunday night, and by then it's yours alone.

Saying yes to everything looks like helpfulness, and it is usually something else: a way of avoiding one uncomfortable minute of conversation by borrowing against a week you haven't got. The conversation you skipped doesn't disappear, though. It just happens later, without you in the room, when somebody asks why the Renner report was late.

And there's a pattern to who ends up carrying a team that can't prioritise. It isn't the difficult people, who were asked once and said no. It's the reliable ones — the ones who said yes because it was quicker than explaining — and the work quietly flows towards them until something breaks that everybody, by then, agrees was their fault.

Two bigger questions

  1. If you always say yes, your manager never learns what your week actually holds — so is making your capacity visible a favour you're doing yourself, or one you're doing them? And who is really responsible when a team's priorities are never spoken aloud: the person who won't choose, or the people who let them off it?
  2. In some workplaces and some cultures, asking a manager to choose between two jobs would be normal and even welcome. In others it would look like insubordination, or like passing your problem upwards. Where does yours sit — and if the honest answer is that this conversation isn't safe where you work, what does that tell you about the place rather than about you?
