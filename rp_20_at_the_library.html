At the Library

New town, empty bookshelf, and a card to your name that hasn't been used since your student days. Time to sign up — and settle a very old score.

The Scene 🎬

You've just moved to a new town, the boxes are half-unpacked, and the one thing that makes anywhere feel like home is missing: somewhere to read. So this morning you've walked round to the local library — high ceilings, that lovely papery hush, and a desk with a librarian who clearly runs the place like a small, benevolent kingdom.

You want three things: to join, to track down a novel whose title you can only half-remember, and to find out what else this place can do for you. What you don't expect is the little surprise waiting on the system when they look you up.

Behind the desk: a librarian who knows every shelf by heart, has forgiven a thousand late returns, and can tell in about four seconds whether you're going to be a pleasure or a problem. Be a pleasure.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last borrow something from a library — a book, a film, a quiet corner to work in? If it's been years, what pulled you away from them?
  2. Imagine every book you've ever loved sat on one shelf, for a stranger to browse. What would that shelf say about you — and which book would you quietly turn face-in so they didn't judge you?

Who's who

You
The New Member 📖

Freshly moved in, keen to belong somewhere. You want a card, a specific book, and the lay of the land — and you'd like to charm rather than bother.

The other voice
The Librarian 🗂️

Guardian of the shelves and the rules. Warm, dry, endlessly patient — and holding a small piece of your past you'd completely forgotten about.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how the member handles the awkward moment — the old fine — without getting defensive. Owning a small mistake gracefully is a proper B2/C1 skill.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the soft openings, the way a possible argument is quietly defused, and how one polite question keeps the visit moving.

LibrarianMorning! You look like someone on a mission. What can I do for you?
YouI was rather hoping to join, actually — sign up for a card, if now's a good moment? I've just moved into the area and my flat is depressingly bookless.
LibrarianWell, we can't have that. Welcome to the neighbourhood. I'll just need some ID and something with your new address on it — a bill, a bank letter, that sort of thing.
YouAhead of you — driving licence and a bank letter. I should say, I think I may have had a card here years ago, back when I was a student. Will that complicate things?

YouOh — there must be some sort of mix-up, I don't remember… actually, no. You know what, that'll be that dreadful poetry anthology I never took back. That's entirely my fault, I'm afraid. Can I settle it now and clear the slate?
LibrarianHonesty like that should be rewarded. It's cleared — pay it whenever suits, no rush. Right, you're a fully paid-up member again. What else can I do for you?
YouSince you ask — I'm trying to track down a book, but I've only got half the title. I wonder if you could point me in the right direction? It's a novel, something about a light between two oceans…

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The old fine could easily have turned into a squabble. How does the member handle the moment they don't recognise it — and what changes the instant they realise it really is theirs?

2. The book the member wants isn't on the shelf. What TWO things does the librarian arrange so they don't walk away empty-handed?

3. The member asks about two different services in a single breath. What are they — and what condition does the librarian quietly attach to each?

1. At first they gently flag it as a possible mistake ("there must be some sort of mix-up") rather than accusing anyone — buying time. The moment they remember it's real (the poetry anthology), they switch instantly to owning it: "that's entirely my fault", and offer to pay. No defensiveness, no drama — and the librarian rewards the honesty by taking the pressure off.
2. The copy is out on loan until a week on Tuesday, so the librarian places a hold/reservation on it AND promises a text when it returns — holding it behind the desk with the member's name on it for a week.
3. A quiet study room (first floor, free — but you must sign in at the desk first) and online renewals (via the app, but only twice, and not if someone else has reserved the title).

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for joining, asking, and smoothing over a small problem — at this library and anywhere a desk stands between you and what you want. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚪 Getting through the door

Phrase 1

You'd like to sign up, but the desk looks busy and you'd rather not sound like you're barking an order. Which opener is warm, tentative, and impossible to be annoyed by?

A. "I want a library card."
B. "Give me a membership, please."
C. "I was hoping to sign up for a card, if now's a good moment?"
C — "I was hoping to sign up for a card, if now's a good moment?"
"I was hoping to…" softens a request into a wish rather than a demand, and "if now's a good moment?" hands the other person control of the timing. Endlessly reusable: "I was hoping to have a quick word, if now's a good moment?"
Phrase 2

You'd like the librarian to check something on the system — but you don't want it to sound like an instruction. There's a four-word tag you can hang on the end that makes it a courtesy: "Could you check that for me, ___ ___ ___ ___?"

"…if you wouldn't mind."
It quietly acknowledges you're asking for their effort, not owed it. Just as handy: "when you get a chance" and "if it's no trouble" — all of them turn a command into a favour.

🔍 Finding what you need

Phrase 3

You're after a book but you can barely remember the title, and you need the librarian on your side, not sighing. What's an elegant way to admit you're a bit lost AND ask for their guidance in one go?

"I'm trying to track down a book — I wonder if you could point me in the right direction?"
"Track down" makes the hunt sound fun rather than annoying; "I wonder if you could…" is one of the gentlest requests in English; and "point me in the right direction" asks for help without demanding they do it all for you.
Phrase 4

The book you want is out on loan. You'd like them to reserve it for you. Put the pieces in order to make the request:

any chanceput a hold on it?I couldIs there
"Is there any chance I could put a hold on it?"
"Is there any chance I could…?" treats a routine service as a small favour, which makes people want to say yes. Note the library verb: to put a hold on / reserve a book that's currently out.

🧾 When something's not right

Phrase 5

The screen shows a fine you don't recognise. Your instinct might be to protest — but you're not certain it's wrong, and picking a fight helps no one. Which reaction leaves you room to be proved wrong gracefully?

A. "That's wrong. I never got a fine."
B. "There must be some sort of mix-up — I don't remember a fine on my account."
B — "There must be some sort of mix-up…"
A accuses; B wonders. "Some sort of mix-up" blames a vague situation, not the person, and leaves you a dignified exit if it turns out the fine is genuinely yours. You've queried it without burning any bridges.
Phrase 6

It turns out the fine IS yours — that book you never returned. Now the classy move is to own it instantly, warmly, no excuses. What do you say?

"You know what — that's entirely my fault, I'm afraid."
Taking the blame cleanly ("entirely my fault") actually makes people like you more, not less. "You know what…" signals the honest change of heart, and "I'm afraid" adds a light, rueful apology. Try it whenever you've slipped up: it disarms almost anyone.
Phrase 7

Suppose the fine were bigger and money were tight. You want to ask them to cancel it as a one-off — but you must sound hopeful, not entitled. What softly-worded plea might just work?

"I don't suppose there's any way you could waive it, just this once?"
"I don't suppose…" politely expects a no; "just this once" promises you won't make a habit of it. Note the exact verb: to waive a fine / a fee = to let someone off paying it.

🛎️ Services & favours

Phrase 8

You'd heard the library has a quiet room to work in, but you're not sure. Rather than baldly asking "do you have one?", how can you check a fact politely, as if you half-know it already?

"Am I right in thinking you've got a quiet study room upstairs?"
"Am I right in thinking…?" is a beautifully smooth way to confirm something — it flatters the listener as the expert and softens the question if you've got it wrong. Everywhere uses: "Am I right in thinking the last train's at eleven?"
Phrase 9

Your loan is nearly up and you'd like to keep the books longer. Which request sounds like a considerate grown-up rather than a demand?

A. "I want to keep these books longer."
B. "Would it be possible to renew these for another couple of weeks?"
B — "Would it be possible to renew these for another couple of weeks?"
"Would it be possible to…?" leaves space for a no and treats the extension as their decision, not your right. Note the verb: to renew a book / a loan / a membership = to extend it.

🌟 A proper goodbye

Phrase 10

They've signed you up, cleared the fine, reserved your book and shown you round. Which exit line will they still be pleased about at closing time?

A. "Thanks."
B. "Thank you for your assistance in this matter."
C. "You've been incredibly helpful — I really appreciate you sorting all that out."
C — "You've been incredibly helpful — I really appreciate you sorting all that out."
A is bare; B sounds like a formal complaint letter. "Incredibly helpful" praises the person, and "I really appreciate you sorting all that out" names exactly what they did — specific thanks always land warmer than a generic "thanks".
Quick test: run the whole visit out loud using all ten, in order — soft opening → smooth questions → the awkward fine → services → goodbye. When it flows without a wobble, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

🌙 The reference-only wall

One more round — no script, no safety net. It's the night before a big exam and the library shuts in twenty minutes. The ONE book that covers everything you need is right here — but it's the only copy, and it's stamped "reference only: not for loan". Worse, that forgotten fine is still blocking your account, so you couldn't borrow anything even if they let you. Start the timer and talk your way through it: could they waive the fine just this once? Could you photocopy the key chapter, camp in the study room until closing, or have them hold the book behind the desk for the second it opens tomorrow? One rule: the instant you sound entitled or start pleading like it's their emergency, the shutters come down — and so does your grade.

The Echo 🕰️

More than two thousand years ago, in the Egyptian city of Alexandria, someone had an outrageous idea: gather every book in the world under one roof. Ships that docked in the harbour were searched — not for smuggled goods, but for scrolls to copy for the shelves.

That dream half-came-true and then, famously, half-burned down. But it never quite died. Every time you tap a card on a desk and walk out with a book that isn't yours to keep, you're a member of the same impossible club: a place that lets a stranger borrow the whole of human knowledge and simply asks them to bring it back on time.

Your fine was two pounds forty. In Alexandria, a late scroll could cost you rather more.
