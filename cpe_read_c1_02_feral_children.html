The question of whether language is innate or learned has haunted linguistics for centuries. Few cases illuminate this debate more starkly than the accounts of children allegedly raised in isolation from human language—and even more rarely, by animals. These cases, though often anecdotal and methodologically flawed, reveal something crucial about the critical period for language acquisition.

Victor of Aveyron, discovered in 1800 in rural France, exemplifies the tragic consequences of linguistic deprivation. The boy, estimated to be twelve years old, had apparently survived alone in the forest for years, with no evidence of human language exposure. When brought into civilisation and placed under the tutelage of Dr. Jean Itard, Victor displayed severe deficits. His vocalizations were crude, consisting largely of grunts and cries. Despite years of intensive training, Itard could teach Victor only a handful of words. Victor never acquired grammatical structure, syntax, or the ability to understand abstract concepts. The case suggested that without exposure to language during childhood, the neural pathways underlying linguistic competence do not develop properly.

More recent and better-documented is the case of Genie, discovered in Los Angeles in 1970. Genie's parents had kept her in isolation and severe abuse from infancy, deliberately preventing language exposure. She was thirteen when discovered. Like Victor, Genie initially displayed almost no language capacity—her vocabulary consisted of single words, with no grammatical understanding. However, unlike Victor, Genie showed dramatic progress during her first few years of exposure to language. She began combining words, understanding syntax, and acquiring new vocabulary at a rate suggesting genuine language learning. Yet even after years of intensive instruction, Genie plateaued. She never achieved native-like fluency or full grammatical competence. Her comprehension remained ahead of her production, and she struggled particularly with syntax and abstract language.

Both cases suggest the existence of a critical period for language acquisition—a window during which the brain is neurologically optimised for acquiring linguistic competence. Modern neuroscience supports this view. The left hemisphere, dominant in language processing, undergoes significant developmental changes through childhood and early adolescence. Damage to language-dominant regions during this period can be partially compensated by the right hemisphere, but after puberty, this plasticity decreases dramatically. Genie's plateau around age eighteen suggests that critical period closure coincides with neurological maturation in adolescence.

What makes these cases particularly tragic is that they reveal language not as a simple tool but as foundational to cognition itself. Children deprived of language during the critical period show deficits not merely in communication but in reasoning, temporal understanding, and even emotional regulation. Language shapes thought; without early exposure, the capacity for complex thought itself is diminished. The cases of Victor and Genie remind us that language acquisition is neither straightforward imitation nor simply dependent on intelligence. Rather, it requires a specific developmental window, adequate input, and—seemingly—a degree of social interaction. Remove any of these elements, and the result is not merely linguistic poverty but cognitive impoverishment.

Innate

Present naturally from birth; not learned but existing from the beginning.

Critical period

A window of time in development when the brain is optimized for acquiring specific skills.

Linguistic deprivation

Lack of exposure to language during developmental years.

Vocalizations

Sounds or utterances produced by the voice.

Syntax

The arrangement of words and phrases to create well-formed sentences.

Plasticity

The brain's ability to reorganize and adapt, especially after damage.

Plateau

To reach a level and stop progressing further.

Impoverishment

The state of being made poor; lacking in necessary resources or abilities.
