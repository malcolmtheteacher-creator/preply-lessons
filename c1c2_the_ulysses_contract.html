The Ulysses Contract

C1/C2 Level · Why we deliberately bind our future selves

The case for binding yourself

Two and a half thousand years ago, Homer's Ulysses had himself bound to the mast of his ship so that he could safely hear the sirens' song without leaping to his death. We have rather romanticised the image ever since — as though pre-commitment were a one-off literary moment rather than the basic posture of a serious life. In a world without sirens, ordinary willpower would do. We are not in such a world. We are now surrounded by an entire professional industry whose business model is to engineer our future selves into bad decisions — in real time, at scale, on devices built to be unputdownable. Against forces of that kind, the Ulysses contract has quietly stopped being a curiosity and become the only honest response.

Speak · press on the position

The working claim of this lesson is straightforward: in modern conditions, binding your future self is the right move, not a confession of weakness. Answer each of these in two or three full sentences. Defend, refine, complicate — but don't drift back into "is it bad faith?" That argument is over.

  1. Where in your own life have you already lashed yourself to a mast — and where do you now suspect you should have, but didn't?
  2. Someone who refuses to pre-commit in modern conditions is, you'd argue, doing what exactly? Choose your words carefully.
  3. We have inherited a rather romantic notion that "real" self-control is willpower in the moment. Argue that this notion is now obsolete — and what has replaced it.
  4. Which industries depend on people not signing Ulysses contracts? What does that quietly tell us about whose side the design of modern life is on?
Speak · sharpen the claim

Below are four sharper versions of the working position. Pick the one you most want to defend, and defend it for two minutes. Then pick the one you find weakest, and shore it up — even if you'd rather discard it.

  • (a) "In an attention economy, the unbound self is not the free one. It is the harvested one."
  • (b) "Every serious adult in 2026 is operating, knowingly or otherwise, a portfolio of Ulysses contracts. The only question is whether they are well-designed."
  • (c) "Pre-commitment is no longer a philosophical exercise. It is a basic act of self-respect under conditions of asymmetric warfare."
  • (d) "The person who insists on facing every temptation 'on its merits, in the moment' has misunderstood the moment."

Eight chunks to put to work

a Ulysses contract A pre-commitment device the present self uses to constrain a future, weaker self — named after the Homeric image.
to bind one's future self To restrict, in advance, what the later version of you will be able to do; the verb phrase at the heart of the topic.
akrasia Greek philosophical term: weakness of will — the strange capacity to know the better course and choose the worse anyway.
pre-commitment The technical term — from economics and decision theory — for any device that locks in a future choice while you can still think clearly.
weakness of will The everyday English equivalent of akrasia — the failure to act on what one knows to be right.
temptation-proof Adjective: structured so that the relevant temptation simply cannot be acted upon, however strongly it is felt.
to second-guess one's earlier wisdom To doubt, in hindsight, a decision the past self made in good faith — often the precise moment a well-designed contract is doing exactly the work it was built to do.
to lash oneself to the mast The central metaphor of the whole topic, useable as a vivid idiom for any deliberate, irreversible pre-commitment.

One paragraph. Then we talk.

Read this once, slowly, aloud. It is here to sharpen the position we have already arrived at — not to reopen it. The conversation that follows is the point.

The Norwegian philosopher Jon Elster, writing in 1979, treated the Ulysses contract as a curious philosophical paradox: the present self binding the future self, both fascinating and faintly suspect. Forty years later, the picture has changed beyond recognition. We now live in conditions Elster could only partially imagine — surrounded by industries whose entire business model is to engineer our future selves into bad decisions, optimised in real time on devices built to be unputdownable. Against such forces, raw willpower is a luxury good, available in vanishing supply at unpredictable moments. The Ulysses contract has quietly stopped being a philosophical curiosity and become, for serious people, the only honest response — not a confession of weakness, but a clear-eyed reading of a battlefield that has, in our lifetimes, been comprehensively redrawn.

Phrasing · read aloud, pausing at each dash and comma. The sentences are deliberately long and need to breathe at their joints.

Two quick orientations before we open it out:

1. In one sentence each, what changed between 1979 and now — and what does that change do to the moral status of the Ulysses contract?

Show Answer
What changed: our future selves are now systematically targeted by industries built to engineer poor decisions on optimised, unputdownable platforms. What that does: moves the contract from "interesting paradox" to "basic self-defence" — an act of self-respect under conditions of deliberate asymmetric pressure, not a small drama of mistrust between yesterday's self and tomorrow's.

2. Why does the writer call raw willpower "a luxury good"?

Show Answer
Because the conditions under which willpower can carry the day — quiet, predictable, low-frequency temptation — have largely disappeared from modern life. The person who tries to rely on willpower alone is implicitly assuming a battlefield that no longer exists; willpower is now available only in unpredictable, exhausting flashes, and counting on it is the privilege of those who have not yet been comprehensively studied by people whose job it is to outflank them.
Speak · sharpen, don't relitigate

No writing. The position is settled: in modern conditions, binding the future self is right. The conversation now is about how, where, and against what — not whether.

  1. Name three industries in your own daily life that depend, structurally, on your refusing to pre-commit. What contract would each of them most want you not to sign?
  2. What does a well-designed Ulysses contract have that a clumsy one does not? Give a worked example of each from your own experience.
  3. Where, right now, do you suspect you are flying without a contract you should have signed? What's stopping you?
  4. Is there a single area of modern life in which raw willpower is still the appropriate tool? If so, what makes it different? If not, what does that tell us?

Formal hypothetical structures & the mandative subjunctive

The topic calls for a higher register: a barrister's English, a careful philosopher's English. Three clusters do most of the work. Read the cards; close them; then go straight into the oral drill below.

Inverted conditionals

Drop the if; invert the auxiliary.

Were he to hear the sirens unbound, he would surely leap.

Had he not lashed himself to the mast, the voyage would have ended in disaster.

Should you hesitate, the moment will pass.

Mandative subjunctive

After suggest / insist / propose / recommend / essential / vital / crucial that: bare infinitive, no -s.

It is essential that the future self not be able to reverse the decision.

She insisted that he be present at the signing.

Formal concessives & lest

Carriers of the higher register.

Be that as it may, the contract stands.

She bound herself in advance, lest she lose her resolve.

However compelling the case for release, …

Speak · oral transformation drill

Your teacher reads a flat sentence in everyday English. You reformulate it on the spot, aloud, using the structure indicated. Don't write. Each item has a model answer hidden below.

1. Flat: "If he hadn't bound himself, the ship would have been lost."   →   use inverted Had…

Show Answer
Had he not bound himself, the ship would have been lost.

2. Flat: "If a court ever enforced such a contract, it would set a dangerous precedent."   →   use inverted Were…

Show Answer
Were a court ever to enforce such a contract, it would set a dangerous precedent.

3. Flat: "If you happen to hesitate, even briefly, the moment will pass."   →   use inverted Should…

Show Answer
Should you hesitate, even briefly, the moment will pass.

4. Flat: "It's really important that he doesn't change his mind in the meantime."   →   use mandative subjunctive after It is essential that…

Show Answer
It is essential that he not change his mind in the meantime. (Note: bare subjunctive after "essential that"; no -s; negation as not, not doesn't.)

5. Flat: "She bound herself in advance because she didn't want to lose her resolve."   →   use lest…

Show Answer
She bound herself in advance, lest she lose her resolve. (Note the bare subjunctive lose, not loses.)

6. Flat: "The contract may look harsh. Still, the original reasoning was sound."   →   use Be that as it may…

Show Answer
The contract may look harsh. Be that as it may, the original reasoning was sound.
Speak · live micro-task

Pick the most controversial Ulysses contract you have ever heard of (yours, someone else's, historical, hypothetical). In sixty seconds, defend it — using at least two of the structures above (inversion, subjunctive, lest, be that as it may, etc.). Speak; don't write.

Three speaking tasks — pick all three

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · the case for the contract (4 minutes)

Tell the story of a specific Ulysses contract you have operated — and make the case for it. This is not a confessional. It is a closing argument.

  1. What were you binding yourself against, and what about modern conditions made willpower-in-the-moment a non-starter?
  2. What mechanism did you use, and why was that one well-designed for the threat?
  3. What did the contract make possible that would otherwise have been impossible?
  4. If a thoughtful friend told you they were going to handle the same problem on willpower alone, what would you tell them?
  5. What's the next contract you should sign, and what's quietly stopping you?

Weave in at least three vocabulary chunks from Tab 2 and at least two formal structures from Tab 4.

Task B · demolish the cliche (3 minutes)

A reasonable-sounding friend says to you, over dinner:

"Honestly, all this pre-commitment stuff is just a sign of weakness. If you really wanted to change, you'd just… do it. Locking yourself out of your own choices is what people do when they don't trust themselves — and you should trust yourself."

Demolish this, in full sentences, for three minutes. Be specific about modern conditions. Use at least one inversion and one mandative subjunctive. Do not concede the ground that "trusting yourself" is the more grown-up posture; argue that the cliche has it backwards.

Task C · role-play across time (4 minutes)

Your teacher plays the version of you ten years from now, looking back at a Ulysses contract you are about to sign today — and quietly resenting it. You — your present self — must explain to that older self why you signed, why the resentment is in fact the very thing the contract was designed to outlast, and why the older self should, on reflection, be grateful rather than aggrieved.

The point of this is not to wobble. The point is to feel, in your own mouth, how the present self carries a kind of responsibility for the future self that the future self — under modern pressure — will not always have the resources to discharge alone.

Final open discussion

  1. If pre-commitment is now the basic posture of a serious life, what does the absence of any well-designed Ulysses contracts in someone's life tell you about that person?
  2. What would it look like to teach a teenager, today, to think in terms of contracts with their future self — without sounding either preachy or paranoid?
  3. Some Ulysses contracts are private (savings locks, blocking software); others are public (announcements, witnessed promises). When does each kind do its job best, and when does each fail?
  4. Imagine an industry, a profession, or even a country deliberately designing itself around well-made Ulysses contracts at every level. What would that look like — and what would it be willing to give up to get there?
