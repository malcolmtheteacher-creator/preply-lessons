The Ulysses Contract

C1/C2 Level · Why we deliberately bind our future selves

Bound to the mast

Two and a half thousand years ago, Homer's Ulysses had himself bound to the mast of his ship so that he could safely hear the sirens' song without leaping to his death. Two and a half thousand years later, we delete apps from our phones, sign contracts we already know we will regret, and pour bottles down sinks before difficult weekends. The mechanism is identical. The phenomenon has a name: a Ulysses contract.

Speak · first reactions

Answer each of these in two or three full sentences, not one. Push past your first instinct.

  1. When did you last deliberately make life harder for your future self? In hindsight, was it the right call?
  2. We trust the present self when we sign these contracts. Why? On what grounds?
  3. Is the freedom to bind oneself a higher form of freedom — or a quiet admission that you do not trust your own judgement?
  4. Some philosophers argue these contracts are inherently a kind of bad faith: the present self silencing the future self before it can object. Steelman that position before you reject it.

Eight chunks to put to work

a Ulysses contract A pre-commitment device the present self uses to constrain a future, weaker self — named after the Homeric image.
to bind one's future self To restrict, in advance, what the later version of you will be able to do; the verb phrase at the heart of the topic.
akrasia Greek philosophical term: weakness of will — the strange capacity to know the better course and choose the worse anyway.
pre-commitment The technical term — from economics and decision theory — for any device that locks in a future choice while you can still think clearly.
weakness of will The everyday English equivalent of akrasia — the failure to act on what one knows to be right.
temptation-proof Adjective: structured so that the relevant temptation simply cannot be acted upon, however strongly it is felt.
to second-guess one's earlier wisdom To doubt, in hindsight, a decision the past self made in good faith — often the moment a Ulysses contract starts to chafe.
to lash oneself to the mast The central metaphor of the whole topic, useable as a vivid idiom for any deliberate, irreversible pre-commitment.

One paragraph. Then we talk.

Read this once, slowly, aloud. It is here to give us shared ground — not to be analysed to death. The conversation that follows is the point.

The Norwegian philosopher Jon Elster spent much of his career on the curious problem the Greeks called akrasia — our well-documented ability, knowing perfectly well what is in our interest, to act against it anyway. In his 1979 book Ulysses and the Sirens, he argued something both simple and discomforting. Most of us, he wrote, are too clear-sighted about our own weakness of will to leave our future selves to chance. We bind those selves in advance — through savings locks, blocking software, public promises, advance directives — precisely because we do not, on the evidence, trust them. A Ulysses contract, on this account, is not a confession of weakness so much as a strange, sober form of self-knowledge: I know what I am like; therefore I shall act now to constrain what I am about to become.

Phrasing · read aloud, pausing at each dash and comma. The sentences are deliberately long and need to breathe at their joints.

Two quick orientations before we open it out:

1. In one sentence each, what is Elster's claim and what is the implicit insult inside it?

Show Answer
Claim: we bind our future selves because we know, on the evidence, that they are not trustworthy. Implicit insult: the future self is being treated, by the present self, as a less reliable agent — one whose freedom must be curtailed by someone wiser, namely me-as-I-am-now.

2. Why does the writer call this "a strange, sober form of self-knowledge"?

Show Answer
Because it requires honestly facing the kind of person you actually are — not the heroic, future-tense self you would like to be. The "sobriety" is the absence of self-flattery; the "strangeness" is that the self-knowledge expresses itself, paradoxically, in an act of mistrust toward yourself.
Speak · press on it

No writing. Pick whichever of these provokes you most, and run with it for as long as you can hold the argument together.

  1. What is the most generous interpretation of Elster's position — and what is the most savage critique?
  2. Where, in your own life right now, do you suspect you are quietly operating a Ulysses contract you have not yet named?
  3. Is there anything in this paragraph you simply do not believe? Defend the disagreement.
  4. Elster says we bind the future self because we do not trust it. Could the same act be done in good faith — out of love or care for the future self — rather than out of mistrust? What would change if so?

Formal hypothetical structures & the mandative subjunctive

The topic calls for a higher register: a barrister's English, a careful philosopher's English. Three clusters do most of the work. Read the cards; close them; then go straight into the oral drill below.

Inverted conditionals

Drop the if; invert the auxiliary.

Were he to hear the sirens unbound, he would surely leap.

Had he not lashed himself to the mast, the voyage would have ended in disaster.

Should you hesitate, the moment will pass.

Mandative subjunctive

After suggest / insist / propose / recommend / essential / vital / crucial that: bare infinitive, no -s.

It is essential that the future self not be able to reverse the decision.

She insisted that he be present at the signing.

Formal concessives & lest

Carriers of the higher register.

Be that as it may, the contract stands.

She bound herself in advance, lest she lose her resolve.

However compelling the case for release, …

Speak · oral transformation drill

Your teacher reads a flat sentence in everyday English. You reformulate it on the spot, aloud, using the structure indicated. Don't write. Each item has a model answer hidden below.

1. Flat: "If he hadn't bound himself, the ship would have been lost."   →   use inverted Had…

Show Answer
Had he not bound himself, the ship would have been lost.

2. Flat: "If a court ever enforced such a contract, it would set a dangerous precedent."   →   use inverted Were…

Show Answer
Were a court ever to enforce such a contract, it would set a dangerous precedent.

3. Flat: "If you happen to hesitate, even briefly, the moment will pass."   →   use inverted Should…

Show Answer
Should you hesitate, even briefly, the moment will pass.

4. Flat: "It's really important that he doesn't change his mind in the meantime."   →   use mandative subjunctive after It is essential that…

Show Answer
It is essential that he not change his mind in the meantime. (Note: bare subjunctive after "essential that"; no -s; negation as not, not doesn't.)

5. Flat: "She bound herself in advance because she didn't want to lose her resolve."   →   use lest…

Show Answer
She bound herself in advance, lest she lose her resolve. (Note the bare subjunctive lose, not loses.)

6. Flat: "The contract may look harsh. Still, the original reasoning was sound."   →   use Be that as it may…

Show Answer
The contract may look harsh. Be that as it may, the original reasoning was sound.

Three speaking tasks — pick all three

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · personal monologue (4 minutes)

Tell the story of a specific Ulysses contract from your own life. Trace it honestly:

  1. What were you binding yourself against, and what evidence did you have that you couldn't be trusted?
  2. What mechanism did you use to make the future option impossible — or merely costly?
  3. When the moment of temptation arrived, did the future self thank you, or curse you?
  4. With hindsight, was the binding self wiser — or simply more frightened than the moment required?
  5. Would you sign the same contract again, knowing what you now know?

Weave in at least three vocabulary chunks from Tab 2 and at least two formal structures from Tab 4.

Task B · devil's advocate (3 minutes)

Argue, with full force and as if you believed it, for the claim:

"All pre-commitment is, at root, a form of bad faith. It is the present self silencing the future self before it can object — and no amount of philosophical decoration changes that."

You may, in fact, disagree. Argue it anyway. Use at least one inversion and one mandative subjunctive. Steelman the position; do not strawman it.

Task C · role-play across time (4 minutes)

Your teacher plays the version of you looking back, ten years from now, on a Ulysses contract you signed today. That older self regrets it. You — your present self — must explain to that older self why you signed it, and why the reasoning still stands.

Then swap. You become the older, regretful self. Your teacher becomes the younger, contracting self. Now make the case for letting yourself off.

The point is not to win. The point is to feel, in your own mouth, how unstable the moral ground actually is.

Final open discussion

  1. Modern life offers more pre-commitment devices than any previous era — savings locks, blocking apps, automatic deductions, public promises. Are we genuinely freer for them, or quietly admitting we cannot govern ourselves?
  2. At what point does a Ulysses contract become an act of bad faith — the past self refusing to let the future self think?
  3. Is there a meaningful moral difference between binding oneself to do something good and binding oneself to avoid something bad?
  4. If we imagine the past self and the future self as two different people, who actually owns the decision — and on what authority?
