The Needham Question

The story, in three parts

A true story in three parts — a man, a question, and the scientists who inherited it. Every experiment, date and number here is real. Read each part; after it, try the quick check from memory, then talk about the question.

Part 1 — Two characters for 'cigarette'

In 1937 Joseph Needham was thirty-six, a Cambridge biochemist with a growing reputation, a wife who was also a distinguished scientist, and no particular interest in China. That autumn three young Chinese scientists arrived in his laboratory to do research. One of them was Lu Gwei-djen, the daughter of a pharmacist from Nanjing, who had trained in biochemistry and who had grown up with a knowledge Needham did not possess: that her civilisation had a scientific and technical past two thousand years deep.

In February 1938 he asked her how to say 'cigarette' in Chinese. She said xiangyan — fragrant smoke — and wrote the two characters, guiding his hand through the strokes. He later said it was the moment his life changed direction. Through 1938 and 1939 he studied Chinese with a passion that alarmed his colleagues, learned the classical language privately with the university's professor of Chinese, and began to read what Lu had told him about: treatises on astronomy, medicine and engineering that were older than anything comparable in Europe.

What he found unsettled everything he had been taught. Paper, printing, the magnetic compass, gunpowder — the four inventions that had, in Francis Bacon's phrase, changed the whole face of the world — were all Chinese, and so were cast iron, the stirrup, the wheelbarrow, the seismograph, and the recognition of sunspots and supernovae centuries before European astronomers accepted that the heavens could change. For most of recorded history China had been the more advanced civilisation. And yet the thing we call modern science — Galileo's experiments, Newton's laws, the idea that nature runs on mathematical rules that any person can test — had happened in a small corner of western Europe in the seventeenth century, and not in China.

Why? That was the question. Needham would spend the remaining fifty-seven years of his life trying to answer it, and it now carries his name: the Needham Question. What he did not yet know, in 1938, was that the question had a second half, and that the second half was about him. Why had a scientist as curious as Needham never once wondered about Chinese science until a colleague wrote two characters on a piece of paper?

Part 2 — Thirty thousand miles

War gave him his chance. From 1942 to 1946 Needham ran the Sino-British Science Co-operation Office in Chongqing, the wartime capital, with the job of getting instruments, chemicals and journals to Chinese scientists cut off by the Japanese occupation. He turned it into an expedition. In lorries, on trains and on foot he travelled the length of unoccupied China — he put the distance at thirty thousand miles — visiting universities that had retreated inland and were teaching in temples and caves, and buying, begging and copying every old scientific text he could find. By the time he came home he had a library, a network of collaborators, and the outline of an answer.

The first volume of Science and Civilisation in China appeared in 1954. Needham planned seven volumes; the project has now passed twenty-seven, and it continued after his death. What he argued, across all of them, was that the Question could not be answered by saying that Chinese people thought differently. Chinese astronomers had observed more carefully than Europeans; Chinese engineers had built better; Chinese doctors had recorded more. The difference was not in the minds. It was in the arrangements around the minds.

His candidates were institutional. Imperial China was run by a scholar-bureaucracy selected by examination in the Confucian classics, which drew the ablest minds of every generation into administration and literature rather than into nature. Merchants — the class that in Europe paid for instruments, voyages and printing — were held in low esteem and kept under the state's thumb. And the Chinese picture of the cosmos was of an organism in harmony, not of a machine obeying laws; there was no divine lawgiver, and so, Needham suggested, the very idea that nature has 'laws' that a human being could discover and write down did not seem self-evident, as it did to Kepler and Newton. Europe, fragmented into competing states, quarrelling churches and free cities, could not stop new questions being asked; China, unified and stable, did not need to.

Needham died in 1995, aged ninety-four. His wife Dorothy had died in 1987, and in 1989, when he was eighty-eight, he married Lu Gwei-djen, who had worked beside him on the books for fifty years. The Question outlived them both. And in the decade after his death it was taken up, without anyone quite intending it, by a different kind of scientist entirely — psychologists, with laboratories, questionnaires and a fish tank.

Part 3 — The fish tank and the rice field

In 2001 Takahiko Masuda and Richard Nisbett showed twenty-second animated underwater scenes to students at Kyoto University and the University of Michigan: a large, fast, brightly coloured fish in the foreground; smaller fish, plants, bubbles, rocks behind. Then they asked what the students had seen. The Americans tended to begin with the big fish — the star of the scene. The Japanese tended to begin with the setting: the water was green, the bottom was rocky, there were plants. On average they mentioned around sixty-five per cent more background detail than the Americans did. Shown the big fish again against a new background, the Americans recognised it easily; the Japanese, more often, did not — for them the fish had been part of a whole, and the whole had changed.

Nisbett and his colleagues found the same shape again and again. Kaiping Peng gave Chinese and American students pairs of arguments that contradicted each other; the Americans tended to pick one and strengthen it, the Chinese more often accepted that both had something in them and looked for a middle way — a habit Peng called dialectical thinking, and which he found in the proverbs each group preferred. Heejung Kim asked Asian-American and European-American students to solve reasoning problems while thinking aloud; talking made no difference to the European Americans and made the Asian Americans worse, because, she argued, one tradition assumes that speech is thinking and the other assumes that thinking is done in silence. Nisbett gathered all of it in a book called The Geography of Thought, and for a while it looked as though the Needham Question had a psychological answer after all: the West isolates the object and asks what rule it obeys; the East sees the field and asks how everything fits. Analytical minds invent physics. Holistic minds invent harmony.

Then the story became more interesting. In 2014 Thomas Talhelm and colleagues tested 1,162 Han Chinese students from across China on the same measures — and found the 'East–West' difference inside China. Students from the wheat-growing north thought in the more analytical, individualist way; students from the rice-growing south thought more holistically and interdependently. Rice needs irrigation and cooperation; wheat can be grown by a family alone. The differences between neighbouring counties on either side of the rice–wheat line were as large as the differences psychologists had reported between Japan and America. It was not 'Eastern culture' that produced holistic thinking. It was, at least in part, what your ancestors had to do together to eat.

And the tidy story had another problem, which any physics student can see. Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang, who in 1956 asked whether an assumption every Western physicist had accepted for thirty years had ever been tested, were born in Shanghai and Hefei. Chien-Shiung Wu, who broke it, was born near Shanghai. The most analytical, rule-breaking question of the mid-twentieth century was asked by three people whose culture, according to the book, sees fields rather than fish. Perhaps the differences are real, and average, and small, and shaped by rice and exams and lawgivers and a hundred other things; and perhaps the habit that makes a scientist — noticing that a fact is really an assumption, and asking whether anyone has checked — belongs to no hemisphere at all. Needham, who learned it from two characters on a piece of paper, would probably have agreed.

The grammar this story runs on: generalising without stereotyping

Every claim in Part 3 is about averages: the Japanese tended to…, Americans were more likely to…. Say the same thing without the hedge — Japanese people see backgrounds — and a finding becomes a stereotype. At C1 you are expected to make general claims. The grammar of hedging is what lets you make them honestly.

1. Verbs and phrases of tendency.
• tend to + verb: "Northern students tended to think more analytically."
• be more / less likely to: "Americans were more likely to recognise the fish."
• have a tendency to / show a tendency towards: "…a tendency towards dialectical answers."
2. Adverbials that mark the scope of a claim.
• on average · in general · as a rule · by and large · to some extent · in part
• Quantifiers instead of 'all': most, many, a majority of, a significant proportion of.
• "On average, Japanese participants mentioned more background detail" — true. "Japanese people notice backgrounds" — not what the study showed.
3. Limiting and correcting the generalisation.
• by no means all / not every / far from: "By no means all southern students were holistic thinkers."
• This does not mean (that)… / It does not follow that…: "It does not follow that an individual from Kyoto will…"
• It would be an oversimplification / a mistake to say that… · The picture is more complicated: · with the important exception of…

2. Turn the stereotype back into a finding

Each sentence overstates what the research showed. Rewrite it honestly, using at least one hedge. Say it, then reveal a model.

1. Asians think holistically and Westerners think analytically.

2. Chinese people can't handle contradiction; Americans can't handle nuance.

3. Thinking aloud doesn't work for Asian students.

4. Southern Chinese are collectivists because they grow rice.

5. China didn't invent modern science because Chinese people don't ask questions.

1. In the studies, East Asian participants tended, on average, to attend more to context, and Americans more to focal objects — a difference of degree, not of kind.

2. Chinese students were more likely to endorse both sides of a contradiction, and Americans more likely to choose one; it does not follow that either group 'can't' do the other.

3. In one experiment, thinking aloud impaired the performance of Asian-American students on average, while European-American students were unaffected; by no means all individuals responded that way.

4. Talhelm's study found that students from rice-growing regions showed a tendency towards more interdependent thinking; the researchers proposed that a history of cooperative farming explains part of the difference.

5. It would be an oversimplification to say Chinese people don't ask questions — Needham argued the causes were institutional, and Lee, Yang and Wu asked the most searching question of their era.

Why this story stays with you

The Needham Question has never been answered, and the best modern historians think it may be the wrong shape: it assumes that modern science was the natural destination of any advanced civilisation, and asks why China 'failed' to arrive, when the strange event that needs explaining is arguably that it happened anywhere at all. But Needham's instinct — to look at examinations, merchants and cosmologies rather than at 'the Chinese mind' — has aged far better than the psychology that briefly seemed to replace it. Talhelm's rice line runs through the middle of China; Nisbett's own later work stressed that the differences are habits, not abilities, and shift within a generation of migration or education.

There is a smaller lesson inside the large one, and it is about classrooms. Kim's experiment suggests that a student raised to believe that thinking happens in silence may perform worse when forced to talk their way through a problem — and that a teacher who takes silence for absence may be wrong. Any language lesson is an exercise in thinking aloud in a foreign tongue. Knowing that the habit of doing so is itself cultural, and learnable, changes how you feel about the pause before you answer.

Look again at the two characters. Needham's life turned on a colleague writing 'fragrant smoke' on a piece of paper. Lu Gwei-djen gave him a language; the language gave him a library; the library gave him a question. How much of what any of us knows depends on who happened to sit at the next bench?
