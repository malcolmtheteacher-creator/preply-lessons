Seven women in a hall

Imagine a long hall in a medieval university. Seven women stand between the columns, each holding something. One has a book, one a pair of scales, one a pair of compasses, one a counting frame, one a lute, one a globe. They are not real women. They are the seven liberal arts, the subjects every student had to study: grammar, rhetoric, logic, arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy.

Artists painted them again and again. They did it because an idea with no face is hard to hold, and an idea with a face, a costume and something to carry is easy.

Who are the seven women?
Personifications of the seven liberal arts, each carrying a symbol.
Talk about it. If you had to paint 'justice' as a person, what would she wear and carry?

The great thirteenth-century thinker Thomas Aquinas wrote about memory, following the old Roman handbooks. He gave advice that sounds modern. Put the things you want to remember in an order. Attach them to striking images, because plain ones slip away. Give them your close attention. And go over them often.

He also noticed the difficulty this lesson is about: the ideas that matter most, such as truth, duty or fairness, are the ones with no picture. His answer was the old one. Use likenesses: a picture that stands for the idea, even if it is not the idea.

What were Aquinas's four pieces of advice?
Put things in order, attach striking images, give close attention, and go over them often.
Talk about it. Which of those four do you find hardest to do?

Later, in the sixteenth century, the philosopher Giordano Bruno designed enormous systems of images for abstract ideas, with wheels of figures that stood for qualities and concepts. They were too complicated to be useful for most people. But the principle was right: an abstract idea needs a body.

You will not need a wheel. You need four small tools, and a habit of asking, whenever you meet an idea, what could I see that stands for this?

What is the principle behind all of these systems?
An abstract idea needs a body: a picture, person or symbol that stands for it.
Talk about it. Think of an abstract word you find hard. What could stand for it?

Four ways to give an idea a body

To remember an abstract idea, make it concrete: turn it into something you can see, hear and feel.

ToolHowExample
A symbolUse an object that traditionally stands for itJustice: scales. Peace: a dove. Time: an hourglass.
A personMake the idea a person doing something, or use a person famous for itCourage: a firefighter in the flames. Wisdom: an old owl-eyed woman.
A sceneShow the idea happeningInflation: prices on a shop sign rise like a balloon and float away.
A feelingPut in the emotion the idea bringsFreedom: running barefoot down a hill with the wind in your hair.
  1. Say what the idea means. In one plain sentence.
  2. Choose a tool. Symbol, person, scene or feeling. A mix is best.
  3. Make it vivid and moving. Add action and a sense.
  4. Place it. On a peg, in a palace place, or in a chain.
  5. Check that it brings the idea back. Look at the picture, then say what it means. If it does not work, change it.

Other kinds of word

Small grammatical words, such as although or because, have no pictures either. Give each a fixed picture from its sound, as in the keyword method (lesson 16): because as a buzzing bee holding a banner that says CAUSE; although as a giant letter A made of dough.

Freedom

Make a scene for freedom. Use at least two of the four tools and one sense.
A runner kicks off her shoes and races barefoot down a green hill. A broken chain is flying behind her like a kite, and the wind roars in her ears.

Inflation

Make a scene for inflation (prices rising). Use a scene and a feeling.
A shopkeeper changes the price of a loaf from 1 to 2 to 4 to 8, and the loaf swells like a balloon and floats off. The queue groans.

Courage and evidence

Make one scene for courage and one for evidence. Use a person for one and a symbol for the other.
Courage: a small child in a cape walks calmly past a giant dragon. Evidence: a detective's magnifying glass finds a footprint, which sits in a glass case under a spotlight.
