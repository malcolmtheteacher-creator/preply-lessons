Predict, then check

The poem begins with a series of questions about creation, then zooms in on one small creature. Why might a poet about nature choose something so small?

Small subjects reward close attention. The poet shows that paying attention is the real subject: if one insect can hold her for a whole description, the reader is invited to do the same with the world.

Your analysis toolkit

Each tool has three parts: name it, point to it, explain the effect. Try to say the effect before you reveal it.

Imagery

Sensory detail that makes a scene visible.

SPEAK: Which image of the insect is the most precise? What does precision suggest about the speaker?

Exact detail shows patient observation. The speaker is not decorating nature; she is studying it, which models the attention the poem recommends.

Direct address and questions

The speaker asks the reader, and herself, questions.

SPEAK: What is the effect of beginning and ending with a question?

It frames the poem as an enquiry, not a lecture. The closing question turns outward and makes the reader responsible for an answer.

Tone shift

A change in attitude partway through.

SPEAK: Where does description turn into confession? How does the tone change?

Curious observation moves into humble, personal uncertainty, where she admits what she does not know. That honesty makes the final challenge more persuasive.

Structure and enjambment

Lines that run on, short and long sentences, the pace of the poem.

SPEAK: Find a place where a line break makes you slow down or speed up. Why there?

Line breaks can imitate attention itself: pausing, lingering, then moving on. Link the pace to the idea of stillness and an unhurried day.

Close reading

Round 1: Model, then you. Here is a weak and a strong comment on the same feature. Spot the difference before you read on.

Weak: The poet uses questions and it makes the poem interesting.

Strong: By opening with questions about creation, the poet sets a tone of wonder, then redirects the same question to the reader at the close, so the poem ends as a challenge rather than a conclusion.

SPEAK: Tell me three things the strong version does that the weak one does not.

It names a specific moment, explains the effect on mood, links the beginning to the ending, and uses precise verbs (sets, redirects) instead of “makes it interesting”.

From poem to argument

Strong analysis links evidence to an argument. Use this frame to turn your close reading into a paragraph.

Point: Oliver suggests that…
Evidence: This is shown when… (a short quotation or precise reference)
Technique: The use of… creates…
Effect: This makes the reader…
Link: Therefore the poem supports the idea that learning to appreciate nature leads to…

SPEAK: Rank these three arguments from weakest to strongest and defend your order.

  1. The poem is about an insect, so it is about nature.
  2. The poem models patient attention as a first step toward caring for the world.
  3. The poem proves that people must stop polluting.
Argument 2 is strongest: it is specific, arguable and evidence-based. Argument 1 only describes. Argument 3 overreaches because a poem about attention does not make an explicit policy claim.

Counter-argument. A strong response considers the other side.

SPEAK: “Admiring nature is not enough. Climate change needs laws and technology, not poems.” How would you answer this in two sentences?

Key words

imagery (noun) — descriptive language that appeals to the senses. Example: "The imagery of the poem lets us see every movement."
tone (noun) — the attitude of the speaker toward the subject. Example: "The tone moves from curiosity to humility."
enjambment (noun) — a sentence that runs over the end of a line without a pause. Example: "Enjambment makes the poem feel like a slow walk."
speaker (noun) — the voice that talks in a poem, not necessarily the poet. Example: "The speaker admits what she does not know."
juxtaposition (noun) — placing two different things side by side for contrast. Example: "The juxtaposition of a tiny insect and huge questions is striking."
evoke (verb) — to bring a feeling or memory into the mind. Example: "The scene evokes a sense of calm."
convey (verb) — to communicate an idea or feeling. Example: "Short lines convey urgency."
stewardship (noun) — responsible care of something that is not only yours. Example: "Stewardship of the planet begins with noticing it."
