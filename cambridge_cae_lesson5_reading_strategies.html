Reading Strategies

Parts 5, 6 & 7 - Where the Big Marks Are

Lesson 5 32 marks total

The Reading Parts: Your Biggest Opportunity

Parts 5-7 are worth 32 marks - that's 41% of the whole paper. Every question is worth 2 marks. Getting these right is the fastest way to improve your score.

Part 5
Multiple Choice (12 marks)
Part 6
Cross-text (8 marks)
Part 7
Gapped Text (12 marks)

The Core Problem

Most students read the text first, then try to answer questions. This is slow and inefficient. You forget details, re-read constantly, and run out of time.

The Solution: Question-First Reading

The Golden Rule

Always read the QUESTIONS before you read the text. Know what you're looking for BEFORE you start reading.

What Each Part Tests

Part Format Tests Time Suggestion
5 1 long text, 6 questions (A-D) Detail, opinion, attitude, purpose, inference 15-18 mins
6 4 short texts, 4 questions Comparing opinions across writers 12-15 mins
7 1 text with 6 gaps, 7 paragraphs to insert Text structure, cohesion, coherence 15-18 mins

Time Management

You have 90 minutes for ALL 8 parts. Many students spend too long on Parts 1-4 and rush the reading. Budget your time!

Part 5: Multiple Choice Reading

6 questions x 2 marks = 12 marks | 1 long text (~800 words)

What Part 5 Really Tests

Question Type What It Asks Signal Words
Detail What does the text say about X? "According to the text", "The writer states"
Opinion/Attitude How does the writer feel about X? "The writer suggests", "feels", "believes"
Purpose Why does the writer mention X? "The writer mentions X in order to", "The purpose of"
Inference What can we understand from X? "implies", "suggests", "can be inferred"
Reference What does "this/it/they" refer to? "In line X, 'this' refers to"

The Strategy: Underline & Locate

  1. Read the question stem only (ignore options A-D at first)
  2. Underline key words in the question (names, concepts, line references)
  3. Locate the relevant part of the text (usually in order)
  4. Read that section carefully (2-3 sentences around it)
  5. Form your own answer before looking at options
  6. Match to the closest option and eliminate wrong ones

The Trap: Attractive Distractors

Wrong options often:

  • Use exact words from the text but change the meaning
  • Say something true but not answering THIS question
  • Are too extreme ("always", "never", "completely")
  • Mix information from different parts of the text

Example: Spotting Traps

Text Extract:

"While some researchers initially believed that social media was entirely harmful to teenagers, recent studies suggest a more nuanced picture. Moderate use of social platforms can actually help young people develop social skills, though excessive use remains a concern."

Question: According to the text, social media...
A) is completely harmful to teenagers
B) helps all young people develop social skills
C) has both positive and negative effects when used by teenagers
D) was initially thought to be beneficial by researchers
Why A is Wrong

"Completely harmful" is too extreme. Text says "initially believed" (past) and "nuanced picture" (not black and white)

Why B is Wrong

"All young people" - text says "can help" (possibility) and "moderate use" (condition). Too absolute.

Why C is Correct

Matches "nuanced picture" - positive (develop social skills) AND negative (excessive use remains a concern).

Why D is Wrong

Opposite! Researchers initially believed it was HARMFUL, not beneficial. Classic reversal trap.

Part 5: Full Practice Example

Text: The Future of Remote Work

Paragraph 1: The COVID-19 pandemic fundamentally transformed how we think about work. What was once considered a rare privilege - working from home - became an overnight necessity for millions. Now, as companies decide their long-term strategies, a fierce debate has emerged about whether this shift should become permanent.

Paragraph 2: Proponents of remote work point to increased productivity as their primary argument. A Stanford study found that remote workers were 13% more productive than their office counterparts. They also cite the elimination of commuting time, which averages 41 minutes each way in major cities, giving workers back nearly seven hours per week.

Paragraph 3: However, critics argue that these productivity gains may be short-term illusions. Professor Sarah Chen of MIT suggests that while individual task completion might improve, "the creative collisions that happen in hallways and cafeterias are impossible to replicate virtually." She notes that several major innovations at tech companies originated from chance encounters between colleagues from different departments.

Paragraph 4: The psychological impact presents another consideration. While some employees thrive in the autonomy of remote work, others report feelings of isolation and disconnection from their teams. Younger workers, in particular, have expressed concerns about missing out on mentorship opportunities that naturally occur in physical offices.

Paragraph 5: Perhaps the most pragmatic approach is the hybrid model, which attempts to capture the benefits of both arrangements. Companies like Microsoft and Google have implemented policies requiring employees to be in the office two or three days per week, while allowing flexibility for the remainder. Early indications suggest this compromise satisfies the majority of workers while maintaining some collaborative opportunities.

Practice Questions

Question 1: According to the Stanford study mentioned in paragraph 2, remote workers...
A) saved an average of seven hours per week.
B) were somewhat more productive than office workers.
C) had 41 minutes less commuting time.
D) preferred working from home to office work.

Answer: B

Why B: "13% more productive than their office counterparts" = "somewhat more productive"

Why not A: The seven hours is about commuting time, not what the Stanford study found.

Why not C: This is about commuting in general, not specifically from the Stanford study.

Why not D: The study measured productivity, not preference. This is not mentioned.

Question 2: Professor Chen suggests that remote work...
A) makes employees more productive at individual tasks.
B) prevents certain types of creative collaboration.
C) should be avoided by tech companies.
D) has led to fewer innovations overall.

Answer: B

Why B: "creative collisions... impossible to replicate virtually" = prevents creative collaboration

Why not A: She actually implies productivity gains may be "short-term illusions" - she's skeptical.

Why not C: Too extreme. She raises concerns but doesn't say "should be avoided."

Why not D: She says innovations came from chance encounters, but doesn't claim total innovation has decreased.

Question 3: In paragraph 4, the writer mentions younger workers in order to...
A) show that remote work affects different groups differently.
B) argue that younger workers prefer office environments.
C) suggest that mentorship programs should be created.
D) criticize companies for neglecting new employees.

Answer: A

Why A: The paragraph contrasts "some employees thrive" with others who feel isolation. Younger workers are another group with specific concerns - showing variation.

Why not B: Text says they're concerned about missing mentorship, not that they prefer offices.

Why not C: No suggestion/recommendation is made about creating programs.

Why not D: This is criticism that isn't in the text - the writer is being descriptive, not critical.

Question 4: The writer's overall attitude toward the hybrid model is...
A) strongly supportive.
B) cautiously optimistic.
C) deeply skeptical.
D) completely neutral.

Answer: B

Why B: "Perhaps the most pragmatic approach" (positive) + "early indications suggest" (cautious - not certain yet) + "satisfies the majority" (optimistic about results)

Why not A: "Strongly supportive" would need more enthusiastic language. "Perhaps" and "early indications" show caution.

Why not C: The paragraph is positive about hybrid, not skeptical.

Why not D: "Most pragmatic approach" shows the writer has an opinion - not completely neutral.

Part 6: Cross-text Multiple Matching

4 questions x 2 marks = 8 marks | 4 short texts (same topic, different writers)

What Part 6 Really Tests

You read 4 short texts by different writers on the SAME topic. Questions ask you to find which writer shares/differs from another writer's opinion.

Typical Question Patterns

  • "Which writer shares Writer A's view on X?"
  • "Which writer has a different opinion from the others about X?"
  • "Which writer agrees with Writer B about X?"
  • "Which writer expresses a similar concern to Writer C?"

The Strategy: Topic Mapping

  1. Read all 4 questions first - identify the TOPICS being asked about
  2. Read Text A - note their position on each topic (positive/negative/neutral)
  3. Read Text B, C, D - compare positions to what you noted
  4. Create a mini-map: "Topic 1: A=positive, B=negative, C=positive, D=neutral"
  5. Match based on your map

Key Skill: Paraphrase Recognition

Writers will express the SAME opinion using DIFFERENT words. You must recognize when two writers are saying the same thing in different ways.

Example: Same Opinion, Different Words

Writer A says:

"The environmental cost of fast fashion is simply too high to ignore. We must fundamentally change how we consume clothing."

Writer C says:

"The ecological damage caused by disposable clothing trends demands urgent action. Our entire approach to fashion purchasing needs a complete overhaul."

These writers AGREE! Look at the paraphrases:

Writer A = Writer C
environmental cost = ecological damage
too high to ignore = demands urgent action
fundamentally change = complete overhaul

Spotting Disagreement

Writer A says:

"Technology in classrooms has been overwhelmingly positive for student engagement."

Writer B says:

"While technology offers some benefits, its impact on deep learning remains questionable."

These writers DISAGREE!

  • Writer A: "overwhelmingly positive" = strong support
  • Writer B: "remains questionable" = doubt/skepticism
  • Watch for hedging language: "while", "some", "questionable" = not fully convinced

Part 6: Full Practice Example

Four experts discuss artificial intelligence in healthcare:

Writer A - Dr. James Morton

AI diagnostic tools have shown remarkable accuracy in detecting certain cancers, often surpassing human doctors. However, I believe we must proceed with caution. The technology cannot replace the human judgment that considers a patient's full life circumstances. AI should be a tool that supports doctors, not supplants them. There's also the critical question of accountability - when an AI makes a mistake, who is responsible?

Writer B - Professor Elena Vasquez

The potential of AI in medicine is being vastly overstated. Yes, algorithms can process images quickly, but medicine is about far more than pattern recognition. The doctor-patient relationship, built on trust and communication, cannot be automated. I'm particularly concerned about the rush to implement these systems without adequate testing. We need decades of research, not the current headlong dash toward AI integration.

Writer C - Dr. Michael Chang

AI represents the greatest advancement in diagnostic medicine since the X-ray. Critics who demand more testing are delaying life-saving technology. Every day we wait, patients die from conditions AI could have caught. The accuracy rates speak for themselves - in radiology, AI now matches or exceeds specialist performance. Yes, doctors remain essential for treatment decisions, but we should embrace AI diagnosis immediately.

Writer D - Dr. Sarah Okonkwo

My view falls between the extremes. AI diagnostic capabilities are genuinely impressive, and dismissing them would be foolish. However, healthcare systems must integrate AI thoughtfully. The question of legal responsibility when errors occur needs resolution before widespread adoption. I advocate for a measured rollout - starting with low-risk applications and expanding as we learn.

Practice Questions

Question 1: Which writer shares Writer A's view that AI should assist rather than replace doctors?
A) Writer B
B) Writer C
C) Writer D

Answer: C (Writer D)

Writer A: "AI should be a tool that supports doctors, not supplants them"

Writer D: "integrate AI thoughtfully" + "measured rollout" = similar careful, supportive approach

Why not B: Writer B is more negative about AI overall ("vastly overstated")

Why not C: Writer C wants to "embrace AI diagnosis immediately" - much more pro-AI than A

Question 2: Which writer has a different opinion from the others about the speed of AI implementation?
A) Writer A
B) Writer B
C) Writer C
D) Writer D

Answer: C (Writer C)

Writer A: "proceed with caution" = slow down

Writer B: "decades of research", "rush to implement" (critical) = slow down

Writer C: "embrace AI diagnosis immediately", "delaying life-saving technology" = speed up!

Writer D: "measured rollout" = slow down

Three writers want caution, C wants speed - C is the outlier.

Question 3: Which writer expresses a similar concern to Writer A about accountability?
A) Writer B
B) Writer C
C) Writer D

Answer: C (Writer D)

Writer A: "when an AI makes a mistake, who is responsible?"

Writer D: "legal responsibility when errors occur needs resolution"

Both writers specifically mention the accountability/responsibility issue.

Why not A (Writer B): B is concerned about testing and the doctor-patient relationship, not accountability specifically.

Why not B (Writer C): C doesn't mention accountability at all - focused on benefits.

Question 4: Which writer disagrees with Writer C's assessment of AI diagnostic accuracy?
A) Writer A
B) Writer B
C) Writer D

Answer: B (Writer B)

Writer C: "accuracy rates speak for themselves" = AI is very accurate

Writer B: "potential is vastly overstated" = skeptical of claimed accuracy

Why not A: Writer A actually acknowledges "remarkable accuracy" - agrees with C on this point

Why not D: Writer D says capabilities are "genuinely impressive" - also agrees on accuracy

Part 7: Gapped Text

6 questions x 2 marks = 12 marks | 1 text with paragraphs removed

What Part 7 Really Tests

A text has 6 gaps where paragraphs have been removed. You have 7 paragraphs (A-G) to put back. One paragraph is extra and doesn't fit anywhere.

This Tests:

  • Cohesion: How sentences link together (pronouns, linking words)
  • Coherence: How ideas flow logically
  • Text structure: Understanding how arguments develop

The Strategy: Find the Links

  1. Read the main text first (with gaps) - understand the overall topic and flow
  2. For each gap, read BEFORE and AFTER - what's the context?
  3. Look for LINK WORDS in the paragraphs: pronouns (this, they, it), time markers (then, later), contrast (however, but)
  4. Match the links - "this problem" must follow a paragraph that mentions a problem
  5. Check your answer makes sense - read the section with your choice inserted
  6. Cross off used paragraphs - don't use the same one twice!

Link Types to Look For

Link Type Examples What to Look For
Pronouns this, that, these, they, it, he, she What noun does it refer back to?
Demonstratives this idea, such problems, these findings What idea/problem/finding was mentioned?
Time/Sequence then, later, previously, after this What happened before/after?
Contrast however, but, on the other hand, yet What opposite idea comes before?
Addition moreover, furthermore, also, in addition What similar point was made?
Result therefore, consequently, as a result What cause was mentioned?
Names/Nouns The scientist, The company, This method Was this person/thing introduced before?

Example: Using Links

Text Before Gap:

"The company launched its new product in January. Sales exceeded all expectations, reaching $10 million in the first month alone."

GAP 3

Paragraph C:

"This remarkable success led the board to increase production capacity. They approved a $50 million investment in new manufacturing facilities."

Paragraph E:

"The marketing team had worked for months on the campaign. Their efforts focused on social media influencers."

Answer: Paragraph C

Why C fits:

  • "This remarkable success" refers back to "$10 million" and "exceeded expectations"
  • "They" refers to "the board" (new subject, but logical: company → board)
  • The ideas flow: success → decision to expand

Why E doesn't fit:

  • "The campaign" - what campaign? None mentioned in the text before the gap
  • This paragraph seems to go BEFORE the launch, not after the success

Part 7: Full Practice Example

The Rise of Urban Farming

Main Text:

Cities around the world are experiencing a green revolution of a different kind. Rooftops, abandoned lots, and even underground spaces are being transformed into productive farms. This movement, known as urban agriculture, is changing how city dwellers think about food.

GAP 1

The benefits extend beyond fresh produce. Urban farms create green spaces that help cool cities during heat waves. They also provide habitats for pollinators like bees and butterflies, which are essential for maintaining biodiversity even in concrete jungles.

GAP 2

Despite these advantages, urban farming faces significant obstacles. Land in cities is expensive, and many potential farming sites are contaminated with industrial pollutants. Water access can also be problematic, as urban infrastructure wasn't designed with agriculture in mind.

GAP 3

Looking ahead, technology may provide some solutions. Vertical farms, which grow crops in stacked layers using artificial lighting, can produce food year-round regardless of weather or available land. While currently expensive, costs are falling rapidly as the technology matures.

Paragraphs to Insert (one is extra)

Paragraph A

However, innovative farmers are finding ways around these challenges. Raised beds filled with clean soil can be placed over contaminated ground. Rainwater harvesting systems reduce dependence on municipal water supplies.

Paragraph B

One major advantage is the reduction in food miles - the distance food travels from farm to plate. Traditional agriculture often ships produce thousands of kilometers before it reaches consumers. In contrast, urban farms can deliver vegetables within hours of harvest.

Paragraph C

These environmental benefits are increasingly valued by city planners. Many municipalities now offer incentives for building green roofs that can support food production. Such policies recognize that urban farms provide services beyond food.

Paragraph D

Rural farmers have long complained about the challenges of reaching urban markets. Transportation costs eat into their profits, and produce often spoils before reaching store shelves. This disconnect between producers and consumers has frustrated both groups.

Match the Paragraphs

Gap 1: Which paragraph fits?

Hint: What would logically follow an introduction to urban farming? What comes after this gap talks about "benefits beyond fresh produce"...

Answer: Paragraph B

Why:

  • Gap 1 needs to introduce a FIRST benefit (the text after says "benefits extend beyond" = there was already one benefit)
  • Paragraph B talks about "food miles" - a clear first benefit
  • "In contrast" connects urban farms to traditional agriculture mentioned at the start
Gap 2: Which paragraph fits?

Hint: Before the gap = environmental benefits. After the gap = "Despite these advantages, urban farming faces obstacles..."

Answer: Paragraph C

Why:

  • "These environmental benefits" refers directly to cooling cities, pollinator habitats (previous paragraph)
  • Continues the positive theme before the text shifts to "obstacles"
  • "Such policies" links back to "incentives" mentioned in the same paragraph
Gap 3: Which paragraph fits?

Hint: Before the gap = problems (expensive land, contamination, water). After the gap = "Looking ahead, technology may provide solutions..."

Answer: Paragraph A

Why:

  • "However" signals contrast with the problems just mentioned
  • "These challenges" refers to contamination, water access (previous paragraph)
  • "Raised beds" solves contamination, "rainwater harvesting" solves water - directly addressing the problems
  • Bridges between "problems" and "future technology solutions"
Which paragraph is EXTRA (doesn't fit anywhere)?

Answer: Paragraph D

Why:

  • This is about RURAL farmers' problems, not urban farming
  • "This disconnect" has no clear reference in the main text
  • It's a distractor - related topic (food/farming) but wrong focus

Key Takeaways

Part 5: Multiple Choice

  • Read question stem FIRST, underline key words
  • Locate the relevant section in the text
  • Form your own answer BEFORE reading options
  • Eliminate distractors (too extreme, wrong focus, reversal)

Part 6: Cross-text Matching

  • Identify the TOPICS in the questions first
  • Note each writer's position: positive/negative/neutral
  • Look for PARAPHRASES - same meaning, different words
  • Watch for hedging language that shows doubt

Part 7: Gapped Text

  • Read main text first to understand the flow
  • Look for LINKS: pronouns, demonstratives, connectors
  • Check what comes BEFORE and AFTER the gap
  • Cross off paragraphs as you use them
  • One paragraph is always EXTRA - expect a distractor

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Part 5: Choosing an option just because it uses words from the text
  • Part 6: Confusing "similar view" with "exactly the same words"
  • Part 7: Ignoring link words and just matching by topic
  • All parts: Spending too long on one question - move on and come back
