Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde — Episode 1

Robert Louis Stevenson's famous story from 1886, in four episodes. This week: a strange door, a cheque with a famous name on it, and a man nobody can describe.

Today: you'll meet a lawyer, a strange door and a man nobody can describe — and you'll practise describing people with he is, he has and he looks.
Warm up — before we read. Talk about these out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • Describe one person in your family in three sentences. What do they look like? What are they like?
  • When you meet a new person, do you decide quickly if you like them? What do you look at first — the face, the clothes, the voice?
  • Your best friend has a new friend. You don't like this new person. Do you say something?

Now — the words for this episode

Ten words. Do the four short activities, and say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Look at the sentence

1. Mr Utterson works with contracts and documents. When people have a problem with the law, they go to him. He is a lawyer.
a lawyer (n) — a person whose job is the law: contracts, documents, and helping people in court. "You need a lawyer."
2. The man did not have a hundred pounds in his pocket. So he wrote the amount on a piece of paper from his bank and signed it. The next day, the family took the cheque to the bank and got the money.
a cheque (n) — a paper from your bank; you write an amount and sign it, and the bank pays the money. "Can I pay by cheque?" (American spelling: check.)
3. "When I die," the doctor wrote, "everything I have goes to my friend Edward Hyde." He signed the paper and gave it to his lawyer. This paper is his will.
a will (n) — a legal paper that says who gets your money and your things when you die. "He left everything to his daughter in his will."

B · Which word means…?

Three words: afraid · secret · key. Say the right word out loud, then reveal.

1. Which word means something you know, but you do not tell other people?
a secret (n) — "Can you keep a secret?" Also an adjective: a secret door, a secret plan.
2. Which word means the small metal thing that opens a door?
a key (n) — "I can't find my keys!" Verb: to lock a door (with a key) / to unlock it.
3. Which word means feeling fear?
afraid (adj) — "I'm afraid of dogs." / "Don't be afraid." Same meaning: scared, frightened.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Every other house in the street has windows and a bell. This house has no windows and no bell, and nobody cleans it. It is a strange house. Strange means…
  (a) very big   (b) not normal; unusual   (c) very old
(b) — strange (adj) = not normal, different, difficult to understand. "That's strange — the door is open." Noun: a stranger = a person you don't know.
2. Enfield says: "His face is not nice to look at. I don't know why, but I don't want to see it again." He thinks Mr Hyde is ugly. Ugly means…
  (a) very tall   (b) very quiet   (c) not beautiful; not nice to look at
(c) — ugly (adj) = not beautiful. "An ugly building." Opposite: beautiful, good-looking.

D · Opposites & word families

1. The opposite of good is bad. But there is a stronger word for a very, very bad person — the devil, for example. It starts with e. What is it? Now use the same word as a noun: "the fight between good and ___".
evil (adj and n) — very, very bad; the opposite of good. "An evil man." / "Good and evil." Utterson thinks Mr Hyde has an evil face.
2. One verb completes both: "Dr Jekyll ___s Mr Hyde — he gives him a key to his house." / "A good friend is someone you can ___." What is the verb? And what is the adjective that ends in -worthy?
to trust (v) — to believe that a person is good and honest. Also a noun: "a friendship built on trust". Adjective: trustworthy. Opposite: to distrust.

Now use them — out loud

Talk before we read:
  • Use strange, key and secret in one short description of a house in your town.
  • Which words today are about people? Which are about money and the law? Sort them out loud.

Episode 1 — The door and the search

This is the beginning of a famous novel. Here are the first two chapters in three parts, in simple English. After each part, answer the questions from memory, then discuss.

Part 1 — The door

Mr Utterson is a lawyer in London. He is tall and thin, and he does not smile very much. He is quiet. But people like him, because he is kind and he does not judge them. Every Sunday he goes for a walk with his cousin, Mr Enfield. They do not talk much on these walks. But they like them.

One Sunday they walk down a small, busy street. All the houses are clean and pretty — except one. This house is old and dirty. It has no windows and no bell. It has one door, and nobody cleans the door. Mr Enfield points to it.

"Do you know that door?" he says. "I have a strange story about it."

Then he tells the story. It was three o'clock in the morning, in winter. The streets were empty. He saw a small man walking fast. And he saw a little girl running. The man and the girl ran into each other at the corner. The man did not stop. He walked over the child and left her crying on the ground. "It was not like a man," says Enfield. "It was like a machine."

Enfield ran after the man and caught him. He brought him back. The girl was not badly hurt, but everybody hated the man. A doctor came. Even the doctor wanted to hurt him. So they said: "Pay the girl's family, or we tell everybody in London what you did." The man said: "Name your price." They said: one hundred pounds.

The man took them to the strange door. He opened it with a key. He came back with ten pounds in gold and a cheque for ninety pounds. The cheque had a famous name on it — a good man's name. Enfield thought the cheque was false. But the next day, the bank paid it. It was real.

"I think it is blackmail," Enfield says. "A good man is paying for a secret from when he was young. I call that place 'Blackmail House'."

Utterson asks: "What is the man's name?" — "Hyde," says Enfield. "What does he look like?" — "I can't say. He is small. There is something wrong with him. He looks… wrong. I hate him, but I don't know why. I think there is something wrong with his body — but I don't know what."

Then Utterson asks a strange question: "Are you sure he had a key?" Enfield is surprised. "Yes," he says. "He has it still. I saw him use it last week."

Utterson says: "I know the name on the cheque. You don't need to tell me." The two men agree: they will never talk about this again.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the small man do to the little girl?

2. Why is the cheque strange?

1. He walked over her and left her crying on the ground. He did not stop.

2. A bad man wrote it — but it has a good, famous man's name on it. And the bank paid it. It is real.

Discuss:
  • Enfield does not ask questions about the strange door. He says: "The stranger it looks, the less I ask." Is that a good rule?

Part 2 — The will

That evening Utterson goes home. He is worried. He opens his safe and takes out a document: the will of his friend, Dr Henry Jekyll. Jekyll wrote it himself, because Utterson didn't want to help him. The will says: when Dr Jekyll dies, everything goes to his "friend" Edward Hyde. And if Dr Jekyll disappears for more than three months, Edward Hyde gets everything too.

Utterson has always hated this will. Before, he didn't know who Hyde was. Now he knows. "I thought this was madness," he says. "Now I am afraid it is something worse."

He goes to see Dr Lanyon, an old friend of Jekyll's. Lanyon is a big, healthy doctor with a red face and white hair. He is happy to see Utterson. But when Utterson asks about Jekyll, Lanyon's face changes.

"I don't see Jekyll now," he says. "Ten years ago he started to have strange ideas — bad science. We stopped being friends."

"Do you know a man called Hyde?" asks Utterson.

"Hyde? No. I never heard the name."

Utterson goes home and cannot sleep. All night he sees pictures in his head: the empty streets, the small man, the child on the ground. And he sees his friend Jekyll asleep in bed — and a man with no face standing next to him. Utterson wants to see this face.

So every day he waits near the door. In the morning, at midday, at night. "If he is Mr Hyde," he thinks, "I will be Mr Seek."

One cold, clear night at ten o'clock, he hears footsteps. A small man in plain clothes comes down the street. He walks straight to the door and takes out a key. Utterson steps out and touches his shoulder. "Mr Hyde, I think?"

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Dr Jekyll's will say?

2. What does Dr Lanyon know about Hyde?

1. When Jekyll dies, everything goes to Edward Hyde. And if Jekyll disappears for more than three months, Hyde gets everything too.

2. Nothing. He has never heard the name. But he tells Utterson that he and Jekyll are not friends now, because of Jekyll's strange ideas about science.

Discuss:
  • In Utterson's dreams, the man has no face. Why is a man with no face more frightening than an ugly man?

Part 3 — The face

Hyde jumps back. He is afraid for a second. But then he answers coldly: "That is my name. What do you want?"

"I am Dr Jekyll's old friend," says Utterson. "My name is Utterson. Perhaps I can come in?"

"Dr Jekyll is not at home," says Hyde. Then he asks: "How do you know me?"

"Will you do something for me?" says Utterson. "Let me see your face."

Hyde turns and looks at him. For a few seconds the two men look at each other. "Now I will know you again," says Utterson.

"Good," says Hyde. "And you should have my address." He gives an address in Soho. Utterson thinks: "Is he thinking about the will too?"

"How do you know me?" Hyde asks again.

"We have the same friends," says Utterson. "Dr Jekyll, for example."

Hyde is suddenly angry. "He never told you about me! You are lying!" He laughs — a horrible laugh — opens the door, and disappears.

Utterson stands in the street. What is wrong with this man? He is small. He is pale. His smile is not nice. His voice is quiet and broken. But that is not enough. "There must be something more," thinks Utterson. "He does not look like a human. Poor Harry Jekyll — your new friend has an evil face."

He walks round the corner to Dr Jekyll's house. It is a big, beautiful house. An old servant, Poole, opens the door. "Is Dr Jekyll at home?" — "I will see, sir." Utterson waits by the fire. He loves this room — but tonight it feels dark and frightening. Poole comes back: Dr Jekyll is out.

"I saw Mr Hyde go in by the laboratory door," says Utterson. "Is that right?"

"Yes, sir," says Poole. "Mr Hyde has a key. We all have orders to obey him."

"Does he come here often?" — "He never eats here, sir. We don't see him much. He comes and goes by the laboratory."

Utterson walks home. His heart is heavy. "Poor Harry Jekyll," he thinks. "He is in deep water. He was wild when he was young. Maybe this is an old secret — and now he is paying for it. And this Hyde has secrets too. If Hyde knows about the will, he may want Jekyll to die. I must help my friend — if he will let me."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Mr Hyde look like?

2. What does Poole tell Utterson about Mr Hyde?

1. He is small and pale. His smile is not nice, and his voice is quiet and broken. Utterson thinks he has an evil face — but he can't say why.

2. Hyde has a key to the laboratory door. He never eats in the house, and the servants don't see him much — but they all have orders to obey him.

Discuss:
  • Utterson thinks Jekyll has an old secret, and Hyde knows it. Do you think he is right? What else could be true?

Grammar — describing people: he is, he has, he looks

Everybody in this story tries to describe Mr Hyde — and nobody can. You can do better. To describe a person in English, you need three small verbs.

1 · is + adjective, job or age.
• She is tall.  ·  He is very friendly.  ·  My brother is a teacher.  ·  She is forty.
• Negative: He isn't old.  ·  Question: Is he tall?

2 · has + noun (a thing, or a part of the body).
• She has long black hair.  ·  He has a big nose.  ·  My uncle has a red car.
• Negative: He doesn't have a beard.  ·  Question: Does she have glasses?
• In British English you also hear He has got a beard / He's got a beard. Both are fine.
3 · looks + adjective  /  looks like + noun.
• is = a fact. looks = what you see. She is thirty, but she looks twenty.
• He looks tired.  ·  She looks happy today.  ·  You look cold!
• With a noun, add like: He looks like a footballer.  ·  She looks like her mother.
• Question: What does he look like? — this is how you ask for a description. (Not "How does he look like?")

1. is or has?

1. Mr Utterson a lawyer.

2. He a big safe in his office.

3. Dr Lanyon a red face and white hair.

4. Mr Hyde very small.

5. Mr Hyde a key to the old door.

6. Dr Jekyll's house big and beautiful.

1. is   2. has   3. has   4. is   5. has   6. is

Look at what comes after the gap. A thing (a safe, a face, a key) → has. An adjective or a job (small, big, a lawyer) → is.

2. looks or looks like?

Write looks before an adjective. Write looks like before a noun.

1. Hyde walks to the door with a key. He a man going home.

2. When Utterson touches his shoulder, Hyde afraid.

3. The old house dirty and strange.

4. Utterson thinks Hyde the devil.

5. Dr Lanyon healthy and happy.

1. looks like   2. looks   3. looks   4. looks like   5. looks

"A man", "the devil" are nouns → looks like. "Afraid", "dirty", "healthy" are adjectives → looks.

Going deeper

Robert Louis Stevenson was a writer from Scotland. He wrote this book in 1885, very quickly, after a bad dream. In his time, people in Britain cared a lot about looking good and respectable — and many of them had secrets.

What the story is doing. Look at what we know, and what we don't know. We know there is a door with no bell in a street of clean, pretty houses. We know a good, famous man paid money for a bad man's crime. We know Dr Jekyll's will gives everything to Mr Hyde. But we do not know what Mr Hyde looks like. Three people see his face, and nobody can describe it. Stevenson doesn't tell us about the face. He tells us what people feel when they see it. That is more frightening.

And notice who tells us the story: Mr Utterson — a quiet, careful lawyer who doesn't like questions. He is the last person who wants to find a secret. And he is the one who is looking for it.
Talk it through:
  • The cheque is real, not false. Why is that more strange than a false cheque?
  • Utterson and Enfield agree: "We will never talk about this again." Then Utterson waits at the door every day. Is he a good friend to Jekyll, or is he too curious?
  • Everybody hates Mr Hyde, but nobody knows why. Do you believe in this kind of feeling? Have you ever felt it?
  • Two houses: a beautiful house in the square, and the dirty door round the corner. Do you think they are connected? How?

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Dr Lanyon knows more about Jekyll's "strange science" than he says.
