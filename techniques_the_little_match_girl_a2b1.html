The Little Match Girl

A famous story by Hans Christian Andersen: a poor girl, a cold night, and small lights full of dreams.

Today: read a famous story by Hans Christian Andersen about a poor girl on a cold New Year's night, and the beautiful things she sees in the light of her matches. Grammar: want and would like.
Warm up — talk before we read. Say as much as you can. There are no wrong answers.

  • What is a small thing that makes you think of a happy memory — a smell, a food, a light?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — A cold night

It was the last evening of the year. It was very cold, and snow was falling. A little girl was walking in the streets. She was barefoot — she had no shoes. She had some old slippers when she left home, but they were too big, and she lost them in the street.

The girl was very poor. She had a lot of small matches to sell, and she held them in her cold hands. But all day, no one bought a match from her. No one gave her any money. She was cold and hungry, and the snow fell on her long hair.

There were bright lights in every window. She could smell food — it was New Year's Eve, and the families were happy inside. The girl sat down in a corner between two houses. She was very cold, but she did not want to go home. She had no money, and her father would be angry. And her home was cold too, with wind coming through the walls.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is the girl in the street? What happened to her shoes?

2. Why does she not want to go home?

1. She is trying to sell matches. Her slippers were too big and she lost them in the street, so now she is barefoot.

2. She has no money because no one bought a match. Her father will be angry, and her home is cold too.

Talk about it:
  • The girl can see the warm, happy homes, but she is outside in the cold. Why is this very hard for her?

Part 2 — The matches and the dreams

Her hands were very cold. "Maybe one match will help me," she thought. She took one match and rubbed it on the wall. A small flame started, warm and bright. For a moment, she felt she was near a big, warm fire. But then the flame went out, and the fire was gone.

She used a second match. Now she saw a table with lots of good food on it — a big dinner for New Year. It looked wonderful. But then the flame went out again, and she saw only the cold wall.

She used a third match. Now she saw a beautiful Christmas tree, with many little lights on it. She looked up at the lights. The flame went out, but the lights went up, up into the sky — and they were the stars. One star fell down. The girl remembered her grandmother, who was not alive now. Her grandmother once said: "When a star falls, someone is going up to be with God." Her grandmother was the only person who was always kind to her.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the girl see in the first three matches?

2. What did her grandmother say about a falling star?

1. In the first match, a warm fire; in the second, a table with lots of food; in the third, a beautiful Christmas tree with lights that become the stars.

2. That when a star falls, someone is going up to be with God.

Talk about it:
  • The girl sees warm things, food and lights — the things she does not have. Why do you think she dreams of these things?

Part 3 — Grandmother, and the morning

The girl used one more match. And now she saw her grandmother in the light — kind and beautiful. "Grandmother!" the girl said. "Please take me with you! I know you will go when the match goes out." So the girl quickly used all her matches, because she wanted her grandmother to stay. The matches made a big, bright light. Her grandmother took the little girl in her arms, and together they went up, high and happy, to a warm place with no cold, no hunger and no fear.

The next morning, people found the little girl in the corner, sitting very still. She had a soft smile on her face, and all her matches were beside her. "She wanted to get warm," the people said. But no one knew about the beautiful things she saw, or that she was now happy with her grandmother, in the new year.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does the girl use all her matches at the same time?

2. What do the people see in the morning? What do they not understand?

1. She sees her grandmother in the light and uses all the matches so her grandmother will stay with her.

2. They see the girl sitting still with a smile and her matches beside her. They think she only wanted to get warm; they do not know about the beautiful things she saw, or that she is now happy with her grandmother.

Talk about it:
  • Andersen tells the sad ending in a soft, gentle way — a smile, a grandmother's arms. Why do you think he tells it like this?

Grammar: want / would like

This story is full of things the girl does not have but hopes for. English has two easy ways to talk about the things we hope for and dream about.

want + to + verb = you hope for something, or you have a strong feeling for it: "She wants to be warm." "I want to go home."
want + noun = "She wants a warm coat." "I want some food."
would like + to + verb = the same idea, but softer and more polite: "She would like to sit by the fire." "I would like to help her."
would like + noun = "She would like a warm dinner." We often write it short: I'd like, she'd like.
Feel the difference:
• want = a strong, direct feeling ("I want food!").
• would like = softer and more polite ("I would like some food, please").

Going deeper

Hans Christian Andersen wrote this story a very long time ago, in 1845. It is short and simple, but people all over the world still read it. It is a sad story, but it is also warm and beautiful — and that is why we remember it.

Two feelings at the same time. The story is sad: a poor girl is cold and alone, and no one helps her. But it is also beautiful: in the light of her matches, she sees warm and happy things, and at the end she is with her kind grandmother, in a place with no more cold or hunger.

Andersen tells the sad ending in a soft, gentle way. He does not use hard words. He shows us a smile, a warm light, and a grandmother's arms. Some people feel happy for the girl at the end; some people feel sad and angry that no one helped her before.

Talk about it — the deeper questions:
  • The dreams. The girl sees a fire, food, a Christmas tree, and her grandmother. Why do you think she sees these things, and not other things?
  • The ending. Is the end of the story happy or sad for you? Can it be both at the same time?
  • The people. Many people walk past the girl and do not help her. What do you think about this? Why do people sometimes not see someone who needs help?
  • Why we still read it. This story is very old. Why do you think people still like it today?
