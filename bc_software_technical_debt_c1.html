Communicating Technical Debt to the Business

C1 Level · Software Development · Cross-Functional Communication

Software Development · Dewey 005

First Thoughts

Your team needs two sprints to pay down technical debt. The product director wants those sprints to ship features. She is not wrong — but she is not seeing what you are seeing.

The challenge is that technical debt is an engineering metaphor that rarely survives the journey into a boardroom. Today, you practise translating.

Take a moment with each question below. Share as much detail as you like — specific examples, what you would actually say, what tripped you up last time.

1. How do you currently explain technical debt to non-engineers? What analogy, if any, do you reach for?

2. Why do business stakeholders so often say yes to features and no to debt paydown, even when they logically accept the argument?

3. When, in your experience, does technical debt finally ‘get heard’ — and what usually changes?

4. Is there debt your team chose deliberately? How did you communicate that at the time?

Quick opener — pick whichever you prefer:

  • Someone once said: “Technical debt is a loan the business doesn’t remember taking out.” What is good about that metaphor — and what does it miss?
  • Your product director says: “We shipped fine last year — why is this suddenly an emergency?” What is your opening sentence?

Key Vocabulary

Eight words and phrases you will actually use in this conversation. Click each card to reveal the meaning, then say the word aloud and try to use it in a sentence from your own experience.

technical debtWork that was deliberately or accidentally left incomplete, which adds friction to every future change.
to pay down (debt)To do the cleanup work that removes the friction — usually by rewriting, refactoring, or retiring old code.
a bottleneckA point in the system where progress is slowed because too much depends on too few components — or too few people.
velocityThe rate at which a team ships value. Technical debt usually drags velocity down invisibly.
brittleDescribing code or systems that work, but break easily when touched. The opposite of robust.
a workaroundA temporary fix that avoids the real problem but leaves it in place for later.
scope creepThe slow growth of a piece of work beyond what was originally agreed — often invisible until it hurts.
a proof of concept (PoC)A small, cheap test of whether an approach works — before committing to building it fully.

Key Language

Useful chunks of language for this conversation. Read each phrase aloud and think about when you would actually reach for it.

Translating the metaphor

  • “Think of this like interest on a loan — we took it on deliberately, but we are now paying it every single sprint.”
  • “Every feature we ship is about twenty per cent slower than it should be, and the cost is compounding.”
  • “We have reached the point where we spend more energy keeping the system upright than we do building on it.”

Making it measurable

  • “Last quarter we lost the equivalent of fourteen engineering days to one brittle deployment path.”
  • “If we invest two sprints now, we estimate velocity improves by about twenty-five per cent for the rest of the year.”
  • “The cost of not doing this is not zero — it just does not show up in a single line on a roadmap.”

Asking for the right thing

  • “I am not asking you to choose debt paydown over features — I am asking you to let us protect fifteen per cent of every sprint for it.”
  • “Give me two sprints and I will give you back a team that ships a feature a week for the rest of the year.”
  • “What I need from you today is permission, not sympathy.”

Handling pushback

  • “I understand. Let me tell you what happens if we defer it again.”
  • “You are right that we shipped fine last year. The point is that we are spending more to ship the same thing this year.”
  • “I would rather do this visibly, with your agreement, than quietly — which is what happens if I do not raise it.”

The Scenario

The situation: You are tech lead on a platform team. Deployment now takes forty minutes (up from eight a year ago). Three of your six engineers quietly flagged in the last retro that they are tired of working around the same five issues. Your product director has a roadmap of feature commitments signed off with the CEO. She has just said in standup: “We do not have time for an engineering holiday.”

Your task: Book a 45-minute meeting with her. Leave with her explicit agreement for either two dedicated paydown sprints, or a permanent fifteen per cent capacity reservation for debt work, justified in business terms.
