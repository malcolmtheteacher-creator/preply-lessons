The Reframe

Strategic Storytelling

Same Facts, Different Story

Every situation can be told at least two ways. The one you choose changes everything.

From memory

Think of a time when someone — a colleague, a leader, a politician — took a situation that looked bad and made it sound good. Or vice versa. What did they do? How did it work?

Today: We're learning the Reframing Ladder — a four-step technique for turning the same facts into a fundamentally different story, without dishonesty.

Consider these two sentences. Same facts. Different frame:

Frame A
"We missed our revenue target by 15%."
→
Frame B
"Despite entering three new markets simultaneously, we retained 85% of our projected revenue — while building the infrastructure for next year's growth."

Neither is a lie. Both are factually accurate. But they tell completely different stories — and lead to completely different conversations in the room. That's the power of reframing.

The Reframing Ladder

Four moves. Each one shifts the story without changing the facts.

1

Change the Timeframe

Zoom out or zoom in. A bad quarter looks different inside a three-year growth curve. A difficult day looks different as "the turning point."

2

Change the Comparison

"Compared to what?" changes everything. Compared to your target, you failed. Compared to the industry average, you're top quartile. Compared to where you started, it's extraordinary.

3

Change the Protagonist

Whose story is this? The company's? The customer's? The team's? Shifting the protagonist shifts what matters. A product failure from the company's perspective is a customer insight from the product team's perspective.

4

Change the Category

Is this a failure story or a learning story? A cost or an investment? A setback or a pivot? The category you assign determines the emotional and strategic weight of every fact inside it.

Watch all four in action

Here's a fact: "The product launch attracted only 200 users in the first month — against a target of 2,000."

Rung 1: Change the timeframe

"Month one gave us 200 users. But those 200 generated the feedback that reshaped the product entirely — and by month six we had 8,000."

Rung 2: Change the comparison

"Our closest competitor's launch? 45 users. We opened with 200 — and unlike them, every one of those users was acquired organically."

Rung 3: Change the protagonist

"200 people chose to try something completely new. They gave us 1,400 pieces of feedback in 30 days. They built this product as much as we did."

Rung 4: Change the category

"We didn't launch a product — we launched a learning experiment. And the data from those 200 users was worth more than any market research we could have bought."

When Not to Reframe

The line between reframing and spin. Where is it?

The ethics question

Reframing is powerful precisely because it can change how people feel about the same facts. That's also what makes it dangerous. Consider these three scenarios:

Scenario A

A pharmaceutical company reframes the side effects of a drug as "manageable trade-offs for significant quality-of-life improvements" — when internal data shows the side effects are more severe than publicly disclosed.

Scenario B

A CEO reframes mass layoffs as "reshaping for the future" — and genuinely believes the restructuring will save the remaining 80% of jobs and create better ones within two years.

Scenario C

A leader reframes their team's failure to hit targets as "ambitious goal-setting that drove unprecedented effort" — knowing the team already feels the same pressure is unsustainable.

For each: is this legitimate reframing or spin? Where's the line? Discuss each one — there are no simple answers.

The reframer's rule

Here's a principle to carry with you: A reframe is honest when it adds a perspective the listener genuinely needs. It's spin when it removes a perspective the listener deserves.
