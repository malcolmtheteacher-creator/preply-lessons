At the Box Office

The show's the hottest ticket in town, you've turned up on the night, and the little red "SOLD OUT" sign is doing its best to ruin your evening. Time to charm the desk.

The Scene 🎬

It's a Friday evening and the whole town seems to be pouring into one glittering old theatre. The show is Sleight — a magician everyone's been whispering about, the kind who makes a borrowed watch vanish and turn up inside a locked box on the other side of the room. You didn't book ahead. You meant to. You didn't.

So here you are at the box office window, five minutes before the house opens, hoping there's some way in. You want two seats, somewhere you can actually see, at a price that won't make you disappear. And the sign says SOLD OUT — but signs, like magicians, don't always tell the whole truth.

Behind the glass: someone who books this theatre inside out — knows which "sold out" shows still have a returned pair floating about, and exactly which seat is secretly the best in the house.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last see something performed live — a play, a gig, a match? What did being in the room give you that a screen never could?
  2. You've got a magician in front of you and one free question — "how did you do the trick?" or "how did you get so good?" Which do you ask, and why?

Who's who

You
The Latecomer 🎩

Optimistic, a little sheepish for not booking, and quietly determined to talk your way into two decent seats without paying through the nose.

The other voice
The Box-Office Clerk 🎟️

Unflappable, dry, faintly amused. Has heard "but we've come SO far" more times than you've had hot dinners — and still finds a seat for the ones who ask well.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Listen for the softeners, the little jokes, and the moment "sold out" quietly becomes "well, there is one thing…".

ClerkGood evening — box office. What can I do for you?
YouEvening! Now, I know the sign says sold out and I only have myself to blame — but I don't suppose you've got a pair going for tonight, have you?
ClerkOfficially? Not a seat in the house. Unofficially… let me have a look. There's always a straggler or two that come back to the desk on the night.
YouOh, you're a lifesaver already. And while you're looking — are you expecting many returns closer to curtain-up? I'm happy to lurk.
ClerkPossibly one or two, but I wouldn't bank on it. Ah — here we are. I've got two that came back this afternoon. Upper circle, right at the side. What we tactfully call "restricted view".
YouRight. And when you say "restricted", how much of the show would we actually lose? It's a magic act — half of it is the bit you're not supposed to see.
ClerkFair point, but you're fine on that score. You'd catch every illusion centre stage — it's only the far wings you'd miss, and nothing important happens out there. Thirty-two for the pair.
YouThat's not bad at all. Although — is there much difference between those and something in the stalls, price-wise? I'd stretch a bit for a proper view.
ClerkStalls are fifty-four a pair when we have them, which tonight we don't — not together, anyway. I could do you two singles down there, but you'd be split up. Rows apart, opposite sides.
YouHmm. To be honest, we don't mind sitting apart, if that's the only way to get up close. What would two singles set us back?
ClerkForty-six the pair, split. So: cheaper and together up top, or dearer and apart down front. Your call.
YouYou've laid that out beautifully. Failing that… I think we'll take the pair up top — I'd rather share the armrest than the magician.
ClerkWise. And between you and me, the side of the upper circle is where the real fans sit — you get a sneaky angle on his hands. Just don't tell him I said so.
YouMy lips are sealed. You've been endlessly patient with a man who should have booked three weeks ago — thank you, honestly.
ClerkAll part of the magic. Enjoy the show — and watch his hands, not his mouth. That's where they always get you.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The clerk's very first answer is basically "no" — yet the latecomer still walks away with tickets. What did the clerk reveal in that same breath that kept the door open?

2. By the end, the latecomer is offered two quite different deals. What are the two options, and what is each one's trade-off — what do you gain and what do you give up with each?

3. "Watch his hands, not his mouth — that's where they always get you." What is the clerk actually telling you about how magic works, and why say it that way instead of plainly?

1. Officially the house is sold out — but the clerk adds that stragglers and returns come back to the desk on the night, and offers to have a look. The "no" and the loophole arrive together, so the latecomer knows to keep asking rather than walk off.
2. Option one: the returned pair in the upper circle — cheaper (thirty-two) and sitting together, but a side, "restricted" view. Option two: two singles in the stalls — dearer (forty-six) and a much closer view, but split up, rows and sides apart. Cheaper-and-together versus dearer-and-closer.
3. A magician steers your attention with patter and eye contact so you don't notice what the hands are doing — that's misdirection. The clerk wraps the tip in a joke rather than a lecture, so it lands as a shared secret between two people in the know, not a spoiler.

The Echo 🕰️

A hundred years ago, the surest "sold out" sign in the world had a name: Harry Houdini. He hung upside down from cranes over crowded streets, escaped from a padlocked tank of water, and packed theatres from New York to London — people queued for hours just for the chance of a seat.

The trick behind the tricks wasn't only the escaping. It was the wanting — Houdini made an audience desperate to be in the room, on the night, in case they missed the one where it went wrong. Every full house you've ever failed to get into owes him a little something.

So when you're at a window begging for a returned pair, take heart: you're standing in a very old queue. People have been talking their way in to see the impossible for well over a century.

Two bigger questions

  1. Houdini could have simply performed his escapes — instead he made sure everyone knew a show might sell out, so they scrambled for tickets. Is manufactured scarcity clever showmanship, or a kind of trick played on the audience before the curtain even rises?
  2. A magician's whole art depends on you not seeing how it's done — yet we pay, gladly, to be fooled. Why do we enjoy being deceived on stage when we'd hate it anywhere else? Where's the line between a delightful trick and a con?
