Talking About Sport

The match was one for the ages. Your friend saw it completely differently. Time to argue about it — nicely.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

Last night's final was the kind people will still be talking about in twenty years — a late winner, a comeback nobody saw coming, and a stadium that nearly took off. You watched every minute, and you have Opinions.

This morning you bump into a friend who watched exactly the same game — and somehow came away thinking almost the opposite. Was it the manager's genius or the players' nerve? Did the better side win, or did they get lucky? You're both grinning, you're both certain you're right, and neither of you is going anywhere until this is settled.

Here's the trick: the whole point of talking about sport is to disagree — loudly, happily, and still be friends at the final whistle. Winning the argument isn't the goal. Keeping it fun is.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. What's the last sporting moment that genuinely made you react out loud — jump up, gasp, groan? What was it, and were you alone or with people?
  2. Some people can argue about a match for an hour and love every second; others find it baffling. Which are you — and can you enjoy a sport you don't really understand?

Who's who

You
The Fan 🧣

Still buzzing from the match, full of strong opinions and happy to defend them. You love a good debate — as long as it stays a debate.

The other voice
A Mate ⚽

Watched the same game, drew the opposite conclusions, and is delighted to tell you why. Winds you up on purpose — but always with a grin.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how they push back, give ground, and keep it warm even when they completely disagree.

MateTell me you watched the final last night. Please tell me you saw it.
YouAre you joking? I was glued to the sofa — I don't think I breathed for the last ten minutes. What a game.
MateWasn't it? I still can't quite believe that winner went in. When it hit the net I shouted so loud I woke the baby.
YouTo be fair, they thoroughly deserved it. They were the better side all night, if you ask me.
MateSee, I'm not so sure about that, actually. For the first hour they barely got out of their own half. I'd say they rode their luck a bit.
YouYou've got a point there — the first half was scrappy, no argument. But you can't deny that second-half performance was something special.
MateNo, there's no denying that. That comeback was one for the ages. I take your point on the second half.
YouThe manager's the real difference, though. The way he changed it at half-time — that's world-class, no question.
MateHmm, I'd give the credit to the players, personally. A manager can only do so much once they've crossed that white line.
YouI see where you're coming from, but a good manager wins you those tight games. That's exactly what he's paid for.
MateEach to their own, I suppose. Anyway — who do you fancy for the trophy now? Surely nobody's stopping them.
YouHonestly? I wouldn't write off the holders just yet. One good night doesn't win you a tournament.
MateNow that I'll agree with. It's a long old road to the final — plenty can still go wrong.
YouSame time next week, then? My place — I'll sort the snacks if you bring the strong opinions.
MateYou're on. Just don't expect me to agree with a single word you say.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The two friends agree the winning team was good, but they disagree about why they won. What are their two different explanations?

2. "You've got a point there… But you can't deny…" — the fan does two opposite things in one breath here. What are they, and why put them in that order?

3. By the end, do they ever actually decide who was right? What does "Each to their own" do to the argument — and why is that a good outcome, not a failure?

1. The fan thinks the manager made the difference — his half-time changes. The mate gives the credit to the players, arguing a manager "can only do so much once they've crossed that white line." Same result, two completely different readings of it.
2. First they concede — "you've got a point, the first half was scrappy" — then they push back — "but you can't deny the second half was special." Giving ground first makes the disagreement land softly: the other person feels heard, so they don't dig in.
3. No — nobody wins. "Each to their own" quietly agrees to disagree and lets both walk away with their opinion intact. That's the point: the goal was never to be proved right, it was to enjoy the argument — and they end by booking the next one.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for having strong opinions and keeping the peace — at this match and every argument after it. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🗣️ Putting your opinion forward

Phrase 1

You want to give your verdict on the match, but you'd rather flag it clearly as your take than announce it as fact. Which opener does that best — and invites the other person to disagree?

A. "Everyone knows they were the better team."
B. "They were the better team. That's a fact."
C. "They were the better team, if you ask me."
C — "…, if you ask me."
It labels the opinion as yours and leaves the door open. A and B slam it shut — "that's a fact" is how a friendly debate turns into a row. Cousins: "the way I see it…", "personally, I'd say…"
Phrase 2

You feel strongly that one player was the difference — you want to make a firm claim but leave zero room for doubt in your own head. There's a two-word tag you add on the end: "That's world-class, ___ ___."

"…, no question."
Or "…, no doubt about it." It stamps your opinion with total conviction — the confident cousin of the softer "if you ask me." Use it when you want to plant a flag, not test the water.

🤝 Agreeing warmly

Phrase 3

Your friend says something you feel 100% too. "Yes" is flat. You want the strongest, most natural way to say you're completely with them. Complete it: "I couldn't ___ ___."

"I couldn't agree more."
Counter-intuitive but true: "couldn't agree more" = you agree totally (there's no more agreement possible). Beware the trap — "I couldn't agree less" means the exact opposite.
Phrase 4

Your friend says exactly what was on the tip of your tongue. There's a lovely idiom for "you just said my thought out loud." Put the pieces in order:

the wordsyou tookout of my mouthright
"You took the words right out of my mouth."
Warm, enthusiastic agreement — it says "we're on the exact same page." A simpler cousin: "That's exactly what I was going to say."

🤔 Disagreeing without falling out

Phrase 5

Your friend claims the winning team got lucky. You think they're wrong — but you want to open your disagreement gently, not head-on. What three-or-four-word phrase eases into a "no" without any bite?

"I'm not so sure about that."
Add "…actually" or "…to be honest" to soften it further. It signals disagreement while sounding thoughtful rather than combative — the opposite of "no, you're wrong."
Phrase 6

The cleverest way to disagree is to agree first. You admit your friend is partly right, then push back. Complete the concession: "You've got a ___ ___, but…"

"You've got a point there, but…"
Give a little ground before you take some — the other person feels heard, so they stay open. Same job: "Fair enough, but…" and "That's true, but even so…"
Phrase 7

Your friend has just made an argument you understand — you're going to disagree, but first you want to show you genuinely get their thinking. Which phrase does that best?

A. "That makes no sense to me."
B. "I see where you're coming from, but…"
C. "Whatever you say."
B — "I see where you're coming from, but…"
It validates their reasoning before you challenge it. A is blunt; C is dismissive and a bit rude. Close relative: "I take your point, but…"
Phrase 8

You've both made your case and neither of you is budging. Rather than argue in circles, you want to good-naturedly close it: "we'll never agree, and that's fine." What's the classic four-word phrase for that?

"Each to their own."
The friendly white flag — it agrees to disagree and lets everyone keep their opinion with no hard feelings. Cousins: "let's agree to disagree" and "we'll have to leave it there."

⚽ Reacting to a big result

Phrase 9

Your team lost, but the other side genuinely earned it — and you're big enough to say so, even though it stings. What phrase lets you admit an opponent deserved the win with good grace?

"To be fair, they thoroughly deserved it."
"To be fair…" signals you're about to be generous, even against your own interest. "Thoroughly deserved it" gives full, gracious credit — the mark of someone who can lose well.
Phrase 10

The winning goal has just gone in and you can't quite process it. You want to react to the sheer drama — half disbelief, half joy. Reorder the pieces into a natural exclamation:

can'tthatI stillwent inbelieve
"I still can't believe that went in!"
The go-to reaction to a jaw-dropping moment. Turn up the volume with: "What a result!", "I nearly fell off the sofa!" or "You couldn't write it!"
Quick test: pick any opinion you actually hold about sport and run the whole arc out loud — state it ("if you ask me…"), agree with an imaginary friend ("couldn't agree more"), disagree softly ("I'm not so sure…"), concede then push back ("you've got a point, but…"), and bow out ("each to their own"). When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your argument. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer to keep the pace of a real back-and-forth going.

Round 1 — you are the fan 🧣

Your mate speaks — you answer. No looking back at the phrases; trust your ear. Remember: strong opinions, soft edges.

MatePlease tell me you stayed up for the final. What a finish, eh?
MateHonestly though, I thought the winners were pretty ordinary. They got lucky, if you ask me.
MateCome on — for the first hour they barely touched the ball. You can't call that deserved.
MateFair enough, the second half was better. But it was all down to the players, not the manager.
MateWe're never going to agree on this one, are we?
MateHa — go on then, who's your pick to win the whole thing now?

Round 2 — now YOU wind THEM up ⚽

Swap sides. This time you're the one with the contrary take — pick any real result you like and argue the unpopular side, all in good fun. Give as good as you get.

FanThat was one of the best games I've ever seen. Best team won, no question.
FanLucky? They scored three goals! How is that lucky?
FanOkay, maybe the second goal was a bit soft. But the winner was pure quality.
FanYou just like disagreeing with me, don't you?
FanRight, forget last night — who wins the final, you reckon?
FanYou're on. Loser buys the snacks next week.
The Twist

🌩️ When your team just lost

One more round — no script, no safety net. This time YOUR team just lost the big one, and your mate supports the team that beat you. They're delighted, they won't let it go, and they're gently rubbing it in. Start the timer and talk your way through it: react to the result without sulking, give the winners the credit they've earned ("to be fair…"), defend your own team where you honestly can, and find at least one genuine positive to hold on to. One rule: the moment you turn sour or make excuses, you've lost the room — a good loser wins the respect the scoreboard didn't.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1930, thirteen teams travelled to Uruguay for the first ever World Cup — a tournament some countries couldn't be bothered to attend. Uruguay won, the losing finalists went home furious, and people have been arguing about the result ever since.

Nearly a century later, here you are on a Monday morning, refusing to concede a single point about a game you didn't even play in. You're part of a very old tradition: for as long as there have been matches, there have been two people who saw the same one and can't agree on a thing.

The trophy gets lifted once. The argument goes on forever — and honestly, that's half the fun.
