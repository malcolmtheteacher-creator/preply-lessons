Words and Pictures

A girl grows up in Tehran and draws her country in black ink. How does a comic tell a story that words alone cannot?

Today: you'll learn how a graphic memoir makes meaning through words, pictures and the gaps between panels, and use a four-step method to write and talk about any page, so you can analyse images with the same precision as sentences.
Before we start — talk these through:
  • Has a picture or a photograph ever told you more than a paragraph of words? What did it show?
  • What can a drawing do that a written description cannot — and what can writing do that a drawing cannot?
  • If you wanted to explain a painful memory to a stranger, would you choose words, pictures or both? Why?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — A memoir that began in France

Marjane Satrapi was born in 1969 and grew up in Tehran, the capital of Iran. As a young adult she left the country, and she later lived in Paris. Between 2000 and 2003 she published her life story in French as a comic, in four volumes, under the title Persepolis — the Greek name for the ancient Persian capital.

It is a memoir: a true account of her own childhood and youth. It begins when she is a child of about ten, in the years when Iran's government fell and a new, much stricter one took its place, and it follows her through war, through teenage years abroad in Europe and back to Tehran.

English readers met it a few years later, and it became one of the best-known graphic memoirs in the world. In 2007 Satrapi and a co-director turned it into an animated film, which shared a Jury Prize at the Cannes Film Festival and was nominated for an Oscar for Best Animated Feature.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. In which language did Persepolis first appear, and over what years?

2. What does it mean to call the book a memoir?

3. What happened to it in 2007?

1. In French, in four volumes, between 2000 and 2003.

2. It is a true account of the author's own life, told by herself.

3. It was made into an animated film, which shared a Jury Prize at Cannes and was nominated for an Oscar.

Discuss:
  • Why might someone choose to tell a true story about their country in a comic, not an essay or a novel?

Part 2 — How a page of comics works

A comic page is built from panels: small boxes, each showing one moment. Words appear in two main ways — in captions, which usually carry the narrator's voice, and in speech bubbles, which carry what characters say. The picture and the words never do exactly the same job.

Between the panels is the gutter, the white space where nothing is drawn. The comics scholar Scott McCloud argued in his book Understanding Comics that readers fill this gap themselves: we see a person raise a fist in one panel and a broken window in the next, and our mind joins them into an event that is not drawn. McCloud called this closure.

So a comic asks more of its reader than it seems to. Part of the story is on the page, and part is made in the reader's head. The author controls what to show, what to hide and how long we spend looking.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What are the two main ways words appear in a comic?

2. What is the gutter?

3. What does the reader do in the gap between panels?

1. In captions (the narrator's voice) and in speech bubbles (what characters say).

2. The blank space between panels.

3. The reader supplies what is missing and joins two moments into one event.

Discuss:
  • Think of a film cut that works like the gutter: two shots, and the audience fills in what happens between them.

Part 3 — Why black and white?

Satrapi's drawings are stark. They use only black ink on white paper, with bold shapes and simple, stylised faces. There are no shadows, no grey and almost no background detail. A page can look childlike at first, as if a child had drawn it.

That simplicity does real work. Critics have suggested that a plain style lets readers in different countries see themselves in the characters rather than treating them as exotic strangers. It also creates a strong juxtaposition: a child's simple drawing style set against the very adult events of revolution, war and loss.

Imagine a panel, drawn in this style, where a small figure sits calmly at a table while the caption describes something frightening in an even tone. The calm voice and the frightening fact pull against each other, and the reader feels the tension without being told to. That is how a stark style can convey more than a detailed one.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Describe Satrapi's drawing style in two or three words.

2. Why do critics think the simple style helps readers?

3. What does the imagined panel show about how words and image can interact?

1. Stark, black and white, simple and stylised.

2. It lets readers from different places see themselves in the characters instead of seeing exotic strangers.

3. A calm picture or tone set against a frightening caption creates tension that the reader feels for themselves.

Discuss:
  • Is a simple drawing style more honest or less honest about a painful subject? Argue one side.

How the tool works

Reading a comic well means reading three layers at once — then saying what they do together. The Panel Cycle gives you four steps that work on any page, from any comic, film still or painting.

Using it, step by step

  1. Words. Read the caption and any speech first. Note the tone: calm, bitter, playful, formal. Write one phrase, not a summary.
  2. Image. Look at what is drawn: size, position, expression, what is shown large and what is small. Describe it as a camera would — before you interpret.
  3. Gap. Ask what the page does not say or show. What do we have to imagine or connect? Is anything left out on purpose?
  4. Effect. Join the three: what does the combination make the reader feel or understand, and how? Say it in one sentence: By setting … against …, the page makes the reader …

Try it yourself

These are observations about an imagined page. Which step of the cycle is each one?

1. The caption says the family is “perfectly calm”.
2. The mother's hands are drawn gripping the edge of the table.
3. We understand that the calm is pretended, although nobody says so.
4. The contrast makes the reader uneasy and a little protective of the family.
5. A tall black figure fills the panel while the child sits tiny in one corner.
6. The caption does not mention fear at all.
7. The reader has to connect two panels to see that time has passed.
8. The plain drawing makes the scene feel both childlike and sinister.

1. Words — it reports what is written, in the narrator's own phrase.

2. Image — it describes what is drawn, as a camera would.

3. Gap — it is something we infer, not written and not drawn.

4. Effect — it states what the combination does to the reader.

5. Image — size and position are visual facts.

6. Words — it is about what is written — and what the written text omits.

7. Gap — the connection happens in the space between panels.

8. Effect — it names the overall impression the page creates.

Where the tool breaks

It can become a checklist. Four steps are a way in, not a formula. The best analysis shows how the steps work together; do not list them one by one.

Not every page has a gap. Sometimes the words and the picture simply agree. Then say so, and ask why the author wanted agreement here.

Effect is a claim, not a fact. Two readers can feel different things. Support your effect with the exact word or detail that caused it.

Talk it through:
  • Which of the four steps do you tend to skip — and what do you miss when you skip it?
  • Try the cycle on a film still or an advert you know well. Which step is hardest to do?

Language — Writing about a text in the present

When you write about a book, a comic or a film, you describe it in the present tense — even if the events in the story happened long ago. The history around the text, and the author's life, go in the past.

1 · The literary present. Describe what happens in the text in the present simple: “The caption describes the calm; the picture shows a tense mother.” Not described or showed.
2 · The author's choices. Use the present for what the author does on the page: “Satrapi uses black ink to…”, “She leaves the gutter empty so that…”.
3 · History and biography. Use the past for events outside the text: “Satrapi published the first volume in 2000.” “The government changed in 1979.”
4 · Events before the moment you describe. Use the present perfect or the past for what has happened earlier in the story: “By this page, the family has already lost a friend.”
5 · Do not mix tenses in one sentence without a reason. The narrator describes the street and then she walked home. → “…and then walks home.”
