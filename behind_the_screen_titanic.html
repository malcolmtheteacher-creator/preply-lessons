BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Titanic

The most successful love story ever filmed sits on top of a real grave of about 1,500 people. So how much of it is true — and what did the film quietly change?

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Titanic — a romance invented and set inside a real disaster — discover how much of the film matches the historical record and where it quietly rewrites it, and master the difference between gradable adjectives (very cold, fairly big) and extreme or absolute ones (absolutely freezing, utterly impossible), together with the intensifiers each of them takes.
Before we begin — talk it through: Think of a real event you know mainly from a film rather than from a book or a lesson. How confident are you that what you picture is accurate? And when a film is built on a real tragedy, does the director owe anything to the people who actually died — or is a film simply a film?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. The banquet was lavish: eleven courses, gold-rimmed plates, orchids on every table and a string quartet playing in the corner all evening.
lavish (adj) — extremely rich, generous and expensive; done on a grand scale, with no attempt to save money.
2. He waved away the guide, refused to take a map and told the rescue team they would not be needed. Three days later his hubris had him lost above the snow line in a storm.
hubris (n) — dangerous over-confidence; the kind of pride that makes a person ignore warnings and invites disaster.
3. As soon as the sun went down the temperature plummeted, dropping from a mild afternoon to well below zero in under an hour.
to plummet (v) — to fall very fast and very far, usually suddenly.
4. Her great-grandparents crossed the Atlantic in steerage, sharing a crowded bunk room far below the deck where the wealthy passengers walked in furs.
steerage (n) — the cheapest accommodation on a ship, low down in the hull, where poorer emigrants travelled. Often called third class.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. futile is closest in meaning to: (a) pointless   (b) urgent   (c) skilful
(a) pointless — futile (adj) means having no chance of succeeding, however hard you try.
6. peril is closest in meaning to: (a) a delay   (b) serious danger   (c) permission
(b) serious danger — peril (n) is grave danger, especially of death. The adjective is perilous.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. For thirty years nobody believed a word of her account. Then new evidence came to light and completely vindicated her.
To vindicate someone is: (a) to punish them; (b) to prove them right after they have been doubted or blamed; (c) to interview them.
(b) — to vindicate (v) — to show that someone was right all along, clearing them of doubt or blame.
8. Every time he told the story the fish grew longer and the storm grew wilder; by the tenth telling he had embellished it beyond recognition.
To embellish a story is: (a) to shorten it; (b) to forget it; (c) to add invented details to make it better.
(c) — to embellish (v) — to add details that are not true in order to make a story more impressive or entertaining.

D. Collocation and odd-one-out

9. English has a fixed phrase for the very first journey a brand-new ship or aircraft makes. Which one is it?
(a) a maiden harbour   (b) a maiden voyage   (c) a maiden crew
(b) a maiden voyage — maiden (adj, in fixed phrases) means first-ever: a maiden voyage, a maiden flight, a maiden speech.
10. Three of these four words describe the same attitude. Which one is the odd one out?
(a) complacency   (b) vigilance   (c) caution   (d) alertness
(a) complacency is the odd one out. The other three are all about staying watchful. Complacency (n) — a comfortable, self-satisfied feeling that everything is fine, which stops you taking proper care. The adjective is complacent.

The film, in three parts

James Cameron's Titanic (1997). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.) The disaster it describes really happened, and around 1,500 people died in it, so we'll treat it seriously.

Part 1 — A treasure hunt and a wedding nobody wants

The film opens not in 1912 but in the present, on a research ship. A treasure hunter, Brock Lovett, is sending submersibles down to the wreck of the Titanic, hunting for a legendary blue diamond. What his team brings up instead is a drawing of a young woman wearing it. The image reaches a 100-year-old survivor, Rose Dawson Calvert, who tells them quietly that the woman in the picture is her — and begins to talk.

Southampton, April 1912. Seventeen-year-old Rose DeWitt Bukater boards the largest ship ever built with her mother and her fiancé, Caledon Hockley, a steel heir. The family money is gone; the marriage is the arrangement that will save them. Rose's world is lavish and airless, and she can see the rest of her life laid out for her like a set table. On the second night she runs to the stern rail meaning to jump.

Jack Dawson, a penniless young artist travelling in steerage on a ticket he won at cards, talks her back over the rail. Their first meeting is a lie told to the crew to explain why he was holding her. It buys him a dinner in first class, and Rose an evening down in third, where the drinking and dancing are louder and more alive than anything upstairs. Cal and Rose's mother watch the friendship harden into something else, and set about crushing it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is Rose on the ship, and why is she so unhappy?

2. How do Jack and Rose actually meet?

1. Her family is secretly ruined, and she is being married off to the wealthy Caledon Hockley to fix it. She feels trapped in a life chosen for her.

2. She goes to the stern intending to jump; Jack, a third-class passenger, talks her back and pulls her to safety.

Discuss:
  • Rose has money, status and a future that most passengers on that ship would envy — and she is in despair. Is her situation a genuine trap, or does the film ask us to feel too sorry for the richest person on board?

Part 2 — Ice, arithmetic and locked gates

Late on the fourth night the lookouts see the iceberg far too late. The ship turns, but she strikes along her starboard side below the waterline. On the bridge the noise seems almost trivial; below, the water is already coming in.

Thomas Andrews, the ship's designer, works it out with a pencil and tells Captain Smith the truth: too many forward compartments are open to the sea, the flooding will spill over from one into the next, and the ship will founder. Then comes the second, colder piece of arithmetic. There are lifeboats for roughly half the people aboard, and in the confusion the first boats are lowered barely half full. Bruce Ismay, the man who runs the line, hovers at the officers' shoulders and eventually steps into a boat himself.

Meanwhile Cal has had a crewman plant the diamond on Jack, who is handcuffed in an office deep in the ship as the water climbs. Rose refuses a seat in a lifeboat, goes back down and frees him. The film's most furious sequence follows: gates held shut across the third-class corridors, families waiting below in their lifebelts, stewards saying it will be their turn soon. Upstairs, the band goes on playing on the boat deck; Andrews stands alone by the fireplace; the captain goes back to the bridge.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Andrews tell the captain after the collision, and why is it fatal?

2. What are the two decisions that make the film's third-class passengers so vulnerable?

1. That too many compartments have been opened to the sea and the water will simply spill from one into the next — the ship cannot stay afloat. It is a mathematical certainty, not bad luck.

2. There are lifeboats for only about half the people aboard, and the gates and corridors keep the third-class passengers below while the boats — many of them half empty — are lowered.

Discuss:
  • Andrews knows within minutes exactly how long the ship has. If you were in charge, would you tell two thousand people the truth immediately, or manage the information? What does each choice cost?

Part 3 — The water is the killer

The bow goes under, the stern lifts, and the ship tears apart between her funnels. The stern falls back, rises almost vertically and slides down. What follows is the part audiences remember least clearly and the film insists on: the ship's sinking is not the disaster. The sea is about −2°C, and the hundreds of people left in the water do not drown so much as freeze, in minutes, calling out until the noise thins into silence.

Jack finds Rose a piece of floating panelling that will hold one person. He stays in the water, holding her hand, and talks to her until he can no longer answer. Only one boat comes back to search, far too late, and it lifts a handful of survivors out of a field of the dead. Rose lets Jack go and signals to it with a whistle taken from a dead officer.

She is picked up by the Carpathia the next morning. Asked her name, she gives Jack's. Cal, who survives, never finds her. Eighty-four years later, the old woman finishes the story, walks to the stern of the research ship in the dark and drops the diamond — worth more than the entire salvage operation — into the sea above the wreck.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What actually kills most of the people who are still alive when the ship goes down?

2. What name does Rose give when she is rescued, and what does she do with the diamond at the end?

1. The cold. The water is around freezing point, and people in it survive only minutes; almost no boats return in time.

2. She gives Jack's surname — Rose Dawson. As a very old woman she quietly drops the diamond into the sea above the wreck, telling nobody.

Discuss:
  • The most shocking fact in the film is that only one lifeboat went back. Why do you think the others didn't — and what would you honestly have done in a boat that still had room?

Grammar — gradable & extreme adjectives

This is a story told in extremes: the biggest ship, the coldest water, the most complete disaster. English handles extremes with a specific grammar, and getting it wrong is one of the clearest markers of a non-native speaker. The rule is not about the intensifier — it's about the adjective it is attached to.

Two families of adjective:
• Gradable adjectives sit on a scale. You can have more or less of them: cold, big, expensive, frightened, tired, good, angry. They take degree words: very, extremely, fairly, rather, quite, a bit, slightly. → "The dining saloon was very grand." "The crossing was fairly rough."
• Extreme (non-gradable) adjectives already contain the top of the scale: freezing, enormous, exhausted, terrified, appalling, devastating, superb. They take: absolutely, utterly, completely, totally, simply. → "The wreck footage is absolutely fascinating." "The passengers in the water were utterly terrified."
Absolute adjectives work the same way. Words like impossible, dead, unique, perfect, essential, identical, empty describe an all-or-nothing state, so degree words sound wrong: not "very essential" but absolutely essential; not "very identical" but completely identical.
Pairs worth knowing: cold → freezing · big → enormous / huge · small → tiny · tired → exhausted · frightened → terrified · surprised → astonished · angry → furious · bad → appalling · good → superb · interesting → fascinating · wet → soaked · hungry → starving.
Three things to notice: really works with both families ("really cold", "really freezing"). Absolutely enormous is correct; very enormous is not. And quite changes its meaning depending on the family: quite cold = fairly cold, but quite extraordinary = completely extraordinary.

1. Which intensifier does the adjective need?

Write very or absolutely in each gap, according to the adjective that follows.

1. The night of the crossing was calm — not a breath of wind.

2. When the lights finally failed, the darkness on deck was total.

3. The first-class staircase was impressive.

4. Rowing back through the wreckage struck some of them as impossible.

1. very (calm is gradable)

2. absolutely (total is absolute — also completely / utterly)

3. very (impressive is gradable — also extremely)

4. absolutely (impossible is absolute — also completely / utterly)

2. Fix the mistake

Each sentence puts the wrong intensifier with the adjective. Write the corrected two-word phrase.

1. The water around the lifeboats was very freezing. →

2. The musicians' decision to keep playing was absolutely brave. →

3. Her account of that night was very unique among the survivors'. →

4. The men at the oars were absolutely tired by sunrise. →

1. absolutely freezing (or utterly / completely freezing — or "very cold")

2. very brave (brave is gradable — or extremely brave)

3. absolutely unique (unique is absolute — or completely unique)

4. very tired (or, if you swap the adjective: absolutely exhausted)

3. Complete the account — mix both families

Fill each gap with very, absolutely or really, choosing the one the adjective allows. Read the whole thing aloud when you're done.

"From the boat," she said, "the ship looked vast, and for an hour the whole thing seemed unlikely rather than serious. Then the lights went out and the noise from the water began. By the time the sun came up we were exhausted, our hands were sore, and the ship was gone."

absolutely / really vast · very / really unlikely · absolutely / really exhausted · very / really sore · completely / absolutely gone

Notice the pattern: gradable adjectives (unlikely, sore) take very; extreme and absolute ones (vast, exhausted, gone) take absolutely and its family. Really is the safe word that fits either side.

Carry this into the Speak tab. When you describe that night, you will reach constantly for extremes — and "very freezing" will give you away every time.

The Real Story — what the film changed, and what it got right

Most films about real disasters play loose with the record. Titanic is stranger than that: some of its most cinematic-looking details are precisely accurate, while some of its quietest, most confident moments are invention. Here is where the line actually falls.

🚢 The lifeboat arithmetic is exactly right — and worse than the film says

She carried 20 lifeboats: fourteen wooden boats, two emergency cutters and four collapsibles, with a total certified capacity of 1,178 people for roughly 2,200 aboard. The sting is the legality. The Board of Trade's rules scaled the number of boats by the ship's tonnage, not by the number of people it carried, and the top band simply stopped at 10,000 tons — vessels above that needed sixteen boats for 960 people. Titanic was 46,328 tons. By carrying twenty boats she was not merely legal; she was over-equipped. The regulation had been written for a world of much smaller ships and had never been updated.

The official British figures put 2,201 aboard, 1,490 lost and 711 saved; other counts run a little higher, around 2,224 aboard and roughly 1,500 dead. Every version of the arithmetic ends in the same place.

Invented people in a room full of real ones

Jack and Rose are entirely fictional, and so is the blue diamond. Almost everyone around them was real: Captain Edward John Smith; Thomas Andrews, the ship's designer, who worked out after the collision that too many compartments were open and gave her roughly ninety minutes; J. Bruce Ismay, who ran the White Star Line; First Officer William Murdoch; the eight musicians under bandmaster Wallace Hartley, none of whom survived; and Margaret Brown. There is even a coincidence the producers only learned about after release: a real crewman named J. Dawson — Joseph Dawson — is buried in Halifax, Nova Scotia.

Margaret Brown is the clearest case of a legend the film simply inherited. She was never called "Molly" in her life — she was Maggie as a girl and Margaret as a woman. The nickname and the whole "Unsinkable Molly Brown" persona were built after her death in 1932, by a Denver Post writer in 1933 and then a popular author, before a Broadway show and a 1964 film fixed it permanently. She was in Boat 6, she did help row, and the women in that boat did want to go back — but it was Quartermaster Robert Hichens at the tiller who refused, and Boat 6 never returned. Only Fifth Officer Harold Lowe, in Boat 14, went back into the wreckage, and he found very few people still alive.

The class question: right conclusion, softer mechanism

The film's anger about class is not misplaced. Of the passengers, roughly 62% of first class survived, 41% of second, and about 25% of third; the crew fared worst of all at around 23%. Break it down further and it is starker still: of the women and children in first class, five were lost out of 148; in third class, 138 were lost out of 259. Overall about 74% of women and 52% of children survived, against 20% of men.

Where the film goes beyond the record is the mechanism. It shows gates deliberately locked to keep steerage below while first class fills the boats. Gates and barriers genuinely existed — American immigration rules of the period required physical separation of third class to control infectious disease — and at least one survivor described a sailor locking a gate, which was then broken open. But a third-class steward testified the gates were opened, historians find no evidence of a policy of leaving those passengers to drown, and the lawyer who represented third class at the inquiry concluded there was no proof of deliberate discrimination in the evacuation. Distance, language, a maze of corridors, late warnings and the reluctance of families to separate did a great deal of the killing. That is less satisfying than a padlock — and it is closer to the truth.

Three myths, one of which the film created

"Unsinkable" was never a slogan. White Star never formally advertised her that way; trade journals called her practically unsinkable, with the qualifier attached. The flat, confident claim really belongs to the days after the sinking, when a company vice-president told the New York Times he had believed her unsinkable. The film's own line about God being unable to sink her is invented.

Ismay pushing the captain for more speed is a staple of popular retellings and appears in the film. There is no evidence for it.

Murdoch's shooting and suicide is the one that caused real harm. Several survivors — among them George Rheims and Eugene Daly — did describe an officer shooting a man near the last collapsible and then shooting himself, and other officers admitted firing warning shots that night. But nothing establishes that the officer was Murdoch, and the film names him. His family and his home town of Dalbeattie in Scotland were distressed. On 15 April 1998, Scott Neeson of 20th Century Fox went to Dalbeattie, said publicly that Murdoch really was a hero in life, apologised for the distress caused to his relatives and the town, and handed £5,000 to Murdoch's nephew for a memorial prize at the local school. The scene stayed in the film.

The light on the horizon — the film's biggest omission

Titanic shows the officers firing rockets at a light in the distance and then lets the light go. It was a real ship, and the argument about her has run for a century. The SS Californian had stopped for the night because of ice. Her single wireless operator, Cyril Evans, shut his set down at about 23:35 and went to bed — minutes before the collision. Her officers watched eight white rockets go up over a nearby vessel and took no action; her master, Captain Stanley Lord, was not roused into doing anything. Both the American and British inquiries of 1912 condemned him, the British one concluding that the Californian might have saved many if not all of those lost. A 1992 reappraisal by the British Marine Accident Investigation Branch still criticised Lord's inaction, but judged that she could not in fact have reached the scene in time — at best she would have done the job the Carpathia did.

Lord's defenders spent decades arguing that the ship the Titanic saw was a third vessel, the Norwegian sealer Samson, whose crew supposedly saw rockets while hunting illegally. Records placing the Samson in an Icelandic port around that date have effectively destroyed that defence.

The ship that did come gets almost no screen time either. The Carpathia was 58 miles away when her operator took the distress call. Captain Arthur Rostron turned her round, shut off the heating and hot water to force every ounce of steam into the engines, drove her at about 17.5 knots — several knots above her rated speed — through the same ice field, and had blankets, soup and doctors waiting. She reached the boats before dawn and lifted just over 700 people out of them. It is the one unambiguously good piece of seamanship in the whole night, and the film gives it a single shot.

The wreck settled one argument — and opened another

Survivors said the ship broke in two. The 1912 British inquiry preferred the flat denial of Second Officer Charles Lightoller, the most senior surviving officer, and the version that went into the history books was of a ship sinking intact. On 1 September 1985, Robert Ballard and Jean-Louis Michel found her at about 3,800 metres, in two main pieces roughly 600 metres apart. Eva Hart, who had watched it as a seven-year-old and said so for seventy-three years, lived to see it. The film shows the break, and in showing it, it takes the survivors' side.

The exact choreography is still argued over. Evidence from the wreck suggests the ship probably broke at a far shallower angle than the near-vertical stern of the film — estimates in the region of 11 to 23 degrees — and experts still disagree about what failed first. Cameron himself dived to the wreck in 1995 before writing the script, and in 2012 he replaced the star field above Rose's raft with the real sky of that night, after the astrophysicist Neil deGrasse Tyson pointed out that the one in the film was wrong.

Two more things the film leaves quieter than the record does. The famous image of Thomas Andrews alone in the smoking room by the clock rests on a single steward's account, and that steward had probably left the ship some forty minutes earlier; other witnesses describe Andrews still working. And how Captain Smith died is simply not known — the accounts contradict each other, and his body was never recovered.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Which of these surprised you most — that the lifeboat shortage was perfectly legal, or that "Molly" Brown never existed under that name?
  • The film's conclusion about class is supported by the numbers; its explanation (locked gates) is not. Is that an acceptable trade — a true point made with an invented cause?
  • A film company apologised to a family and a town, gave money to a school, and left the scene in. Was that enough? What would "enough" look like?
  • What's real, what's invented — and does it matter, if the emotional truth of the night survives?

Task B · Does a film owe the dead the truth? (4 minutes)

The film names a real officer and shows him shooting a passenger and then himself, on evidence that does not identify him. It also invents locked gates to explain a real inequality. Build an argument: when a film is set inside a real disaster with named, real, recently-living people, what may it invent and what may it not? Does a caption, an apology or a donation settle the debt — or is the only honest option to change the name? Bring in other films about real events that you know.

Task C · Rules written for a smaller world (4 minutes)

The lifeboat shortage was legal, because the rule counted tonnage instead of people and had never caught up with the ships. Where else do you see regulation lagging behind reality? Make your case.
