The Deadline

It's Tuesday, the delivery is due Friday, and this morning you finally admitted it to yourself: it isn't going to happen. Every hour you sit on that knowledge, it gets more expensive. The call you're about to make is the difference between a supplier who slipped and a supplier who can't be trusted.

The Scene 🎬

Missing a deadline rarely destroys a working relationship. Hiding one almost always does.

You run a small studio — design, software, translation, machining; the trade doesn't matter — and Rothman & Co is your biggest client. The delivery promised for Friday is not going to be ready: a supplier failed you, a technical problem ate a week, and the honest completion date is next Wednesday. Nothing about this is unusual. Every business that makes things has this Tuesday.

What separates professionals is what happens next. The amateur goes quiet — answers emails a little slower, hopes for a miracle, and delivers the bad news on Friday at 4pm, when it's a crisis and an insult at once. The professional picks up the phone on Tuesday, while the client still has time to adjust — which converts the same bad news from a betrayal into an inconvenience.

The call has rules. Lead with the headline, not the excuses. Bring the new date — one you'd bet on, not one you're hoping for. Ask what the slip breaks at their end, because their real problem may be smaller or different than you fear. Offer what CAN arrive on time. And put a cost on your own failure, unasked. It's twenty minutes of discomfort that buys years of being believed.

The Conversation 💬

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for delivering a slipped deadline — to clients, bosses, colleagues, in-laws expecting you at six. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The whole call rests on one exchange rate: bad news early is cheap, bad news late is ruinous. Deliver the headline in your first breath — every sentence of preamble reads as squirming — and never, ever give a second date you aren't certain of. The first slip costs a deadline. The second costs the relationship.

📢 The headline, first

Phrase 1

The job: opening the call. Which version?

A. "So, it's been a really challenging couple of weeks, and I want to give you the full context before—"
B. "That's why I'm calling — I'd rather tell you now than on Friday."
C. "Everything's mostly on track! Just one or two small timing things to flag."
B — "I'd rather tell you now than on Friday."
A is the excuse-story arriving before the news — and the listener knows something's wrong from word one, so the preamble reads as squirming. C is worse: minimising language ("small timing things") that will be quoted back to you bitterly next week. B does two jobs in nine words: warns that bad news is coming, and frames the call itself as an act of respect — you're paying for the news with earliness. Which is exactly the currency it costs.

Take it anywhere: "I'd rather tell you now than ___." The opener that makes early delivery of bad news part of the message.
Phrase 2

The job: the news itself, unpadded. Complete it:

"The honest ___ is: we're not going to make the fifteenth."

"The honest answer is: we're not going to make the fifteenth."
Say the actual sentence. Not "we may be looking at some slippage", not "the timeline's under pressure" — the plain fact, with the date in it. "The honest answer is" earns its place: it signals you've stopped negotiating with reality and are reporting it, which is what lets the listener stop wondering what you're softening. Then attach the new date in the same breath — bad news without a new date is just panic, shared.

Take it anywhere: "The honest answer is: ___." For every status update tempted to blur itself.
Phrase 3

The job: the reason, in one line, refused as an excuse. Complete it:

"I'll spare you the long version — the one thing that ___ is ___."

"I'll spare you the long version — the one thing that matters is the supplier delivered wrong parts."
The client deserves the cause; they don't deserve the saga — sagas are for sympathy, and you're not calling for sympathy. One line of reason, then the crucial move: re-owning the promise anyway. "The supplier is my problem, not your excuse to absorb." Blame passed down the chain impresses nobody; a supplier who owns the whole promise, including the parts others broke, is the one who gets the next contract.

Take it anywhere: "I'll spare you the long version — the one thing that matters is ___." Then own the promise, whoever broke it.

🔧 Shrinking the damage

Phrase 4

The job: the question that resizes the whole problem. Complete it:

"What does this actually ___ at your end?"

"What does this actually break at your end?"
The turning point of the whole call — and the question guilty suppliers never ask, because they're too busy apologising to find out what they're apologising for. Your deadline is a means to THEIR end, and their end may need less than you think: here, one working unit for a Monday board, not the full delivery. You can't offer the rescue until you know the real damage. Ask, then be quiet and listen.

Take it anywhere: "What does this actually break at your end?" Every delay, every cancellation, every change of plan — measure the real damage before you price the apology.
Phrase 5

The job: the partial rescue. Complete it:

"Which part do you need ___? Because that part you can have on ___."

"Which part do you need first? Because that part you can have on time."
Deadlines feel like one thing; deliveries almost never are. Somewhere in your late shipment is an on-time piece — the working unit, the draft chapter, the headline figures — and offering it converts "everything is late" into "the thing you need is on time; the rest follows". This is not spin: it's logistics done with the client's actual Monday in mind. It saved this contract in one sentence.

Take it anywhere: "Which part do you need first?" Split every late delivery into the urgent piece and the rest — then land the piece.
Phrase 6

The job: making the new date believable — the second date is sacred. Put the pieces in order:

I can stand behind.Thursday is a dateNot hope for —stand behind.
"Thursday is a date I can stand behind. Not hope for — stand behind."
Her scepticism was earned — "the last supplier who gave me a revised date revised it twice more." A first slip is forgivable; a slipped RESCUE date is fatal, because it proves the problem isn't the schedule, it's your relationship with truth. So the new date must be built differently: on things that exist ("parts physically in my workshop"), with margin in it, and named as a commitment, not a forecast. If you're not certain, give the date you ARE certain of — even if it's uglier.

Take it anywhere: "That's a date I can stand behind." Only ever say it about one you can.

💷 Paying for it before you're asked

Phrase 7

The job: defending the delay itself. Complete it:

"I'd rather lose a week than hand you something ___-___."

"I'd rather lose a week than hand you something half-done."
The one moment the delay gets defended — not excused, defended — because the alternative to late is usually worse: on-time and broken, followed by a month of apologising in instalments. Clients who've lived that (most have — "that, I've lived") hear this sentence as maturity, not spin. It only works if it's true, and it's only true if the extra week actually produces the finished thing. Which is also the test of whether to say it.

Take it anywhere: "I'd rather be late than hand over something half-done." The quality defence — use sparingly, mean entirely.
Phrase 8

The job: killing the chasing before it starts. Complete it:

"You'll get updates ___ asking — every morning until it ___."

"You'll get updates without asking — every morning until it lands."
After a slip, silence is poison — every quiet day, the client wonders if it's happening again, and every chase they have to send costs you a point of trust. The fix is a broadcast schedule: fixed rhythm, two lines, even when the news is boring — ESPECIALLY when the news is boring, because "no change, still Thursday" is exactly what rebuilds the credit. The updates aren't information. They're evidence you've changed.

Take it anywhere: "You'll get updates without asking — every ___ until it's done." The standing repair for every trust you've dented.
Phrase 9

The job: pricing your own failure, unprompted. Complete it:

"This one's on us — you'll see that on the ___."

"This one's on us — you'll see that on the invoice."
The move almost nobody makes: compensation offered before it's demanded. The amount matters less than the direction — money moving towards the client, uninvited, says the apology has weight behind it. And there's cold logic under the grace: "I'd rather price my own mistake than have you price it for me." Self-imposed penalties are almost always cheaper than extracted ones — and infinitely cheaper than the silent penalty of a client who just stops calling.

Take it anywhere: "This one's on us — you'll see it on the invoice." When it's your miss, reach for your wallet before they reach for theirs.
Phrase 10

The job: the standing promise that closes the call. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "If anything moves by even a day, you'll hear it from me the same morning."
The call ends by turning this one painful Tuesday into policy: not "sorry about this time" but "here is how information will reach you from now on, permanently." It's the promise that makes the whole episode net-positive — her strange admission ("suppliers who delivered on time left me trusting them less than this call") is what it buys. Then comes the only hard part: keeping it, forever, including the mornings you'd rather not dial.

Take it anywhere: "If anything moves, you'll hear it from me the same day." The standing promise — the whole lesson in one sentence.

⚡ The twist

Round 1 again — but before you can dial on Tuesday, your business partner catches your arm: "Do NOT make that call. The Echo 🕰️

"Before this decade is out." The most public deadline in history — a president promising the Moon, on television, with the engineering not yet invented. And in January 1967 the programme hit the kind of Tuesday every supplier dreads at scale: the Apollo 1 fire killed three astronauts on the launchpad, and every schedule NASA held became waste paper overnight.

What happened next is why the deadline held. NASA didn't go quiet, didn't minimise, didn't ship the next capsule on time and hope. They stopped flying for twenty months, told Congress and the public exactly what had failed and why, redesigned the spacecraft — and rebuilt the schedule on things that existed rather than things they hoped. The slip was enormous, public and priced. And in July 1969, with five months of the decade to spare, the deadline that had slipped twenty months was met — because the dates given after the fire were dates they could stand behind.

Your Friday is smaller than the Moon. The physics of the promise is identical: deadlines survive honest slips and die of hidden ones — and the date you give after the failure is the one your whole reputation stands on.
