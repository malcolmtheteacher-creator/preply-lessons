Car Trouble

A noise you can't name, a light that won't go off, and a bill you can't see coming. Time to talk to the mechanic.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

For a week now your car has been telling you something's wrong, in the only language it has. There's a low grinding noise every time you brake, and a little orange engine light on the dashboard that flicked on, flicked off, and has now settled in for good. You've been driving with the radio a notch louder and hoping it sorts itself out. It hasn't.

So this morning you've swallowed your pride and pulled into the local garage. The trouble is, you can hear the fault but you can't name it — and the person who can name it also decides what it costs. Getting a fair deal today won't be about knowing engines. It'll be about how clearly you describe the problem, and how calmly you handle the number at the end.

Behind the counter: a mechanic with oil under the fingernails and a healthy suspicion of anyone who says "just make it go away." Straight-talking, a little dry, and — if you're honest with them — genuinely on your side.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When something you own breaks — a car, a phone, a boiler — are you the sort who investigates first, or the sort who hands it straight to an expert and hopes for the best? Why?
  2. Think of a time you were quoted a price that made you wince. What did you say? Looking back, would you handle it differently now?

Who's who

You
The Driver 🚗

No mechanic, but no fool either. You know how the fault sounds, when it happens, and roughly what you can afford — and you'd like all three respected.

The other voice
The Mechanic 🔧

Has heard every strange noise a car can make. Reads people as well as engines, and rewards a customer who describes the problem honestly instead of demanding a miracle.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how the driver paints the fault in words, and how a big number gets softened instead of swallowed.

MechanicMorning. What brings you in?
YouMorning — I don't suppose you could squeeze me in today, could you? She's been playing up and I'm a bit nervous about driving her much further.
MechanicDepends what she's doing. Talk me through it.
YouWell, there's this grinding sort of noise whenever I brake — low and a bit metallic — and the engine warning light's been coming on and off.
MechanicDoes the noise come and go, or is it every time?
YouMore or less every time now, and getting louder. It was only under hard braking at first; now it's most stops.
MechanicAnd that light — is it flickering, or has it stuck on?
YouStuck on, this last week. I'll be honest — I've been ignoring it and hoping for the best.
MechanicThe nation's favourite repair plan, that. Right — the grinding'll be your brake pads by the sound of it, maybe the discs too if they've been left a while. The light could be a dozen things; I'll have to plug it in to read the code.
YouFair enough. Roughly how much am I looking at, all in?
MechanicHard to say till she's up on the ramp. Pads, possibly discs, plus the diagnostic… you could be the wrong side of five hundred pounds.
YouRight. That's a fair bit steeper than I was expecting, to be honest. Is there any way of bringing that down at all?
MechanicI can fit pattern parts instead of the manufacturer's — same job, roughly half the price — and if the discs are still within spec, we leave them. I only replace what actually needs doing.
YouThat'd be a big help. And the warning light — do I actually need that sorting today, or is it something that can wait? While I'm here, any idea how long she'll be off the road?
MechanicLet me read the code first — if it's just a sensor, it'll keep a week or two; if it's the engine running lean, I'd not send you off with it. Pads only, you'll have her back this afternoon; discs to order, tomorrow morning. And I'll ring you before I touch anything over a hundred quid.
YouPerfect — I'd far rather know before than get a nasty surprise on the bill. You've been really straight with me; I appreciate it. I'll leave her with you, then. Here are the keys.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The driver never just says "it makes a noise." What TWO things do they add that turn a useless complaint into something a mechanic can actually work with?

2. The quote lands much higher than expected. The driver neither argues nor simply pays. Trace exactly what they say and do — and what the mechanic offers in return.

3. "I'll ring you before I touch anything over a hundred quid." Why does the driver treat this as good news — what particular fear does it lay to rest?

1. They describe the sound precisely — "a grinding sort of noise, low and a bit metallic" — and they say when it happens and how it has changed: only under hard braking at first, now on most stops and getting louder. Sound + timing is what lets the mechanic guess "brake pads, maybe discs" before touching the car.
2. The driver registers the shock politely — "a fair bit steeper than I was expecting, to be honest" — then asks one open question: "Is there any way of bringing that down at all?" That invites the mechanic to find the savings for them, which they do: pattern parts at half the price, leaving the discs if they pass, and replacing only what genuinely needs it.
3. It kills the fear of an out-of-control final bill — the nasty surprise where the car's already in pieces and the price has doubled. By promising a call before any big spend, the mechanic hands the driver the power to approve or refuse each costly step in advance.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that get you a fair diagnosis and a fair price — at this garage and everywhere else. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🔧 Opening softly

Phrase 1

You need them to look at the car today, but they're clearly busy and you'd rather sound like you're asking a favour than issuing an order. Which opener does that best?

A. "I need this looked at today."
B. "Can you fix my car today?"
C. "I don't suppose you could take a look at it today, could you?"
C — "I don't suppose you could take a look at it today, could you?"
"I don't suppose…" politely expects a no, so it costs them nothing to give one — and makes a yes feel generous. The "…could you?" tag on the end softens it even further. The least pushy way in English to ask a busy person for a favour.
Phrase 2

You want to say the car has been misbehaving for a few days — not fully broken, just not right. There's a two-word phrasal verb, very British and very casual, that means exactly "misbehaving / not working properly": "It's been ___ ___ for the last few days."

"It's been playing up for the last few days."
"Play up" is the everyday word for a machine (or a knee, or a child) that's being troublesome without having failed outright: "My laptop's playing up." "His back's been playing up again."

🔊 Describing the fault

Phrase 3

You're no mechanic, so describe the sound in plain, hedged words — vague enough to be honest, clear enough to help. Put the pieces in order:

there'ssort of noisea grindingwhen I brake
"There's a grinding sort of noise when I brake."
Two tricks worth stealing. "A grinding sort of noise" lets you name a sound without committing to the exact word — cousins: "a rattling sort of noise", "a whining sort of noise". And "when I brake" pins down the moment, which is half the diagnosis.
Phrase 4

A mechanic's first question is nearly always "when does it happen?" Your fault isn't constant — it only shows up first thing, before the engine has warmed up. Give the single sentence that pins that down.

"It only does it when the engine's cold."
"It only does it when…" is the most useful sentence frame in the whole conversation — it narrows an intermittent fault to a condition: "…when I go over fifty", "…when I turn left", "…first thing in the morning." Precision here saves you money later.

💷 Cost & time

Phrase 5

You want a ballpark price before they start, but a blunt "how much?" feels a bit sharp. There's a casual four-word tail that means "what's the likely cost?": "Roughly how much ___ ___ ___ ___?"

"Roughly how much am I looking at?"
"What am I looking at?" = "what's the likely figure?" — softer and more grown-up than "how much?". Pair it with "roughly" or "all in" and you signal you understand it's only an estimate: "Roughly how much am I looking at, all in?"
Phrase 6

You need the car back for work tomorrow. Ask how long it'll be out of action — in natural, non-technical English rather than "what is the estimated duration of the repair?"

"Any idea how long it'll be off the road?"
"Any idea how long…?" is the relaxed way to ask for a timeframe, and "off the road" is the idiom for a vehicle out of use. Relatives: "…how long it'll be out of action?", "…when I can pick it up?"

🧾 Questioning the quote

Phrase 7

The number is far higher than you hoped. You want to flag that WITHOUT sounding aggressive or accusing them of overcharging. Which reaction keeps them on your side?

A. "That's way too expensive."
B. "That's a rip-off."
C. "That's a fair bit steeper than I was expecting, to be honest."
C — "That's a fair bit steeper than I was expecting, to be honest."
A and B attack the person; C simply reports your own surprise. "A fair bit steeper" is soft understatement, and "to be honest" makes it a confidence rather than a complaint. You've registered the blow without picking a fight.
Phrase 8

Now invite them to reduce it — not by demanding a discount, but by opening a door and letting them find the savings. Build the sentence:

is therebringing that downany way ofat all?
"Is there any way of bringing that down at all?"
"Is there any way of + -ing" hands the problem back to them as a puzzle to solve, not a fight to win — and "at all?" makes it almost apologetic. Note the phrasal verb: to bring a price down = to reduce it.

🔑 One more check — and a straight goodbye

Phrase 9

Not everything on the list is urgent. You want to ask whether one item can be postponed, so you can spread the cost. Complete the question: "Do I actually need that ___ now, or could it ___?"

"Do I actually need that doing now, or could it wait?"
"Need that doing" (need + -ing) is the natural British way to say "need to have it done". The word "actually" gently pushes them to separate the must-do from the nice-to-do — and "could it wait?" gives you room to breathe on cost.
Phrase 10

They've been honest, fair and clear — no upselling, no scare stories. Which parting line rewards that, and leaves them glad they were decent to you?

A. "OK, cheers, bye."
B. "Thank you for your service in this matter."
C. "You've been really straight with me — I appreciate it."
C — "You've been really straight with me — I appreciate it."
A is fine but forgettable; B sounds like a form letter. "Straight with me" praises the exact virtue that matters at a garage — honesty — and "I appreciate it" makes it personal. A line that earns you an honest mechanic for life.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole visit out loud using all ten, in order — soft opening → describe the fault → cost and time → question the quote → one last check → goodbye. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your car and your money. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer to keep the pressure of a busy garage behind you.

Round 1 — you are the driver 🚗

The mechanic speaks — you answer. No looking back at the phrases; trust your ear. Remember: describe clearly, then handle the price calmly.

MechanicMorning. What brings you in today?
MechanicRight. Talk me through it — what's it actually doing?
MechanicSounds like brake pads, could be the discs. The light I'll have to plug in to read. Leave it with me a couple of hours.
MechanicAll in, you could be looking at the wrong side of five hundred pounds.
MechanicI can do pattern parts, and we'll leave the discs if they pass. Anything else while you're here?
MechanicFair enough — I'll ring you before I do anything pricey. That the lot?

Round 2 — now YOU are the mechanic 🔧

Swap chairs. It's your garage, your ramp, your prices — and your call on what really needs doing. Invent the fault, the figures, the parts. Give the mechanic a personality.

The Twist

🌩️ It's worse than we thought

One more round — no script, no safety net. The mechanic rings you at lunchtime: it wasn't just the pads. The discs are gone, there's an oil leak they've found, and the warning light is a failing sensor — the bill's crept up to nine hundred pounds. And here's the catch: you're supposed to drive two hundred miles to a family wedding on Saturday. Start the timer and talk your way through it: what's genuinely unsafe to drive on and what can wait, whether cheaper parts or a payment plan are possible, a courtesy car, doing the essential half now and the rest next month. One rule: the second you turn rude or start accusing them of inventing faults, you lose their goodwill — and your best price.

The Echo 🕰️

Before they ever left the ground, Wilbur and Orville Wright fixed and built bicycles for a living. Their days were spent exactly where you've just been standing: listening to a machine, chasing a rattle, working out which part had worn and what it would cost to put right.

They trusted no expert's word. When the flight data of the day didn't match what they saw, they built their own wind tunnel and measured it themselves. That stubborn habit — diagnose the machine, test everything, believe only what you can check — is what carried them into the air at Kitty Hawk in December 1903.

So the next time your car makes a noise you can't name, take heart: the two men who gave the world flight started in a workshop just like this one, trying to work out what on earth was wrong.

Two bigger questions

  1. Fewer and fewer of us can fix our own machines — cars, phones, boilers — so we hand them to an expert who could, in theory, tell us anything. How do you decide whether to trust a diagnosis and a price you can't check yourself?
  2. The Wrights succeeded partly because they refused to take experts at their word and tested everything themselves. Where's the line between healthy scepticism and simply refusing to trust people who genuinely know more than you do?
