Land of Smiles: Is Every Smile the Same?

Thailand is called the “Land of Smiles”. But a Thai smile can say thank you, sorry, “no problem” — or “I am embarrassed”.

The stereotype: “Thai people smile all the time because they are always happy.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The nickname

Thailand is often called the “Land of Smiles”, a phrase that tourism promoters have used for many years. Visitors often notice friendly smiles in shops, markets and hotels. It is true that smiles are very common and valued, and that Thai people greet strangers with warmth. But the nickname makes it sound as if every smile means “I am happy”, which is not what Thai people mean by it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who has used the phrase “Land of Smiles”?

2. What is the nickname's problem?

1. Tourism promoters.

2. It makes every smile seem to mean “I am happy”.

Discuss:
  • Which nickname does your own country have, and is it true?

Part 2 — Many smiles

Thais often say that there are many different kinds of smile. A smile can mean “thank you”, “sorry”, “excuse me”, “I do not agree but I won't argue”, or “I am embarrassed”. Some ideas help to explain it: “kreng jai” means not wanting to impose on others; “jai yen”, a “cool heart”, means keeping calm; and “mai pen rai” means “never mind, it's fine”. A smile helps people avoid conflict and keep a relationship comfortable.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does “jai yen” mean?

2. What does “kreng jai” mean?

1. A “cool heart” — keeping calm.

2. Not wanting to impose on others.

Discuss:
  • Why might smiling be better than arguing in some situations?

Part 3 — Reading a smile

To understand a smile, look at the whole situation. A smile with a gentle apology may mean a real problem is being politely ignored, and a smile while someone is shouting may show embarrassment. Losing your temper in public is usually seen as a failure of composure, and it can make the person you are shouting at feel ashamed. Visitors who stay calm, smile and ask politely tend to get far better results than those who show anger. Similar smiles and ideas about “face” exist in Indonesia, Vietnam and elsewhere in the region.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How is losing your temper in public seen?

2. What works better than showing anger?

1. As a failure of composure.

2. Staying calm, smiling and asking politely.

Discuss:
  • How do you react when you are angry in public?

Grammar — Gerunds & Infinitives

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
Gerunds and infinitives:
• verb + -ing: “Thai people avoid showing anger in public.” (enjoy, avoid, keep, mind, suggest)
• verb + to + infinitive: “They want to keep the peace.” (want, decide, hope, manage, refuse)
• meaning changes: “stop to smile” (stop in order to smile) vs. “stop smiling” (end the action)
• after prepositions: “They solve problems by smiling.”

1. Choose the right form

Complete each sentence about this topic.

1. Thai people try to avoid (show) anger in public.

2. Many people decide (smile) instead of arguing.

3. They solve many problems by (stay) calm.

1. showing

2. to smile

3. staying

2. Now try these

1. Visitors should remember (ask) politely.

2. He kept (smile) even though he was upset.

3. She stopped (talk) to the stranger when the bus came.

1. to ask

2. smiling

3. to talk (or talking)

Going deeper

Smiles are among the most social parts of body language: people use them to manage relationships more than to show feelings. In many cultures a smile is mostly an expression of joy, so a visitor may feel “lied to” by a smile that hides a problem. The better question is not “is it real?” but “what is this smile doing?” — thanking, avoiding conflict or asking for patience.

Worth noticing: Gerunds and infinitives: enjoy / avoid / keep / mind + -ing; want / decide / hope / manage + to + verb; stop / remember / try change meaning; prepositions + -ing (“by smiling”).
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: When you meet a smile in a difficult moment, don't assume it means “yes”. Stay calm, smile back and ask a polite question such as “Is there anything difficult about this?”. Avoid raising your voice: calm behaviour is respected and often leads to a solution.
