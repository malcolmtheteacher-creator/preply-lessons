BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Apollo 13

An oxygen tank explodes 200,000 miles from home, and a Moon landing becomes a fight just to survive. Almost every fix on screen really happened.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Apollo 13 — three astronauts stranded far from home after a routine mission turns into a fight for survival — discover exactly how much of the crisis and its solutions were real, and master the language English uses to explain why people act and what happens as a result: purpose and result clauses.
Before we begin — talk it through: Have you ever had to fix something serious using only what was already on hand — no proper tools, no expert to call? What did you do? And when a plan suddenly falls apart, do you think calm, careful thinking matters more than speed — or the other way round?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. Cut off from every proper workshop, the two hikers repaired the broken axle with pure ingenuity, turning a bootlace and a bent tent pole into a tool that actually worked.
ingenuity (n) — cleverness and skill in inventing or solving something, especially with very limited resources.
2. With no proper spare part anywhere on the farm, she had to improvise, bolting together three unrelated pieces of scrap metal until the pump finally ran again.
to improvise (v) — to make or do something quickly using whatever is available, without any advance preparation.
3. Balanced on a single icy ledge with the rope nearly worn through, her position was precarious — one wrong move and she would fall.
precarious (adj) — not securely held or fixed in place; likely to fail or collapse at any moment; dangerously uncertain.
4. Before the operation, the surgeon went through a meticulous checklist twice, checking every instrument and every reading down to the smallest detail.
meticulous (adj) — extremely careful and precise, paying close attention to every small detail.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. catastrophic is closest in meaning to: (a) minor   (b) disastrous   (c) convenient
(b) disastrous — catastrophic (adj) describes an event that causes sudden, severe harm or damage on a huge scale.
6. negligible is closest in meaning to: (a) enormous   (b) too small to matter   (c) illegal
(b) too small to matter — negligible (adj) so small in size or importance that it can safely be ignored.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. After the storm cut the roads, the family had to ration what little food was left, allowing themselves only a small portion each day.
To ration something is: (a) to throw it away; (b) to carefully limit and share it out so it lasts; (c) to sell it quickly.
(b) — to ration (v) — to allow only a fixed, limited amount of something, especially so that a supply lasts as long as possible.
8. When the news broke, the company's share price didn't just fall — it plummeted, losing half its value within an hour.
To plummet is: (a) to rise steadily; (b) to fall suddenly and very fast; (c) to stay exactly the same.
(b) — to plummet (v) — to fall suddenly, steeply and very quickly.

D. Infer from context — two bigger ideas

9. Engineers calculated the rocket's trajectory precisely — the exact curved path it would follow from the launch pad to a target thousands of miles away.
a trajectory (n) — the curved path followed by an object moving through space or air; more broadly, the general direction something is heading in.
10. Good pilots always have a contingency: a backup plan, ready to use the moment something unexpected goes wrong.
a contingency (n) — a possible future event or situation that is planned for in case it happens; a backup plan for the unexpected.

The film, in three parts

Ron Howard's Apollo 13 (1995). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — "Houston, we have a problem"

April 1970. Commander Jim Lovell is leading NASA's third mission to land on the Moon, alongside Jack Swigert — a late replacement after the original pilot was pulled off the crew for exposure to measles — and Fred Haise. Two days in, the flight is going so smoothly that the television networks have stopped bothering to broadcast it live; nobody, it seems, is interested in watching astronauts do their jobs well.

Then an oxygen tank in the service module explodes. Warning lights flood the cabin, power begins draining away, and Swigert radios back the words that will define the mission: "Houston, we have a problem." Within minutes, the Moon landing — the entire purpose of the flight — is abandoned. The only goal left is getting three men home alive.

On the ground, engineers and flight controllers scramble to understand a disaster nobody trained for, working from incomplete data as the spacecraft drifts further from Earth.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is on board, and why is Swigert flying instead of the original pilot?

2. What happens two days into the flight, and what is abandoned as a result?

1. Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert and Fred Haise; Swigert replaced the original pilot late, after he was exposed to measles.

2. An oxygen tank explodes, power starts draining away, and the Moon landing itself is abandoned so the crew can focus entirely on survival.

Discuss:
  • Before the accident, this mission was thought too routine to even broadcast. Does danger feel more frightening when it's unexpected, rather than something we're braced for?

Part 2 — the lifeboat in the freezing dark

With the command module dying, the crew crowd into the lunar module — built to keep two men alive for two days, now home to three men for four. To save what little power remains, Mission Control orders almost everything switched off: heating included. The temperature drops close to freezing; condensation beads on every surface, and the astronauts' breath hangs visibly in the air.

A second, quieter danger builds alongside the cold: carbon dioxide. The lunar module's air scrubbers were designed for two men, not three, and the gas begins climbing towards dangerous levels. The command module has spare canisters — but they're the wrong shape for the lunar module's sockets. Overnight, engineers on the ground build a working adapter out of a plastic bag, a sock, cardboard torn from a manual cover, and tape, then talk the crew through assembling an identical one in space.

It works. The readings fall back into the safe range, and the makeshift device — nicknamed for its boxy shape — becomes one of the mission's quiet triumphs.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does the lunar module suddenly become so overcrowded and cold?

2. What is the danger with carbon dioxide, and how is it solved?

1. It was built for two astronauts for two days but must now support three for four days, so nearly everything, including heating, is switched off to save power.

2. The lunar module's air scrubbers can't cope with three people, so carbon dioxide rises dangerously; engineers on the ground design a makeshift adapter from a bag, cardboard and tape so the command module's canisters will fit.

Discuss:
  • The engineers on the ground built the fix from whatever was lying around, against a strict deadline, without ever being fully sure it would work. Could you think clearly under that kind of pressure?

Part 3 — powering up a frozen ship

The crew swing around the far side of the Moon and fire the lunar module's engine to bend their path back towards Earth. But one huge task remains: the command module, dead and freezing for days, must be brought back to life for re-entry — using a startup procedure nobody has ever tried in this order. On the ground, astronaut Ken Mattingly, pulled from the mission himself over the same measles scare, spends hours in a simulator working out a sequence that won't drain the last of the batteries, then reads it up to the crew line by line.

Damp instrument panels raise fears of a short circuit. Then comes re-entry: the spacecraft must hit the atmosphere at exactly the right angle, and radio contact is expected to black out for a few tense minutes. This time, the silence stretches longer than predicted, and Mission Control waits without a word from space.

Finally, static breaks — the crew's voices return, parachutes bloom over the Pacific, and Apollo 13 splashes down safely. A mission that should have ended in tragedy ends, instead, in celebration.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who helps devise the procedure to restart the frozen command module, and how?

2. What is the tense final moment before splashdown?

1. Ken Mattingly, the astronaut bumped from the mission over measles exposure, rehearses the startup in a ground simulator and radios the steps up to the crew.

2. A re-entry communications blackout that lasts longer than expected, leaving Mission Control in silence until the crew's voices finally return.

Discuss:
  • Mattingly never reached the Moon, yet his work on the ground may have saved his crewmates' lives. Is that a form of heroism as real as the one in space?

Grammar — purpose & result clauses

Every decision on this mission was made for a reason, and every decision produced a consequence. English has a set of structures built exactly for this: clauses of purpose (why someone acts) and clauses of result (what happens because of it).

Purpose — to say WHY:
• to / in order to / so as to + infinitive: "They switched off the heating to save power." "Engineers tested the checklist twice in order to be certain it worked." So as to is a little more formal, and its negative form is common: "They moved slowly so as not to waste any oxygen."
• so that + clause (often with would/could/will/can): "They rationed the water carefully so that it would last the whole journey home."
Result — to say WHAT HAPPENED because of it:
• so + adjective/adverb + that: "The cabin grew so cold that ice formed on the walls."
• such + (a/an) + adjective + noun + that: "It was such a serious fault that engineers worked through the night without sleep."

1. Purpose — to / in order to / so as to

Complete each sentence with the infinitive form the meaning needs. More than one connector can be correct — the reveal shows the options.

1. The crew shut down almost every system on board (save) their last reserves of power.

2. Ground engineers spent the whole night testing the new checklist (make sure) it would really work before reading it up to the crew.

3. The astronauts moved carefully around the cabin (not / waste) any of their dwindling oxygen supply.

1. to save / in order to save / so as to save

2. to make sure / in order to make sure / so as to make sure

3. so as not to waste (the negative form of "so as to")

2. Purpose — so that + modal

Complete each sentence with so that already in place — you just need the correct modal + verb.

1. Mission Control powered down the guidance computer so that the batteries (last) until splashdown.

2. Engineers built a makeshift adapter from tape and cardboard so that the carbon dioxide level (not / rise) any further.

3. The crew moved into the lunar module so that the freezing command module (stay) untouched and ready for the final hours.

1. would last

2. would not rise

3. would stay

3. Result — so … that / such … that

Complete each sentence with so or such (a).

1. The cold in the cabin became intense that the astronauts could see frost forming on the walls.

2. It was serious problem that engineers worked without sleep for two days straight.

3. Power grew scarce that every non-essential system had to be switched off at once.

1. so (so + adjective + that)

2. such a (such + a + adjective + noun + that)

3. so (so + adjective + that)

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

Apollo 13 is unusual among space films: the crisis, the improvised fixes and the safe return all really happened. NASA itself later called the mission a "successful failure" — the landing failed completely, but the rescue succeeded completely too.

🚀 April 1970 — the real timeline

Apollo 13 launched on 11 April 1970, NASA's third planned Moon landing. Two days later, on 13 April, an oxygen tank in the service module ruptured, crippling power and life support for the command module Odyssey. The crew moved into the lunar module Aquarius and used it as a lifeboat, exactly as shown on screen — improvising a carbon-dioxide scrubber from a plastic bag, a sock, cardboard and tape when the command module's canisters turned out to be the wrong shape for the lunar module's sockets. Engineers also used the lunar module's engine to place the ship on a free-return trajectory, letting lunar gravity swing it back towards Earth without a risky separate burn. The crew — Jim Lovell, Jack Swigert (a late replacement for Ken Mattingly, who was pulled from the flight after exposure to measles) and Fred Haise — splashed down safely in the Pacific Ocean on 17 April 1970.

The line everyone remembers — and the line that was actually said

The film's most famous line, "Houston, we have a problem," is a rewrite. What astronaut Jack Swigert actually radioed down was: "Okay, Houston, we've had a problem here" — past tense, describing something that had already happened — and commander Jim Lovell repeated a near-identical version moments later. Screenwriters tightened it into the punchier present tense for the film, and that version is now the one almost everyone quotes, even though it was never actually spoken during the mission.

"Failure is not an option" — and what Gene Kranz really said

The film's other iconic line, spoken by the character based on flight director Gene Kranz, was never said during the real mission either. Screenwriters invented it to capture the spirit of Mission Control's determination. Kranz himself liked the phrase so much that he later used it as the title of his own memoir, published in 2000. What he actually told his team, by his own account, was closer to a personal promise: that he had never lost an American in space, and he wasn't about to start now — this crew was coming home, and everyone had to believe it.

Take these into the discussion — what's real, what's invented, and does it matter?
  • Before this lesson, did you assume "Houston, we have a problem" was the exact, historical wording?
  • Does it change how you see the film, knowing its two most quoted lines were both written for the screenplay rather than actually spoken?
  • NASA called this mission a "successful failure." Do you think that phrase captures it well — can failure and success really coexist in the same event?
