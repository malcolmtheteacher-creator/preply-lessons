Encounter

KEY TERM

Brahman (ब्रह्मन्)

The ultimate reality in Hinduism. Not a "god" in the Western sense, but the infinite, eternal, unchanging source of all existence. Brahman is beyond form, beyond description, beyond human comprehension. Everything that exists IS Brahman — the universe, the gods, all living things.

Think of it like: If God were not a person but a cosmic force or ultimate reality that everything belongs to and comes from.

The most famous mantra in Hinduism is "Tat Tvam Asi" (tat tvam asi), which means "Thou Art That" — indicating that the deepest self (Atman) of each person is actually Brahman itself.

• • •

The Trimurti: Three Faces of God

To help humans understand the abstract concept of Brahman, Hindu theology teaches the Trimurti — three great gods who represent different aspects of creation and cosmic function. They are not separate gods competing for power; they are expressions of the one divine reality.

Brahma
The Creator
Creates the universe at the beginning of each cosmic cycle (Kalpa). Represents the creative force of Brahman.
Vishnu
The Preserver
Sustains and maintains the universe. Takes human form (incarnates) as avatars like Rama and Krishna to restore dharma (righteousness) when needed.
Shiva
The Destroyer/Transformer
Destroys the old to make way for the new. Transformation and renewal are not negative but part of the eternal cycle. Associated with meditation and yoga.

The Cosmic Cycle (Yugas)

Hindu cosmology teaches that time is cyclical, not linear. Brahma creates, Vishnu preserves, Shiva destroys, and the cycle begins again — eternally.

Avatars: God in Human Form

🔒 Hidden for retrieval — try from memory first!
KEY TERM

Avatar (अवतार)

Sanskrit: "descent." A divine incarnation — when God (usually Vishnu) takes human form on Earth to restore dharma (righteousness) and defeat evil.

Key avatars of Vishnu:
• Rama — the hero of the epic Ramayana, model of virtue and duty
• Krishna — the cowherd prince of the Bhagavad Gita, divine teacher and charioteer

Krishna Speaks: The Bhagavad Gita 4:7–8

In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna explains the purpose of avatars:

"Whenever there is a decline in righteousness and a rise in unrighteousness, then I manifest myself. For the protection of the righteous and the destruction of the wicked, and for the establishment of righteousness, I come into being, age after age."

Bhagavad Gita 4:7–8 (paraphrased)

Murtis: Images and Worship

KEY TERM

Murti (मूर्ति)

A physical representation or image of a deity used in worship. Often mistaken by outsiders for "idols," but Hindus do not worship the statue itself. Instead, the murti is a focal point for meditation and devotion — a window through which to experience the divine.

Understanding Murti Worship

When a Hindu prays to a murti, they are not worshipping wood or stone. They are using the image as a means to focus their mind on the divine qualities it represents. It is like a photograph helping you think of someone you love — the photo is not the person, but it helps you connect with them. For some Hindus, the murti becomes a channel for experiencing Brahman.

Activity 2: Categorise the Labels

Read these four statements from Hindu believers. Is each statement best described as Monotheist, Polytheist, or Something else? Explain why.

Statement A: "There are many gods, but they are all expressions of the one Brahman."

Statement B: "I pray to Krishna because he is a complete incarnation of God."

Statement C: "Brahman cannot be described, but the Trimurti helps us understand the cosmic functions."

Statement D: "We worship many gods, each powerful in their own way."

A. Something else — both many gods and one ultimate reality.

B. Monotheist — Krishna as a complete manifestation of the one God/Brahman.

C. Something else — the non-dualistic nature of Brahman.

D. Polytheist — treats gods as separate powers, though many Hindus would argue this misses the deeper unity.

Key point: Hinduism resists simple "monotheist" or "polytheist" labels.
