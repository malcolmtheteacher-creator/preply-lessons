At the Airport

The board says DELAYED, the queue behind you is fraying, and you have to be somewhere tonight. Time to stay calm and talk your way onto another flight.

The Scene 🎬

It's a Friday evening and the departures hall is doing its Friday-evening thing: too many people, not enough seats, and a board full of times that keep quietly changing. You're flying to Lisbon — there's something waiting at the other end you really can't miss — and an hour ago your phone buzzed with the word every traveller dreads: delay.

You wheel your case up to the check-in desk and do a quick sum in your head: one bag to drop, a seat you can actually sit in, and the truth about how late this flight is really going to be. Then you take a breath. Tonight won't be about what you ask for. It'll be about how calmly you ask when the news is bad.

Behind the desk: an agent who has delivered a hundred delays today, absorbed a hundred sighs, and can spot an about-to-be-difficult passenger from three metres away. Be the easy one, and doors have a funny way of opening.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. What's the longest you've ever been stranded at an airport — and how did you keep yourself sane, or didn't you?
  2. Some people go quietly furious at a delay; others just shrug and buy a coffee. Which are you, honestly — and has losing your temper ever actually got you anywhere?

Who's who

You
The Traveller ✈️

Tired, a little anxious, somewhere to be by morning. You'd far rather charm your way onto a new flight than shout your way off the old one.

The other voice
The Check-in Agent 🛄

Unflappable, has seen every meltdown twice. Quietly bends the rules for the calm and the kind — and freezes solid for anyone who raises their voice.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Listen for the softeners, the understatement, and the moment a two-hour delay quietly turns into a plan.

AgentGood evening — where are we flying to tonight?
YouEvening. Lisbon, on the eight-forty — though I've had a text about a delay and I'm honestly not sure how bad it is.
AgentLet me take a look for you. Passport and booking reference whenever you're ready… lovely. Just the one bag to check?
YouJust the one, if that's alright. And I don't suppose there's any chance of an aisle seat? I'm all elbows on a plane.
AgentAisle it is — row 22. Pop the bag on the scale for me.
YouHere goes. I'll be honest — I think I might be a kilo or two over. Is that going to be a problem?
Agent…twenty-three on the nose. You're fine — a whisker under, in fact. Checking it straight through to Lisbon?
YouPlease — I'd rather not have to grab it in Madrid. Now, about that delay: how long are we really looking at, do you think?
AgentI have to be straight with you. The eight-forty's been pushed back to half ten, and with weather coming through Madrid, I'd quietly plan for eleven.
YouAh. That's frustrating, but these things happen. Is there any way of getting me on something earlier — even a different route?
AgentThere's a Porto connection at nine… but it's tight, and between you and me, I can't promise your bag makes it across in time.
YouHmm. Whatever's easiest for you, really — I just need to be in Lisbon by morning. And forgive me for asking, would I be entitled to a meal voucher for the wait?
AgentYou would, for a delay this long — here you are. Tell you what: I'll keep you on the direct flight, it's the safer bet for your luggage. Gate 14, but keep half an eye on the boards.
YouThat's really good of you. You've been very patient with all my questions — thanks ever so much for sorting it out.
AgentNot at all. Go and find yourself an overpriced airport coffee — you've earned the wait. Safe flight.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The traveller is braced for an overweight bag. What actually happens on the scale — and what does the agent's "a whisker under" tell you about how close it really was?

2. The traveller is offered an earlier flight through Porto but doesn't grab it. Why the hesitation — and what plan does the agent finally settle on instead, and why?

3. "That's frustrating, but these things happen." The news is genuinely bad, so why answer it so lightly — and how does staying this calm actually work in the traveller's favour?

1. The bag weighs twenty-three kilos exactly — right on the limit — so it's fine, but only just. "A whisker under" is a gentle, almost fond way of saying it scraped through by nothing; the agent is reassuring the traveller rather than pouncing on a near miss.
2. The Porto connection is tight, and the agent warns the bag might not make the transfer in time. Because the traveller stays flexible ("whatever's easiest… I just need to be in Lisbon by morning"), the agent chooses the direct flight — later, but safer for the luggage. Getting there with your suitcase beats getting there sooner without it.
3. The traveller is deliberately taking the blow on the chin — registering the annoyance without making a scene. Because they don't turn it into the agent's fault, the agent stays on their side and volunteers extras (the voucher, the safer routing) that an angry passenger would never have been offered.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1937, Amelia Earhart was two-thirds of the way around the world — the first person to attempt flying it along the equator — when she vanished over the Pacific, somewhere between two tiny dots on the map. No delay board. No rebooking. No calm agent to slot her onto a later flight.

She navigated by dead reckoning and the stars, with a radio that kept cutting out and a fuel gauge she could only half trust. You wait, safe and dry, for someone to fix the weather; she flew straight into it.

So the next time the board says DELAYED, try to hear it differently: it's the sound of an entire system working very hard to make sure you land. She would have loved the problem you're grumbling about.

Two bigger questions

  1. Flying is now the safest way to travel and one of the most complained-about. Why do we take the miracle for granted and fixate on the two-hour wait? What would it take to feel genuine wonder at a departure gate again?
  2. Earhart knew each flight might be her last, and went anyway. Where's the line between brave and reckless — and does modern life still leave room for that kind of risk, or have we quietly engineered it out?
