At the Airport

The board says DELAYED, the queue behind you is fraying, and you have to be somewhere tonight. Time to stay calm and talk your way onto another flight.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

It's a Friday evening and the departures hall is doing its Friday-evening thing: too many people, not enough seats, and a board full of times that keep quietly changing. You're flying to Lisbon — there's something waiting at the other end you really can't miss — and an hour ago your phone buzzed with the word every traveller dreads: delay.

You wheel your case up to the check-in desk and do a quick sum in your head: one bag to drop, a seat you can actually sit in, and the truth about how late this flight is really going to be. Then you take a breath. Tonight won't be about what you ask for. It'll be about how calmly you ask when the news is bad.

Behind the desk: an agent who has delivered a hundred delays today, absorbed a hundred sighs, and can spot an about-to-be-difficult passenger from three metres away. Be the easy one, and doors have a funny way of opening.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. What's the longest you've ever been stranded at an airport — and how did you keep yourself sane, or didn't you?
  2. Some people go quietly furious at a delay; others just shrug and buy a coffee. Which are you, honestly — and has losing your temper ever actually got you anywhere?

Who's who

You
The Traveller ✈️

Tired, a little anxious, somewhere to be by morning. You'd far rather charm your way onto a new flight than shout your way off the old one.

The other voice
The Check-in Agent 🛄

Unflappable, has seen every meltdown twice. Quietly bends the rules for the calm and the kind — and freezes solid for anyone who raises their voice.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't just follow what the traveller asks for — watch how they take the bad news. The delay is the same either way; what changes everything is the tone.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the softeners, the understatement, and the moment a two-hour delay quietly turns into a plan.

AgentGood evening — where are we flying to tonight?
YouEvening. Lisbon, on the eight-forty — though I've had a text about a delay and I'm honestly not sure how bad it is.
AgentLet me take a look for you. Passport and booking reference whenever you're ready… lovely. Just the one bag to check?
YouJust the one, if that's alright. And I don't suppose there's any chance of an aisle seat? I'm all elbows on a plane.
AgentAisle it is — row 22. Pop the bag on the scale for me.
YouHere goes. I'll be honest — I think I might be a kilo or two over. Is that going to be a problem?
Agent…twenty-three on the nose. You're fine — a whisker under, in fact. Checking it straight through to Lisbon?
YouPlease — I'd rather not have to grab it in Madrid. Now, about that delay: how long are we really looking at, do you think?
AgentI have to be straight with you. The eight-forty's been pushed back to half ten, and with weather coming through Madrid, I'd quietly plan for eleven.
YouAh. That's frustrating, but these things happen. Is there any way of getting me on something earlier — even a different route?
AgentThere's a Porto connection at nine… but it's tight, and between you and me, I can't promise your bag makes it across in time.
YouHmm. Whatever's easiest for you, really — I just need to be in Lisbon by morning. And forgive me for asking, would I be entitled to a meal voucher for the wait?
AgentYou would, for a delay this long — here you are. Tell you what: I'll keep you on the direct flight, it's the safer bet for your luggage. Gate 14, but keep half an eye on the boards.
YouThat's really good of you. You've been very patient with all my questions — thanks ever so much for sorting it out.
AgentNot at all. Go and find yourself an overpriced airport coffee — you've earned the wait. Safe flight.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The traveller is braced for an overweight bag. What actually happens on the scale — and what does the agent's "a whisker under" tell you about how close it really was?

2. The traveller is offered an earlier flight through Porto but doesn't grab it. Why the hesitation — and what plan does the agent finally settle on instead, and why?

3. "That's frustrating, but these things happen." The news is genuinely bad, so why answer it so lightly — and how does staying this calm actually work in the traveller's favour?

1. The bag weighs twenty-three kilos exactly — right on the limit — so it's fine, but only just. "A whisker under" is a gentle, almost fond way of saying it scraped through by nothing; the agent is reassuring the traveller rather than pouncing on a near miss.
2. The Porto connection is tight, and the agent warns the bag might not make the transfer in time. Because the traveller stays flexible ("whatever's easiest… I just need to be in Lisbon by morning"), the agent chooses the direct flight — later, but safer for the luggage. Getting there with your suitcase beats getting there sooner without it.
3. The traveller is deliberately taking the blow on the chin — registering the annoyance without making a scene. Because they don't turn it into the agent's fault, the agent stays on their side and volunteers extras (the voucher, the safer routing) that an angry passenger would never have been offered.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that turn a bad-news board into a workable plan — at this airport and everywhere else you travel. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🛫 Checking in — opening softly

Phrase 1

The flight looks full, but you'd love an aisle seat. You want to ask without sounding like you expect one. Which opener fishes for the favour and lets them say no painlessly?

A. "Give me an aisle seat."
B. "I want an aisle seat, please."
C. "I don't suppose there's any chance of an aisle seat…?"
C — "I don't suppose there's any chance of an aisle seat…?"
"I don't suppose there's any chance of…" politely expects a no, which makes it effortless to refuse and rather nice to grant. It's the least demanding way in English to ask for a small favour.
Phrase 2

You want your suitcase sent all the way to your final airport, not collected and re-dropped at the connection. There's a polite request-opener — a way of saying "can you" that sounds far more considerate. Complete it: "___ ___ ___ ___ to check this through to Lisbon?"

"Would it be possible to check this through to Lisbon?"
"Would it be possible to…" treats your request as something that might not be doable, which is exactly why people bend to grant it. Note the useful travel verb too: to check something through = send your bag straight to the final destination.

🧳 Bags & security — heading off problems

Phrase 3

You suspect your case is over the weight limit and you'd rather flag it lightly than have it flagged for you. Put the pieces in order:

I might beI'm afraidis that going to be a problem?a kilo or two over —
"I'm afraid I might be a kilo or two over — is that going to be a problem?"
Understatement does the work: "a kilo or two" sounds harmless even if it's more, and owning up first ("I'm afraid…") turns a possible telling-off into a shared little problem you're solving together.
Phrase 4

At security you're not sure whether your laptop comes out of the bag, and you'd rather check than get told off in front of the queue. A short hedge lets you ask without looking clueless. Complete it: "___ ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ ___, do I need to take the laptop out?"

"Just to be on the safe side, do I need to take the laptop out?"
"Just to be on the safe side…" frames the question as sensible caution rather than ignorance — you're double-checking, not confessing you don't know. It works before almost any careful question.

⏳ When the flight is delayed — staying calm

Phrase 5

The board flips to DELAYED. You're annoyed — but you know the calm passenger gets better help than the furious one. Which reply keeps the agent firmly on your side?

A. "This is an absolute disgrace — I demand to know what's going on."
B. "Well, that's just typical. Somebody had better sort this out."
C. "That's frustrating, but these things happen — how long are we looking at?"
C — "That's frustrating, but these things happen — how long are we looking at?"
It names the annoyance without blaming the person in front of you, then pivots straight to a useful question. "How long are we looking at?" is a lovely, easy way to ask for an estimate. A and B make an enemy of the one person who can help you.
Phrase 6

The official delay is "half ten", but you sense that's optimistic. You want the agent's honest, off-the-record read on whether it might slip even further. What could you say? Say your version out loud, then check a model.

"Is there any indication it might be delayed further, or is that the worst of it?"
"Is there any indication…" invites an honest guess without demanding a promise, and "…or is that the worst of it?" gently asks for the true ceiling. It signals you can handle the real answer — which is often what unlocks it.

✈️ Getting rebooked — building plan B

Phrase 7

The wait's too long; you want to be moved to an earlier or alternative flight — without sounding as though you're ordering it. Build the request:

is there any wayon something earlier?of getting me
"Is there any way of getting me on something earlier?"
"Is there any way of + -ing" hands the problem to them to solve and quietly assumes their goodwill. Note the grammar: any way of getting, never "any way to getting". Swap in anything: "…of getting me a refund / an earlier slot / a window seat?"
Phrase 8

A long delay may mean you're owed something — a voucher, even a hotel — but you want to ask about your rights without sounding like you're spoiling for a fight. Complete it: "___ ___ ___ ___ to a meal voucher for the wait?"

"Would I be entitled to a meal voucher for the wait?"
"Would I be entitled to…" asks about your rights as a polite enquiry, not an accusation — you're checking, not claiming. It lets the agent grant it as a kindness rather than surrender it under pressure.

🌟 Cooperating — and a proper goodbye

Phrase 9

The agent offers a fiddly rebooking that might just work. You want to come across as easy to help, not fussy. Which line makes you the passenger they'll go the extra mile for?

A. "I'm not getting on some tiny connecting flight — just put me on a direct one."
B. "Whatever's easiest for you, really — I just need to get there tonight."
B — "Whatever's easiest for you, really — I just need to get there tonight."
By flexing on the how and holding firm only on the what (arriving tonight), you make yourself simple to solve for. "Whatever's easiest for you" is disarming — people work harder for the person who isn't fighting them.
Phrase 10

The agent has heard you out, bent a rule or two, and sorted both your bag and your voucher. Which exit line leaves them glad they bothered?

A. "OK. Bye."
B. "Thank you for your assistance with my booking."
C. "You've been very patient with all my questions — thanks ever so much for sorting it out."
C — "You've been very patient with all my questions — thanks ever so much for sorting it out."
A is bare; B sounds like a form letter. C thanks the person for a specific effort ("patient with all my questions"), and "ever so much" warms it up without a trace of stiffness.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole trip out loud using all ten, in order — soft check-in → bags & security → the bad news → plan B → cooperate → goodbye. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your flight. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer to keep the pressure of a real departures board behind you.

Round 1 — you are the traveller ✈️

The agent speaks — you answer. No looking back at the phrases; trust your ear. Remember: soften first, and take any bad news on the chin.

AgentGood evening — where are we flying off to tonight?
AgentLet me take a look. Just the one bag to check in?
AgentPop it on the scale for me… hm, that's cutting it fine.
AgentI'm afraid I have to be honest with you — your flight's been pushed back at least a couple of hours.
AgentCould be longer, with the weather. Do you want to wait it out, or shall I see what else there is?
AgentRight — leave it with me. Gate 14, and keep an eye on the boards.

Round 2 — now YOU are the agent 🛄

Swap chairs. It's your desk, your airline, your rules — and your rules can bend for the right passenger. Invent the flights, the times, the small print. Give the agent a personality.

TravellerOh no. That's frustrating — I've got a connection in Rome. Is there any way of getting me there sooner?

The Twist

🌩️ The last flight of the night

One more round — no script, no safety net. The final flight home has just been cancelled: the weather's closed the whole airport. You absolutely must be home by morning — you decide why (a wedding you're in? a job interview? your own front door and your own bed after two weeks away?). There are no more flights tonight, the desk is about to close, and a long queue of tired, angry passengers is forming behind you. Start the timer and work every angle: standby on the dawn flight, a seat on a rival airline, an overnight hotel voucher, the last train, a full refund. Which do you fight hardest for — and what will you settle for? One rule: the second you raise your voice, you drop to the back of the queue — and lose the room.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1937, Amelia Earhart was two-thirds of the way around the world — the first person to attempt flying it along the equator — when she vanished over the Pacific, somewhere between two tiny dots on the map. No delay board. No rebooking. No calm agent to slot her onto a later flight.

She navigated by dead reckoning and the stars, with a radio that kept cutting out and a fuel gauge she could only half trust. You wait, safe and dry, for someone to fix the weather; she flew straight into it.

So the next time the board says DELAYED, try to hear it differently: it's the sound of an entire system working very hard to make sure you land. She would have loved the problem you're grumbling about.
