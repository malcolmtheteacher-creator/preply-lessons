The Furnished Room

O. Henry's story of a cheap room in New York, a young man looking for a lost girl, and a landlady with a secret.

Today: you'll read about a young man who rents a room in New York and looks for the girl he loves — and you'll practise short answers for when you are not 100% sure: I think so, I don't think so, I'm not sure, maybe.
Warm up — before we read. Talk about these questions out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • Did you ever stay in a hotel room or a rented room? What was it like?
  • Is there a smell that makes you think of a person — your mother, a friend, a grandparent?
  • Is it OK to say "I don't know" when you really know? When?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words open the story. Do the four short activities. Say your answer out loud first, then click Reveal.

A · What does the word mean here?

1. The room had a bed, a table, two chairs and a mirror. You didn't need to bring anything. It was a furnished room.
furnished (adj) — with furniture in it. Careful: furniture is uncountable — some furniture, a piece of furniture.
2. The woman owned the house. People paid her money every week to live in her rooms. She was the landlady.
a landlady (n) — a woman who rents rooms or flats to people. A man is a landlord.
3. The carpet was old, the chairs were broken, and the walls were dirty. The room was shabby.
shabby (adj) — old and in bad condition. shabby clothes, a shabby hotel.

B · Which word means…?

Three words: rent · smell · drawer. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means to pay money to live in a room or a house that is not yours?
to rent (v) — "I rent a flat in London." Also a noun: "The rent is £800 a month."
2. Which word means what you notice with your nose?
a smell (n), to smell (v) — "The room smelled of flowers." A nice smell is also a scent or perfume.
3. Which word means a box in a table or cupboard that you pull out to put things in?
a drawer (n) — "My keys are in the top drawer." Say it like /drɔː/.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. The young man had a big hope: to find Eloise. Then the hope disappeared. Disappeared means…
  (a) got bigger   (b) went away   (c) came back
(b) — to disappear = to go away so people can't see it. Opposite: to appear.
2. The landlady didn't tell him the truth. She had a secret. A secret is…
  (a) something you don't tell other people   (b) a kind of drink   (c) a room
(a) — a secret (n). keep a secret = don't tell it. "Can you keep a secret?"

D · Word families & word partners

1. Complete with look for or find: "I ___ my keys for an hour, and then I ___ them in my bag."
I looked for my keys (= tried to find) · I found them (= success!). find, found, found.
2. Complete: "The room smelled ___ flowers." · "It smells ___!" (two possible answers: nice / of)
smelled of flowers (smell of + noun) · It smells nice (smell + adjective). Not smells nicely!

Now use them — out loud

Talk before we read:
  • Use rent, landlady, smell and secret: what do you think happens in this story?
  • Which words are about a room? Which are about feelings? Say them in two groups.

The story, in three parts

This is a famous American story, told here in simple English. Read each part. Then answer the questions from memory, and talk about one question.

Part 1 — The room

New York, about 1900. In the old red houses of the West Side, many people lived in cheap furnished rooms. They stayed for a week or a month, and then they moved to another room. Many of them worked in theatres. They had no real home.

One evening, a young man knocked on the door of one of these houses. The landlady, Mrs Purdy, opened it. "I have a room on the third floor," she said. "It's a nice room. Do you want to see it?" The room was small and shabby, but the young man said, "I'll take it," and he paid for one week.

Then he asked her a question. He asked everybody this question. "Do you remember a young woman called Eloise Vashner? She is a singer. She has gold hair, and a small dark mark next to her left eye." "No," said Mrs Purdy. "I don't think so. Theatre people come and go. They change their names all the time. I don't remember that name."

For five months, the young man was looking for Eloise. He loved her, and she left her home town and came to New York. He looked in theatres, in hotels and in houses like this one. But nobody knew her.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What kind of people live in the furnished rooms?

2. Who is the young man looking for? What does Mrs Purdy say?

1. People with no real home who move a lot. Many work in theatres.

2. Eloise Vashner, a singer with gold hair. Mrs Purdy says, "I don't think so… I don't remember that name."

Discuss:
  • Mrs Purdy says "I don't think so", not "No, she wasn't here." Is there a difference? Why?

Part 2 — The smell of flowers

The young man sat alone in the room. It was old and sad. The carpet had holes in it. People had written their names on the walls. Hundreds of people slept in this room before him, but nobody loved it.

Then, suddenly, the room was full of a sweet smell: mignonette, a small flower. It was Eloise's favourite perfume! "Eloise!" he cried. "She was here! In this room!"

He looked everywhere. He opened every drawer. He found a hairpin, a handkerchief, a theatre ticket and some old sweets. But nothing was hers. Nothing had her name. And the sweet smell was everywhere.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the young man smell?

2. What does he find in the drawers?

1. Mignonette — a flower, Eloise's favourite perfume.

2. A hairpin, a handkerchief, a theatre ticket and some old sweets — but nothing with Eloise's name.

Discuss:
  • Was Eloise really in the room? What do you think?

Part 3 — The secret

He ran down the stairs to Mrs Purdy. "Who stayed in my room before me?" he asked. "Two theatre ladies," she said. "One had black hair. Before them, a man. Before him, a woman with two children. I'm not sure about the others."

He went back to his room. Now the smell was gone, and his hope disappeared too. He was very tired and very sad. He closed the window and the door. He put pieces of the sheet in every hole. Then he turned on the gas, and he lay down on the bed.

Downstairs, Mrs Purdy was having a drink with her friend Mrs McCool. "I rented the third-floor room tonight," she said. "To a young man." "Did you tell him?" asked Mrs McCool quietly. "No, I didn't. Nobody wants a room after a young woman kills herself in it." "Yes, it was very sad," said Mrs McCool. "She was very pretty, with that little dark mark next to her left eye. Have another drink, Mrs Purdy."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the young man do at the end?

2. What is Mrs Purdy's secret?

1. He closes the room and turns on the gas. He kills himself.

2. A young woman killed herself in that room the week before. It was Eloise — she had the dark mark next to her left eye.

Discuss:
  • We know the truth, but the young man never knows it. How do you feel at the end?

Grammar — I think so / I don't think so / I'm not sure

Mrs Purdy doesn't say "No." She says "I don't think so." When we are not 100% sure — or when we don't want to say the truth! — we use these short answers.

From sure to not sure:
• 100% yes: Yes, she was.  ·  about 80% yes: I think so.
• 50%: Maybe. / Perhaps. / I'm not sure. / I don't know.
• about 80% no: I don't think so.  ·  100% no: No, she wasn't.
Be careful:
• Say I think so, not I think it or I think yes.
• Say I don't think so — this is more common than I think not.
• I'm not sure + if / about: I'm not sure if she was here. I'm not sure about the others.
• Also useful: I hope so! / I hope not!   I'm afraid so. / I'm afraid not. (= sorry, it's bad news)

1. How sure are you?

Write I think so, I don't think so or I'm not sure.

1. "Is it going to rain tomorrow?" — ". The sky is very dark." (80% yes)

2. "Is the shop open on Sunday?" — ". It's usually closed." (80% no)

3. "What time is it?" — ". I haven't got a watch." (50%)

1. I think so   2. I don't think so   3. I'm not sure

2. so or not?

Write so or not.

1. "Will Eloise come back?" — "I hope !"

2. "Is the room empty?" — "I'm afraid . Someone rented it this morning."

3. "Is the gas dangerous?" — "I'm afraid . Be careful."

Going deeper

O. Henry (1862–1910) was an American writer. His real name was William Sydney Porter. He wrote about 600 short stories, many about ordinary people in New York. He is famous for surprise endings. He wrote "The Furnished Room" in 1904.

The heart of it. Most O. Henry endings are happy surprises. This one is a sad surprise, and only the reader knows it. Look at Mrs Purdy's words at the start: "I don't think so", "I don't remember that name". She doesn't say "No". She doesn't really lie — but she doesn't tell the truth. Look at the room, too: hundreds of people came and went, and nobody loved it. And the smell of flowers: is it Eloise calling him, or only old perfume? O. Henry doesn't tell us. The young man and Eloise were in the same room, one week apart — so near, and they never found each other.
Talk it through:
  • Is Mrs Purdy a bad person, or just a woman with a business?
  • Why does the writer tell us about all the old things in the room?
  • Is the smell of flowers a ghost, or just perfume? What do you think?
  • The last words are "Have another drink." Why does the writer finish like this?
