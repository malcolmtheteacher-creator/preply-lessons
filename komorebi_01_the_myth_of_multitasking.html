The Myth of Multitasking

B2/C1 Level · Why our brains can't really do two things at once

Are you really doing two things at once?

We tend to pride ourselves on doing several things at once — replying to an email while a podcast plays, scrolling our phones halfway through a film, holding a conversation while half-watching a child. We call this "multitasking", and we treat it as something close to a virtue. But the neuroscientists who study attention have a much less flattering name for what is actually going on: rapid, exhausting, error-prone task-switching. The very thing many of us admire in ourselves may be the thing that is quietly wearing us out.

Think about this:

1. What's a typical "multitasking" moment from your own day? On reflection, were you really doing both things — or doing each of them slightly badly?

2. Some people insist they "work better with five tabs open". Does that strike you as honest self-knowledge, or as a rather convenient form of self-deception?

3. Where, in your view, does the genuine line lie between being adaptable and being scattered?

Quick prediction

Below are five "multitasking" situations. Which two do scientists believe we can genuinely do at the same time without losing performance, and why?

a) walking and chatting   b) writing an email and listening to song lyrics   c) cooking from a new recipe and answering work calls   d) folding laundry and listening to a podcast   e) driving on a busy road and using a hands-free phone

Show Answer
Research strongly suggests (a) and (d) — both pair an automatic, well-practised motor task with a relatively passive cognitive one. The brain handles them on separate "channels" and neither pulls heavily on our limited attentional resources. (b), (c) and (e), by contrast, all compete for the same conscious processing system, even when they don't feel as if they do. Driving with a hands-free phone, in particular, has been shown to leave drivers cognitively comparable to those who have been drinking.

Reading: A Word That Escaped the Laboratory

In the 1960s, computer scientists coined the word "multitasking" to describe how a single processor could juggle several jobs by switching between them faster than the human eye could detect. The word was a flattering metaphor for what computers did. By the 1980s, however, it had quietly escaped the laboratory and reattached itself to human beings. We were now expected to multitask too — to do everything, at once, and to look good while we did it.

Phrasing · read this paragraph aloud and break it into natural thought groups. Mark a tiny pause at each comma and notice how the meaning lands more cleanly when you do.

The trouble is that the analogy was wrong. The American neuroscientist David Strayer, who has spent decades studying attention in his Utah laboratory, estimates that only about 2% of the population are genuine "supertaskers" — people whose brains can run two demanding tasks in parallel without measurable cost. The rest of us are doing something subtly but importantly different: we are engaged in rapid task-switching, jumping back and forth between jobs and paying the cost of context-switching with every single jump.

Sentence stress · read aloud and underline the three or four content words in each sentence that take the heaviest stress. Function words (the, of, with, are) should slip past almost unnoticed.

A landmark 2009 study at Stanford University tested students who described themselves as gifted multitaskers. The researchers expected to discover that these students had developed superior cognitive control. Instead they found the opposite. Heavy multitaskers performed worse on every measure tested — they were more easily distracted, slower at filtering out irrelevant information, weaker at switching tasks deliberately. Their attention had been spread far too thin; the cognitive load they were carrying was heavier than they realised; and they were running, in effect, on autopilot through the very activity they thought they had mastered.

Linking · notice how, in fluent speech, words bleed into each other — "studies_at Stanford", "more_easily distracted", "in_effect". Practise the linking; don't separate every word.

What is striking, however, is that the multitaskers themselves had no idea. Their habits felt deceptively productive: the constant switching produced a steady sensation of busyness — and busyness is something our culture has, perhaps mistakenly, learned to mistake for effectiveness. Worst of all, almost none of them had compartmentalised their day in any meaningful way; work, messages, half-formed plans and personal life all bled into the same hour. The cost was real, but invisible from the inside.

Intonation · this paragraph turns on a contrast — felt productive against performed badly. Listen for the falling intonation on each side of the pivot, with a small rise on the contrast word itself.

The implication is uncomfortable. If we want to do good work, we may have to give up a story we rather like about ourselves. Single-tasking — the unfashionable opposite — sounds slow and old-fashioned. Yet on every quiet measure that genuinely matters, it appears to win. The deeper problem may not even lie with us. We now live inside an attention economy whose entire business model is to fragment that single task before we ever get the chance to finish it.

Shadow reading · try reading this paragraph aloud half a beat behind your teacher, matching pace, stress and intonation. The aim is rhythm, not perfection — lock onto the music of the sentence.

1. How did the meaning of the word "multitasking" change between the 1960s and the 1980s?

Show Answer
It originally described what computers did — a processor switching between jobs faster than the eye could detect. By the 1980s it had been re-applied to human beings, who were now expected to do the same. The transfer happened almost unnoticed, despite the fact that human brains don't work the way processors do.

2. According to David Strayer's research, what is a "supertasker", and how rare are they?

Show Answer
A supertasker is someone whose brain can genuinely run two demanding tasks in parallel without paying a measurable switching cost. Only around 2% of the population qualify — the vast majority of self-identified multitaskers are simply task-switching very fast.

3. Why was the result of the 2009 Stanford study surprising?

Show Answer
The researchers expected heavy multitaskers to have developed superior cognitive control through sheer practice. They found the exact opposite: heavy multitaskers were worse on every measure of attention tested. The very habit they prided themselves on had quietly trained them to be inefficient.

4. Why did the multitaskers themselves not realise they were performing badly?

Show Answer
Because the constant switching produced a sensation of busyness, and our culture has trained us to mistake busyness for effectiveness. From the inside, doing many things at once feels like doing well, even when the work itself tells a different story.

5. Inference: why does the writer call single-tasking "the unfashionable opposite"?

Show Answer
Because in a culture that quietly equates busyness with success, doing one thing at a time looks slow, modest, almost lazy — even when the evidence suggests it is the more effective approach. The phrase captures the social pressure that makes single-tasking feel like a step backwards even when it isn't.

Discourse markers of contrast

When we argue about multitasking — or about anything where surface and reality disagree — we're constantly setting two ideas against each other. The right contrast marker can make the same point sound either fluent or clunky. Here are the five that do most of the heavy lifting at C1.

Although / Even though

Joins two clauses; the contrast is built in.

Although she felt productive, she was making more mistakes than usual.

Despite / In spite of

Followed by a noun or an -ing form, never a full clause.

Despite feeling productive, she was making more mistakes.

However / Nevertheless

Sentence adverbs — punctuated off, formal in tone.

She felt productive. However, she was making more mistakes.

Yet / But

Short, sharp contrast within a single sentence.

It all felt productive — yet the work was quietly suffering.

Whereas / While

Parallel contrast: two things compared side by side.

Some tasks pair easily, whereas others compete for the same attention.

Gap-fill 1: which marker fits?

1. ____________ the constant interruptions, she somehow finished the report by lunchtime. (despite / although)

Show Answer
Despite — followed by a noun phrase ("the constant interruptions"). "Although" would need a full clause: Although there were constant interruptions...

2. He insists he is a supertasker; ____________, every test result so far says otherwise. (however / whereas)

Show Answer
However — sentence adverb, punctuated off after a semicolon. "Whereas" would imply a parallel comparison rather than a flat contradiction.

3. She likes background music while she works, ____________ her colleague needs total silence. (whereas / although)

Show Answer
Whereas — the sentence sets up a parallel contrast between two people. "Although" would imply concession rather than comparison.

4. ____________ being exhausted, he still insisted on answering one more email. (despite / although)

Show Answer
Despite — followed by an -ing form. "Although" would need a clause: Although he was exhausted...

5. The technology was sold to us as a productivity tool. ____________, most studies suggest the opposite. (nevertheless / whereas)

Show Answer
Nevertheless — sentence-initial, formal in register, marks a strong contrast with what has just been claimed.

6. The constant pings felt productive, ____________ they were quietly draining her concentration. (yet / whereas)

Show Answer
Yet — short, sharp contrast inside a single sentence. The two halves point in opposite directions and "yet" lets you pivot in a single beat.

Gap-fill 2: rewrite for emphasis

7. Original: "She finished the report. The interruptions kept coming."   →   Use despite.

Show Answer
Despite the constant interruptions, she finished the report. (Or: Despite there being constant interruptions...)

8. Original: "Heavy multitaskers feel productive. They actually perform worse."   →   Use however.

Show Answer
Heavy multitaskers feel productive. However, they actually perform worse.

9. Original: "Some people work best with music. Others need silence."   →   Use whereas.

Show Answer
Some people work best with music, whereas others need silence.
