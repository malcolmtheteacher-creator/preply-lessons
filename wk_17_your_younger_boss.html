Your Younger Boss

She got the job on Thursday. She's twenty-nine, you're forty-six, and you were here a long time before she was. Half an hour, a small room, and the whole tone of the next two years gets set in it.

The Scene 🎬

The email went round on Thursday afternoon. Priya Ravel, who joined three years ago and sits two desks down from you, is now running the team. You shouted "congratulations" across the office at the time, the way people do, and then went back to your screen and felt something you'd rather not examine too closely.

It's Tuesday. She's booked you half an hour in the small room with the broken blind. And here's the thing nobody warns you about: she is more frightened than you are. She has to manage someone who knows where everything is buried, who was doing this job while she was still at university, and who might — she has no way of knowing — spend the next year quietly making her look silly. She can't tell how much of that fear is reasonable. She'll be reading your face for it.

So the half hour isn't really about the Friday reports or the Halcott account. It's about which version of you walks out of that room: the one she has to manage around, or the one she can simply talk to. You get to choose, and you get to choose in roughly the first ninety seconds.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Have you ever been managed by someone much younger, or managed someone much older? What was the unspoken thing in the room — and did either of you ever say it out loud?
  2. When a new manager arrives, most people wait to be told how things will work. What does waiting actually cost you — and what would you have to risk in order to go first instead?

Who's who

You
Twenty-two Years In 🗂️

You know the work, the clients, and every reason the Friday report is the way it is. None of that is the point today. Today you're deciding to be easy to manage — on purpose, out loud, before anyone thinks to ask you.

The other voice
Priya Ravel · 29 · Four days in the job 📋

Sharp, decent, and quietly braced for a fight she half expects from you. She hasn't worked out yet how much rope to give anyone. She has rehearsed at least one thing in the lift on the way up.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't follow what they decide about the reports — that isn't the lesson. Watch how many of the older one's turns end in a question rather than a statement, and notice what happens to the room each time. Watch, too, how the awkward things get said early and lightly, rather than carefully and late.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's a Tuesday morning, they've both had too much coffee, and neither of them has done this before.

PriyaRight — sorry, have you got the — is it the full half hour you've got? Only I've booked us thirty minutes and I've genuinely no idea what to do with them.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Early on, the older one puts a thought on the table that Priya has never said and probably never would. What was that thought — and why is saying it out loud safer than leaving it sitting there unmentioned?

2. When Priya announces she wants to change the Friday reports, the answer isn't only in the words — the speaker admits something about their own reaction in that moment. What did they admit, and why does admitting it help rather than hurt?

3. The last question of the half hour is about disagreement, asked at a point when there is nothing yet to disagree about. Why ask it now rather than the first time it actually matters?

1. That they might want her job, or think it should have been theirs. She is half braced for exactly that, which is why she says she'd expected a fight. A suspicion nobody mentions doesn't fade on its own — it simply sits there colouring everything, so the person who names it first takes the weapon out of the room and lets her stop guessing.
2. That their face gave them away — they didn't like it, visibly, for a second. Pretending otherwise would have been useless, because she saw it; admitting it means she learns that what shows on their face is a reaction rather than a plot, which is a far easier thing to work with over two years.
3. Because the rule is easy to agree while it's still hypothetical, and horrible to negotiate in the middle of the row. Asked now, it's a friendly question about how she likes to work; asked later, it becomes an argument about an argument.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten lines for the first proper conversation with a new manager — or with anyone who has power over you and is nervous about having it. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. The whole thing hangs on tone, because every one of these lines can be said generously or said as a small threat, and the words are more or less identical either way. Aim for light and quick rather than careful and weighted — a thing said in passing sounds true, whereas the same thing said slowly, with steady eye contact, sounds like a warning. Hand her the choices rather than announcing your terms: most of these are questions, and they should go up at the end, not down. And when you've said the generous thing, don't pause to see whether it landed — the pause is what makes it sound like a performance.

🎉 Getting the warm bit said first

Phrase 1

She got the job a week ago. You said something across the office at the time, but you've never said it properly, and you're about to sit down and ask her for things. Which of these actually sounds like you mean it?

A. "Congratulations on the promotion. Well deserved."
B. "Congratulations, by the way — genuinely."
C. "Congratulations! You must be absolutely delighted!"
B — "Congratulations, by the way — genuinely."
The work is being done by two small words that look like nothing at all. "By the way" makes it sound as though the thought arrived on its own rather than being prepared, which is what stops it feeling staged; and "genuinely" only ever gets added by someone who knows the standard version sounds automatic, so it quietly says I'm aware this is what everyone says, and I'm not doing that. A is the correct, complete, well-formed version, which is exactly why it reads as a formality — nobody who meant it would say it that neatly. C is enthusiasm aimed at her feelings rather than at yours, so it tells her nothing about where you stand, which is the only thing she wants to know.
Phrase 2

You've decided to settle the whole status question in one sentence, early, before it can sit underneath everything else for a year. The sentence concedes the point and then immediately asks for something. Put it in order:

how you like to work.You're the boss,I just want to knowthat's fine by me —
"You're the boss, that's fine by me — I just want to know how you like to work."
The order matters more than the words do, because you give away the thing she's worried about before you ask for anything, so the request that follows can't be read as a condition attached to it. "That's fine by me" is deliberately casual, since saying it more formally would suggest it had cost you something to decide. And notice what you're actually asking for — not what she wants done, but how she likes to work, which is a question about her rather than about the tasks, and is therefore rather hard to be defensive about.

🧭 Finding out how she actually works

Phrase 3

You want the practical version of the same question — not the strategy, not the year ahead, just what the ordinary weeks are going to feel like. Complete it: "How do you want this to work, ___ to ___?" (The two missing words are the same word twice.)

"How do you want this to work, day to day?"
"Day to day" pulls the question down out of the abstract, where a nervous new manager will only answer you in slogans, and puts it into the actual week — the emails, the meetings, the moment she wonders what you're up to. It's a small kindness as well as a useful question, because she almost certainly can't tell you her grand plan yet, and this is one she can answer.
Phrase 8

She's four days in and hasn't worked out how much rope to give anyone, including you. Rather than let her find out the hard way, you put the choice to her yourself — one short question, two options, no preamble. Say it out loud, then reveal.

"Do you want me to just get on with it, or check in?"
Offering someone a choice between two things you're equally willing to do is about the easiest gift you can give a new manager, because the hard part of her week isn't deciding — it's asking. If she has to ask you to check in more often, she first has to work out whether you'll take it as an insult; if you offer it, she just picks one. The word doing the work is "just", which makes the whole question sound small, as though nothing much rides on the answer.

🪞 Naming your own experience before she has to

Phrase 5

Your experience is the awkward object in the room — it's the reason you're useful and the reason she's nervous. You want to name it yourself, and name the problem with it in the same breath. Which line does both?

A. "I've done this a long time, so I know how it works."
B. "I've done this a long time, which is useful right up until it isn't."
C. "I've done this a long time — a lot longer than you have."
B — "I've done this a long time, which is useful right up until it isn't."
It's the second half that buys you the first half. You're allowed to claim your twenty-two years so long as you also admit, in the same sentence, that twenty-two years is exactly the thing that stops people seeing a better way — so the line names your value and your blind spot together, and leaves her nothing to argue with. A claims the value and hides the cost, which is precisely what she's braced for. C is perfectly true and does nothing whatever except remind her of the gap she's already worried about.
Phrase 6

You've admitted the blind spot exists. Now you hand her permission to point at it — and you promise, lightly, not to sulk. Complete it: "Tell me if I'm doing the ___-___ thing — I won't take it badly." (Two words joined by a hyphen. The first is the opposite of "new"; the second is built out of the word "time" and means someone who has been somewhere a very long while.)

"Tell me if I'm doing the old-timer thing — I won't take it badly."
An old-timer is the person who's been somewhere so long that they answer every new idea with "we tried that in 2011", and calling it "the old-timer thing" is you getting there first, in your own words, with a joke in it. Saying it yourself is the entire point, because a name she's allowed to use is a name she'll use early and gently, whereas a name she isn't allowed to use just becomes something she says about you to somebody else. And "I won't take it badly" is a promise you have to keep the very first time she tries it, or the whole thing was worthless.

🔁 Taking a change you didn't want

Phrase 4

You want her to know that the polite, roundabout version of bad news is no use to you at all — you'd rather have it plain and early. Complete it: "I'd rather you told me straight than went ___ ___ ___." (Three words. It's an old expression for getting somewhere by the longest possible route rather than the direct one.)

"I'd rather you told me straight than went round the houses."
To go round the houses is to take the long, winding way — the picture behind it is somebody walking past every house in the street instead of crossing straight to the one they want — so it means saying eight careful things before you say the one thing you actually meant. Managers do it in order to be kind, new ones especially, and it lands as evasive rather than kind. What you're asking is that she spend the kindness on the timing instead: say it plainly, and say it early.
Phrase 7

She's changing something you built and have run for nine years. You think she's probably wrong, and she's still the boss. Which line disagrees about the method while genuinely accepting the decision?

A. "That's not how we've done it."
B. "That's not how we've done it, but I'm happy to try it your way."
C. "You're the boss — do what you like."
B — "That's not how we've done it, but I'm happy to try it your way."
Both halves are carrying weight. The first half is honest — you don't pretend to like it, and pretending would be transparent anyway, since your face has already told her — and the second half is what makes the first half safe to say, because it turns an objection into a piece of information rather than a refusal. "Try" is quietly useful too: it's a real commitment without promising you'll agree at the end of it. A is the same objection with the agreement removed, which leaves her guessing whether you'll actually do it. C sounds like agreement and is nearer to a threat, because you're refusing to have a view at all, and she will notice.

🫱 The two things nobody says out loud

Phrase 9

There will be a day when you think she's wrong in front of eleven other people, and how that goes is worth settling now, while it's still hypothetical. You ask her to pick. Put it in order:

or before it?would you ratherIf I disagree with you,hear it in the meeting
"If I disagree with you, would you rather hear it in the meeting or before it?"
The question smuggles in something rather large: it assumes you will disagree, out loud, and that this is a normal thing rather than a problem. Because it's phrased as a choice about scheduling rather than a request for permission, she'll almost certainly agree to it without noticing what she's agreed to — and now the disagreement, whenever it eventually comes, arrives as something the two of you arranged rather than something you did to her.
Phrase 10

There's a thought she may well be having and will never, ever say: that the person she's now managing wanted this job, or thinks it should have been his. You're going to say it for her, and let her off having had the thought at all. Say the sentence out loud, then reveal.

"I'm not after your job, if that ever crosses your mind."
When something crosses your mind it arrives on its own and you didn't invite it, which is precisely why the ending matters so much: you're not accusing her of suspecting you, you're granting that the thought might turn up uninvited, and offering the answer in advance for when it does. Saying "if that ever crosses your mind" lets her have had the thought without ever admitting she had it — she doesn't have to deny anything, agree to anything or apologise for anything, she just gets the answer and keeps her dignity. Said quickly it's generous; said slowly and solemnly it becomes an accusation, so keep it light and move straight on to something else.

🔑 Make them yours

These aren't for "a new manager" — they're for the person who'll actually be sitting opposite you, in a room you can picture. Pin three of them to something real, and say each answer out loud until it comes without hunting for it.

Think of someone at work who has power over you and is newer at it than you are at your job. What's the unsaid thing between you — the one you'd both deny if anybody asked?

Your own version of the blind spot: what's the thing about you that's genuinely useful and genuinely a problem? Both halves, one sentence.

One choice you could hand your manager this week instead of waiting to be told — two options you'd honestly be happy with either way.

The Twist

🎯 Three weeks later

Everything went beautifully. The new reports went in, you said all the right things, and you meant them at the time. Now it's three weeks on, she's stopped you by the lifts, and she says this: "You know how you said I should tell you straight? Right. When you talk in the Monday meeting, people stop listening to me. They turn round and look at you. I'm not saying you're doing it on purpose. I'm asking you not to talk in Mondays." She isn't wrong about the room. She may well be wrong about the fix. And you told her, more or less in writing, that you wouldn't take this badly. Start the timer and answer her live, standing by the lifts, with people walking past. You don't get to go away and think about it, and "fine, whatever you want" isn't an answer — it's you making her pay for having asked. Decide out loud whether "that's fine by me" was actually true, and what you're going to say if it wasn't.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said the warm thing before I asked for anything, and I said it quickly rather than solemnly.
  • I put the awkward thought on the table myself instead of waiting to see whether she'd raise it.
  • I offered her a choice at least once, rather than waiting to be told or telling her how it would be.
  • I said I didn't like something and agreed to do it anyway, in the same breath, and meant both halves.
  • When she took me up on my own invitation and it stung, I didn't punish her for it.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that'll cost you in the real room. Run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Most people, handed a much younger boss, do nothing at all. They're perfectly civil, they turn up, they answer what they're asked — and they wait to be managed, which looks like cooperation and works like a slow refusal.

What the half hour is really for is giving away the thing you were never going to use anyway. You weren't going to sabotage her. You just hadn't said so, and for as long as you hadn't said so, she had to plan around the version of you who might.

The cost is small and it's real: for about ninety seconds you have to be the one who's visibly trying, in front of someone junior to you, with nothing at all guaranteeing she meets you halfway. There are people who would genuinely rather have two bad years than those ninety seconds.

Two bigger questions

  1. Going first — saying the generous thing before you know whether it'll be returned — costs you something if the other person doesn't return it. Is that a risk worth taking with someone who now decides your working life, or is it simply handing over your only leverage? What does the answer depend on?
  2. Experience is supposed to be an asset, and yet the older person here spends half the conversation apologising for having it. Are they being wise, or are they being talked out of something valuable — and who benefits from the idea that twenty-two years is mainly a liability?
