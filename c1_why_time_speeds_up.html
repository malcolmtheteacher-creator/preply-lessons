Why Time Speeds Up As We Age

Warm-up: a strange certainty

The Vanishing Year

If you are forty years old, a single year is one fortieth of your life. If you are eight, that same year is one eighth. The mathematics alone is unforgiving. To a child, a year is a vast country. To an older adult, it is a brief season — the same calendar, measured against very different totals.

This proportionality theory was first proposed in the nineteenth century by the French philosopher Paul Janet, and it remains the most intuitive explanation for the universal sense that time accelerates with age. But proportionality is only part of the story. The deeper answer lies in how the brain records experience.

Memory, neuroscientists tell us, is built from change. The brain is interested in novelty — in what is new, surprising, or unfamiliar — and it largely ignores what it already knows. A child's life is a flood of firsts: first school, first friendship, first journey, first heartbreak. Each of these is encoded richly, with detail and weight. By contrast, an adult's Tuesday in October may be almost indistinguishable from the Tuesday before it. The brain, recognising the pattern, encodes very little.

The result is a strange asymmetry. While we live a routine week, it may feel ordinary in real time. But when we look back at it weeks later, there is hardly anything there. The week has, in effect, evaporated. Multiply that across a year of similar weeks, and the year itself seems to have passed in a blur. This is what psychologists call the retrospective compression of experience: lived time and remembered time obey different rules.

There is a hopeful corollary. If novelty stretches subjective time, we are not entirely at the mercy of the calendar. The traveller, the language-learner, the person who deliberately disrupts their routine — each is, in a small way, lengthening their life from the inside. A week in an unfamiliar city, packed with new tastes, new languages, new faces, will be remembered as longer than a month of ordinary commuting. The years do not have to shrink. They shrink because we let them.

This is not a mystical claim. It is, the researchers suggest, simply a matter of giving the brain something to encode. Learn an instrument. Walk a route you have never walked. Speak to someone whose life is nothing like yours. The mechanism is unglamorous and the prescription almost banal — but the consequence is real. A life of milestones remembers itself as long. A life of monotony remembers itself as a single, brief afternoon.

Comprehension

1. According to Paul Janet, why does a year feel shorter at forty than at eight?

Because the year represents a much smaller proportion of the total life lived so far — one fortieth rather than one eighth.

2. What does the brain do with experiences that are repetitive or familiar?

It encodes very little of them. It is interested in novelty, so familiar patterns are largely ignored and leave few traces in memory.

3. Explain "retrospective compression" in your own words.

The phenomenon by which a period of time feels normal while you are living it, but appears very short when you look back at it — because the brain stored almost nothing from it.

4. What practical advice does the passage offer for slowing the apparent speed of time?

Introduce novelty deliberately — travel, learn something new, vary your routine, meet people unlike yourself. Anything that gives the brain new material to encode.

5. Find a phrase in the passage that means "completely disappeared".

"Has, in effect, evaporated."

Discussion

Take your time with these. Aim for full, considered answers — these are the kind of questions where a sharp candidate can really show range.

Part A — your own experience

  1. Think of the longest-feeling year of your life so far. What made it feel so long? Was it pleasant, difficult, or simply full?
  2. Are there weeks in your recent life that have, in your own words, "evaporated"? What did those weeks have in common?
  3. If novelty is what makes time feel rich, would you say modern life — with its routines, screens, and predictable comforts — is making years pass faster than they used to?

Part B — wider implications

  1. Some people argue that monotony is the price we pay for security — a stable job, a familiar home, a known neighbourhood. Do you think it is a price worth paying?
  2. Travel is often described as a way of "extending one's life from the inside." Is this a luxury reserved for those with means, or can the same effect be achieved without leaving one's city?
  3. Children remember their childhoods as enormous. Could there be any benefit, as an adult, in deliberately living more like a child — noticing more, repeating less?
  4. If you could engineer one new habit, starting next week, that would generate genuine novelty for your brain to encode, what would it be?

Part C — a sharper question

  1. The passage suggests that a life of milestones "remembers itself as long" and a life of monotony "as a single, brief afternoon." Which kind of life do you think most people are actually living? And which would you prefer?
