Setting the Scene

You are the Chief Communications Officer. You're at work on a Tuesday morning. At 10 a.m., a major publication is breaking a story about your company. The story is critical, based partly on fact and partly on rumor. You have until 10 a.m. to decide what you're going to say—and more importantly, what you're not going to say.

This is the moment communication becomes strategic. You have 4 hours. You need to: brief the CEO, prepare a response, decide which facts to lead with, decide which attacks to address and which to ignore, and build a narrative that survives the first 48 hours of coverage.

Why this matters at C-suite level: At senior levels, communication is no longer about truth-telling. It's about truth-selection. You're choosing which truths to emphasize and which to downplay. You're deciding what story you want the world to believe. This is not dishonest—it's strategy. And it separates leaders who shape narratives from leaders who are shaped by them.

Strategic communication is about controlling the narrative frame. You can't control the facts, but you can control which facts matter and how they're interpreted. The leader who says nothing loses the narrative to others. The leader who speaks first and clearly shapes how the story gets told. Your words are your power.

Language Moves for Strategic Communication

These moves are the toolkit of narrative control. Use them carefully. They're powerful.

1 Narrative Control Language +

This is the move where you provide an alternative frame for the facts. You're not denying the story. You're offering a different interpretation of what it means.

What media will say

"Company hid safety concerns from regulators."

Your frame

"We identified a product safety concern in testing. We immediately took the product off shelves, notified users, and launched an investigation. We self-reported to regulators. We're putting safety before sales."

Same facts. Different story. One frames you as transparent. The other frames you as hiding things.

2 Messaging Architecture +

The move is to build a message structure that supports your narrative. You have three key messages. You reinforce them relentlessly. Every spokesperson says the same thing.

Example message architecture

Message 1: "We take this seriously." (Signals accountability)
Message 2: "We've already taken action." (Signals leadership)
Message 3: "We're committed to doing better." (Signals the future)

Every interview, every statement, every press release reinforces these three. You never deviate. Media will attack all three. You defend all three. By day 3, that's the narrative that sticks.

3 The Proactive Statement +

The move is to get ahead of the story. You announce bad news before journalists do. You control the frame by being the first voice.

What doesn't work

Journalists break the story. Then you respond. By then, the frame is set.

Strong

You announce: "We discovered a compliance issue in our Q3 reporting. We've corrected it and self-reported to the SEC." Now when journalists report it, they're confirming what you already said. You own the narrative.

4 Managing What You Don't Say +

This is a critical move. Silence is part of strategy. You don't answer every question. You don't respond to every attack. You stay on message even when attacked.

What feels natural

Journalist: "Are you covering this up?" You: "No, we're not. Here's what we're doing..."

Strong

Journalist: "Are you covering this up?" You: "We're focused on fixing the problem and earning back trust. Here's what we're doing..." You never dignify the accusation with a direct denial. You pivot to your message.

5 Press Briefing Register +

When you brief the press, your tone is "serious and in control." You're not defensive. You're not casual. You're professional, accountable, and forward-focused.

Tone matters

Too defensive: "That's not true. We didn't..." Signals guilt.
Too casual: "Yeah, we messed up, but whatever." Signals indifference.
Right tone: "We acknowledge the concern. Here's what happened. Here's what we're doing."

6 Reputational Risk Framing +

The move is to frame the crisis as a risk to reputation that you're actively managing, not as evidence of bad character.

What sounds defensive

"We're good people. We didn't mean to do this."

Strong

"Our reputation is built on trust. This incident puts that at risk. We're treating it as a reputational crisis, not just an operational issue. That means we're investing in how we fix this—not just fixing the problem, but rebuilding confidence."

Live Scenario: Crisis Communications in Real Time

The Situation: It's 6 a.m. A major publication is breaking a story at 10 a.m. about a compliance issue at your company. The story is incomplete—they don't have all the facts. You have 4 hours to decide: Do you get ahead of it or respond after? What do you say and what do you not say?

The facts: Your company discovered a compliance issue in how customer data was handled. You've already corrected it and are self-reporting to regulators. The journalists have a partial story that makes it sound like you hid it.

PART A: Brief the CEO (5 minutes)

You're in the CEO's office at 6:15 a.m. You have 5 minutes to brief them on the situation, the strategy, and the decision: do we get ahead or do we wait?

Speak & Record Brief the CEO. Explain the situation, the risks, your recommendation (proactive or reactive), and the 3-point message architecture you're proposing. Speak for 5 minutes. Assume they need to make a decision immediately.
5:00

Framing scaffold

FACT What happened. How bad is it. Do we need to disclose more?
FRAME How media will tell it vs. how we want to tell it.
DECISION Proactive announcement + press statement? Or wait for coverage?
NARRATIVE Three messages we'll use to frame this.

PART B: Prepare the Press Statement (4 minutes)

You decide to get ahead of it. You're going to release a statement in 30 minutes—30 minutes before the story breaks. This statement sets the narrative. You control the frame.

Speak & Record Draft a press statement (or speak it aloud as if you're writing it). Acknowledge the issue. Explain what you've done. Frame it as transparency and accountability. Do not say "we apologize." Use your message architecture. Speak for 4 minutes.
4:00

Statement structure

LEAD The good news first: "We discovered... and immediately acted."
CONTEXT Why this happened and how we found it.
ACTIONS What we've done. How we're fixing it. Timeline.
COMMITMENT "We're committed to doing better. Trust is earned."

Crisis Communication Pressure Tests

Five scenarios that test your ability to manage narrative under fire.

A journalist in a press conference: "Your statement says you 'discovered' this issue, but internal emails show people flagged it weeks ago. Weren't you hiding it?"

Your Response (2 minutes) They have internal emails. You can't deny the timeline. But you can reframe what the timeline means. Go.
Social media is exploding. Hashtags are calling for boycotts. Customer trust is collapsing. A board member asks: "Should we do more than just a statement? Should we go on TV? Should the CEO resign?"
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) You need to decide: does the CEO go on camera? Does someone resign? You need to recommend based on what will actually restore trust. Go.
A competitor leaks an old email where you said something unflattering about the same compliance issue. Now the story is worse: "Company ignored warning signs."
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) You have new facts that contradict your first statement. Do you revise? Do you ignore it? What's the smart play?
An employee goes on social media and says: "The company is lying. We knew about this for months. I have proof." Now you have an internal leak that's more damaging than the journalist story.
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) The story is no longer controlled. Do you fight the employee (which looks defensive) or do you engage with them (which validates their platform)? What's the play?
A regulatory body opens an investigation. They subpoena all your internal communications. You realize some executives will look very bad in those communications. Do you volunteer context or let them draw conclusions?
2:00
Your Response (2 minutes) This is where narrative control and legal risk intersect. You can't control what regulators see. But you can shape how you talk about it publicly. How?

Synthesis & Reflection

Discussion 1: Truth and Narrative

There's a difference between telling the truth and telling the whole truth. In strategic communication, you emphasize truths that support your narrative and downplay truths that don't. Is this ethical? When does it become dishonest?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) Have you ever felt that an organization was technically truthful but fundamentally misleading? How did they do it? What did they leave out?
2:00

Discussion 2: The Power of Silence

In a crisis, silence is a choice. Sometimes it's strategic to not answer a question, not engage with an attack, and simply stay on message. But silence can also look like evasion. How do you know the difference?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) When has a leader's refusal to answer a question made you trust them less? When has it made you trust them more?
2:00

Discussion 3: Winning the Narrative vs Solving the Problem

Sometimes the best communication strategy and the best operational response are different things. You might win the narrative with a certain message while actually making the problem worse operationally. How do you choose?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) Have you seen an organization win the PR battle but lose the trust battle? What was the cost?
