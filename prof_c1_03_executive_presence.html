One minute. One idea. Make them listen.

Count the words you used

Hedging language: "um," "sort of," "kind of," "basically," "I think," "to be honest."

Every one of those words signals uncertainty. In the boardroom, those words kill you. Replace them with silence. Silence is better than filler.

Six Moves for Boardroom Presence

Click each move. These are the technical moves that make you sound like you belong in the room.

1. The One-Minute Structure: Situation → Recommendation → Rationale → Ask +

Why it matters: Executives don't have time for wandering. They need to know immediately: what is this about, what are you recommending, why, and what do you need.

Situation: "We've had three key client requests this quarter for feature X."
Recommendation: "We should prioritize it in the next sprint."
Rationale: "It addresses 30% of our churn risk and two of these clients represent 15% of revenue."
Ask: "I need engineering resources from the current project. How do you want me to handle the trade-off?"

The benefit: You've given them everything they need to decide in 60 seconds.

2. Silence as a Tool +

Why it matters: In regular conversation, silence feels awkward. In the boardroom, silence feels powerful. A pause before your most important point signals "I'm not nervous — I just said something significant."

"Three clients have requested this feature. [Pause.] That's a signal. And when you look at the revenue at risk, it's not small."

The benefit: That pause gives people time to absorb. It also shows you're thinking, not just talking.

3. Commanding Language: Active, Definite, No Hedging +

Why it matters: Hedging language signals weakness. Certainty signals you've thought this through.

Weak: "I think we might want to consider possibly prioritizing feature X if we have time and resources."
Commanding: "We should prioritize feature X. Here's why. Here's what it costs."

The shift: Replace "I think," "maybe," "sort of" with "This is," "We should," "The data shows."

4. Handling Interruption: Firm and Gracious +

Why it matters: If you get interrupted and crumble, you're done. If you stay firm, you signal "I have something important to say."

Interrupted: "Just a second — let me finish that thought." [Pause, resume.] "The recommendation is based on three data points."
Not aggressive. Not apologetic. Just clear.

The benefit: You've signalled respect for the room but also conviction in what you're saying.

5. The Strategic Frame: Reframing to Show Thinking +

Why it matters: People think you're strategic when you reframe the problem to show you've thought about it differently than everyone else.

They're asking: "Should we cut costs?" You say: "The question isn't whether to cut costs — it's whether we compete on price or differentiation. Once we decide that, the cost cuts follow."

The benefit: You've gone from answering a question to showing you understand the real strategic issue. That's executive thinking.

6. The Clear Ask: Every Communication Ends With One +

Why it matters: Rambling executives never get what they want because they never ask. Clear executives ask for a specific decision.

Rambling end: "...and we think it might be worth considering at some point."
Clear end: "What I need from this room is a decision on resource allocation by Friday."

The benefit: You know whether you won or lost. And the room knows you meant something.

Your Boardroom Briefing

Deliver a 1-minute executive briefing on a professional challenge or decision you're facing. You have 90 seconds to prepare, 60 seconds to speak. Harsh timer.

Choose a real scenario or use one of these:

A. The Resource Trade-off: Two high-priority projects. You can't do both well. You need a decision on which one gets the team.

B. The Strategic Pivot: Market research shows your core offering is losing to a cheaper competitor. You think the answer is to differentiate, not compete on price. You need buy-in for a shift in strategy.

C. The Difficult Personnel Decision: A high-performer is becoming a team problem. You think they should move roles or leave. You need to frame the business case.

Reflection & Language Bank

Executive Presence Isn't Charisma

It's not about being likeable. It's about being clear. Being decisive. Knowing what you want.

It's not about confidence. It's about preparation. You sound confident because you've thought about this more than they have.

It's not about talking more. It's about saying less, more clearly. Every sentence should do work.
