What is PEEL?

Every strong paragraph in persuasive writing follows the same four steps. Together they form PEEL.

P
Point
State your main idea clearly in one sentence.
"Bringing back the Christmas Island bat would restore the ecological balance of the island."
E
Evidence
Give a fact, example, or data that supports your point.
"Before its extinction, the bat controlled populations of night-flying insects, preventing damage to native plants."
E
Explain
Say why your evidence proves your point.
"This shows that the bat had a specific, irreplaceable role — no other native species currently fills this gap."
L
Link
Connect back to your main argument or lead into the next point.
"Without action, the insect population will continue to grow unchecked — and that is why we must act now."

The order matters

Skipping Explain is the most common mistake. Writers give a point and evidence but never say so what? — why does this evidence actually prove anything? That middle step is where the argument becomes convincing.

Counter-Arguments

Raising the other side and then defeating it is one of the most powerful moves in persuasive writing. It shows you have thought carefully — and that you are still convinced.

The four-step counter-argument

1
Raise the objection Introduce the other side fairly. Don't make it too weak — a strong objection that you defeat is more convincing than a weak one. "Some people argue that we should focus on more famous extinct species, such as the woolly mammoth..."
2
Acknowledge it Briefly admit what is reasonable about the other view. This makes you sound fair and confident. "It is true that larger animals capture public attention more easily..."
3
Rebut it Now explain why your argument is still stronger. Use "however", "nevertheless", "yet" or "but" to signal the turn. "However, size is not the point. What matters is ecological fit — and the Christmas Island bat already had an established niche on the island."
4
Evidence for your rebuttal Give a specific reason, fact, or logic that proves your rebuttal is correct. "Reintroducing a species that once thrived there is far more likely to succeed than introducing one that never lived on the island at all."

Weak rebuttal vs strong rebuttal — what's the difference?

❌ Weak rebuttal
"Maybe now you're thinking: why should we bring back such a disgraceful animal? Well, sometimes even the smallest creature can be a better ecological role than a big one."
Missing Why is small better here? No evidence. The reader is not convinced.
✓ Strong rebuttal
"Some argue we should revive larger animals instead. However, the bat's advantage is not its size — it is the fact that it already belonged here. It evolved on this island, filling a precise ecological gap that no other species currently fills."

Use these sentence starters if you need them:

Step 1 — Raise the objection:
"Some people argue that..." / "Critics might say that..." / "One common objection is..."
Step 2 — Acknowledge:
"It is true that..." / "Admittedly..." / "There is some truth in this..."
Step 3 — Rebut:
"However..." / "Nevertheless..." / "Yet the key point is..." / "But this misses the real issue..."
Step 4 — Evidence:
"This is because..." / "The evidence shows that..." / "In fact..."
