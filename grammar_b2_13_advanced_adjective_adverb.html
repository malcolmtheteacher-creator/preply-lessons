Check your answer CLICK
Opinion always comes first. "Lovely" (opinion) has to lead, then "little" (size), then "old" (age), then "stone" would come right before the noun (material). Swap opinion out of first place and native speakers notice immediately — even if they can't say why.
Today: how to stack adjectives in the right order, how to match "very" or "absolutely" to the right adjective, and which preposition follows words like good, keen, worried and proud — so your descriptions sound natural, not like a list.

A Lovely Little Old House

When you stack adjectives before a noun, English has a running order you follow without thinking — once you know it.

Spot the pattern

  • "I bought a beautiful small antique wooden mirror at the market."
  • "They live in a lovely little old stone cottage by the river."
  • "She was wearing a stunning long black silk dress."

Each one stacks three or four adjectives before the noun — always in the same kind of order. What comes first, and what comes right before the noun? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin → material → noun. Opinion (what you think — beautiful, lovely, stunning) always leads. Material (what it's made of — wooden, stone, silk) always sits right next to the noun. Everything else lines up in between, in that order.
opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin → material → NOUN
The classic trap: opinion has to go first. "A big lovely house" sounds off; "a lovely big house" sounds right — opinion before size, every time.

When "Very" Isn't Enough

Some adjectives sit on a scale. Others are already at the extreme. The intensifier you choose has to match.

Spot the pattern

  • "I'm really tired — I didn't sleep well."
  • "After the marathon, I was absolutely shattered."
  • "This tea is very hot, be careful."
  • "Don't touch that pan — it's absolutely boiling."

Tired and hot sit on a scale — you can be a little tired or very tired. Shattered and boiling don't work that way. What's different about how each pair is intensified? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Gradable adjectives (tired, hot, good, big) describe a scale, so they take very, really, quite, a bit, extremely. Extreme adjectives (shattered, boiling, amazing, huge, freezing) are already at the top of the scale, so they take absolutely, totally, completely, utterly instead. Mix them up — "very shattered", "absolutely tired" — and both sound false to a native ear.
gradable + very / really / quite / a bit / extremely
extreme + absolutely / totally / completely / utterly
Quick ear test: would you say "a bit amazing"? No — amazing is already extreme. Would you say "absolutely good"? No — good is gradable; it needs very or really.

Quick check

1. You walked twenty kilometres yesterday and could barely stand by the end. Use the strong word for "very tired":

"By the time we got home, we were ."

2. You left your gloves at home and waited for a bus for forty minutes in the snow. Use the strong word for "very cold":

"By the time the bus came, I was ."

3. A friend recommends a film. It's fine, but not the best you've ever seen — describe it honestly:

"It wasn't bad — I'd say it was , but not the best I've seen."

Good At, Keen On, Proud Of

Certain adjectives pair with one fixed preposition. There's no logic to it — just the pairs, memorised.

Spot the pattern

  • "I've always been good at drawing, but hopeless at maths."
  • "She's really keen on cycling to work these days."
  • "He's worried about the interview tomorrow."
  • "I'm quite proud of how the garden's turned out this year."

Each adjective drags its own preposition along with it. Could you swap any of them for a different one and keep the same meaning? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
No — each pairing is fixed: good/bad/hopeless at, keen/interested on/in, worried/afraid about/of, proud/famous of/for. There's no logical reason "keen" takes "on" rather than "for" — it's simply the pairing native speakers learned, and getting it wrong is one of the fastest ways a sentence sounds foreign, even when everything else is perfect.
good / bad / hopeless + AT
keen / interested + ON / IN
worried / afraid / scared + ABOUT / OF
proud + OF   famous + FOR
Common slips: "good in maths" (should be AT), "keen of" (should be ON), "afraid from" (should be OF).

Quick check

1. Your little brother won't go near the dog next door. Describe him:

"He's always been dogs, even friendly ones."

2. Your local café is known all over town for one thing:

"That little café's its carrot cake — people come from miles around."

3. You've never really followed sport. Admit it:

"To be honest, I've never been that football."
