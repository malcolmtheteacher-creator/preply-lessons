The Doctor Who Could Not Say Why

In 1847 a doctor in Vienna cut the death rate on his ward with one simple rule, and most of his profession did not believe him. He had a claim and he had the numbers. What he never gave his listeners was the step between them.

Today: you'll see why strong evidence can fail when the reasoning is left unsaid, build a three-part 'claim, link, evidence' statement, and use it so that any listener could repeat how you got from the fact to the conclusion.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — Two clinics, one hospital

In 1846 a young Hungarian doctor, Ignaz Semmelweis, began work as an assistant in the maternity department of the Vienna General Hospital, one of the largest in Europe. The department had two clinics. In the first, babies were delivered by doctors and trainee doctors. In the second, they were delivered by midwives.

Women were admitted to one clinic or the other on alternate days, so the patients were much the same. The results were not. In the years before Semmelweis arrived, roughly one woman in ten in the doctors' clinic died of childbed fever, an infection that struck soon after the birth. In the midwives' clinic the figure was well under half that. The difference was known across the city, and women begged to be sent to the second clinic.

Semmelweis set out to find the reason. The usual explanations were bad air and overcrowding, but the two clinics shared the same air, and the midwives' clinic was the more crowded of the two. He changed one detail of the routine after another, and the deaths continued.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Who delivered the babies in each of the two clinics?

2. How was it decided which clinic a woman went to?

3. Why did he reject bad air and overcrowding as explanations?

1. Doctors and trainee doctors in the first clinic; midwives in the second.

2. By the day she arrived: the clinics admitted patients on alternate days.

3. Both clinics shared the same air, and the safer clinic was in fact the more crowded one.

Discuss:
  • Everyone in the hospital knew the two figures. Why do you think nobody before him had treated the difference as a question that needed an answer?

Part 2 — A friend's death

In March 1847 a colleague and friend of his, Jakob Kolletschka, a professor of forensic medicine, died. During an autopsy a trainee's knife had accidentally cut his finger, and within days he was dead. When Semmelweis read the report on his friend's body, he recognised what it described: the same signs he had seen in the women who died of childbed fever.

That gave him his connection. Doctors and trainees in the first clinic often came straight from dissecting dead bodies to examining women in labour. Midwives did no dissection. He concluded that something from the bodies, which he called 'cadaverous particles', was being carried into the ward on the doctors' hands. Ordinary washing with soap did not remove the smell of the dissecting room, so he assumed that it did not remove the particles either.

In May 1847 he ordered everyone to wash their hands in a solution of chlorinated lime before examining a patient. In April, about eighteen women in every hundred in the first clinic had died. Over the following months the figure fell to about two in a hundred, and in the year after that it was close to the figure for the midwives' clinic.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Kolletschka come to die?

2. What did the doctors and trainees do that the midwives did not?

3. What happened to the death rate after the new rule was introduced?

1. His finger was cut by accident during an autopsy, and he died within days.

2. They dissected dead bodies, and often went straight from there to examine women in labour.

3. It fell from about eighteen in a hundred in April to about two in a hundred within months.

Discuss:
  • His idea began with one death, not with a table of figures. Think of a time when a single case made you see a pattern that the numbers had not shown you.

Part 3 — Right, and not believed

The numbers were remarkable, and they did not win the argument. This was years before Louis Pasteur and others showed that germs cause disease, so Semmelweis could not say what his particles were or how they killed. Most doctors believed that a disease like this had many causes, such as bad air or the patient's own constitution. His claim also carried an accusation that was hard to hear: that doctors had been carrying death to their own patients.

He made the task harder for himself. For years he published almost nothing. The first accounts of his work were written by colleagues, and readers abroad often misunderstood what he was claiming. He left Vienna in 1850, and his own book did not appear until 1861, fourteen years after the discovery. It was long and repetitive, and when it was criticised he replied with open letters that called leading professors murderers.

In 1865 he was taken to an asylum, where he died two weeks later, aged forty-seven. In the years that followed, the work of Pasteur and of the surgeon Joseph Lister gave doctors the explanation that had been missing, and washing with antiseptic became routine. Historians still argue about how to share the blame between a profession that would not listen and a man who never properly explained. This lesson takes the second half of that question: what would he have needed to say?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why could he not explain how the particles killed?

2. When did his own book appear, and how did he answer its critics?

3. What did the work of Pasteur and Lister provide?

1. Nobody had yet shown that germs cause disease, so he had no way of saying what the particles were.

2. In 1861, fourteen years after the discovery; he replied with open letters calling leading professors murderers.

3. The explanation that had been missing: how something carried on the hands could cause the disease.

Discuss:
  • He had a claim and he had evidence. In your own words, what was missing between them, and whose job was it to supply it?

How the tool works

The doctors of Europe heard a claim (wash your hands) and a number (deaths fell). What they did not hear, clearly and early, was how the one led to the other, and without that step the number could be put down to chance. The philosopher Stephen Toulmin, writing in 1958, gave the missing step a name: the warrant, the reason that allows you to move from the evidence to the claim. In speech it is usually one clause beginning with because or which means. Speakers leave it out for an honest reason: to them it is obvious. To the listener it is the whole argument.

Using it, step by step

  1. Claim. Say what you want the listener to accept, in one sentence: Everyone should wash their hands in the solution before entering the ward.
  2. Link. Say the connection out loud, with because, the reason is that or which means: …because whatever causes the fever is carried on our hands from the dissecting room. This is the part you will be tempted to skip.
  3. Evidence. Give the one fact the link rests on: Since we started washing, deaths have fallen from about eighteen in a hundred to about two.
  4. Test it. Imagine the listener asking How does that follow? If they could repeat your claim and your number but not your reasoning, the link is still in your head. Put it into words.

Try it yourself

These sentences come from three short arguments. What job does each one do? Choose, then check.

1. We should move the weekly meeting to Monday.
2. Deaths in the first clinic fell from about eighteen in a hundred to about two.
3. Because whatever causes the fever is carried on our hands from the dissecting room.
4. I think everybody here can see what I am getting at.
5. Which means the delay comes from the approval step, not from the team.
6. The second design is the one we should build.
7. Four of the last five projects waited more than two weeks for a signature.
8. The figures speak for themselves.
9. The reason is that customers who wait more than a minute rarely call back.
10. Calls answered within a minute have doubled since we changed the rota.

1. Claim — it says what the speaker wants the listener to accept, and nothing yet about why.

2. Evidence — a fact with numbers; it does not yet say what produced the fall.

3. Link — it explains how the washing could have produced the fall.

4. Cut it — it asks the listener to do the speaker's work; say the point instead.

5. Link — which means turns a fact into a reason for the claim.

6. Claim — a position the listener is being asked to agree with.

7. Evidence — a fact the listener could check.

8. Cut it — figures never do; this sentence puts a hope where the link should be.

9. Link — the reason is that announces the connection directly.

10. Evidence — a measured change, with a number and a starting point.

Where the tool breaks

You do not have the link yet. This was Semmelweis's real difficulty: he had a pattern and only half an explanation. Say so, and ask for something small: I can't yet tell you how it works. What I can tell you is what happened when we tried it, and I'd like one more ward to test it.

The link is weak, and because hides it. The word is persuasive even when the reason after it is empty. A well-known experiment from the 1970s found that people were far more willing to let someone go ahead of them at a photocopier when the request contained the word because, even if the reason added nothing. Do not use the word to cover a gap.

The listener already has the link. With close colleagues, spelling out every step can sound as though you doubt their intelligence. Say it once and briefly, and watch their faces: if they nod, move on.

Talk it through:
  • Which do you leave out more often when you speak: the claim, the link, or the evidence? Why that one?
  • When someone tells you that the figures speak for themselves, what do you usually find they have not said?

Language — Because and its cousins

The link is nearly always a clause of reason. English has several ways to build one, and the choice depends on what follows the linking word and on which part you want the listener to notice.

Clause or noun? because, since, as, given that are followed by a clause with a verb: Deaths fell because everyone washed their hands. because of, owing to, as a result of are followed by a noun: Deaths fell because of the new rule.
Putting the reason first: The reason (why) deaths fell is that… is followed by that, not because, in careful speech. Since and given that at the start of a sentence present the reason as something the listener already accepts: Since the midwives did no dissection, their hands carried nothing into the ward.
From the evidence to the claim: which is why…, it follows that…, that only makes sense if…. When the link is a possibility and not a certainty, say so: the most likely explanation is that…

1. Clause or noun?

Use each item from the box once: because · because of · which is why · the reason.

1. The death rate fell the new rule.

2. The death rate fell everyone washed their hands before entering the ward.

3. The midwives did no dissection, their clinic was safer.

4. so few people believed him is that he could not explain how it worked.

1. because of — a noun phrase follows (the new rule).

2. because — a full clause follows (everyone washed…).

3. which is why — it moves from the fact to what the fact explains.

4. The reason — followed later in the sentence by is that.

2. Choose the link

Choose the word or phrase that fits the grammar and the strength of the evidence.

1. The reason sales fell is (because / that) we raised our prices in March.

2. (Given that / Owing to) three of our four suppliers are late, we should expect delays.

3. The launch was delayed (since / owing to) a fault in the software.

4. Complaints fell in the first month after the change, (which proves / which suggests) that the change helped.

5. (Since / Because of) the two groups were the same in every other way, the difference must come from the treatment.

1. that — after the reason… is, careful speakers use that.

2. Given that — a clause follows; owing to needs a noun.

3. owing to — a noun phrase follows (a fault in the software).

4. which suggests — one month of figures is not enough to prove anything.

5. Since — a clause follows, and the reason is one the listener already accepts.

The claims

Work and projects

You are asking a manager to agree to something.

  1. We should close the Saturday shift. Only six customers came in last Saturday.
  2. We should hire two more people. Overtime has doubled this year.
  3. The deadline needs to move. Two of the five testers are away until the end of the month.
  4. We should stop the Friday report. Nobody has opened it since March.
  5. The new training is working. Errors have halved since January.

Customers and sales

The listener controls the budget.

  1. We need a second supplier. Three of the last five deliveries were late.
  2. We should answer every call within a minute. Half the callers who wait longer hang up.
  3. The price rise was a mistake. Orders from small customers fell by a third.
  4. We should keep the old packaging. Returns doubled when we changed it.
  5. The website needs a simpler order form. Four in ten people leave on the second page.

Health and science

The listener is intelligent and not a specialist.

  1. Hospital visitors should clean their hands at the door. Most infections are carried by touch.
  2. The medicine should be taken with food. One patient in five felt sick during the trial.
  3. Teenagers should start school later. Their body clock runs about two hours behind an adult's.
  4. The trial should be repeated. It was carried out on only forty people.
  5. The ward needs more night staff. Most falls happen between midnight and six.

Cities and society

You are speaking at a public meeting.

  1. The city should build more cycle lanes. A third of car journeys are under three kilometres.
  2. The library should stay open in the evening. Most of its members work during the day.
  3. The speed limit near the school should be lower. The road is crossed by about four hundred children a day.
  4. The bus should run every ten minutes. Passenger numbers doubled on the route where that was tried.
  5. The old market should be repaired, not replaced. It brings in more visitors than the shopping centre.

Everyday life

You are persuading a friend.

  1. You should back up your files tonight. That laptop is six years old.
  2. We should leave an hour earlier. There are roadworks on the motorway.
  3. You should read the contract again. It is eleven pages long and you signed it in five minutes.
  4. We should book now. There are only two rooms left at that price.
  5. You should see a doctor. The cough has lasted a month.

How it sounds

Six examples of the shape. Try the question first, then open the answer.

We should close the Saturday shift. Only six customers came in last Saturday.

Claim: I think we should close the Saturday shift.

Link: The reason is that it costs more to open than it brings in: we need three people on the floor however few customers come.

Evidence: Last Saturday we served six customers.

The new training is working. Errors have halved since January.

Claim: The new training is working.

Link: I say that because the training is the only thing we changed in January. The staff, the system and the workload are the same as before.

Evidence: And since January, errors have halved.

We need a second supplier. Three of the last five deliveries were late.

Claim: We need a second supplier.

Link: Because every late delivery stops the production line, and with one supplier we have no way round it.

Evidence: Three of the last five deliveries were late.

The city should build more cycle lanes. A third of car journeys are under three kilometres.

Claim: The city should build more cycle lanes.

Link: Because a journey that short is one most people could make by bicycle, if they felt safe doing it.

Evidence: About a third of the car journeys in this city are under three kilometres.

The trial should be repeated. It was carried out on only forty people.

Claim: I think the trial should be repeated.

Link: The reason is that with so small a group, two or three unusual patients are enough to change the result.

Evidence: It was carried out on only forty people.

You should back up your files tonight. That laptop is six years old.

Claim: You should back up your files tonight.

Link: Because a machine of that age can fail without warning, which means that anything kept only on it could be gone tomorrow.

Evidence: That laptop is six years old.

Make it harder

  • Evidence first: say it in the opposite order: the fact, then which means…, then the claim. Which order would you choose for a doubtful listener?
  • Interrupted: your partner says How does that follow? straight after your claim. Answer at once with the link, in one clause.
  • A second link: give a different reason that connects the same fact to the same claim. Which of the two would this listener accept more easily?
  • The weak point: say what would have to be true for your link to be wrong, and how you would check.
  • Add the so-what: finish with what should happen next: So I recommend that we…
