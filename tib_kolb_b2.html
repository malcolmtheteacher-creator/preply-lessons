The Doers and the Watchers

An American psychologist argued that we learn by going round a circle — doing, reflecting, thinking and trying again. Then his idea of “learning styles” took on a life of its own.

Part 1 — We learn by doing

The idea that experience teaches is very old. More than two thousand years ago Aristotle wrote (in one English translation): “for the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them.” But doing on its own is not enough — plenty of people repeat the same mistake for years.

In the twentieth century, several thinkers asked what turns experience into learning. The American philosopher John Dewey wrote that “successive portions of reflective thought grow out of one another and support one another.” The social psychologist Kurt Lewin and the psychologist Jean Piaget, who studied how children's thinking develops, also explored how experience shapes the way we think.

David Kolb, born in 1939 in Moline, Illinois, earned a PhD in social psychology at Harvard in 1967. In the early 1970s, working with Ron Fry, he drew on Dewey, Lewin and Piaget to build a model of learning as a cycle. His 1984 book, Experiential Learning, defined learning as “the process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience.”

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Aristotle say about learning?

2. Which three thinkers did Kolb draw on?

3. How did Kolb define learning?

1. That we learn the things we need to do by doing them.

2. John Dewey, Kurt Lewin and Jean Piaget.

3. “The process whereby knowledge is created through the transformation of experience.”

Part 2 — Round the circle

Kolb's cycle has four stages. You have a concrete experience. You look back and think about it — reflective observation. You draw a general idea from it — abstract conceptualisation. And you test that idea — active experimentation, which gives you a new experience. Then round you go again.

Kolb also noticed that people tend to prefer some stages to others. Some of us, he and Alice Kolb wrote, carefully watch and reflect, “while others choose to jump right in and start doing things. The watchers favor reflective observation, while the doers favor active experimentation.”

In 1971 he had created the Learning Style Inventory, a questionnaire that placed people in one of four learning styles: Diverging, Assimilating, Converging and Accommodating. It became very widely used, especially in management and business education.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What are the four stages of the cycle?

2. What is the difference between the watchers and the doers?

3. What did the Learning Style Inventory do?

1. Concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualisation, active experimentation.

2. Watchers prefer to observe and reflect; doers prefer to jump in and try things.

3. It placed people in one of four learning styles.

Discuss:
  • Are you more of a watcher or a doer? Does it depend on the situation?

Part 3 — The trouble with styles

The cycle itself is used on many training courses: people are asked to look back at an experience before deciding what to do differently. One well-known set of questions for doing this, from Jacobson and Ruddy, is simply: Did you notice? Why did that happen? Does that happen in life? Why does that happen? How can you use that?

Learning styles are a different matter. The idea that each person has a style, and learns best when taught in that style, became enormously popular. But in 2008 a team of psychologists led by Harold Pashler reviewed the research on learning styles in general. They found “no adequate evidence base to justify incorporating learning-styles assessments into general educational practice.”

Still, the circle and the labels are two different ideas. You can doubt the labels and still find the circle useful: an experience you never think about may teach you very little. The problems start when a description of how learning moves becomes a label stuck on a person.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Give two of Jacobson and Ruddy's questions.

2. What did Pashler's team conclude in 2008?

3. What is the difference between the cycle and the labels?

1. Any two: Did you notice? Why did that happen? Does that happen in life? Why does that happen? How can you use that?

2. That there was no adequate evidence to justify using learning-styles tests in general education.

3. The cycle describes how learning moves; the labels put people into fixed types.

Discuss:

How the tool works

Four stages in a circle. You can start anywhere, but you only really learn when you go all the way round.

Using it, step by step

  1. Experience: choose one real event — a presentation, a difficult conversation, a meal you cooked.
  2. Reflect: what happened, exactly? What did you notice and feel? What did others do?
  3. Conceptualise: what general idea explains it? What would you advise someone else to do?
  4. Experiment: plan one thing to do differently next time — then do it, and the circle starts again.

Try it yourself

Mia gave a presentation at work that went badly. Which stage of Kolb's cycle is each thought or action?

1. “People only started listening when I told a story about a real customer.”
2. Mia presents to her team; halfway through, people start looking at their phones.
3. “Audiences remember stories better than lists of numbers.”
4. At the next meeting, she opens with a customer story and uses only three slides.
5. “I noticed I was reading from the slides most of the time.”
6. “A good presentation should be built around one example, not around data.”

1. Reflection — looking back at what happened and noticing.

2. Experience — the event itself.

3. Conceptualisation — a general idea drawn from the experience.

4. Experimentation — testing the idea in practice.

5. Reflection — observing her own behaviour afterwards.

6. Conceptualisation — a general rule — abstract thinking.

Where the tool breaks

Styles aren't destiny. The evidence doesn't support teaching people only in their “style”.

Reflection takes time. In a busy job, the circle often stops straight after the experience.

Some things shouldn't be learnt by trial and error. Nobody wants a surgeon or a pilot to learn mainly by doing.

Grammar — past simple and past continuous

Kolb's cycle starts with telling the story of an experience. To tell it well, you need two past tenses: one for the main events and one for the background.

Past simple — finished actions, often one after another: the main events of the story.
“I stood up, opened my laptop and started the presentation.”
Past continuous (was/were + -ing) — an action in progress at a moment in the past: the background.
“At ten o'clock I was waiting outside the room.”
Together: a longer action interrupted by a shorter one — when + past simple, while/as + past continuous.
“I was speaking when the fire alarm went off.”  “While I was talking, two people left.”
Two actions in progress at the same time — both continuous.
“I was talking, but nobody was listening.”
Watch out: state verbs (know, want, believe, understand) don't usually go in the continuous: ~~I was knowing the answer~~ → “I knew the answer.”
