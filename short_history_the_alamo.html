The Alamo

Thirteen days, a crumbling mission-fort, and a stand that turned a defeat into a rallying cry.

Today: you'll tell the story of a thirteen-day siege in Texas in 1836 — a small band of defenders, an overwhelming army, and three words that outlived them all — and you'll master reported speech, the tool English uses to pass on what people said, promised and demanded.
Warm up — talk before we read.
  • Have you ever heard someone say "Remember the Alamo"? What do you think happened there, and why might three words outlive the men who inspired them?
  • Sometimes a side loses the battle but wins the story. What turns a military defeat into a symbol people rally around for generations?
  • Picture a walled mission in 1836 Texas: cut off, short of water, a large army camped outside. What would the days of waiting be like for the people inside?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten new words are waiting in four short tasks. Do each one out loud first, then reveal to check. Don't skip the thinking — that's where the word sticks.

A. Work it out from the situation

In each sentence the situation points at the bold word. Say what you think it means, then reveal.

1. For thirteen days the army sat outside the walls. Nothing got in — no food, no water, no help — and nobody could get out. This was a siege.
a siege (n) — a military operation in which a force surrounds a place to cut it off and force it to give in.
2. Hour after hour the guns pounded the walls. The noise never stopped; the men inside could not sleep, and the stone began to crack under the endless bombardment.
a bombardment (n) — a continuous, heavy attack with guns or artillery.
3. On one side stood a few hundred men. On the other stood an army many, many times their size. The defenders were badly outnumbered.
outnumbered (adj) — having far fewer people or forces than the other side.

B. Which word fits the meaning?

Read each meaning and recall the word. The first letter is there to help. Say it, then reveal.

4. A settlement built by religious workers, usually around a church, to spread their faith — the kind of place the fort had once been. Starts with m___.
a mission (n) — a settlement built by religious workers, often with a church. Example: the old mission's thick walls made it a natural fort.
5. A large, heavy gun that fires balls or shells, often mounted on a wall or on wheels. Starts with c___.
a cannon (n) — a large, heavy gun that fires balls or shells. Example: the defenders aimed their cannon down at the advancing troops.
6. A sudden, violent attack, usually launched all at once by a large force. Starts with a___.
an assault (n) — a sudden, violent attack, usually launched all at once. Example: before dawn the soldiers rushed the walls in one final assault.

C. Odd one out & near-meanings

7. Three of these are ways of giving up or being spared; one is the opposite. Which is the odd one out?   surrender · mercy · assault · quarter
assault is the odd one out — it means attacking, not giving up. The other three are all about stopping the fight or being spared. And quarter (n) here means mercy shown to a beaten enemy — so "no quarter" means the losers will not be spared.
8. Their commander sent out a letter vowing he would never give up the fort. Which everyday word is closest in meaning — guessing, promising, or asking?
Closest is promising. to vow (v) — to make a solemn, serious promise. It is stronger and more formal than an ordinary promise.

D. Complete the phrase

Finish each chunk, then reveal the word and why it belongs there.

9. The men lost the battle, but their story lived on for generations. Complete the phrase: they became a l___ — a famous story from the past, part true and part retold.
a legend (n) — a famous story from the past, often part true and part romanticised. Word family: the events became legendary (adj).
10. Complete the phrase: "Remember the Alamo!" became a fierce rallying ___ — the shout that pulled soldiers together for the fight to come.
a rallying cry (n) — a phrase or slogan that unites people and stirs them to act. The word rally means to bring people together; a rallying cry is the call that does it.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A fort on the edge of a revolution

In the 1830s, Texas was a northern province of Mexico, but it had filled up with settlers from the United States who chafed under the government far to the south. When the Mexican president, General Antonio López de Santa Anna, tore up the constitution and gathered power into his own hands, the settlers — and many Texans of Mexican descent — rose in rebellion. They wanted self-rule, and some already wanted independence. This was the Texas Revolution.

In the town of San Antonio de Béxar stood an old Spanish mission called the Alamo: a walled compound of a chapel and low buildings, never designed as a fortress, but ringed by thick stone walls. Late in 1835, Texan rebels took it. As 1836 opened, a small garrison held the crumbling fort, low on men and supplies. Two very different figures shared the command — William Barret Travis, a young and ambitious lawyer-turned-officer, and Jim Bowie, a famous, hard-living frontiersman whose name was already attached to a large knife.

On 23 February 1836, Santa Anna arrived at the head of an army far larger than anything the defenders could match. He raised a blood-red flag over the town: no quarter. The message was blunt — surrender, and you die anyway. The siege had begun.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why were the settlers and Texans in rebellion against Santa Anna?

2. What was the Alamo originally, and who shared command of its garrison?

1. Santa Anna had torn up the constitution and seized power; the settlers and many Texans wanted self-rule or independence, sparking the Texas Revolution.

2. An old Spanish mission being used as a fort; command was shared by William Travis and Jim Bowie.

Discuss:
  • A young lawyer and a famous frontiersman were asked to share command of the same fort. What problems can arise when two strong leaders have to work side by side?

Part 2 — Thirteen days

For nearly two weeks, Santa Anna's guns pounded the walls while his lines crept closer. Inside, perhaps around two hundred defenders held on. Bowie fell seriously ill early in the siege and was confined to a cot, which left Travis effectively in sole command. From that fort Travis wrote one of the most famous letters in American history, addressed "To the People of Texas and All Americans in the World." In it he refused to surrender or retreat, promised to fight to the end, and signed off with the words "Victory or Death." He begged, again and again, for reinforcements.

Only a small group answered the call, slipping through the enemy lines to join a fight almost everyone knew was hopeless. Among the defenders was one of the most famous men in America: David "Davy" Crockett, a former congressman from Tennessee, frontier celebrity and crack shot, who had come to Texas looking for a new life and walked instead into a legend.

A well-loved story — impossible now to prove — says that in the final days Travis gathered the men, told them plainly that no help was coming and that to stay meant almost certain death, and drew a line in the sand with his sword, inviting all who would stay and fight to step across it. Whether or not it truly happened, it captures something real: the men inside chose to hold a fort they were almost certain to lose.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Travis say in his famous letter from the Alamo, and how did he sign it?

2. Who was Davy Crockett, and what is the "line in the sand" story?

1. He refused to surrender or retreat, vowed to fight to the end and begged for reinforcements; he signed it "Victory or Death."

2. A former Tennessee congressman and famous frontiersman who joined the defenders. The (unproven) legend says Travis drew a line and invited the men to step across it to stay and fight.

Discuss:
  • Some of the "line in the sand" story may be legend rather than fact. Does it matter whether a famous historical moment happened exactly as told?

Part 3 — The fall, and the echo

Before dawn on 6 March 1836, Santa Anna ordered the final assault. Thousands of soldiers advanced on the walls in the dark. The first waves were driven back with heavy losses, but the defenders were too few to hold every point, and at last the attackers poured over the walls and into the compound. The fighting was close and brief. By sunrise the battle was over, and nearly all of the roughly two hundred defenders — Travis, Bowie, Crockett among them — were dead. It was a total defeat.

Santa Anna spared a handful of survivors, most famously Susanna Dickinson, the wife of a defender, and her infant daughter. He sent her away to carry the news across Texas, meaning the story of the Alamo's fall to spread fear and break the rebellion's will. It did the opposite. Word of the slaughter, and of the "no quarter" order, hardened Texan resolve rather than shattering it.

Six weeks later, on 21 April 1836, the Texan general Sam Houston caught Santa Anna's army resting near the San Jacinto River. His soldiers charged, some of them shouting "Remember the Alamo!", and overran the camp in less than twenty minutes. Santa Anna himself was captured, and Texas won its independence. The men of the Alamo had lost their battle — but their thirteen days became a rallying cry, and their defeat helped win the war.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the outcome of the final assault on 6 March 1836?

2. How did the Alamo, though a defeat, help the Texans win their independence?

1. Santa Anna's army overran the fort and nearly all the defenders, including Travis, Bowie and Crockett, were killed — a complete defeat.

2. News of the fall and the "no quarter" order hardened Texan resolve; six weeks later Houston's men, shouting "Remember the Alamo!", beat Santa Anna at San Jacinto and won independence.

Discuss:
  • Santa Anna sent a survivor away to spread fear, and it backfired completely. Why do stories of defeat sometimes inspire people more than stories of victory?

Grammar — Reported speech

So much of the Alamo survives as words: Travis's letter, Santa Anna's demand, the (perhaps invented) speech at the line in the sand. When you retell what someone said, you'll usually pass it on in reported speech rather than quoting it directly — and that means shifting tenses, pronouns and time words back a step.

The core shift — one step back in time:
Direct: Travis said, "I will never surrender." → Reported: Travis said (that) he would never surrender.
• present → past ("I am here" → he said he was there)
• past → past perfect ("we lost" → he said they had lost)
• will → would, can → could, may → might, must → had to
Pronouns and time/place words shift too:
• I / we → he / she / they; my → his / her / their
• now → then; today → that day; here → there; tomorrow → the next day
Reporting verbs beyond "say": for questions use ask (with if/whether or a question word, and normal word order): "Are they coming?" → He asked whether they were coming. For orders use tell / order + object + to: "Hold the fort!" → He ordered them to hold the fort. Also: promise to, refuse to, warn that, vow that.

1. Report it — shift the tense

Rewrite the reported sentence by filling the gap. Watch the tense, the pronoun and any time words.

1. Travis said, "I will never surrender this fort." → Travis said that he never surrender the fort.

2. Santa Anna announced, "The defenders must give up now." → Santa Anna announced that the defenders give up then.

3. Crockett told them, "We are running out of ammunition." → Crockett told them that they running out of ammunition.

1. would (will → would)

2. had to (must → had to; and "now" → "then")

3. were (present "are" → past "were")

2. Choose the reporting verb and form

Report each line using the verb in brackets. Write the missing words.

1. "Is help coming?" the men wondered. (ask) → The men asked help was coming.

2. "Hold the walls!" Travis said. (order) → Travis ordered the men the walls.

3. "I promise I will send reinforcements," a commander wrote. (promise) → A commander promised reinforcements.

1. if / whether (reported yes/no question)

2. to hold (order + object + to + base verb)

3. to send (promise + to + base verb)

3. Retell the moment — complete the report

Fill each gap so the paragraph reports the speech correctly (tense, pronoun and time words all shift). Read it aloud once you're done.

Travis wrote that he never surrender or retreat, and he begged for reinforcements. He warned that no help arrived yet. He told the men that they to decide for themselves whether to stay.

would (will → would) · had (present perfect "has arrived" → past perfect "had arrived") · had (must / have → had to; here "had to decide")

Notice the pattern: every verb steps one tense back, and "I/we" becomes "he/they."

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The Alamo was a crushing defeat that became a source of pride. How does a nation decide which of its defeats to remember and which to forget?
  2. Much of what we "know" about the Alamo — the line in the sand, Crockett's last stand — mixes fact with legend. Does that make the story less valuable, or more powerful?
