The Hedonic Treadmill

B2/C1 Level · Why winning the lottery rarely makes anyone happier

The strangest finding in happiness research

Win the lottery, the research suggests, and roughly a year later you will be about as happy as you were before. Suffer a serious accident, and roughly a year later you will, on average, also be about as happy as you were before. Of all the strange things psychology has discovered about human happiness, this may be the strangest: we adapt to almost everything — faster than we expect, more thoroughly than we want, and to outcomes we would have sworn would mark us forever.

Think about this:

1. What is one thing — a possession, a job, a relationship status — that you were absolutely sure would change your life if you ever got it? Did it?

2. Are there parts of your current life that you suspect, honestly, you've stopped really noticing — that you're now taking for granted?

3. Why might it be a useful piece of self-knowledge to know that almost any improvement, however dramatic, will fade?

Quick prediction

Below are five life changes. Which two does the research suggest produce the longest-lasting rise in everyday happiness, and why?

a) a substantial pay rise   b) moving to a beautiful new home   c) building a closer friendship   d) acquiring a long-wanted luxury item   e) developing a regular exercise habit

Show Answer
(c) building a closer friendship and (e) a regular exercise habit produce the longest-lasting effects, because both involve ongoing, slightly variable experiences rather than a single static gain. (a), (b) and (d) produce a real, often substantial spike — that fades, sometimes alarmingly fast, as the new state becomes the new normal. The treadmill is hardest on improvements that don't keep generating new moments of their own.

Reading: The Most Disquieting Finding in Happiness Research

In 1978, the American psychologist Philip Brickman and his colleagues published a study with a memorable result. They had compared the reported happiness of two unusual groups: people who had recently won large lottery prizes, and people who had recently been paralysed in serious accidents. A year on, the two groups were nowhere near as different as anyone had expected. Both had drifted back, slowly but unmistakably, toward a baseline level of happiness not very different from what they had reported beforehand.

Phrasing · read this paragraph aloud and break it into natural thought groups. Pause briefly at each comma; the long sentences are designed to be chunked.

Brickman coined a phrase to describe what was happening: the hedonic treadmill. We keep walking, hard, in the direction of more — better salary, bigger house, smarter phone, slightly different partner — and the scenery does change. Our happiness, however, tends to stay roughly where it was. The mechanism behind this is now well documented: hedonic adaptation, the brain's quiet, almost automatic recalibration to whatever level it currently has. The brain is good at noticing change. It is bad at continuing to notice change once that change has become the new normal.

Sentence stress · read aloud and underline the three or four content words in each sentence that take the heaviest stress. Function words (the, of, with, are) should slip past almost unnoticed.

The treadmill is propelled by two engines, both of which run almost entirely outside our awareness. The first is comparison: the more comparatively well-off we become, the more we tend to compare ourselves with people doing slightly better still. The second is expectation: the more we have, the more we expect, and the smaller the surprise — and therefore the joy — produced by each new improvement. Together, these two produce the famous law of diminishing returns. The hundredth pound of income lifts the mood far less than the first.

Linking · notice how, in fluent speech, words bleed into each other — "compared_with", "lifts_the mood", "slightly_better still". Practise the linking; don't separate every word.

The most painful version of the trap is what the writer Pamela Druckerman calls a moving target: the conviction that once we get the next thing, we'll be settled. The promotion, the house, the relationship, the qualification — all delivering, when they arrive, a brief and genuine spike of joy that quickly proves to be a vanishing pleasure. The target moves on. We aim for the next one. We take for granted what we have, almost without realising we are doing it, while pursuing what we do not. Whole decades can be spent this way without anyone in particular noticing.

Intonation · this paragraph turns on a contrast — the spike of arrival, set against the fade. Listen for the falling intonation on each side of the pivot, with a small rise on the contrast word itself.

The implication is not, as it first sounds, depressing. The same research has begun to map what does seem to last. Strong relationships. Variable, ongoing experiences. Habits of savouring — deliberately noticing what is already in our lives. Acts of gratitude, performed not to feel virtuous but to interrupt the slow fade. None of these escape adaptation entirely. But they do appear to slow the treadmill enough that a person can, with a little attention, feel themselves actually moving forwards rather than running on the spot.

Shadow reading · try reading this paragraph aloud half a beat behind your teacher, matching pace, stress and intonation. The aim is rhythm, not perfection — lock onto the music of the sentence.

1. What did Brickman's 1978 study compare, and what was unexpected about the result?

Show Answer
It compared the reported happiness of recent lottery winners with that of people recently paralysed in serious accidents. A year on, the two groups were nowhere near as different as expected — both had drifted back to roughly their previous baseline level of happiness. The dramatic life event had had a much smaller long-term effect than common sense would predict.

2. According to the passage, what are the two engines that propel the hedonic treadmill?

Show Answer
Comparison and expectation. The more comparatively well-off we become, the more we tend to measure ourselves against people doing slightly better still. And the more we have, the more we expect, so each new improvement produces a smaller surprise. Together they generate the law of diminishing returns: each new unit of "more" delivers less happiness than the last.

3. What does the writer mean by "a moving target"?

Show Answer
The conviction that, once we get the next specific thing — promotion, house, relationship, qualification — we will finally be settled. Each target produces a brief, genuine spike of joy on arrival, then fades; the target moves on, and the chase resumes. Whole decades can be spent this way without anyone really noticing.

4. According to the writer, what kinds of things do seem to last against adaptation?

Show Answer
Strong relationships, variable ongoing experiences, deliberate habits of savouring (noticing what is already in our lives), and acts of gratitude performed to interrupt the slow fade. None escape adaptation entirely, but they appear to slow the treadmill enough that a person can feel they are actually moving forwards.

5. Inference: why does the writer call the implication "not, as it first sounds, depressing"?

Show Answer
Because once you understand that adaptation is doing most of the work, you stop chasing one-off arrivals and start choosing differently. Investing in relationships, savouring what's already there, deliberately noticing — all are within reach of almost everyone. The treadmill is real, but recognising it gives you, for the first time, the option of stepping off it.

Comparatives · the more …, the more …

The treadmill is a topic about comparison: our state against our past state, our state against other people's, our state against our expectations. The grammar of comparison is the natural home of this lesson. Three structures do most of the work.

Standard comparatives

-er than   /   more X than   /   less X than

The new house is quieter than the old one.

He is far less satisfied than he expected to be.

(not) as X as

Equality, or the lack of it.

The pay rise wasn't as life-changing as she'd imagined.

She was twice as happy as her colleague at the start. A year later, no different at all.

The more …, the more …

Linked change: the engine of the treadmill itself.

The more we have, the more we expect.

The richer he became, the less satisfied he felt.

Gap-fill 1: comparatives

1. The new flat is ____________ (much / quiet) than the old one, but I'm only marginally happier.

Show Answer
much quieter

2. Lottery winners aren't ____________ (anywhere / near / happy) we'd expect, a year on.

Show Answer
anywhere near as happy as — "(not) as X as" with intensifier.

3. Their second house was ____________ (twice / large) the first, and they barely noticed within six months.

Show Answer
twice as large as — multiple "as X as".

4. The pay rise was ____________ (far / dramatic) than she'd hoped, but the joy faded ____________ (much / fast) than she'd anticipated.

Show Answer
far less dramatic   much faster

Gap-fill 2: the more …, the more …

5. ____________ (more / earn / he), ____________ (less / satisfied / feel / he).

Show Answer
The more he earned, the less satisfied he felt.

6. ____________ (long / wait / we), ____________ (small / pleasure / it / produce / when arrives).

Show Answer
The longer we wait, the smaller the pleasure it produces when it arrives.

7. ____________ (closely / compare / she / herself / colleagues), ____________ (less / content / become / she).

Show Answer
The more closely she compared herself with her colleagues, the less content she became.

Gap-fill 3: rewrite

8. "The fact that we have more makes us expect more." Rewrite using the more…, the more….

Show Answer
The more we have, the more we expect.

9. "The flat is much smaller than the last one, but I'm not in any meaningful sense less happy." Rewrite to compress the contrast.

Show Answer
The flat is much smaller, yet I'm no less happy than before.
