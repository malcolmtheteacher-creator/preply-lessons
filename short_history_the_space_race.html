1. On the launch pad, engineers made their final checks on the booster, a slender tower of metal packed with fuel and built to throw its cargo far beyond the clouds.
a booster (n) — the powerful rocket stage whose burning fuel produces enough thrust to lift a spacecraft off the ground and out of the atmosphere.
2. The little metal satellite circled the planet once every ninety minutes, its radio beeping steadily as it passed over one country after another.
a satellite (n) — an object placed in orbit around the Earth (or another body) to circle it.
3. Once the engines cut out, the capsule settled into a stable orbit, tracing the same great loop around the Earth again and again.
an orbit (n) — the curved path an object follows as it travels around a planet, star or moon.
4. The first cosmonaut was strapped into a tiny sphere on top of a converted missile, with almost no control over the machine that would carry him.
a cosmonaut (n) — a space traveller trained in the Soviet (later Russian) programme; the American term is astronaut.
5. Each new ascent was broadcast live to the world: the countdown, the roar of ignition, and then the launch pad shrinking away beneath a pillar of flame.
an ascent (n) — an upward climb or journey, especially a steep or demanding one.
6. It was less a scientific project than a national prestige exercise — a way for one side to prove to the whole planet that its system was superior.
prestige (n) — the respect and admiration a nation or person earns from great achievement or status.
7. Both sides poured money into the arms race, each stockpiling more missiles than the other in a contest that never seemed to end.
an arms race (n) — a competition between rivals to build up ever larger or more advanced weapons.
8. The mission's legacy outlasted the men who flew it: the technology, the images and the sheer ambition still shape how we explore space today.
a legacy (n) — what remains from the past; the lasting effect of an event or achievement.
9. It was a stunning feat of engineering — sending a fragile human being a quarter of a million miles and bringing him safely home.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A beep from the sky

After the Second World War, two former allies became rivals. The United States and the Soviet Union each believed its way of life — capitalism or communism — was destined to win the future, and neither could risk letting the other look stronger. This was the Cold War: no direct fighting between the two superpowers, but a decades-long struggle for prestige, waged through propaganda, spies and an ever-growing arms race. Both sides had captured German rocket scientists at the end of the war, and both were building ever larger missiles. The same rockets that could carry a warhead across an ocean could, in theory, carry something else — a machine, or a man — into space.

On 4 October 1957, the Soviets struck first. They launched Sputnik, a polished metal ball the size of a beach ball, into orbit around the Earth. It did almost nothing except circle the planet and beep — but that beep, picked up on radios everywhere, was heard as a warning. If the Soviets could put a satellite over America, many feared, they could one day drop something far more dangerous. A month later they launched Sputnik 2, carrying a dog named Laika. She was the first living creature to orbit the Earth, though the technology to bring her home did not yet exist, and she did not survive the flight.

In the United States the shock was profound. The Americans had assumed they were going to win any technological contest easily; now they were the ones playing catch-up. Their first attempt to answer Sputnik ended in humiliation when a rocket rose a metre off the pad and exploded on live television. The government poured money into science education and, in 1958, created a new agency, NASA, to fight the contest properly. The race was on — and, for now, the Soviets were ahead.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the same technology matter for both weapons and space travel?

2. What were Sputnik and Sputnik 2, and why did they alarm the United States?

1. The rockets powerful enough to launch a satellite were the same kind that could carry a warhead across the world — so success in space signalled military strength.

2. Sputnik was the first artificial satellite; Sputnik 2 carried the dog Laika, the first creature to orbit Earth. They showed the Soviets were ahead and could put objects over America, which frightened people.

Discuss:
  • A small beeping ball caused near-panic in the world's richest country. Why can a symbol frighten people more than the thing it actually is?

Part 2 — The first human in space

The Soviets pressed their advantage. On 12 April 1961, a 27-year-old pilot named Yuri Gagarin climbed into a small spherical capsule on top of a rocket, said the single word "Poyekhali!" — "Let's go!" — and became the first human being to leave the Earth. He orbited the planet once, in a flight lasting just 108 minutes, and returned a hero. Overnight he was the most famous man alive, paraded through Moscow and flown around the world as living proof that the Soviet system could do what no one had ever done. It was a propaganda triumph of the highest order.

For the Americans, this was a second humiliation in four years. Their own first astronaut, Alan Shepard, flew just three weeks later, but his flight was shorter and did not even reach orbit. Something bolder was needed — a goal so distant that being second to Sputnik and Gagarin would no longer matter. In May 1961, President John F. Kennedy stood before Congress and committed the nation to a staggering promise: to land a man on the Moon and return him safely to Earth before the decade was out. At the time, the United States had barely fifteen minutes of human spaceflight to its name.

Through the early 1960s the two sides traded firsts. The Soviets sent the first woman, Valentina Tereshkova, into space in 1963, and their cosmonaut Alexei Leonov made the first spacewalk in 1965. But these spectacular stunts hid a growing weakness. The Soviet programme depended heavily on one brilliant, secretive chief designer, Sergei Korolev, whose very name was a state secret. When he died suddenly in 1966, much of the drive behind the Soviet effort died with him — just as the Americans, through the methodical Gemini and Apollo programmes, were beginning to pull ahead.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was Yuri Gagarin and why did his flight matter so much beyond the science?

2. What was Kennedy's response, and why choose a goal as distant as the Moon?

1. Gagarin was the first human in space (April 1961). His single orbit was a huge propaganda victory, presented as proof that the Soviet system could achieve what no one else had.

2. Kennedy committed the US to landing a man on the Moon by the end of the 1960s — a goal far enough ahead that being second at earlier stages would no longer decide the race.

Discuss:
  • Kennedy set an almost impossible target on purpose. When is it wise for a leader to promise something they cannot yet deliver?

Part 3 — The Moon, and what the race left behind

The American effort became the largest peacetime project in history, employing some 400,000 people and consuming a vast share of the national budget. It was not achieved without tragedy: in 1967, a fire swept through the Apollo 1 capsule during a ground test, killing all three astronauts inside. The Soviets suffered losses of their own. But the programmes pushed on, and by the end of the decade the Americans had reached the edge of Kennedy's promise. In December 1968, Apollo 8 carried three men around the Moon and back — the first humans to leave Earth's orbit and see our planet rise, small and blue, over another world.

Then came the moment the whole race had been building towards. In July 1969, Apollo 11 landed two men on the Moon's surface, and hundreds of millions of people on Earth watched or listened as a human being stepped onto another world. The United States had done what it had promised, with months to spare. The Soviets, whose own giant Moon rocket kept failing on the launch pad, quietly gave up the race to land a person there. The contest that Sputnik had begun was, in the eyes of the world, decided.

The Space Race left a long echo. It had been driven far more by politics and fear than by pure curiosity, yet it produced extraordinary things: satellites that now carry our phone calls and forecast our weather, materials and computing advances that reshaped daily life, and the first photographs of the whole Earth, which helped launch the modern environmental movement. It cost enormous sums, and some lives. And it left a question we are still asking today, as new nations and private companies reach for space: should we go there to compete — or to explore together?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Apollo 8 and Apollo 11 each achieve, and how did the Soviets respond?

2. Name two lasting effects — the "long echo" — the Space Race left behind.

1. Apollo 8 was the first crewed flight around the Moon (1968); Apollo 11 landed people on the Moon (1969). With their own Moon rocket failing, the Soviets gave up trying to land a cosmonaut there.

2. Any two: everyday satellites (phones, weather, navigation); advances in materials and computing; the first images of the whole Earth that fed the environmental movement — set against its huge cost and the lives lost.

Discuss:
  • The race was fuelled by rivalry, yet it gave the world tools we now share. Can competition between enemies still produce something good for everyone?

Grammar — future in the past

This whole story is told looking backwards — yet at every moment the people in it were staring forward, not knowing what would come. English has a neat set of forms for exactly this: standing in the past and pointing at a future that, for them, had not yet arrived. We call it the future in the past.

The core forms, side by side:
• was / were going to + base verb — a past plan or expectation (whether or not it happened): "The Americans thought they were going to win easily."
• would + base verb — seen from the past, this was still ahead: "That beep would soon change the world." "No one knew the race would last twelve years."
• was / were about to + base verb — on the very edge of happening: "Gagarin was about to become the first human in space."
• was / were to + base verb — a planned or destined event, often formal: "Apollo 11 was to land two men on the Moon."
Feel the difference:
• was going to = the plan or expectation as it stood back then (often used when reality turned out otherwise).
• would = simply the future, viewed from a past vantage point — great for narrating what came next.
• was about to = it was on the point of happening, any moment.
• was to = it was arranged, scheduled or, with hindsight, destined.
