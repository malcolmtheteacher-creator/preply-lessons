Encounter: Christian Approaches to War

The Big Divide

Christians have never agreed on war. Some believe that fighting can be the right thing to do in certain situations. Others believe that following Jesus means never using violence. Both sides use the Bible to support their position.

Just War Theory

This is the most widely accepted Christian teaching about when war can be morally right. It was developed by St Augustine (354–430 CE) and later refined by St Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274 CE). It sets out strict conditions that must all be met before a war can be considered "just."

Just Cause

The war must be fought for a good reason — usually self-defence or protecting innocent people from attack.

Last Resort

Every peaceful option must have been tried first. War should only happen when nothing else has worked.

Legitimate Authority

Only a proper government or ruler can declare war — not private groups or individuals.

Right Intention

The real reason for fighting must be the just cause — not hidden motives like greed, revenge, or power.

Chance of Success

There must be a reasonable chance of winning. It is wrong to send people to die in a hopeless war.

Proportionality

The good achieved by the war must outweigh the harm caused. Civilians must not be deliberately targeted.

Key point: Just War Theory influenced the modern Geneva Conventions — the international rules about how wars should be fought, including protecting civilians and treating prisoners humanely.

Christian Pacifism

Some Christians — especially Quakers, Mennonites, and some Methodists — believe that war is always wrong. They take Jesus's teachings about non-violence literally and absolutely.

"Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you." — Matthew 5:44
"Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God." — Matthew 5:9

Christian pacifists argue that if Jesus told his followers to love their enemies and turn the other cheek, then killing — even in war — goes against his teaching. Many pacifists have chosen to suffer and even die rather than use violence.

Situation Ethics

A third position, developed by Joseph Fletcher, says that moral decisions depend on the situation. A Christian might conclude that violence could be justified if it protects vulnerable people — even though killing is generally wrong. The most loving action in each situation is what matters.

Christian Peacemakers in Action

Desmond Tutu

Anglican bishop who fought apartheid in South Africa through non-violence. Led reconciliation efforts after apartheid ended, showing that forgiveness could heal a divided nation.

Martin Luther King Jr.

Baptist minister who led the American Civil Rights movement through non-violent protest. Inspired by Jesus and Gandhi, he showed that peaceful resistance could change unjust laws.

Dietrich Bonhoeffer

German pastor who believed in pacifism but concluded that plotting to assassinate Hitler was the moral choice. He was executed by the Nazis. His story shows how hard these decisions can be.

Evaluate: Which Position Is Strongest?

The Core Disagreement

Christians who support Just War Theory and Christians who are pacifists both claim to follow Jesus. They use the same Bible but reach opposite conclusions. Which position is more convincing?

Just War Theory is the strongest position

It recognises that the world is imperfect and sometimes force is needed to protect the innocent. It sets moral limits on warfare. It has influenced international law and the Geneva Conventions. Even Jesus used force when he drove money-changers from the temple.

Pacifism is the strongest position

Jesus clearly taught non-violence — "love your enemies," "turn the other cheek," "blessed are the peacemakers." Governments often claim their wars are "just" when they're really about power. Pacifists like MLK and Desmond Tutu proved that non-violence can achieve real change.

Situation Ethics offers the most realistic approach

Real life is complicated. Bonhoeffer was a pacifist who concluded that killing Hitler was the moral choice. Rigid rules — whether "always fight" or "never fight" — don't work in every situation. The most loving action depends on the circumstances.

Key Terms

Just War Theory
Christian teaching that war can be morally right if strict conditions are met, developed by Augustine and Aquinas.
Pacifism
The belief that all violence and war are morally wrong. Christian pacifists base this on Jesus's teaching about loving enemies.
Situation Ethics
The idea that moral decisions depend on the situation. The most loving action is always the right one, even if it breaks general rules.
Proportionality
A Just War condition: the good achieved by war must outweigh the harm caused. Force must not be excessive.
Conscientious Objector
Someone who refuses to fight in a war because of their moral or religious beliefs. Many Quakers were conscientious objectors.
Geneva Conventions
International rules about how wars should be fought, including protecting civilians and treating prisoners humanely. Influenced by Just War thinking.
Reconciliation
The process of restoring friendly relations after conflict. Desmond Tutu led reconciliation in South Africa after apartheid.
Non-violent Resistance
Opposing injustice through peaceful methods like protests, boycotts, and civil disobedience rather than violence.
