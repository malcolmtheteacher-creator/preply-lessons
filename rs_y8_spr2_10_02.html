Christian Teachings on Peace and War

How do Christians approach violence and peacemaking?

What You Already Know

Christianity is one of the world's largest religions, with about 2.4 billion followers spread across hundreds of denominations. Christians have interpreted their faith's teachings about peace and war in very different ways. Some have been warriors. Some have been committed pacifists. Some have developed complex philosophies about when war might be acceptable. Let's explore this complexity.

Question 1: What does it mean to be a pacifist?

🔒Think about the word!

Answer: A pacifist is someone who believes that violence and war are morally wrong, even in self-defence. They refuse to fight or participate in warfare.

Question 2: Can you think of a phrase from Jesus about responding to violence?

🔒Think about forgiveness!

Answer: "Turn the other cheek" — Jesus taught this in the Sermon on the Mount as a way of responding to insult or violence without retaliation.

Meeting the Key Christian Positions

Position 1: Just War Theory

This is perhaps the most influential Christian teaching about when war can be morally justified. It was developed by Augustine of Hippo (354-430) and refined by Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274). Just War theory says war can be morally right under certain strict conditions.

The Six Criteria for Just War:
  1. Just Cause: War must be fought for a morally good reason—usually self-defence or protecting the innocent
  2. Right Intention: The real reason must be the just cause, not hidden motives like greed or power
  3. Last Resort: War must only happen after all peaceful options have been exhausted
  4. Legitimate Authority: Only a government can declare war, not private individuals
  5. Probability of Success: There must be a reasonable chance of achieving the just cause
  6. Proportionality: The good achieved by war must outweigh the harm caused

Just War theory also includes rules about how war should be fought—it must not target civilians deliberately, and force must not be excessive. This influenced modern laws of war and the Geneva Conventions.

Position 2: Pacifism

Some Christians, particularly Quakers, Mennonites, and some Methodists, believe that war is always wrong. They emphasise Jesus's teachings about forgiveness, loving enemies, and non-violence.

Key Biblical Passages:
"Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you." (Matthew 5:44)
"Do not repay evil with evil. Do not take revenge." (Romans 12:19)
"Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called sons of God." (Matthew 5:9)

Christian pacifists argue that followers of Jesus should never kill, even in self-defence. Instead, they should trust in God and respond to violence with suffering love. Many pacifists have been willing to face persecution and death rather than fight back.

Position 3: Situation Ethics

Modern theologian Joseph Fletcher developed "situation ethics," which says moral decisions depend on context. A Christian might conclude that in some situations, violence could be justified if it protects the vulnerable—even though killing is generally wrong.

Exploring the Historical Reality

The Crusades: A Christian War?

In the 11th-13th centuries, Christian popes called crusaders to fight Muslims for control of the Holy Land. The Church preached Just War theory to justify these wars. Yet the crusades involved massacres of civilians, theft, and terrible cruelty. Some Christians at the time questioned whether these wars were truly "just."

The Reformation and Religious Wars

When the Protestant Reformation happened (16th century), Christians fought Christians. Catholic and Protestant armies killed each other in the name of faith. Both sides used Just War theory to claim their wars were just. But these wars were also about politics and power.

Christian Peacemakers

Yet Christianity also inspired remarkable peacemakers:

  • Desmond Tutu: An Anglican bishop who fought apartheid in South Africa through non-violence and later led reconciliation efforts
  • Martin Luther King Jr.: A Baptist minister who led the Civil Rights movement through non-violent resistance, inspired by Gandhi and Jesus
  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer: A German pastor who resisted Nazi evil, eventually being executed for plotting to assassinate Hitler
Bonhoeffer's Dilemma: Bonhoeffer believed in pacifism, but he concluded that sometimes taking a life to prevent a greater evil (the Holocaust) was the moral choice. This shows how even deeply Christian thinkers wrestled with these questions.

Applying Christian Teaching

Scenario 1: Defence Against Invasion

A country is invaded by an aggressive military power. Using Just War criteria, would Christians be justified in fighting back?

Just War analysis: Just Cause (self-defence)? Yes. Last Resort? Yes, they tried diplomacy. Legitimate Authority? Yes, the government declares war. Pacifist analysis: War is always wrong; trust in God instead.

Scenario 2: Humanitarian Intervention

A government is committing genocide against a minority group. Can outsiders use military force to stop it?

Just War analysis: Just Cause (protecting the innocent)? Yes. But other criteria are harder: Will military force actually stop the genocide? What will be the cost? A pacifist would say find non-military ways to help.

Question 3: What would a Christian pacifist do if attacked?

🔒Think about non-violence!

Answer: They would refuse to fight back. They might try to escape, appeal to the attacker's conscience, or accept suffering. They believe that non-violent suffering can be morally superior to killing.

Evaluating Christian Positions

Strengths of Just War Theory

  • Recognises that sometimes defending the innocent requires force
  • Sets moral limits on warfare (don't target civilians, don't use excessive force)
  • Influenced real laws of war and human rights protections
  • Takes seriously both the need for justice and the evil of war

Criticisms of Just War Theory

  • Governments often claim their wars are "just" when they're really about power or resources
  • Hard to know if conditions are truly met before war begins
  • Wars always harm civilians, whatever the theory says
  • Can make Christians too comfortable with warfare

Strengths of Pacifism

  • Stays true to Jesus's radical teaching about non-violence
  • Pacifists often do remarkable work for peace and reconciliation
  • Shows that alternatives to war are possible
  • Challenges the assumption that violence is necessary

Criticisms of Pacifism

  • What about defending the innocent when they're being attacked?
  • Could pacifism enable evil to triumph?
  • Requires extraordinary faith and courage—not everyone has it
  • In practice, pacifists depend on others to defend them militarily

Question 4: Which approach is more Biblical—pacifism or Just War?

🔒Look at the evidence!

Answer: That's what Christians genuinely disagree about. Jesus taught non-violence, but the Old Testament includes just wars. Both pacifists and Just War theorists use Scripture to support their positions.

Consolidating Your Understanding

Key Ideas

  • Christians disagree on peace and war. Just War theory and pacifism are both serious Christian positions supported by Scripture.
  • Just War theory sets moral limits. It says some wars can be just, but only under strict conditions.
  • Pacifism takes Jesus literally. Some Christians believe Jesus's teaching about non-violence is absolute.
  • History shows the tension. Christians have been both warriors and peacemakers throughout history.

Key Vocabulary

  • Just War: A war fought for a morally justified reason, using moral means
  • Pacifism: The belief that violence and war are always morally wrong
  • Non-violence: Opposing evil or injustice without using force
  • Proportionality: The balance between the good achieved and the harm caused
