Who Takes the Profit?

In 1979 a young Harvard professor with an engineering degree asked why some industries make plenty of money and others hardly any. His answer — five forces — became one of the best-known ideas in business.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — An engineer at business school

Michael Porter was born in 1947 in Ann Arbor, Michigan. He studied aerospace and mechanical engineering at Princeton, where he also played in the university golf squad, and then went to Harvard Business School for an MBA, followed by a PhD in business economics.

At Harvard he was influenced by a branch of economics called industrial organisation, which studies how industries are structured. Business strategy at the time often relied on simple tools such as SWOT analysis. Porter wanted something more rigorous.

In 1979, as a young associate professor, he published his first article in the Harvard Business Review: “How Competitive Forces Shape Strategy”. Years later he summed up the starting point: “In essence, the job of the strategist is to understand and cope with competition.” But competition, he argued, is much wider than your direct rivals.

Part 2 — Five forces

Porter argued that the profits of an industry are shaped by five forces. The first is the obvious one: rivalry between existing competitors. The other four are easy to forget: the threat of new entrants, the threat of substitutes, the bargaining power of buyers, and the bargaining power of suppliers.

When the forces are strong, profits are low. The airline industry is a classic example. Think of the pressures: many rival airlines, a few powerful aircraft makers, passengers who will switch for a cheaper fare, and alternatives such as trains. Even when planes are full, little profit is left over.

When the forces are weak, a business can do very well. The lesson for managers is not only to beat their rivals, but to find a position where the forces are weaker — or to change the forces themselves.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Rivalry, new entrants, substitutes, the power of buyers, the power of suppliers.

2. Because the forces are strong, so profitability is low.

3. Find a position where the forces are weaker — or change them.

Part 3 — Strategy in the real world

In 1983 Porter co-founded a consulting firm, Monitor Group, to put his ideas into practice. Decades later Monitor filed for bankruptcy, and in January 2013 its sale to Deloitte was approved. It is hard to miss the irony: a firm built on competitive strategy had struggled to compete.

Other thinkers added to the model. In the mid-1990s, Adam Brandenburger and Barry Nalebuff pointed out that some companies are not rivals but complementors — businesses whose products make yours more valuable. Some people call them a sixth force.

The forces also change. When Netflix was still a small company, its founders offered to sell it to Blockbuster — and, as one of them later recalled, were “laughed out of the room”. Blockbuster didn't see a substitute coming. A five forces analysis is only as good as the imagination of the person doing it.

How the tool works

Five pressures on an industry's profits. Rivalry sits in the middle; the other four push in from outside. The stronger the forces, the lower the profits.

Using it, step by step

  1. Define the industry carefully: “coffee shops in my city”, not just “food”.
  2. For each force, ask how strong it is — high, medium or low — and why.
  3. Find the strongest forces: they explain where the profit goes.
  4. Ask what you can do: find a position where the forces are weaker, or change them — for example, by making customers loyal.

Try it yourself

A small independent bookshop is analysing its industry. Which force does each point describe?

1. Many people now read e-books or listen to audiobooks instead.
2. Two other bookshops in the same street keep running special offers.
3. A handful of large publishers decide the prices of most bestsellers.
4. Customers check prices on their phones and ask the shop to match the cheapest website.
5. A café owner is planning to open a shop selling books and coffee next door.
6. Streaming series and video games compete for people's free time.

1. Substitutes — a different product that does the same job.

2. Rivalry — existing competitors fighting for the same customers.

3. Supplier power — a few powerful suppliers can set the terms.

4. Buyer power — customers using information to push prices down.

5. New entrants — a newcomer coming into the market.

6. Substitutes — other ways to meet the same need — entertainment.

Where the tool breaks

A snapshot, not a film. Forces change — new technology can create a substitute very quickly.

Competition isn't everything. Companies also cooperate; the original model leaves out complementors.

Where does the industry end? Is a bookshop competing with other bookshops, with websites, or with streaming?

Grammar — cause and effect: because of, due to, as a result, lead to

The five forces are all about causes and effects: because buyers are strong, prices fall; as a result, profits shrink. English has many ways of linking a cause to its effect — and they fit into sentences differently.

because + clause but because of / due to + noun.
“Profits fell because fuel became more expensive.”  “Profits fell because of the rise in fuel prices.”  “The delay was due to bad weather.”
As a result, / Therefore, / Consequently, — start a new sentence with the effect.
“Buyers can compare prices online. As a result, shops have to keep prices low.”
lead to / result in / cause + noun — the cause is the subject.
“Strong rivalry leads to price wars.”  “The new law resulted in higher costs.”
…, which means (that) … — add an effect to the end of a sentence.
“There are only two suppliers, which means they can set the price.”
Watch out: ~~because of prices rose~~ → “because prices rose” or “because of the price rise”. And it's lead to, not ~~lead in~~: “It led to job losses.”
