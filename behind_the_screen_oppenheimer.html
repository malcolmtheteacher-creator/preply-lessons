Oppenheimer

A physicist builds the weapon that ends a war and opens a new age of fear — then spends the rest of his life reckoning with what he made. The history is real.

Today: you'll tell the story of Oppenheimer — the physicist who led the race to build the first atomic bomb, then spent the rest of his life wrestling with what he had unleashed — discover how closely the film follows the true history, and master the language English uses to look back and judge the past: modal perfects of regret and criticism (should have, shouldn't have, could have, needn't have).
Before we begin — talk it through: If you built something that could either save your country or destroy a city full of strangers, would you build it? Once a terrible weapon exists, can it ever be "un-invented"? And should scientists be held responsible for how governments use their discoveries?

The film, in three parts

Christopher Nolan's Oppenheimer (2023). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it. This is a true story about real, grave events, so we'll handle the human cost soberly.)

Part 1 — Building the bomb

It's 1942, and physicist J. Robert Oppenheimer is one of many scientists gripped by a single, terrifying fear: that Nazi Germany might build an atomic bomb first. The US Army, desperate to win that race, puts General Leslie Groves in charge of a vast, clandestine effort — the Manhattan Project — and Groves makes an unlikely choice of scientific director: Oppenheimer, a brilliant but politically awkward theoretical physicist with left-wing friends and no experience running anything.

Oppenheimer chooses a remote mesa in New Mexico and turns it into Los Alamos, a hidden laboratory town that officially does not exist. He persuades the finest physicists in the world — many of them refugees from fascism in Europe — to disappear into the desert with their families for years, cut off from outside contact, racing to solve a problem no one has ever solved: how to release the energy locked inside the atom, and turn it into a weapon.

The work is thrilling and appalling in equal measure. These are scientists who love ideas for their own sake, suddenly building something whose only purpose is mass destruction. Oppenheimer holds the whole project together — juggling physicists, engineers, generals and his own doubts — driven by the same fear that started it all: what if the Nazis get there first?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does the US Army start the Manhattan Project, and why is Oppenheimer an unlikely choice to lead it?

2. What is Los Alamos, and why does Oppenheimer build it in a remote desert?

1. The US fears Nazi Germany might build an atomic bomb first; Oppenheimer is chosen despite being a theoretical physicist with no management experience and left-wing associations that make him a political risk.

2. Los Alamos is a secret laboratory town Oppenheimer establishes in the New Mexico desert so the world's best physicists can work on the bomb in total secrecy, isolated from outside contact.

Discuss:
  • The scientists at Los Alamos are driven partly by fear of the Nazis and partly by pure scientific curiosity. Which motive do you think is stronger in Oppenheimer — and is either one a good enough reason to build a weapon like this?

Part 2 — Trinity, and the bomb is used

By July 1945, the design is ready, and the team gathers in the desert to find out if years of secret work have actually produced a working weapon. In the pre-dawn dark, Oppenheimer watches the test device — nicknamed "the gadget" — hang above the sand. When it detonates, the blast is far more violent than almost anyone predicted: a light brighter than the sun, a fireball, and then a shockwave that reaches observers miles away. Oppenheimer later says a line from Hindu scripture ran through his mind as he watched — he had become death, the destroyer of worlds.

Weeks later, with Germany already defeated but Japan still fighting, the decision is made — far above Oppenheimer's head — to use the weapon against two Japanese cities. Oppenheimer is not consulted on whether to drop it, only on how. The bombs kill huge numbers of people instantly, and many more in the months that follow. Japan surrenders. The war that started this race is finally over.

At Los Alamos, the initial reaction is euphoria — the impossible has worked, and the war is won. But for Oppenheimer, the euphoria curdles almost immediately into something closer to horror, as the true scale of what has happened in Hiroshima and Nagasaki becomes clear.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Oppenheimer think and feel as he watches the test device detonate in the desert?

2. What happens in the weeks after the test, and how does the mood at Los Alamos change?

1. He is awed and shaken by the blast's violence, and recalls a line from Hindu scripture about becoming "death, the destroyer of worlds" — sensing, even in triumph, something monstrous in what he has helped create.

2. The bombs are dropped on two Japanese cities, killing huge numbers of people and ending the war; the initial celebration at Los Alamos quickly turns into private horror, especially for Oppenheimer, once the human cost becomes clear.

Discuss:
  • Oppenheimer helps build the bomb but has no say in whether it is used. Does that limit his moral responsibility for what happens afterwards — or not at all?

Part 3 — The reckoning

After the war, Oppenheimer becomes the most famous scientist in America — and also its most haunted. He tells President Truman, "I feel I have blood on my hands," and campaigns publicly against the far more powerful hydrogen bomb, arguing the world doesn't need weapons that could end civilisation itself. His growing caution, his old left-wing friendships, and his outspokenness make him enemies in Washington, at exactly the moment America's fear of communism is reaching fever pitch.

In 1954, those enemies get their chance. A closed-door government hearing is convened, ostensibly to review Oppenheimer's security clearance. In practice, it becomes something closer to a trial of his loyalty and his character, built partly on old associations, partly on the private testimony of people he considered friends. Old letters, old lovers, and old political meetings are all dragged into the light. Oppenheimer is humiliated, and his clearance — and his influence over nuclear policy — is stripped away.

He spends his remaining years a diminished, disgraced figure: still respected as a scientist, still teaching, but shut out of the corridors of power he once dominated. The film leaves him — and us — with an uncomfortable question that has no tidy answer: can a person ever really settle accounts for something as vast as what he helped unleash upon the world?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Oppenheimer do after the war that puts him at odds with parts of the US government?

2. What happens at the 1954 hearing, and what is the result for Oppenheimer?

1. He tells Truman he feels he has "blood on my hands" and publicly opposes development of the hydrogen bomb, angering officials who see this as dangerously soft at the height of Cold War tension.

2. A security hearing turns into a character trial using old friendships, letters and private testimony against him; his security clearance is revoked and his influence over government nuclear policy ends.

Discuss:
  • Was the 1954 hearing really about security — or was it punishment for a man who dared to have doubts, in public, about the weapon he'd helped build? What do you think drove it?

Grammar — modal perfects of regret & criticism

No film needs this language more than Oppenheimer. English uses modal + have + past participle to look back and judge the past: what should have happened but didn't, what shouldn't have happened but did, what could have happened but never got the chance, and what didn't need to happen at all.

The four forms:
• should have + past participle — criticising a past action that wasn't done, but would have been right: "They should have consulted the scientists before deciding."
• shouldn't have + past participle — criticising a past action that was done, but was wrong: "He shouldn't have kept his doubts to himself for so long."
• could have + past participle — a past possibility or option that existed but didn't happen: "The government could have handled the hearing very differently."
• needn't have + past participle — an action that was done, but turned out to be unnecessary: "She needn't have worried — the letter never reached the committee."
Feel the difference:
• should(n't) have = a moral judgement about right or wrong, looking back.
• could have = a road not taken — a real option that was available but wasn't used.
• needn't have = wasted effort or worry — the action happened, but it didn't have to.

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

Here's what makes Oppenheimer unusual: this isn't a film loosely inspired by history — it's built, scene by scene, on a meticulously researched biography, and nearly every major event survives the journey from page to screen intact.

📖 The book behind the film

Christopher Nolan's Oppenheimer (2023) is based on American Prometheus: The Triumph and Tragedy of J. Robert Oppenheimer (2005), a Pulitzer Prize–winning biography by Kai Bird and Martin J. Sherwin, built from decades of research, interviews and declassified documents. The recruitment of Los Alamos, the Trinity test, and the 1954 hearing are all drawn directly from the historical record.

The Manhattan Project and the Trinity test

The Manhattan Project was real, and so was the fear that drove it: in the early 1940s, American and British scientists worried that Nazi Germany might be racing towards its own atomic bomb. Under General Leslie Groves, the US government built secret facilities across the country, with the bomb's design and assembly centred on the hidden laboratory town of Los Alamos, New Mexico, which Oppenheimer led as scientific director from 1943.

The first-ever nuclear explosion, code-named Trinity, took place at 5:30 a.m. on 16 July 1945, in the Jornada del Muerto desert of New Mexico. Oppenheimer really did later say that a line from the Hindu scripture the Bhagavad Gita came to mind as he watched: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds." (Scholars note the original Sanskrit is more often translated as "time," not "death" — but Oppenheimer's own phrasing, recalled years afterwards in a television interview, is the one history remembers.)

Hiroshima, Nagasaki, and the 1954 hearing

Three weeks later, the United States dropped atomic bombs on two Japanese cities: Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki on 9 August 1945. Together the bombings killed well over one hundred thousand people, the overwhelming majority of them civilians, with many more dying afterwards from injuries and radiation sickness. Japan surrendered days later, ending the Second World War — but the bombings remain one of the most morally fraught events of the twentieth century, and historians still debate whether they were necessary to end the war.

The 1954 hearing shown in the film is also historically accurate in its essentials: amid the intense anti-communist suspicion of the McCarthy era, the US Atomic Energy Commission reviewed Oppenheimer's security clearance over old left-wing associations and his opposition to the hydrogen bomb. The panel voted, in June 1954, to revoke his clearance, ending his formal influence over US nuclear policy. In December 2022 — nearly seventy years later, and more than fifty years after his death — the US Department of Energy formally vacated that decision, calling the original process "flawed."

Take these into the discussion:
  • The film shows Oppenheimer's quote at Trinity almost exactly as he recalled it years later. Does it matter that scholars disagree about how accurately that ancient verse was translated?
  • The US government eventually admitted, nearly seventy years later, that the 1954 hearing was unfair. Does a correction that late change anything for the people affected?
  • Knowing the history sits this close to the film, does that make the moral questions feel heavier — or does dramatising real events always risk oversimplifying them?
