The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — You cannot prevent everything

Imagine you run a small hospital. This morning your list of worries includes a broken lift, a nurse shortage at night, a cupboard of medicines with similar-looking boxes, a leaking roof, and a computer system that is ten years old.

You cannot fix all of them today. So which one do you start with? The loudest complaint? The cheapest repair? The one in yesterday's newspaper?

Through the twentieth century, industries that could kill people — mining, chemicals, nuclear power, aviation — learned an uncomfortable lesson. You can never remove every hazard. What you can do is be honest about which hazards deserve your money and your attention first. And for that, you need to ask about each one not one question but two.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why can't the hospital fix everything?

2. Which industries learned this lesson first?

3. How many questions does each hazard need?

1. There isn't enough time or money — you have to choose.

2. Dangerous ones: mining, chemicals, nuclear power, aviation.

3. Two.

Discuss:
  • In your workplace or home, how do people usually decide what to fix first?

Part 2 — Two questions, one grid

The two questions are these. How likely is it to happen? And how bad would it be if it did?

Answer both — usually on a scale from low to high — and every hazard lands in a box on a grid. The bottom-left corner holds things that probably won't happen and wouldn't matter much. The top-right corner holds things that are likely and would be catastrophic. That corner gets your money, today.

The grid also solves an old argument. People disagree loudly about danger, because they are usually arguing about different questions. One person is thinking about likelihood (“that has never happened here”), the other about consequence (“if it did, someone would die”). The grid makes them answer both questions separately, and suddenly the argument becomes a conversation.

Its power is that it works for a nuclear plant, a school trip or a wedding.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What are the two questions?

2. Which corner of the grid gets attention first?

3. Why do people argue about danger, according to the story?

1. How likely is it? How bad would it be?

2. The top-right: likely and catastrophic.

3. Because they are answering different questions — one thinks about likelihood, the other about consequence.

Discuss:
  • Think of an argument you've heard about safety. Were the two sides answering different questions?

Part 3 — The rare and the terrible

The grid has a famous weak spot: the box where something is very unlikely but would be catastrophic. A huge flood. A new disease. A bank collapsing. Because the likelihood is low, these risks sit quietly in a corner of the page for years — until the year they don't.

There is a second problem. The numbers look scientific, but somebody chose them. Give the same hazard to two teams and they will often score it differently, especially when the person scoring it is also the person who would have to pay for the repair.

So good teams use the grid as the start of a conversation, not the end of one. They add a rule: anything that could kill someone gets attention whatever its score. And they record the near misses, because a near miss is the cheapest warning you will ever get.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Which box is the famous weak spot?

2. Why can two teams score the same hazard differently?

3. What extra rule do good teams add?

1. Very unlikely, but catastrophic.

2. Because the scores are human judgements — and the scorer may be the person who would pay for the repair.

3. Anything that could kill someone gets attention whatever its score (and they record near misses).

Discuss:

How the tool works

Score each hazard twice. Along the bottom: how likely is it? Up the side: how serious would it be? The colour of the box tells you how fast to act.

Using it, step by step

  1. List the hazards plainly — what could go wrong, and to whom.
  2. Score each one for likelihood, using evidence: has it happened here before? How often?
  3. Score each one for consequence: the realistic worst case, not the imaginable worst case.
  4. Start at the red corner. For each one, either reduce the likelihood, reduce the harm, or both — then score it again.

Try it yourself

A language school is planning a day trip to the mountains. Which box does each risk belong in? Choose, then check.

1. Rain during the walk. It rains most weeks, and everyone would get wet and uncomfortable.
2. A student with a serious nut allergy is given the wrong packed lunch. The kitchen prepares them by hand, and this nearly happened last term.
3. The bus breaks down on the mountain road. The company's buses are old and it happened last year; the group would be stuck for hours in the cold.
4. A student twists an ankle on the path. It happens on about one trip in three, and means a slow walk back.
5. A meteorite hits the bus.
6. Somebody forgets their sun hat.

1. Medium — very likely, but only a minor consequence.

2. Extreme — likely (it nearly happened already) and possibly fatal.

3. High — likely, and the consequences are serious.

4. Medium — fairly likely; painful but not severe.

5. Low — catastrophic, but so rare that no plan is possible.

6. Low — it may well happen, but it is easily fixed.

Where the tool breaks

The scores are opinions in disguise. Two honest teams often score the same hazard differently.

The rare-but-terrible box. Low likelihood pushes real dangers off the page for years.

It can become paperwork. A finished grid is not a safer school — the actions are.

Talk it through:
  • Which risk on the trip would you spend money on first?
  • Should the person who pays for repairs be the person who scores the risks?

Grammar — likely, may well, bound to

This grid runs on probability. English has a rich set of ways to say how likely something is — far beyond maybe.

be likely / unlikely + to + verb — the everyday way to score a chance.
“Prices are likely to rise next year.”  “He is unlikely to pass without help.”
Also: “It is likely that prices will rise.”
may well / might well — a real possibility, stronger than may.
“The meeting may well finish early.”
be bound to / be sure to — you are almost certain.
“With this traffic, we are bound to be late.”
There's a good / slight chance (that)…
“There's a slight chance of snow.”
Watch out: likely is an adjective, not a verb: ~~It likely to rain~~ → “It is likely to rain.” And after bound to we use the bare verb: “bound to be late”.
