🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: An omnibenevolent God is perfectly loving, perfectly good, and desires the wellbeing of all creation. Such a God would want to prevent suffering and would care deeply about the pain of all creatures.

🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: If God is all-powerful, God could stop suffering. If God is all-loving, God would want to stop suffering. Yet suffering exists everywhere. How can we reconcile these beliefs with the reality of evil? This apparent contradiction is called the Problem of Evil.

Encounter: The Logical Problem of Evil

The Core Puzzle: Three Incompatible Beliefs

The Problem of Evil is a logical puzzle. It asks: How can the following three statements all be true at the same time?

1. God is omnipotent (all-powerful)
2. God is omnibenevolent (all-loving and perfectly good)
3. Evil exists
These three statements appear logically incompatible.

If God is all-powerful, God can prevent any evil. If God is all-loving, God would want to prevent evil. So logically, no evil should exist. Yet we observe evil and suffering everywhere. The question is not just "Why does God allow suffering?" but rather "How can we maintain belief in a God who is both all-powerful and all-loving when evil clearly exists?"

Historical Origins: A Crucial Attribution

Important: This trilemma is commonly misattributed to the ancient philosopher Epicurus. This is historically inaccurate. The earliest written version of this argument comes from Lactantius (c.300 CE), a Christian author who attributed it to Epicurus to show the weakness of atheistic philosophy. The trilemma was then refined and popularised by Pierre Bayle in the 17th century and especially by David Hume in his work Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779). Epicurus himself actually believed in gods and criticised atheism—he was not arguing against divine existence. We must be careful to give credit to the correct philosophers.

David Hume's Formulation (1779)

Who Was David Hume?

David Hume (1711-1776) was a Scottish philosopher and historian, one of the greatest empiricist philosophers. He argued that our knowledge comes from experience, not reason alone. He was sceptical of religious claims and natural theology—the idea that we can prove God's existence from observing the natural world.

Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion

Published posthumously in 1779, this work presents conversations between three characters debating whether God's existence can be proven from the design of the natural world. Through these dialogues, Hume presents the Problem of Evil as a devastating objection to the argument from design.

Hume's Challenge: Hume argued that the appearance of design in nature could be explained by natural processes (evolution, though he didn't use that word) without needing God. And more importantly, even if the world shows design, that design is imperfect and mixed with disorder, pain, and waste. Why would an all-powerful, all-loving designer create a world filled with so much suffering?

The Evidential Problem: Not Just That Evil Exists, But How Much

The Problem of Evil has two forms:

The Logical Problem

Is the existence of any evil logically incompatible with God's omnipotence and omnibenevolence? This version asks whether it's even possible for an all-powerful, all-loving God and evil to coexist.

The Evidential Problem

Even if some evil might be logically possible, is the amount and distribution of evil in our world too much to be compatible with God? This asks not just "Why does evil exist?" but "Why SO MUCH evil? Why such terrible kinds of suffering? Why do innocent children suffer?"

The evidential problem is perhaps more powerful. We might imagine scenarios where some suffering could be justified—perhaps it builds character, teaches lessons, or leads to greater goods. But can we really justify the amount of suffering we observe? The deaths of children from disease? The brutality of war? The helplessness of animals torn apart by predators? This is the challenge that makes the problem acute for many believers.

J.L. Mackie's Logical Argument from Evil (1955)

Who Was J.L. Mackie?

J.L. Mackie (1917-1981) was an Australian philosopher who wrote extensively on logic, metaphysics, and philosophy of religion. His article "Evil and Omnipotence" (1955) presented one of the clearest modern formulations of the logical problem of evil.

Mackie's Core Argument

Mackie argued that the existence of evil is logically incompatible with the traditional conception of God. His reasoning:

  1. If God is omnipotent, God can eliminate evil.
  2. If God is omnibenevolent, God would want to eliminate evil.
  3. Evil exists.
  4. Therefore, there cannot be a being who is both omnipotent and omnibenevolent.
  5. But traditional theism claims God is both.
  6. Therefore, traditional theism is logically incoherent.

Mackie distinguished between logically necessary evil (evil that must exist for some greater good) and logically contingent evil (evil that just happens to exist). His claim: Much of the evil we observe appears logically contingent—God could have made the world without it. An omnipotent God could create free beings who always freely choose good (just as God supposedly does). Therefore, the existence of so much unnecessary evil makes theism logically incoherent.

Alvin Plantinga's Free Will Defence (1974)

The Free Will Defence: A Philosophical Response

Alvin Plantinga (born 1932), an American philosopher, offered one of the most influential responses to Mackie's argument. His strategy: grant that God is omnipotent and omnibenevolent, but show that the existence of some evil is logically compatible with God's nature—if moral freedom is sufficiently valuable.

Plantinga's Key Insight: Free will is genuinely free—even an omnipotent God cannot logically force a free being to always freely choose good. If a choice is forced, it's not free. Therefore, a world with free beings who sometimes choose evil might be better than a world with no freedom at all. God could not have created a world with genuine moral freedom and guaranteed that all free beings would choose only good. This is not a limitation on God's power; it's a logical truth.

Example: Imagine God wants to create beings who love God freely. God could make them robots who always obey, but that's not free love. Or God could give them genuine freedom, knowing they might reject God and harm each other. Plantinga argues the second option is better—and justifiable—even if it allows for moral evil.

Important distinction: The free will defence addresses moral evil (caused by human choice) but leaves natural evil (earthquakes, disease) still unexplained. Critics point out that the existence of natural evil—suffering caused by nature, not human choice—cannot be justified by the free will defence.

Defence vs. Theodicy: A Crucial Philosophical Difference

A Defence

A defence shows that belief in God and the existence of evil are logically compatible. It doesn't claim to explain why God actually allowed evil; it just shows there's no logical contradiction. Plantinga's free will defence is a defence in this sense.

A Theodicy

A theodicy goes further. It claims to explain God's actual reasons for allowing evil—what good purposes evil serves. It argues that this particular world, with this particular amount and distribution of evil, is justified by God's purposes. Classical theodicies include the "soul-making" defence (suffering develops character) or the "greater good" defence (evil allows for greater goods like courage).

Plantinga himself argued for a defence rather than a full theodicy. He's not claiming to know why God actually allows evil; he's just showing that belief in God needn't be abandoned because of evil's existence. Critics argue that even if a defence succeeds logically, it doesn't address the emotional or experiential dimension—it doesn't comfort those who suffer.

Why This Matters: The Stakes of the Problem

The Problem of Evil matters because:

  • It challenges the foundations of belief. If God is not all-powerful or not all-loving, then God is not God as traditionally understood in Christianity, Islam, and Judaism. The problem strikes at the heart of what these traditions teach about God's nature.
  • It addresses human experience. The Problem of Evil isn't abstract—it arises from real suffering. When someone experiences profound loss or injustice, philosophical arguments about God matter deeply.
  • It divides believers. Some believers accept theodicies and find ways to justify God's allowance of evil. Others reject theodicies as offensive to suffering and maintain faith while admitting they cannot explain evil. Humanists argue the problem is unsolvable and shows why we should focus on reducing suffering ourselves rather than waiting for divine intervention.
  • It shapes worldview. How one answers the Problem of Evil determines whether one believes in God, what kind of God one believes in, and how one responds to suffering in the world.

Comparing Philosophical Positions

Traditional Theism (Christianity, Islam)

Position: Maintains that God is omnipotent and omnibenevolent despite evil. Often appeals to divine mystery—we cannot fully understand God's purposes.

Challenge: Critics argue this asks believers to accept a logical contradiction or to retreat into mystery when facing hard questions.

Plantinga's Free Will Defence

Position: Shows that God and evil are logically compatible if moral freedom is sufficiently valuable. God cannot force free beings to freely choose good.

Challenge: Addresses moral evil but not natural evil. Doesn't claim to know why God actually allowed evil—only that it's possible.

The Greater Good Defence

Position: Evil serves a greater good—builds character, develops virtues, allows for heroism and compassion.

Challenge: Seems to justify terrible suffering as "necessary" for growth. Does God really need children to suffer horribly to teach lessons?

Humanism

Position: The Problem of Evil shows why belief in an omnipotent, omnibenevolent God is unjustified. We should focus on human responsibility to reduce suffering.

Challenge: Abandons the comfort and meaning that religious belief provides. Leaves fundamental questions about suffering's meaning unanswered.

Evaluate: Can the Problem Be Solved?

Two Opposing Views

Argument 1: The Problem of Evil is Fatal to Belief in God

J.L. Mackie and other philosophers argue that the existence of evil—especially its amount and distribution—is logically incompatible with belief in an omnipotent and omnibenevolent God. The free will defence might explain some moral evil, but it cannot explain natural evil (earthquakes, childhood diseases, animal suffering). Moreover, even if free will justifies some moral evil, the sheer quantity of unnecessary suffering seems to far exceed what any reasonable theodicy can justify. An all-powerful God could have created a world with free beings who rarely chose evil, or a world with much less natural evil. The fact that this world contains so much suffering suggests there is no omnipotent, omnibenevolent God.

Argument 2: The Problem of Evil Can Be Answered Philosophically

Philosophers like Plantinga argue that the existence of evil is not logically incompatible with God. The free will defence shows that an omnipotent God might have a morally sufficient reason to permit moral evil—namely, the value of genuine freedom. Other theodicies appeal to the possibility that God has purposes we cannot yet understand, or that suffering serves goods (character-building, compassion, spiritual growth) that justify its existence. While these responses don't provide complete certainty, they show that belief in God and the existence of evil can coexist logically. Belief in God remains rationally defensible.

Key Terms Summary

Problem of Evil
The philosophical and theological challenge: If God is all-powerful and all-loving, why does evil and suffering exist?
Omnipotence
All-powerfulness; the quality of being able to do anything. A key attribute of God in traditional theism.
Omnibenevolence
Perfect goodness and love. The quality of wanting the best for all creation. A key attribute of God in traditional theism.
Inconsistent Triad / Trilemma
Three statements that appear logically impossible to hold together: God is omnipotent, God is omnibenevolent, and evil exists.
Logical Problem of Evil
The philosophical argument that the existence of ANY evil is logically incompatible with an omnipotent and omnibenevolent God.
Evidential Problem of Evil
The philosophical argument that the AMOUNT and DISTRIBUTION of evil in the world is too great to be compatible with an omnipotent and omnibenevolent God.
Lactantius (c.300 CE)
Christian author who formulated the earliest written version of the Problem of Evil, incorrectly attributing it to Epicurus.
David Hume (1711-1776)
Scottish philosopher who popularised the Problem of Evil through his Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779).
J.L. Mackie (1917-1981)
Australian philosopher who presented a clear logical formulation of the Problem of Evil, arguing it is logically incompatible with traditional theism.
Alvin Plantinga (born 1932)
American philosopher who developed the free will defence, arguing that God and evil can be logically compatible if freedom is sufficiently valuable.
Free Will Defence
Philosophical response showing that God can be omnipotent and omnibenevolent while allowing moral evil, because free will is so valuable that God permits beings to freely choose evil.
Defence (in philosophy of religion)
A philosophical argument showing that two apparently incompatible beliefs (like God's existence and evil's existence) can logically coexist. It doesn't claim to know God's actual reasons.
Theodicy
A philosophical or theological argument that attempts to explain and justify God's actual reasons for allowing evil. It goes further than a defence by claiming to know God's purposes.
Natural Evil
Suffering caused by natural processes—earthquakes, diseases, floods—not by human choice.
Moral Evil
Suffering caused by human free choices—murder, theft, abuse, injustice, cruelty.
Natural Theology
The attempt to prove God's existence from observation of the natural world, particularly from apparent design. Hume and Mackie both critiqued this approach.
Central Insight from This Lesson: The Problem of Evil is not merely a puzzle to be solved but a genuine philosophical challenge to belief in God. It forces believers and philosophers to examine their assumptions: Is God omnipotent? Is God omnibenevolent? Does evil really exist as we experience it? What is the nature of free will? Philosophers have offered responses—particularly Plantinga's free will defence—but whether these fully resolve the problem remains contested. Humanists argue the problem shows why we should focus on human action to reduce suffering rather than relying on divine intervention. The Problem of Evil remains one of the most significant challenges in philosophy of religion.
