Today, we're going to look at the logical structure of the problem of evil—the actual philosophical puzzle that makes this question so tricky.

Encounter: The Inconsistent Triad

Philosophers have identified a logical problem with believing in both God and evil at the same time. It's called the inconsistent triad because three statements seem impossible to hold together.

Can these three statements ALL be true?

1. God is all-powerful
2. God is all-good
3. Evil and suffering exist

Let's explore why this creates a problem:

The Logical Problem:

If God is all-powerful, God has the ability to eliminate all evil and suffering. Nothing would be impossible for God to do.

If God is all-good, God would want to eliminate all evil and suffering. An all-good being would desire the wellbeing of all creation.

If both are true, then evil and suffering should not exist. Yet they clearly do.

Therefore, at least one of these three claims must be false. Either God is not all-powerful, or not all-good, or evil does not exist.

The Ancient Challenge: Epicurus

This problem is ancient. Around 300 BCE, the Greek philosopher Epicurus (not the pleasure-lover people think of!) formulated the classic challenge:

Epicurus's Challenge:

"Is God willing to prevent evil but not able? Then he is not omnipotent. Is he able but not willing? Then he is not benevolent. Is he both able and willing? Then why is there evil?"

This argument has troubled believers and philosophers for over two thousand years. It's so powerful because it seems to be pure logic—not an opinion, but a logical structure that cannot be denied.

Explore: Two Types of Problems

Philosophers distinguish between two different ways evil creates a problem for belief in God:

The Logical Problem of Evil

The claim that the statements "God is all-powerful," "God is all-good," and "evil exists" are logically contradictory. You cannot hold all three as true at the same time without breaking the laws of logic.

In other words: Belief in God is irrational. It defies logic itself.

The Evidential Problem of Evil

The claim that even if there's no strict logical contradiction, the sheer amount and intensity of suffering in the world provides very strong evidence against God's existence. It makes God's existence improbable, even if not impossible.

In other words: God might be possible, but the evidence of suffering makes belief in God unreasonable.

What's the Difference?

Think of it this way:

  • Logical problem: Like saying "I am both in London and not in London at the same time." This is impossible by definition.
  • Evidential problem: Like saying "It's probably raining because the sky is very dark." The sky being dark is evidence for rain, but not absolute proof.

Many philosophers today think the logical problem has been answered by theodicies (we'll explore those next), but they still debate whether the evidential problem defeats belief in God.

Key Thinkers

David Hume (1711-1776): Scottish philosopher who pressed the evidential problem. He argued that the design of the world—with its suffering, disease, and natural disasters—suggests a being that is either limited in power or not perfectly good, not an omnipotent, omnibenevolent God.
Alvin Plantinga (b. 1932): Modern philosopher who argued that the logical problem of evil can be solved through the free will defence. He claims it's not logically impossible for God to be all-powerful and all-good while evil exists.

Apply: Analyse Arguments

For each statement below, decide whether it's raising a logical problem or an evidential problem with God's existence.

Scenario 1:

"If God is all-good, He must want children not to suffer. If God is all-powerful, He must be able to prevent children's suffering. Yet children do suffer from disease and accidents. This is a logical contradiction."

Is this logical or evidential?

Scenario 2:

"Look at the amount of suffering in the world. Millions die from starvation, disease, and violence. This much suffering makes it extremely unlikely that a loving God exists, even though it doesn't prove God doesn't exist logically."

Is this logical or evidential?

Scenario 3:

"The existence of evil shows that either God doesn't exist, or God is not all-powerful, or God is not all-good. One of these three must be false because they cannot all be true together."

Is this logical or evidential?

Scenario 4:

"We see natural disasters that kill thousands and cause immense suffering. This suggests that if God exists, He is limited in power or does not care about human suffering. This seems more likely than the traditional idea of an all-powerful, all-loving God."

Is this logical or evidential?

Evaluate: Is the Problem Solved?

Since Epicurus posed his challenge 2,300 years ago, believers have tried to solve this problem. In the next lessons, we'll look at specific responses from Christianity, Islam, and Buddhism. But first, consider: can the problem even be solved?

The Optimist's View

Some philosophers believe the logical problem of evil has been solved. They argue that there are good reasons why God might allow suffering—reasons that are compatible with God being all-powerful and all-good. Free will is a classic example: perhaps God allows moral evil so that humans can be genuinely free.

The Sceptic's View

Other philosophers argue that no theodicy truly solves the problem. They claim that every attempt to justify God's allowance of suffering either doesn't work logically, or compromises God's omnipotence, omniscience, or goodness. For them, the problem remains fundamentally unsolved.

Review: Key Concepts

The Inconsistent Triad

Three statements that seem impossible to hold together:

  1. God is all-powerful (omnipotent)
  2. God is all-good (omnibenevolent)
  3. Evil and suffering exist

The Logical Problem of Evil

The claim that belief in God is logically contradictory or irrational because God's traditional attributes (omnipotence, omniscience, omnibenevolence) seem incompatible with the existence of evil.

The Evidential Problem of Evil

The claim that even if belief in God is not strictly logically impossible, the sheer amount and severity of suffering in the world makes belief in an all-good, all-powerful God unreasonable or improbable.
