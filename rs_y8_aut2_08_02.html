Question 1: What is the difference between natural evil and moral evil? Give one example of each.

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Natural evil is suffering caused by nature—not by human action. Examples: earthquakes, hurricanes, disease. Moral evil is suffering caused by human choices and actions. Examples: violence, theft, cruelty.

Question 2: If God is omnipotent (all-powerful), what should God be able to do?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: An omnipotent God should be able to do anything—to prevent any evil, stop any suffering, or intervene in any situation.

Question 3: What does it mean to say God is omnibenevolent (all-loving)?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: An omnibenevolent God is perfectly loving, perfectly just, and wants the best for all creation. God would want to prevent suffering.

Question 4: Explain briefly why the existence of widespread suffering creates a philosophical problem for theism.

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: If God is all-powerful, God could stop suffering. If God is all-loving, God would want to stop suffering. Yet suffering exists. This appears to show God cannot be both omnipotent and omnibenevolent.

Encounter: The Logical Problem of Evil

The Core Puzzle: Three Incompatible Beliefs

The Problem of Evil is a logical puzzle. It asks: How can the following three statements all be true at the same time?

1. God is omnipotent (all-powerful)
2. God is omnibenevolent (all-loving and perfectly good)
3. Evil exists
These three statements appear logically incompatible. Something must give.

If God is all-powerful, God can prevent any evil. If God is all-loving, God would want to prevent any evil. Yet evil and suffering are clearly present in our world. So either God cannot prevent evil (not omnipotent), or God doesn't want to prevent evil (not omnibenevolent), or evil doesn't really exist (but this seems obviously false).

Historical Origins: A Crucial Attribution

Important: This trilemma is commonly misattributed to the ancient philosopher Epicurus (341-270 BCE). However, there is no evidence that Epicurus formulated this argument. The earliest surviving version comes from later sources. The argument became famous through David Hume's 18th-century work.

David Hume's Formulation (1779)

Who Was David Hume?

David Hume (1711–1776) was a Scottish philosopher and historian, one of the greatest empiricists in Western philosophy. He was deeply sceptical about many traditional religious arguments and used logic to challenge them.

Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion

Published posthumously in 1779, this work presents conversations between three characters discussing God's existence and nature. One of the most powerful exchanges focuses on the problem of evil and whether the world shows evidence of divine design.

Hume's Key Point

Hume argued that if we look at the world objectively, we see a mixture of good and evil, design and chaos. The presence of unnecessary suffering—especially the suffering of innocent creatures—is hard to reconcile with the idea of a perfect, all-powerful creator. Why would such a God create a world with so much pointless pain?

Hume didn't claim this proves God doesn't exist, but he showed that the apparent design in nature doesn't necessarily point to the God of traditional theism.

Explore: Three Major Philosophical Responses

Philosophers and theologians have developed several responses to the logical problem of evil. Here are three of the most important:

1. The Free Will Defence

Key idea: Moral evil (caused by human choices) is the price of human free will, which is a great good.

If God created humans with genuine free will—the real ability to choose between right and wrong—then God must allow for the possibility of moral evil. A world in which humans are forced to always choose good is not a world of free choice; it's a world of puppets.

God could have created a world with no free will and no moral evil, but such a world would be less good than one where free creatures can make real choices, even if some choose badly.

Limitation: This defence explains moral evil (caused by humans) but has difficulty explaining natural evil (earthquakes, disease) which isn't caused by human choice.

2. The Soul-Making Defence

Key idea: Suffering serves a purpose: it allows humans to develop morally and spiritually.

A world without challenges, pain, or hardship would be a world where courage, compassion, patience, and forgiveness couldn't exist. We grow morally through facing difficulty. A parent might allow a child to experience disappointment or failure because this helps the child develop resilience and wisdom.

On this view, a perfectly good God might permit suffering not as punishment, but as an essential ingredient in human development.

Limitation: Some suffering seems excessive and pointless—it doesn't clearly contribute to anyone's moral growth (e.g., childhood cancer).

3. The Skeptical Theist Response

Key idea: We cannot expect to understand God's reasons for permitting evil.

Just as a child might not understand why a parent allows them to experience something painful (like a vaccine or a difficult school), humans—with our limited understanding—cannot expect to comprehend why an infinite God would permit suffering. There might be reasons we simply cannot grasp.

This doesn't mean evil is good or that God doesn't care. It means we should be humble about our ability to judge God's purposes.

Limitation: Critics argue this response is too convenient—it seems to shut down inquiry by saying "we just can't understand it."

Comparing the Responses

Which response is strongest? Philosophers disagree. The free will defence is popular because it makes logical sense, but critics point out it doesn't explain natural evil. The soul-making defence explains why suffering might be necessary, but seems to struggle with extreme or pointless suffering. The skeptical theist response is intellectually honest about human limitations, but some say it avoids the problem rather than solving it.

Question 1: In your own words, explain what the "logical problem of evil" is. What three beliefs does it claim are incompatible?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: The logical problem of evil is a philosophical puzzle about whether three core beliefs can be held simultaneously: (1) God is omnipotent (all-powerful), (2) God is omnibenevolent (all-good and all-loving), and (3) evil and suffering exist. The problem claims these three are logically incompatible because if God is all-powerful and all-loving, God should prevent all evil. The fact that evil exists seems to suggest God is either not all-powerful or not all-loving.

Question 2: What is David Hume's contribution to discussions about the problem of evil?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: David Hume formulated a clear, influential version of the problem of evil in his 1779 work Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion. He argued that the world shows a mixture of good and evil, and that the presence of unnecessary suffering—especially of innocent creatures—is difficult to reconcile with the idea of a perfect, all-powerful creator. He suggested that the design in nature doesn't necessarily point to the God of traditional theism.
