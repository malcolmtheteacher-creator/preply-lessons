Formal Collocations

These word combinations sound professional in business writing:

Excellent service
Very high quality service; professional and reliable
Reputable supplier
A company with a good reputation and trustworthy record
Raise concerns
To bring up or mention worries or problems formally
Regret to inform
Formal phrase meaning to deliver bad or unwelcome news
Long-standing relationship
A business relationship that has existed for many years
Draw attention to
To point out or highlight a problem or issue

More Important Collocations

Complete: "We have a relationship with this company."

long-standing. This shows the company has worked with them for a long time.

Complete: "We need to timely resolution."

achieve. A timely resolution means solving the problem quickly.

Complete: "We a significant change in their service."

regret to inform you of. This is the formal way to deliver bad news.

Non-Defining Relative Clauses

These clauses add extra information about a noun. They are separated by commas.

Which sentence is correct?

Correct: The staff member I spoke to, who was rather rude, could not provide a delivery date. The clause "who was rather rude" gives extra information about the staff member.

Is this correct? "Alice said she would deal with it, that she didn't."

No. It should be: "Alice said she would deal with it, which she didn't." Use "which" for non-defining clauses about a situation, not "that".

Is this correct? "Your services, which were recommended highly, did not meet our expectations."

Yes, but add commas: "Your services, which were recommended very highly, did not meet our expectations." Non-defining clauses need commas on both sides.

Is this correct? "Items that we ordered did not arrive, which caused many problems."

Yes. "Several items that we ordered did not arrive, which caused a number of problems." The clause "which caused problems" adds extra information about the result.

Problem Scenarios for Your Email

Choose one of these problems and write an email. You could also use your own workplace problem.

Scenario 1: Equipment Failure

The Problem: Your new photocopier has broken again. You have only had it for three months and this is the third time. You would like a completely new replacement.

Who to write to: The photocopier company

What you want: Full replacement, not just a repair

Scenario 2: Poor Work Quality

The Problem: Your office was refurbished last month. You are not satisfied with the standard of work. Also, the company has not finished tiling one of the new bathrooms.

Who to write to: The refurbishment company

What you want: Either completion of work or a refund of part of the payment

Scenario 3: Delivery Service Issues

The Problem: Your factory has a contract with a distribution company for your product. Things have not been going well. Some drivers are regularly late and one has been rude to an important customer.

Who to write to: The distribution company manager

What you want: Improved service or contract termination

Email Structure

A formal complaint email has four clear paragraphs:

Paragraph 1 - Introduction: State your relationship with the company and mention the service or product you are writing about.

Paragraph 2 - Background: Explain the problem in detail. When did it happen? What exactly went wrong?

Paragraph 3 - The Issue: Say how the problem has affected you or your business. What has been the consequence?

Paragraph 4 - Request: State exactly what you want them to do (replace, repair, refund, improve service).

Email Writing Checklist

Choose your problem scenario
Plan the four paragraphs with relevant information
Use at least two formal collocations
Include at least one non-defining relative clause
Write in formal, professional language
Check for clear punctuation and spelling
Include appropriate greeting and closing
