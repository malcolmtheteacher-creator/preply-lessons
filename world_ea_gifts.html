The stereotype: “East Asian gift-giving is a set of strange superstitions — or a way of buying favours.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Red envelopes

In China and many Chinese communities, people give hongbao — red envelopes containing money — at Lunar New Year, weddings and birthdays. Red is the colour of luck. The amount matters less than the gesture: parents and grandparents give envelopes to children, and bosses give them to staff. The envelope is usually offered and received with both hands. Today many people also send digital red envelopes through phone apps.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

2. How is an envelope usually given and received?

1. Red is the colour of luck.

2. With both hands.

Discuss:

Part 2 — Face and reciprocity

Many Chinese, Japanese and Korean people think carefully about “face” — a person's dignity and reputation in the eyes of others. A good gift gives face to the receiver. A gift that is too cheap can look careless, but one that is too expensive can make the other person feel they must repay it. In Japan, small gifts from trips, called omiyage, are shared with colleagues, and presents are usually opened later in private, so nobody has to react in front of others.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is “face”?

2. Why might a present be opened in private?

1. A person's dignity and reputation in the eyes of others.

2. So nobody has to react or compare in front of other people.

Discuss:
  • Why can an expensive gift be uncomfortable for the person who receives it?

Part 3 — Numbers and rules

Numbers and objects carry meanings. The word for four sounds like the word for “death” in Chinese, Japanese and Korean, so sets of four are avoided. Eight sounds like “wealth” in Chinese, so it is lucky. A clock is often a poor gift in Chinese because giving a clock sounds like “attending a funeral”. People know these customs, though many younger people treat them lightly. And rules exist to keep gifts separate from bribes: China, for example, limits the gifts that officials may accept.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which number is often avoided, and why?

2. Why do some governments limit gifts to officials?

1. Four, because it sounds like the word for “death”.

2. To keep gifts separate from bribes.

Discuss:
  • Which customs of this kind do you follow even if you do not fully believe in them?

Grammar — Conditionals — If, Unless, Provided That

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
Real situations use if + present simple, with will, should or an imperative in the result:
• “If you visit a home, bring a small gift.”
• unless = if … not: “Don't open the present unless the giver asks you to.”
• provided that / as long as = only on this condition: “A gift is welcome as long as it is not too expensive.”

Going deeper

Gift-giving looks complicated because a gift carries a message: “I respect our relationship, and I will remember it.” In many societies relationships are built through small exchanges over a long time, and reciprocity — returning a gift in kind — is the glue. Western cultures have gifts too, such as wine for a host or birthday presents, but fewer fixed rules about colours, numbers or timing. The difference is one of degree, not of kind.

Worth noticing: Conditionals: if + present → will / should; unless = if … not; provided that / as long as = only if.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: If you are invited to a home in East Asia, bring a small, nicely wrapped gift — good fruit, sweets or something from your own country — and offer it with both hands. If you do not know the rules, avoid sets of four and plain black or white wrapping. The thought counts, and your host will notice the effort.
