Building Trust at Scale

C2 Level · Organisational Leadership

Maister's Trust Equation

David Maister, Charles Green, and Rob Galford created a deceptively simple formula for understanding trust:

Trust = (Credibility + Reliability + Intimacy) ÷ Self-Orientation

This means that trust is the sum of three positive factors divided by one negative factor. Even small self-orientation can devastate trust. Conversely, even high self-orientation can't destroy trust if credibility, reliability, and intimacy are strong enough.

Breaking Down the Equation

Credibility: Do you know what you're talking about? Have you done this before? Do you have expertise? People need to believe you're competent.

Reliability: Do you do what you say? Are you consistent? Can people count on you? This is about follow-through.

Intimacy: Do I feel you care about me? Not friendship—but genuine concern for my wellbeing. Do you understand my situation?

Self-Orientation: Is this about you or about me? If someone perceives you're making decisions based on what's best for you, not them, trust collapses.

Psychological Safety

Amy Edmondson's research shows that the best teams have "psychological safety"—a climate where people believe it's safe to take interpersonal risks. They can speak up without fear of being humiliated, ignored, or punished.

Psychological safety comes from leadership. When a leader:

  • Invites input and genuinely listens
  • Acknowledges mistakes and learns from them
  • Doesn't punish dissent or disagreement
  • Creates norms where it's okay to say "I don't know" or "I need help"

In psychologically safe teams, people experiment, take calculated risks, and speak up when they see problems. The organisation learns faster.

Reflection: Think of an organisation or leader you trust. Where do they score high on credibility, reliability, and intimacy? Where is their self-orientation low?

How Leaders Build Trust in Large Organisations

1. Clear, consistent communication: When something's uncertain, say so. "Here's what we know. Here's what we don't. Here's when we'll know more." This is better than silence, which people interpret as hiding something.

2. Be accessible: A CEO who walks around, asks questions, and listens builds more trust than one who only appears in scripted presentations. People believe you care when you're willing to listen to their concerns.

3. Follow through on commitments: If you say you'll fix something, fix it. If you can't, explain why and what you're doing instead. Reliability is earned through consistency.

4. Admit mistakes: "I got that wrong" is powerful. It signals that learning matters more than ego. It gives permission for others to admit mistakes too.

5. Make decisions visibly: Let people see your reasoning. Why are you cutting this project? Why are you investing in that one? When decisions are transparent, people may disagree but they trust the decision-maker.

6. Show genuine concern for people: Not performative—genuine. Do you ask about someone's family? Do you check in on how they're handling the stress? This is intimacy at scale.

The Crisis: Rebuilding Trust After Layoffs

You're the new CEO of a 500-person company. Six months ago, the previous CEO laid off 20% of staff with minimal warning. Morale is devastated. People are updating their resumes. Your job is to deliver a town hall that rebuilds trust. You have 10 minutes to deliver a speech that will be recorded and shared.

Your Opening

You need to acknowledge the elephant in the room. You can't pretend the layoffs didn't happen. What's your opening?

Example opening: "I know many of you are hurting right now. Six months ago, people you worked with, people you cared about, were let go with little warning. That was handled badly. I'm not here to defend that decision or pretend it didn't hurt. I'm here to talk about what comes next and what I'm committing to."

Why this works: Acknowledges the pain. Doesn't defend the indefensible. Shows empathy. Sets up what you're going to do differently.

Address the uncertainty

People are scared. Are there more layoffs coming? Is the company stable? You need to address this directly. What do you say?

Example: "I want to be transparent about where we stand. We're not in crisis mode. But we're also not pretending everything's fine. Our revenue is down 15% year-over-year. We made cuts because we needed to. Are there more changes coming? Possibly. Some teams may grow; others may shrink based on market demand. What I'm committing to: I won't make cuts without explanation. You'll hear directly from me before you hear rumours. And we'll move through this as openly as I can."

Why this works: Honest, not sugar-coated. Acknowledges uncertainty without promising false certainty. Commits to transparency.

Show you care about your people

People need to believe you value them beyond their utility to the company. How do you communicate this?

Example: "I believe we have an obligation to you. Not just to pay you fairly, though that matters. But to help you grow, to respect your time, to build an environment where you want to come to work. To listen when something's wrong. That means I'm going to be visible. You'll see me in the office, in your teams. I'll be asking you what I'm missing, what I'm getting wrong, what you need. And I'll be listening."

Why this works: Moves beyond transactions. Shows genuine care. Commits to being accessible.

Close with a vision

You want to leave people with something to believe in. Not false optimism, but a sense of direction. How do you end?

Example: "We have talented people here. We have products customers love. We're going to come out of this stronger and more focused. Not because I'm going to wave a magic wand. Because we're going to do the hard work together. I can't promise you that the next six months will be easy. But I can promise you that you'll be treated fairly, that your work matters, and that I'm as invested in rebuilding this as you are. Thank you for staying. Thank you for your trust. Let's prove we can do this."

Why this works: Acknowledges difficulty ahead. Builds hope without false certainty. Expresses gratitude. Invites collective action.

Trust Repair After Betrayal

Sometimes a leader or organisation breaks trust dramatically. A CEO is caught lying. Layoffs are handled cruelly. Harassment is covered up. Trust doesn't rebuild quickly.

The Steps to Repair

1. Acknowledge fully. Don't minimise. "We failed you" is better than "There was a miscommunication."

2. Take responsibility. Not just "it happened." But "I should have known. I should have acted. I was wrong."

3. Show remorse. Genuine regret, not performative apology.

4. Explain what you'll do differently. Specific, concrete changes. Not just promises, but systems that prevent it happening again.

5. Be patient with doubt. You don't get trust back immediately. People will test you. They'll assume the worst until proven otherwise. That's fair.

6. Do the work repeatedly. One apology doesn't fix it. Over time, through consistent different behaviour, trust rebuilds.

Remote Trust-Building

Trust is harder to build remotely. You can't read body language. You can't bump into each other. You can't see the casual interactions that build rapport. This requires more intentional effort:

  • Regular 1-on-1 conversations, not just status updates
  • Video on during meetings, not just audio
  • Sharing personal information (family, hobbies, challenges) so people see your humanity
  • Being more explicit about what you're thinking and why, since you don't have non-verbal communication
  • Consistency in your communication and availability

Generational differences in trust: Younger generations often trust institutions less than older generations. They've grown up with privacy breaches, corporate scandals, and social media toxicity. They're more skeptical of authority. A leader building trust with Gen Z needs to earn it through transparency and demonstrated values, not assume it based on position.

The Trust Mindset

Leaders often approach trust backwards. They think, "How do I get people to trust me?" Then they manufacture trust through communication strategies and image management. This doesn't work. People see through it.

The alternative: Start with genuine care for your people's wellbeing. Make decisions with their interests in mind, even when it costs you. Follow through on what you say. Be honest about what you don't know. Show your humanity. Over time, people will trust you because you've earned it through your actions, not because you're good at talking.
