Difficult Workplace Conversations

C2 Level · Leadership Communication

Why We Dread Difficult Conversations

Every leader knows the feeling: your stomach tightens, you delay the meeting, you rehearse what you'll say multiple times. The conversations we avoid are usually the most important ones.

The Conversations Leaders Dread Most

Firing or redundancy: Telling someone their job is ending. This is existential—it affects their livelihood, identity, and family security.

Performance issues: Confronting a colleague or peer about persistent underperformance or behavioural problems.

Destructive feedback: Delivering news that someone's potential isn't what they thought, or that a significant career move they want isn't going to happen.

Boundary violations: Addressing hygiene, ethics, or interpersonal behaviour that's affecting the team.

Upward conversations: Challenging a senior leader's decision or behaviour respectfully.

Why We Avoid Them

Fear of emotional reaction: We worry they'll cry, get angry, or shut down. We're uncomfortable with emotions we can't control.

Fear of conflict: We associate honesty with confrontation. We think directness means being unkind.

Guilt: We feel bad being the bearer of bad news or the enforcer of standards.

Uncertainty: We don't know what to say. The "right words" feel elusive. So we say nothing, or we hint at the problem indirectly.

Relationship risk: We worry that honesty will damage the relationship. Ironically, avoidance usually damages it more.

The cost of avoidance: Delayed feedback allows problems to compound. An employee doesn't know they're underperforming, so they don't improve. A boundary violation isn't addressed, so it happens again. Team dynamics corrode because the unspoken issue poisons the room. The conversation you avoid is rarely less painful when you finally have it—usually it's worse because there's history and resentment.

The DESC Framework

Describe the situation objectively. Stick to facts, not interpretations. "In the last three meetings, you've arrived 15 minutes late" is better than "You're always late."

Express the impact. Help them understand why this matters. "This affects our ability to start on time and puts pressure on the team."

Specify what you need. Be clear about the change. "I need you to arrive by 2 p.m. for our 2 p.m. meetings."

Consequences (natural or stated). "If this doesn't improve, we'll need to discuss whether you're the right fit for this role."

The SBI Framework (Situation-Behaviour-Impact)

Situation: "In yesterday's client call..." (sets context, not judgment)

Behaviour: "You interrupted the client three times..." (observable action, not character judgment)

Impact: "...and it appeared disrespectful. The client seemed uncomfortable." (specific outcome)

SBI is particularly useful because it separates the person from the behaviour. You're not saying "You're rude." You're saying "That behaviour was disrespectful."

Maintaining Dignity in Hard Conversations

The conversations that destroy relationships are usually not the hard ones—they're the ones where someone feels shamed or humiliated. Dignity is the antidote.

Four ways to maintain dignity:

1. Privacy. Have difficult conversations one-on-one, in a private space. Public humiliation never forgets.

2. Respect their identity. Acknowledge that they're more than this mistake or this performance issue. "You're a thoughtful person, and that's why I'm surprised by this behaviour."

3. Assume positive intent initially. "I'm wondering if something else is going on?" rather than "You clearly don't care."

4. Offer a path forward. Don't leave them in despair. Even in a firing conversation, clarity and support matter. "Here's what happens next. Here's how we'll help."

Scenario 1: You're telling someone they're being made redundant.

This person has been with the company for 5 years. The decision is due to restructuring—it's not about performance. They have a family and a mortgage. How do you open this conversation?

Example opening: "I've asked you in because I have some news that's going to be difficult to hear. The company has made a decision to restructure the department, and your role is being made redundant. This is not about your performance—your work has been valued. Let me explain what this means and how we're going to support you through this."

Key elements: Direct (no prolonging), compassionate tone, clarity on what's happening and why, immediate assurance of support.

Scenario 2: A team member has a persistent hygiene issue affecting the team.

They're a good person and a solid performer, but their personal hygiene is affecting the morale of nearby colleagues. You've been putting this off for months. What do you say?

Example approach: "I have something sensitive to raise with you. I care about you and this team, which is why I'm bringing this up directly rather than letting it become a bigger issue. I've had a couple of colleagues mention concern about [specific, objective observation—e.g., 'general personal hygiene']. I want to make sure everything's okay and see if there's anything we can support you with. Is everything alright?"

Key elements: Assumes positive intent (maybe there's a health issue), gives them space to explain, shows you're addressing it early, demonstrates care, not shame.

Scenario 3: A senior colleague has been dismissive and somewhat bullying toward a junior team member.

You're peers. This person is well-liked and politically connected. The junior employee has come to you (not their manager) and you feel some responsibility. How do you address this with the senior colleague?

Example approach: "Can we grab a coffee? I wanted to talk to you about something. I respect you, and I think you're an excellent leader, so I'm raising this as a peer. I've heard from someone on my team that they've felt dismissed or belittled in interactions with you. I don't think that's intentional, but it's affecting how they feel about working here. I'm curious about your take on the relationship and whether there's something we should address."

Key elements: Acknowledges their value, makes it safe to listen (framing as unintentional), specific behaviour (not personality attack), asks for their perspective, doesn't take a prosecutorial stance.

Courage in Leadership Communication

Difficult conversations require courage, but not the kind you might think. It's not the courage to be harsh or to "tell it like it is." It's the courage to care enough about someone to tell them the truth.

The Paradox of Kindness and Clarity

Many leaders believe that kindness means avoiding difficult conversations. They think delaying feedback or softening bad news is compassionate. But consider the alternative: a person who doesn't know they're underperforming keeps underperforming. An employee who doesn't know their behaviour is affecting others repeats it. Someone made redundant would rather know immediately than hear rumours for months.

Real kindness includes honesty. The most unkind thing you can do is withhold truth while smiling. The most kind thing is to tell the truth with genuine care for the person's wellbeing.

Cultural Approaches to Difficult Conversations

Direct cultures (e.g., Dutch, German, American): Expect clarity and low context. "I need to give you some feedback" followed by specific criticism is normal and expected. Indirectness feels patronising.

Indirect cultures (e.g., Japanese, Thai, many Middle Eastern): Prefer context and relationship preservation. Direct criticism can be deeply shaming. Skilled leaders in these contexts communicate difficult messages through implication, private feedback, or via a trusted intermediary.

Hierarchical cultures: Senior leaders deliver difficult messages formally. Challenging upward is delicate. Peer feedback is unusual.

Egalitarian cultures: Peers can challenge peers. Feedback flows in all directions. Titles don't exempt people from accountability.

The key is to adapt your directness to the cultural context without abandoning honesty.
