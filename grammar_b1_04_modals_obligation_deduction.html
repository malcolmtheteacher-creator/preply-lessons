Rules That Come From Outside: Must vs Have To

Both mean "it's necessary" — but who's making the rule changes which one sounds natural.

Spot the pattern

Three things people say about obligation:

  • "I have to renew my passport before the trip — it expires in June."
  • "I really must call my sister back, I keep forgetting and it's bothering me."
  • "You have to show ID at the reception desk, it's building policy."

Before you check: in which sentences is the rule coming from the speaker's own feeling, and in which is it an outside rule or fact? Say it before you look.

Check the pattern CLICK
The passport and the ID rule are facts imposed from outside — English almost always uses have to for those. The phone call is the speaker's own sense of duty or guilt — that's exactly where must feels natural. In everyday speech, "have to" is far more common overall; "must" is reserved for personal, urgent, or official-sounding obligation.
have to / has to + base verb  (outside rule, most everyday obligation)
must + base verb  (personal feeling, or a written/official rule)

One rule, two ways to say it

"The airline says: bags must not exceed 20kg." (official notice)
→
In conversation: "Your bag has to be under 20kg, or they'll charge you."

Same rule — but a friend telling you about it in conversation reaches for "has to", not "must". Notice too: there's no "must to" — after must, the verb is bare, no "to".

No Obligation or Not Allowed? Mustn't vs Don't Have To

These two look similar but mean opposite things — one of the most common mix-ups at this level.

Spot the pattern

Two flatmates talking about the flat:

  • "You don't have to do the dishes tonight, I already did them."
  • "You mustn't leave the front door unlocked — it's a fire risk, the landlord was really clear about that."

Which one means "it's not necessary, it's your choice", and which one means "this is forbidden, don't do it"? Decide before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Don't have to = no obligation, free choice — the dishes are done, so there's simply nothing to do. Mustn't = forbidden, a real prohibition — leaving the door unlocked is against the rule. This is the opposite of what many learners guess, because in their own language the negative of "must" often just means "no obligation" — in English it means "don't you dare."
don't/doesn't have to + base verb  → not necessary, your choice
mustn't + base verb  → forbidden, prohibited

Related: permission and expectation

Two more everyday phrases for the same territory:

  • be allowed to — permission: "Are we allowed to bring guests to the pool?"
  • be supposed to — an expectation, often broken: "You're supposed to book online, but people just turn up."
be (not) allowed to + base verb
be (not) supposed to + base verb

Looking Back: "You Should Have Told Me!"

For advice about the past — something didn't happen the way it should have, and now it's too late to change it.

Spot the pattern

Three moments of hindsight:

  • "You should have called me — I was worried sick when you didn't turn up."
  • "I shouldn't have eaten that whole pizza, I feel awful now."
  • "We should have left earlier — now we're stuck in traffic and we'll miss the start."

What's the structure, and is the speaker talking about now or about the past? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
should(n't) have + past participle (called, eaten, left). It's entirely about the past — a good idea that came too late, or a mistake that's already been made. Nothing can be done about it now — that's exactly what gives it that regretful feel. In speech it's often said fast: "should've" or "shoulda".
should have + past participle  (a good idea, too late now)
shouldn't have + past participle  (a mistake, already done)

From advice-in-the-moment to regret-after-the-fact

In the moment: "You should tell your boss about the mistake."
→
Too late now: "You should have told your boss — now it's much worse."

Watch the participle: not "should have tell" and not "should have told" spelled as "should have telled" — the verb goes into its past-participle form, the same one you'd use after "have" anywhere else.

Your turn

1. Your friend didn't bring an umbrella and got soaked walking home in the rain.

2. You spent your whole Saturday on your phone instead of finishing the project that's now overdue.

Admit it to yourself: "I so much time on my phone — now I'm behind."

One possible answer: "I used to go running" describes a past habit that has stopped ("I used to go running every morning, but I don't anymore"). "I go running" is a present habit that's still true now ("I go running every Sunday").
