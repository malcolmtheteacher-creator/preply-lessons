Consulting Client-Facing B2-C1

Resetting Expectations When a Project is Behind Schedule

Learn how to deliver difficult news to clients while maintaining trust and moving the relationship forward.

The Real Challenge

You're two weeks into a consulting engagement. The original timeline said you'd deliver the first draft of the strategy document by Friday. It's Wednesday, and you know Friday isn't happening.

Maybe the scope expanded. Maybe data collection took longer than expected. Maybe the client's internal politics complicated things. The reason matters less than the reality: you have to tell the client the timeline has slipped.

"The longer you wait to deliver bad news, the worse it gets. Clients can forgive delays. They don't forgive surprises."

— Partner at a Big Four consulting firm

Why This Conversation Goes Wrong

What Consultants Often Say
"Hi James, I wanted to flag that we've hit some challenges with the data collection and we're going to need a bit more time on the deliverable. Sorry about that — we'll get you something as soon as we can."
What the Client Hears
"We're behind. I don't know why. I don't know how long it will take. I don't have a plan. Please don't be mad at me."

Vague language signals lack of control. Even if the delay isn't your fault, how you communicate it determines whether the client sees you as a trusted partner or a vendor making excuses.

The Goal

By the end of this lesson, you'll be able to:

  • Deliver difficult news clearly and confidently
  • Explain the "why" without sounding defensive
  • Present a realistic revised plan
  • Maintain (and even strengthen) client trust

The RESET Framework

When you need to reset expectations with a client, follow the RESET structure to maintain credibility and move forward productively.

RESET
R Reality State the situation clearly
E Explanation Give the key reason (briefly)
S Solution Present your revised plan
E Evidence Show why this plan is solid
T Together Invite their input

Why RESET Works

R - Reality: Don't bury the news. Open with the key message: "We won't hit the Friday deadline." Clients respect directness.

E - Explanation: Give context, not excuses. One or two sentences explaining what happened. Avoid blame language — focus on circumstances, not people.

S - Solution: This is the heart of the conversation. Don't just announce a problem — present a plan. Specific dates, specific deliverables, specific next steps.

E - Evidence: Explain why this plan will work. What have you done to de-risk it? What's different now?

T - Together: End by inviting collaboration. "Does this work for your timeline?" or "What do we need to adjust?" This shows you see them as a partner, not someone to manage.

The Key Principle

Don't apologise for the situation. Apologise for the impact. Then show you have a plan.

"I'm sorry for the delay" sounds weak. "I know this creates challenges for your board presentation — here's how we can address that" sounds like leadership.

Phrases That Work

Click each phrase to see when and how to use it.

Opening: State Reality Clearly

"I want to give you an update on our timeline. We won't be able to deliver the full draft by Friday as planned."
When to use: Opening the conversation. Direct, clear, no hedging.
Why it works: "I want to give you an update" signals proactivity. You're controlling the narrative, not reacting to their complaint.
"I need to flag a change to our original timeline."
When to use: A softer opening when the relationship is good.
Why it works: "Flag" is professional consulting language. It implies you're managing risks proactively.

Explanation: Context Without Excuses

"The data collection phase took longer than we anticipated. We uncovered some complexity in the sales data that required additional validation."
When to use: When delays are due to technical/scope issues.
Why it works: "Uncovered complexity" suggests thoroughness, not failure. You're not blaming anyone — you're explaining what happened.
"Getting access to the regional teams took longer than we'd planned. We've now completed all the interviews."
When to use: When delays were partly caused by the client (diplomatically).
Why it works: States the fact without accusation. The second sentence shifts to progress — you're not dwelling on problems.

Solution: The Revised Plan

"Here's what I'm proposing: we deliver a working draft by Tuesday, which gives you time to review before the steering committee on Thursday."
When to use: Presenting the new timeline with client constraints in mind.
Why it works: Shows you understand their world. You're not just picking a date — you're solving their problem.
"I'd like to suggest we split the delivery: the market analysis on Monday, and the recommendations by Thursday. This way you have something to work with earlier."
When to use: When you can deliver partial value sooner.
Why it works: Phased delivery shows flexibility and gives the client something to show for the time.

Evidence: Why This Plan Will Work

"We've now completed all the data gathering, so the remaining work is synthesis and writing — which we can control."
When to use: Showing the risk has been removed.
Why it works: Distinguishes between "things that depend on others" and "things we control." Builds confidence.
"I've blocked the team's calendar for the next three days to ensure we hit this date."
When to use: Showing you've taken action to protect the new deadline.
Why it works: Concrete action demonstrates commitment.

Together: Inviting Collaboration

"Does this revised timeline work with your internal deadlines? I want to make sure we're aligned."
When to use: Closing the conversation.
Why it works: Gives them agency. They're not being told — they're being consulted.
"Is there anything on your end that's changed that I should factor into this plan?"
When to use: Opening dialogue about their constraints.
Why it works: Sometimes clients have flexibility they haven't mentioned. This question gives them permission to share it.

Guided Practice

Choose a scenario to practice. Write how you would communicate the delay using the RESET framework.

Scenario 1: Scope Creep
You're doing a market entry strategy. The original scope was 5 markets. After the kick-off meeting, the CEO asked you to add 3 more markets "while you're at it." This wasn't formally added to the scope, but you felt you couldn't say no. Now you're behind.
Scenario 2: Client-Side Delays
Your project depends on data from the client's finance team. They promised it by last Monday. It arrived on Thursday, and half of it was incomplete. You've lost nearly a week through no fault of your own.
Scenario 3: Underestimated Complexity
During the proposal phase, you estimated 3 weeks for the analysis. Now that you're in the work, you've realised the data is messier and the stakeholder landscape more complex than expected. You need an extra week.

Your Response

Write how you would communicate this to your client. Use the RESET framework: Reality → Explanation → Solution → Evidence → Together.

📝 Framework Check
    Model Answer: Scope Creep
    "Hi Sarah, I wanted to give you an update on our timeline. We won't be able to deliver the full market analysis by the 15th as originally planned.

    Since our kick-off, the scope has grown from 5 markets to 8. That's the right call given your expansion ambitions, but it does impact our timeline.

    Here's what I'd like to propose: we deliver the analysis for the 5 original priority markets by the 15th as planned, and the additional 3 markets by the 22nd. This way you have what you need for the board meeting, and we deliver the complete picture the following week.

    We've already completed the data gathering for all 8 markets, so the remaining work is analysis and synthesis — which is fully within our control.

    Does this work with your internal timeline? If the board needs all 8 markets together, we can discuss options."
    Model Answer: Client-Side Delays
    "Hi David, I need to flag a change to our timeline. We won't be able to deliver the full financial analysis by Friday.

    The data from finance arrived later than planned, and we needed to do some additional validation on the regional figures. We've now completed that work.

    I'm proposing we deliver on Tuesday. This gives us the weekend to complete the analysis properly, and you'll still have it before your Wednesday leadership meeting.

    We're in a much stronger position now — all the data is validated and the model is built. The remaining work is running the scenarios and writing up the findings.

    Does Tuesday work? I want to make sure we're aligned with what you need for Wednesday."
    Model Answer: Underestimated Complexity
    "Hi Michelle, I wanted to have a transparent conversation about our timeline.

    Now that we're deep into the analysis, we've found that the stakeholder landscape is more complex than we anticipated during scoping. This is actually a good thing — it means our recommendations will be more nuanced and more implementable — but it does mean we need an additional week.

    I'm proposing we move the final delivery from the 20th to the 27th. I'd suggest we use the time in between to share an interim read-out with you on the 22nd, so you can see where we're heading and give us feedback before we finalise.

    This approach means the final document will be stronger, and there won't be surprises when you see it.

    What are your thoughts? Is there anything about your timeline I should be factoring in?"

    Real-Time Simulation

    Time to put it all together. You have 3 minutes to respond to this situation.

    The Situation
    You're a senior consultant on a transformation project. Your manager is on leave this week, and the client sponsor (James, the CFO) has just sent you this email. The team hasn't made as much progress as planned because two team members were pulled to another project for three days.

    From: James Chen (CFO)
    Subject: Quick check on Friday deliverable

    Hi,

    Just wanted to confirm we're still on track for the operating model options on Friday. I've scheduled time with the CEO on Monday to walk her through them. Let me know if there are any issues.

    Thanks,
    James

    3:00

    Your Reply to James

    📝 Response Analysis
      Model Response
      "Hi James,

      Thanks for checking in. I need to flag a change to our timeline.

      We won't have the full operating model options ready by Friday. The team hit some resourcing constraints earlier this week that set us back a few days.

      Here's what I'd like to propose: we send you a draft on Saturday morning, which gives you Sunday to review before your Monday session with the CEO. The core content will be complete — we just need the extra day for final quality checks.

      Alternatively, I could walk you through what we have on Friday afternoon — it won't be polished, but it would give you a sense of where we're landing and let us incorporate any feedback before the final version.

      The team is fully focused on this now, and the analysis is largely done — the remaining work is synthesis and presentation.

      What would work best for you and the CEO meeting?

      Best,
      [Your name]"

      Wrap-Up

      What You Learned

      🎯

      Be Direct

      State the reality clearly upfront — don't bury bad news

      🔄

      Use RESET

      Reality → Explanation → Solution → Evidence → Together

      📋

      Lead with Solutions

      Don't just announce problems — present a plan

      🤝

      Invite Collaboration

      End by asking for their input — they're partners, not problems

      Key Phrases to Remember

      • "I want to give you an update on our timeline."
      • "We won't be able to deliver [X] by [date] as planned."
      • "Here's what I'm proposing..."
      • "The remaining work is [X], which we can control."
      • "Does this work with your internal deadlines?"

      Common Mistakes to Avoid

      • ❌ Vague language: "We might need a bit more time"
      • ❌ Over-apologising: "I'm so sorry, this is terrible"
      • ❌ Blaming others: "The client didn't send us the data"
      • ❌ No solution: Announcing the problem without a plan
      • ❌ Waiting too long: Telling them on Thursday about a Friday deadline
      Clients don't expect perfection. They expect professionalism. How you handle setbacks reveals more about your capabilities than how you handle things going right.
