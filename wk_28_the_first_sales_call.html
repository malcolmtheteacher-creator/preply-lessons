The First Sales Call

You sell something good. She has never heard of you, didn't ask you to call, and has a meeting in six minutes. Everything you'll ever win from this number is decided in your first two sentences.

The Scene 🎬

The hardest conversation in professional English: the one the other person never asked for.

You work for a firm that makes energy-monitoring kit for factories — genuinely good kit, the kind that pays for itself in a year. Your list says Ferreira, operations director, mid-sized plant, currently with a competitor. Your native-speaker colleagues make these calls on charm. You're going to make them on structure — and structure, it turns out, beats charm.

The cold call has a physics all its own. She owes you nothing. Her default answer is no, her finger is on the red button, and every second of her attention is borrowed, not given. So the call is built as a ladder of small honest permissions: twenty seconds → two minutes → one page by email. Never the meeting, never the deal — not today. The seller who asks small and true gets further than the one who asks big and shiny.

And the ending matters more than it seems. Most of these calls end in no. The craft is leaving each no so cleanly that the door stays warm — because the supplier she's "well looked after" by today will, one day, fail her. On that day, the rep who left well gets the call.

The Conversation 💬

Tuesday, 9:50am — the golden window between the morning rush and the meetings. She picks up on the second ring.

Ms FerreiraFerreira.
YouMs Ferreira — my name's Alex Costa, from Meridian Energy Systems. You don't know me, and this is a sales call. Have I caught you at a terrible time, or do you have two minutes?
Ms Ferreira…Points for honesty. You've got until my coffee's poured. Go.
YouThen I'll be quick. The reason I'm calling you specifically is that your plant runs three shifts, and three-shift plants are where our monitoring pays back fastest — usually inside a year. Before I say anything else, though — is energy cost something you deal with at all, or does that sit elsewhere?
Ms FerreiraNo, that's mine. It sits painfully on my desk, in fact. But we've got monitoring — we're with Enerlog, have been for years.
YouEnerlog's solid kit. Can I ask what you're using it for at the moment — the full metering, or just the site-level dashboard?
Ms Ferreira…Site-level. The machine-level stuff was quoted, but the price was absurd.
YouThat's the gap we live in, as it happens. Machine-level monitoring, but priced per line rather than per point — compared to what that quote probably looked like, it usually works out at about a third.
Ms FerreiraA third. Mm. Everyone's cheaper on the phone, Mr Costa. And I'm not switching suppliers because of one call, you understand — the audit alone would eat any saving.
YouCompletely fair — and I'm not asking you to change anything today. Enerlog stays, the dashboard stays. Machine-level is an addition, not a replacement — it sits alongside what you have.
Ms Ferreira…Alongside. You didn't lead with that.
YouI should have. So — how about I send one page: what it measures, what it costs per line, and the payback maths for a three-shift plant. If it's not relevant, you bin it and I won't chase you.
Ms FerreiraOne page. Not a deck, Mr Costa. If a deck arrives, I delete it on principle.
YouOne page, no deck — you have my word. And if it does look interesting, when's a better week to try you? I know this one's bad.
Ms FerreiraAfter the twentieth. Budget review's done by then, which — as you clearly know, since you knew my shift pattern — is not a coincidence you invented.
YouI'd love to claim that level of planning. After the twentieth, then. And if the page convinces you you're fine as you are — fair enough. It sounds like you're well looked after.
Ms FerreiraWe are. Mostly. …Send the page. And Mr Costa — "this is a sales call" was the only reason you got past hello. Most of you pretend to be my old friend.
YouMost of us should stop. If anything changes before the twentieth, you've got my number — thanks for hearing me out, Ms Ferreira.

  2. What's the real gap You finds — and which question uncovered it?
  3. What exactly has been agreed by the end of the call? List every commitment, both sides.
1. "You don't know me, and this is a sales call" — the honest label; and "have I caught you at a terrible time, or do you have two minutes?" — asking permission instead of stealing time. She says herself it's the only reason the call survived hello.

2. She has site-level monitoring but not machine-level — the quote was "absurd". Uncovered by "Can I ask what you're using it for at the moment — the full metering, or just the site-level dashboard?" Questions found the gap; the pitch only had to fit it.

3. You sends exactly one page (no deck, no chasing); she reads or bins it; any follow-up waits until after the twentieth, when budget review is done. Nobody bought anything — and the call was a success.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for calling strangers who didn't ask you to — sales, partnerships, recruitment, fundraising. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The cold caller's tell is speed — racing to fit the pitch in before the hang-up. Slow down to the speed of a person with something genuinely useful to say. And practise the first two sentences until they're automatic, because you will never say them calmly while you're still deciding them.

⏱️ The first twenty seconds

Phrase 1

The job: asking for time you haven't been given. Which opening?

A. "Hi! How are you today? Lovely weather we're having!"
B. "Have I caught you at a terrible time, or do you have two minutes?"
C. "This will only take thirty seconds of your valuable time, I promise."
B — "Have I caught you at a terrible time, or do you have two minutes?"
A is the fake-friend opening that gets calls killed on principle. C makes a promise everyone knows is false — no sales call has ever taken thirty seconds. B does something rare: it offers a genuine exit ("terrible time") before asking for anything, and it names an honest amount ("two minutes"). People given a real chance to say no often don't — and a yes you were given is worth ten you stole.

Take it anywhere: "Have I caught you at a terrible time, or do you have two minutes?" Any unrequested call — including internal ones.
Phrase 2

The job: proving this isn't a script — you chose her. Complete it:

"The reason I'm calling you ___ is that your plant runs three shifts."

"The reason I'm calling you specifically is that your plant runs three shifts."
Specifically is the word that separates you from the list-readers — but only if what follows proves it. One researched fact about her operation ("three shifts") buys more trust than five minutes of product praise, because it answers the question every cold-called person is silently asking: why me? No real answer to that question, no real call.

Take it anywhere: "The reason I'm calling you specifically is ___." Do the homework the sentence demands, or don't dial.
Phrase 3

The job: checking she's even the right person — before the pitch, not after. Complete it:

"Before I say anything else — is energy cost something you ___ ___ at all?"

"Before I say anything else — is energy cost something you deal with at all?"
The qualifying question, and the discipline is in "before I say anything else": you're refusing to pitch until you know the pitch has an owner. Wrong person? You've saved you both three minutes and can ask who's right. Right person? She's just told you the problem is hers — "it sits painfully on my desk" — and now she's participating, not receiving.

Take it anywhere: "Before I say anything else — is ___ something you deal with at all?" The three-second filter that saves whole afternoons.

🔍 Questions before pitch

Phrase 4

The job: mapping what she already has — respectfully. Complete it:

"Can I ask what you're ___ at the ___?"

"Can I ask what you're using at the moment?"
The amateur hears "we're with Enerlog" and starts attacking Enerlog. The professional asks this — and listens. The status quo always has a gap (here: no machine-level metering, "the price was absurd"), and the gap is the only honest place a pitch can live. Note the manners of "can I ask" — you're requesting information about her house, and asking permission at the door.

Take it anywhere: "Can I ask what you're using at the moment?" Sales, hiring, consulting — map before you pitch.
Phrase 5

The job: pricing against what they know, not in the abstract. Complete it:

"Compared to what it's ___ you now, it usually works out at about a third."

"Compared to what it's costing you now, it usually works out at about a third."
Numbers without anchors bounce off. "Nineteen thousand a year" means nothing down a phone line; "about a third of the quote that made you wince" lands instantly, because she supplies the other number herself. "Usually" keeps you honest — you haven't seen her plant yet, and pretending precision you don't have is how credibility dies.

Take it anywhere: "Compared to what it's costing you now, it usually works out ___." Price is a comparison; always name the thing you're comparing to.
Phrase 6

The job: collapsing the threat that's making her defensive. Put the pieces in order:

to change anythingI'm not asking youtoday.
"I'm not asking you to change anything today."
The turning point of the whole call. Her defences were built against a rip-and-replace sale — the switch, the audit, the risk. This sentence dismantles the threat she was actually resisting, and what's left is a small, safe addition "alongside what you have". Watch for the moment the other person is saying no to something you're not selling; this is the sentence for it.

Take it anywhere: "I'm not asking you to change anything today." Lower the stakes and the wall comes down with them.

🚪 Closing small, leaving warm

Phrase 7

The job: the ask — so small that refusing takes more effort than accepting. Complete it:

"How about I send one page — and if it's not relevant, you ___ it?"

"How about I send one page — and if it's not relevant, you bin it?"
Bin it — throw it away, no ceremony. The genius is in giving her the exit as part of the offer: one page, her verdict, no chasing. You're not asking for a meeting, a demo or a decision — you're asking for thirty seconds of reading she can abandon. Small honest asks convert; big shiny ones get "send me some information", which means goodbye.

Take it anywhere: "How about I send one page — and if it's not relevant, bin it?" The close that respects its way through the door. Then keep the promise: one page.
Phrase 8

The job: turning "this is a bad week" into an appointment. Complete it:

"When's a better ___ to ___ you?"

"When's a better week to try you?"
"Now's not a good time" is the softest brush-off in business — and most callers accept it as a no, which it isn't. This question converts it into a date, and it makes her the author of the follow-up: "after the twentieth" is her appointment now, not your intrusion. When you call on the twenty-first, you're keeping her arrangement.

Take it anywhere: "When's a better week to try you?" Every "not now" contains a "when" — go and get it.
Phrase 9

The job: taking the no with grace — and meaning it. Complete it:

"Fair enough — it sounds like you're well ___ ___."

"Fair enough — it sounds like you're well looked after."
The hardest sentence in sales, because it concedes the round completely — no "but", no last jab at the competitor, no door wedged open with your foot. And that's exactly why it works: praising her current arrangement marks you as someone who tells the truth even when it costs a sale. Suppliers stumble. Contracts end. The rep who said this is the one who gets remembered kindly when they do.

Take it anywhere: "Fair enough — it sounds like you're well looked after." Lose the round; win the relationship.
Phrase 10

The job: the last line — the door left open without a foot in it. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "If anything changes, you've got my number — thanks for hearing me out."
Two gifts in one line. "If anything changes" plants the only seed worth planting — not buy from me but remember me when the world moves. And "thanks for hearing me out" acknowledges what she actually gave you: borrowed minutes she didn't owe. Gratitude for attention, not for money, is what separates a professional from a pest.

Take it anywhere: "If anything changes, you've got my number." Then — the real skill — actually don't chase.

🔑 Make them yours

These ten work on any stranger's phone number you'll ever dial with a purpose. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"The reason I'm calling you specifically is ___." — pick a real prospect, client or contact. What's the researched fact that earns the word "specifically"?

"Before I say anything else — is ___ something you deal with at all?" — build your qualifying question for what you actually sell or do.

"Fair enough — it sounds like you're well looked after." — think of a no you took badly. What would taking it this way have cost — and saved?

⚡ The twist

Round 1 again — and mid-call, Ms Ferreira goes quiet, then says: "Actually. Our supplier failed their audit last month — I'm not supposed to say that. If your kit is real: two thousand points, installed by March, or don't waste my time. Can you do it?"

The door you've been knocking on politely just swung wide open — and the honest answer is that March is impossible; May, realistically. She's offering you the deal and the deadline in one breath. Overpromise and you'll lose her forever in April. Tell the truth and you might lose her now. Sell it straight.

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you label the call honestly and still keep the line open?
  • Can you prove "specifically" with one researched fact?
  • Do you qualify and map before a word of pitch?
  • Is your ask small enough to accept in one breath?
  • Can you take a no so well they'd answer your next call?

The Echo 🕰️

Around 1482, a thirty-year-old Leonardo da Vinci wanted a job at the court of Ludovico Sforza, ruler of Milan. He didn't send a portfolio of paintings. He sent what may be history's most famous cold pitch: a letter of ten numbered points, every one about Ludovico's problems — portable bridges for his campaigns, siege engines, cannon, armoured vehicles. The painting, the sculpture, the genius? One line, near the end, almost an afterthought.

Leonardo had researched his prospect. Milan was arming for war; the Duke didn't need a portrait, he needed engineering. So the greatest artist alive led with the customer's need and buried his own brilliance — and it worked. He got Milan, and Milan eventually got The Last Supper, a mural that began, in a sense, as the upsell.

Five centuries of selling have added nothing to the method: know why you're calling this person, speak to their problem before your product, and keep your masterpiece for the second meeting.
