The Gap Between Thinking and Speaking

You already know how to think like a historian. You evaluate sources, weigh evidence, construct arguments, challenge interpretations. The problem is expressing all of that in English with the right level of precision. Look at the difference:

Imprecise

"I think Rome fell because of barbarians. Many people say this. It was a big problem and many things happened. Some historians don't agree."

Precise

"The evidence suggests that external pressure was a contributing factor, though it would be an oversimplification to reduce the collapse to a single cause. Scholars like Ward-Perkins argue for catastrophe, whereas Brown's work points to a more gradual transformation."

The thinking is the same. The language is completely different. Today you'll get the English toolkit to express what you already know how to think.

Four Tools for Academic Discussion

These are the core language patterns you need. Click each one to expand.

◆ Tool 1: Hedging Certainty
What it does

Shows you understand that historical claims are interpretations, not facts. This is the difference between a student and a scholar.

The evidence suggests that... Use when the data points in a direction but doesn't prove it definitively
It could be argued that... Introduces a position you're presenting as one possible reading, not the only one
This might indicate... Careful inference — you're drawing a conclusion but flagging uncertainty
There is reason to believe... Stronger than "might" but still hedged — you have grounds but not proof
Example: "The archaeological record suggests that trade networks survived well into the 5th century, which might indicate that economic collapse was less sudden than Gibbon assumed."
Try it now: Think of a widely-held historical belief in your area of expertise that you think is too simplistic. Express your doubt using at least two hedging phrases. Don't say "I think it's wrong" — say it like a scholar.
◆ Tool 2: Evaluating Evidence
What it does

Lets you talk ABOUT evidence — its strengths, limitations, and what it does and doesn't tell us.

Based on [source], we can infer... Drawing a logical conclusion from specific evidence
The main limitation of this evidence is... Identifying what a source CANNOT tell us
This source tells us... but doesn't show... Separating what we know from what we're assuming
We need to consider the context in which... Signalling that a source must be read within its historical moment
Example: "Procopius gives us a vivid account of Justinian's reign, but the main limitation of this evidence is that Procopius had personal grievances against the emperor. We need to consider the context in which the Secret History was written before treating it as reliable testimony."
Try it now: Think of a primary source you know well from your own research. Evaluate it aloud: what does it tell us, what doesn't it show, and what limitation should a reader be aware of?
◆ Tool 3: Structuring Two-Sided Arguments
What it does

Lets you present competing interpretations fairly before giving your own position. Essential for academic debate.

On one hand... on the other... Classic two-sided structure — use sparingly, it can sound formulaic
While it's true that... it's worth noting... Concedes a point to the other side, then adds your counterpoint
The strongest argument for X is... however... Shows you've engaged with the best version of the opposing view
[Scholar A] argues... whereas [Scholar B] contends... Attributing positions to specific scholars — the hallmark of academic discussion
Example: "While it's true that the Weimar Republic faced severe economic pressures, it's worth noting that many democracies survived similar crises in the 1930s. The question is not whether the economy was bad, but why Germany's political institutions were uniquely vulnerable."
Try it now: Take a historiographical debate you know well. Present BOTH sides fairly in 60 seconds, using at least two of these structures. Then say which side you lean towards and why.
◆ Tool 4: Disagreeing Like a Scholar
What it does

Lets you challenge an interpretation without being dismissive. In academic culture, HOW you disagree signals your credibility.

I'd take issue with the claim that... Direct but respectful — targets the CLAIM, not the person
That's a fair point, but it overlooks... Acknowledges merit before identifying a gap
I'm not entirely convinced, because... Soft disagreement — leaves room for persuasion
That reading doesn't fully account for... Challenges the completeness of an interpretation, not its validity
Example: "I'd take issue with the claim that the Treaty of Versailles caused the Second World War. That reading doesn't fully account for the domestic political choices made in Germany between 1929 and 1933, which were not inevitable consequences of the treaty."
Try it now: Think of a historical argument you've encountered that you believe is flawed or incomplete. Disagree with it aloud using two of these expressions. Remember: target the argument, not the person.

The Pattern

Notice that all four tools share a structure: acknowledge first, then add nuance. Academic discussion isn't about proving the other person wrong — it's about showing that the picture is more complex than a single interpretation allows.

Building Arguments: From Model to You

Listen to this response:

The question is: "Was the French Revolution inevitable?"

"That's a question historians have debated for over two centuries, so I'll try to be precise about what I mean. The evidence suggests that France's fiscal crisis created the conditions for revolution, but conditions are not causes. While it's true that the monarchy was under severe financial pressure, it's worth noting that other European states faced comparable crises without collapsing. I'd take issue with purely structural explanations because they don't fully account for the contingent events of 1789 — the Estates-General being called, the Tennis Court Oath, the fall of the Bastille. The strongest argument for inevitability is the depth of social inequality, however, inequality alone doesn't produce revolutions. My position is that the revolution was probable but not inevitable — and that distinction matters."

Show Analysis

Hedging: "The evidence suggests" + "probable but not inevitable" — careful about certainty levels

Evidence evaluation: "conditions are not causes" — distinguishes between different types of explanation

Two-sided structure: "While it's true that... it's worth noting" + "The strongest argument for... however" — presents both sides before concluding

Academic disagreement: "I'd take issue with" + "they don't fully account for" — challenges a position respectfully

Now let's build one together

The question: "Did the Roman Empire fall or transform?"

This is a famous debate between two positions. Use your historical knowledge freely — but focus on expressing it with the toolkit language.

Step 1: Open with a hedge

Don't jump to your answer. Start by signalling the complexity. Use one of the hedging phrases to frame the question. Say it aloud.

Step 2: Present both sides

Give the catastrophist position AND the transformation position, each in 2-3 sentences. Use "While it's true that..." or "[Scholar] argues... whereas..." to structure it.

Step 3: Evaluate the evidence

Pick ONE piece of evidence (archaeological, textual, economic — whatever you know) and evaluate it. What does it tell us? What doesn't it show? Use the evaluation phrases.

Step 4: Take a position

Now say what YOU think, and why. If you disagree with one side, use an academic disagreement phrase. Your position should be nuanced, not absolute.

Your turn — a question from your own field

The Debate: Arguing With Evidence

Here are four historical questions with conflicting evidence. Each one has real complexity — there is no "right answer."

Select 2 questions that interest you. For each one, speak for 4-5 minutes: present both sides, evaluate the evidence, and argue your position. Use all four toolkit areas naturally. You'll be challenged with follow-up questions.
1

Was the Printing Press Revolutionary?

Elizabeth Eisenstein argued the printing press fundamentally transformed European culture — spreading literacy, enabling the Reformation, accelerating the Scientific Revolution. Adrian Johns challenged this, arguing that print culture was messy, unreliable, and pirated — and that the "revolutionary" narrative is a retrospective construction. Who's closer to the truth? What does the evidence actually show?

Target: 4-5 mins | Use: "The evidence suggests..." + "While it's true that... it's worth noting..." + "I'd take issue with..."
2

Does Great Man History Still Have Value?

The discipline moved towards structural, social, and cultural history decades ago. Yet popular history still centres on individuals — and even academic historians sometimes struggle to explain change without agency. Was Carlyle wrong, or was the reaction against him an overcorrection? Can you write meaningful history without protagonists?

Target: 4-5 mins | Use: "It could be argued that..." + "That's a fair point, but it overlooks..." + "The strongest argument for X is... however..."
3

How Should We Use Oral History?

Oral history gives voice to people excluded from the written record — but memory is unreliable, shaped by the present, and mediated by the interviewer. Alessandro Portelli argued that the "errors" in oral testimony are themselves historically significant. Others say oral sources must be corroborated or they're just anecdote. Where do you stand? What status should oral evidence have?

Target: 4-5 mins | Use: "Based on [evidence], we can infer..." + "The main limitation of this evidence is..." + "I'm not entirely convinced, because..."
4

Can History Be Objective?

Ranke wanted to show the past "as it actually was." Postmodernists like Hayden White argued that history is a literary act — historians impose narrative structures on chaotic events. Most working historians land somewhere in between. But where exactly? Is objectivity a meaningful goal, a useful fiction, or a harmful illusion?

Target: 4-5 mins | Use: "[Scholar A] argues... whereas [Scholar B] contends..." + "That reading doesn't fully account for..." + "There is reason to believe..."

Coaching Note

Listen for two things: (1) whether the toolkit language is doing real work in the argument or just decorating it, and (2) whether the student is calibrating their certainty — do they distinguish between what they're sure of and what they're speculating about? The goal is precision, not vocabulary display.

4. What's the difference between "The evidence proves..." and "The evidence suggests..."?

Check
"Proves" claims certainty — almost never appropriate in historical discussion. "Suggests" acknowledges that evidence points in a direction without closing off other interpretations. Scholars hedge because historical evidence is almost always incomplete and open to multiple readings.
