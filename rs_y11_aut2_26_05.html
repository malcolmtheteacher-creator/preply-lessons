Id-ul-Fitr: The Festival of Breaking the Fast

Id-ul-Fitr (also called Eid al-Fitr) marks the end of Ramadan, the month of fasting in Islam. It is a joyful celebration of spiritual achievement and communal gratitude.

When?

  • Celebrated on the 1st of Shawwal (the month following Ramadan)
  • In 2026, expected around April 12-13
  • Lasts 1-3 days depending on country and tradition

Why?

After a month of spiritual discipline, prayer, and fasting from dawn to sunset, Muslims celebrate with their families and communities.

Qur'an 2:185: "The month of Ramadan... So whoever is present with you of the month, let him fast it."

Key Rituals of Id-ul-Fitr

Zakat ul-Fitr

Giving charitable alms before the Eid prayers:

  • Each person (or each family) gives to the poor
  • Usually food (rice, dates, money equivalent)
  • Ensures the poor can also celebrate Eid
  • Obligatory act of worship, not optional

Special Prayers

  • Community gathers before sunrise for special Eid prayers
  • Prayers include extra glorifications of Allah (Takbir)
  • Sermons explain Ramadan's spiritual lessons

Social Celebration

  • New clothes, particularly for children
  • Festive meals and family gatherings
  • Visiting friends and extended family
  • Exchange of gifts and greetings

Ashura: A Festival of Mourning (Particularly in Shi'a Islam)

Ashura is observed on the 9th-10th of Muharram (the first month of the Islamic calendar). It has very different meanings in Sunni and Shi'a Islam.

Sunni Tradition

In Sunni Islam, Ashura commemorates the safe passage of Prophet Moses and the Israelites across the Red Sea, escaping from Pharaoh. The Qur'an records this event in Surah Al-A'raf.

  • Some Sunni Muslims observe a voluntary fast on the 9th-10th Muharram
  • This fasting is considered highly rewarded but not obligatory
  • The focus is on gratitude for divine deliverance and protection

Hadith (Sahih Bukhari): Prophet Muhammad fasted on the Day of Ashura and encouraged others to do so.

Shi'a Tradition — Mourning of Husayn

In Shi'a Islam, Ashura has profound emotional and political significance. It commemorates the martyrdom of Husayn ibn Ali (grandson of Prophet Muhammad) at the Battle of Karbala in 680 CE.

The Historical Event

  • Husayn was invited to Kufa (Iraq) to lead an uprising against the oppressive Umayyad Caliph Yazid I
  • On the 10th Muharram (Ashura), he and his followers were killed at Karbala
  • Husayn's refusal to submit to tyranny became a symbol of resistance to injustice
  • For Shi'a Muslims, Husayn is the ultimate martyr (shahid) of Islam

Shi'a Muslims often say: "Every day is Ashura, and every place is Karbala" — meaning the struggle against injustice continues.

Shi'a Commemoration Practices

  • Mourning rituals: Wearing black clothes, public expressions of grief
  • Passion plays (Ta'ziyeh): Dramatised recreations of Husayn's martyrdom
  • Processions: Organised marches through cities commemorating the tragedy
  • Self-flagellation: Some practice ritual chest-beating or self-striking (Tatbir) to express sorrow — controversial even within Shi'a Islam
  • Poetry and sermons: Religious scholars deliver sermons (Majlis) recounting Husayn's sacrifice

Important note: Self-harm practices are debated within Shi'a Islam. Many modern Shi'a scholars discourage extreme forms of self-injury, arguing they contradict Islamic teachings on the sanctity of life.

The Significance of Ash ura in Shi'a-Sunni Differences

Ashura highlights an important theological difference between Sunni and Shi'a Islam:

The Caliphate Dispute

After Prophet Muhammad's death (632 CE), Muslims disagreed about succession:

  • Sunni view: Community chooses the Caliph (leader). All four early Caliphs were legitimate.
  • Shi'a view: Ali ibn Abi Talib (the Prophet's cousin and son-in-law) was divinely appointed as the first Imam (leader). Only the Prophet's family (Ahlul Bayt) should lead Muslims.

Husayn's Significance

For Shi'a Muslims, Husayn's martyrdom represents the ultimate injustice — the rightful Imam was killed by the illegitimate Umayyad Caliph. Commemorating Ashura is a form of political and religious protest against tyranny.

This is why Ashura remains politically charged in countries like Iran and Iraq, where Shi'a majority populations use the commemoration to challenge authority.

Modern Context

  • In Iran, Ashura processions are major political events
  • In Iraq, Ashura draws millions of pilgrims to Karbala
  • In the UK, both Sunni and Shi'a Muslims observe Muharram, but with different emphases

12-Mark Exam Practice

Question: Explain the differences between how Sunni and Shi'a Muslims observe Ashura. What does Ashura reveal about religious diversity in Islam? (12 marks)

Mark scheme:

  • Definition of Ashura (1 mark)
  • Sunni understanding: Moses and the Red Sea, optional fasting (2 marks)
  • Shi'a understanding: Husayn and Karbala, mourning traditions (2 marks)
  • Description of Shi'a practices: Ta'ziyeh, processions, etc. (2 marks)
  • Historical context: Caliphate dispute and succession (2 marks)
  • What this reveals about diversity and interpretation in Islam (1 mark)

Guidance

Key exam tips:

  • Avoid stating that one tradition is "right" and the other "wrong"
  • Use accurate historical dates (680 CE for Husayn's martyrdom)
  • Explain that Ashura shows how the same religion can have different practices
  • Discuss self-flagellation sensitively — it's debated even within Shi'a Islam
  • Connect Ashura to broader Sunni-Shi'a theological differences

Key Terms

  • Id-ul-Fitr: Festival of Breaking the Fast, celebrating the end of Ramadan
  • Zakat ul-Fitr: Obligatory charity given before Eid prayers on Id-ul-Fitr
  • Ashura: 9th-10th Muharram; Sunni: Moses's deliverance; Shi'a: Husayn's martyrdom
  • Husayn ibn Ali: Grandson of Prophet Muhammad, martyr at Karbala (680 CE)
  • Karbala: City in Iraq where Husayn was killed
  • Ta'ziyeh: Passion plays depicting Husayn's martyrdom
  • Muharram: First month of Islamic calendar
  • Shawwal: Month following Ramadan
