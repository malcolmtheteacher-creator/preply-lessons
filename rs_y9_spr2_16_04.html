Encounter: Religious Attitudes to Sexuality

Important note: This is an academic, respectful exploration of diverse viewpoints. Religious traditions contain many views. Individual believers may disagree with official teachings. The goal is understanding, not judgment.

Key Terms

  • Heterosexual: Attraction to the opposite gender
  • Homosexual: Attraction to the same gender
  • Bisexual: Attraction to multiple genders
  • Celibacy: Choosing not to marry or have sexual relations (e.g., Catholic priests)

Christianity

Conservative view: Sex is intended only within heterosexual marriage.

"Do not lie with a man as one lies with a woman; that is detestable." (Leviticus 18:22)
"Men committing indecent acts with other men… will receive in themselves the due penalty for their error." (Romans 1:26–27)

Liberal/Progressive view: Love is the highest Christian value. Committed same-sex relationships can be blessed. Sexual orientation is not a moral issue.

Recent development (Church of England, 2023): Approved prayers of blessing for same-sex couples, marking a shift in official stance.

Quakers (Religious Society of Friends): Have fully affirmed LGBT+ members and unions since 1963.

Islam

Traditional teaching: Homosexuality is forbidden.

The Qur'an recounts the story of Lut, whose people committed sexual acts "that no people in creation had done before you" (Qur'an 7:80–81). This is interpreted as referring to homosexuality.

Sexual ethics: Sex is permitted only within marriage. Pre-marital and extra-marital relations are forbidden for both heterosexual and same-sex couples.

Evolving perspectives: Some Muslim scholars and organisations (such as Imaan, a support group for LGBT+ Muslims) work to support queer Muslims while maintaining faith commitments. They argue that compassion and inclusion are core Islamic values.

Judaism

Orthodox Judaism: Homosexuality is prohibited based on Levitical law.

"Do not lie with a man as one lies with a woman; that is an abomination." (Leviticus 18:22)

Reform and Conservative Judaism: Fully affirm LGBT+ members. Same-sex marriage has been permitted and celebrated since the 1990s. Many Reform and Conservative rabbis are openly gay or lesbian and serve their communities.

Humanism

Sexual orientation is not a moral issue. What matters is consent, respect, and mutual care.

  • People should be free to love whomever they choose
  • Same-sex relationships deserve equal rights and recognition
  • Religion should not be used to discriminate

Why This Topic Generates Strong Feelings

For many people, sexuality is deeply personal. Religious teachings touch on identity, belonging, and the right to love. When traditions condemn homosexuality, LGBT+ members may feel rejected by their faith community. Conversely, some religious believers feel their tradition is being pressured to change fundamental teachings. Understanding these feelings — even if we disagree — is crucial.

Explore: Viewpoints (Academic Discussion)

Read each viewpoint respectfully. Consider why intelligent, thoughtful people might hold different positions.

Conservative Christian Perspective:

"The Bible is clear that homosexuality is contrary to God's design for sexuality. This isn't about hate; it's about obedience to scripture. We love gay people and call them to celibacy or repentance, just as we call all people to live according to God's word."

Progressive Christian Perspective:

"Jesus taught love as the greatest commandment. When we understand the historical context of scripture, we see that prohibitions were about exploitation, not consensual same-sex love. Blessing gay marriages honours God's love."

Humanist Perspective:

"Sexual orientation is a natural variation of human sexuality. Laws and religions should protect equal rights and dignity for all people. What consenting adults do in private relationships is not a moral issue."

LGBT+ Believer Perspective:

"I am both gay and Muslim/Christian/Jewish. My faith is precious to me. I interpret my tradition's teachings in ways that affirm my identity. I am not rejecting my faith — I am claiming a place within it."

Reflection: What questions do these perspectives raise for you? Can people of good faith disagree on this?
