2. Encounter

Now we'll apply Smart's 7 dimensions to real religions. The claim: every major worldview expresses all seven dimensions, but in different ways.

The Big Idea

If religion is really just seven dimensions in different combinations, then all worldviews—from Christianity to Humanism—should have all seven. The difference is not whether they have them, but how they express them.

Chunk 1: The Story and the Belief (Narrative + Doctrinal)

Every worldview has a story about who we are, where we came from, and where we're going. Every worldview has core beliefs about meaning and truth.

Christianity

Narrative: God created the world, humans fell into sin, Jesus came to save us, and we'll face judgment in an afterlife.
Doctrinal: Jesus is God's Son. The Trinity. Salvation through faith in Christ.

Islam

Narrative: God (Allah) created all; prophets were sent to guide humanity; the Qur'an is God's final revelation; the Day of Judgment will come.
Doctrinal: Tawhid (absolute oneness of God). Muhammad is the final prophet. The Five Pillars define right living.

Buddhism

Narrative: Siddhartha discovered that life contains suffering (dukkha) caused by craving; he found the path to end suffering; others can follow the same path.
Doctrinal: The Four Noble Truths. The Eightfold Path. No creator God, but a path to enlightenment.

Humanism

Narrative: Humans evolved; we have no cosmic destiny; meaning comes from the here-and-now; we're responsible for making the world better.
Doctrinal: Reason and evidence matter. The supernatural doesn't. Human flourishing is the goal.

• • •

Chunk 2: How to Live and How to Practice (Ethical + Ritual)

Every worldview gives ethical guidance (how to treat others, how to live well). Every worldview has rituals or practices that bind the community and focus the mind.

Judaism

Ethical: The Torah commands justice, mercy, and love of neighbour. The Talmud teaches how to apply these in daily life.
Ritual: Shabbat (weekly rest), kashrut (dietary laws), festival observances, Bar/Bat Mitzvah, prayer.

Hinduism

Ethical: Dharma — your duty based on your stage of life, caste, and role. Right action leads to good karma.
Ritual: Puja (worship), yoga, meditation, pilgrimage to sacred sites, observance of festivals like Diwali and Holi.

Sikhism

Ethical: Honesty, hard work, sharing with others. "Vand Chakna" — share the fruits of your labour. All people are equal before God.
Ritual: Prayer, langar (communal meal open to all), wearing the Five Ks, Gurdwara worship.

Humanism (revisited)

Ethical: Treat others as you'd want to be treated. Empathy and reason guide ethics. Harm reduction matters more than doctrine.
Ritual: Humanist ceremonies (weddings, funerals, naming ceremonies), community gatherings, philosophical discussions.

• • •

Chunk 3: Experience, Community, and the Physical (Experiential + Institutional + Material)

Every worldview allows for powerful personal experiences and deep emotional connections. Every worldview has institutions and leaders that keep it alive. Every worldview involves physical things — buildings, objects, bodies, art.

KEY INSIGHT

All Seven Dimensions in Action

Take Christianity at Christmas. You see all seven:

  • Narrative + Doctrinal: The birth story of Jesus
  • Ethical: Messages of peace and goodwill
  • Ritual: Church services, carol singing, lighting advent candles
  • Experiential: Awe when you hear a church choir; joy with family; tears at nativity plays
  • Institutional: Churches organize services; clergy lead; charities do outreach
  • Material: Christmas trees, nativity scenes, holly, church buildings, hymn books

Now look at Buddhism's Bodhi Day (celebrating the Buddha's enlightenment):

  • Narrative + Doctrinal: The story of Siddhartha's awakening; the nature of enlightenment
  • Ethical: Commitment to compassion and mindfulness
  • Ritual: Temple meditation, lighting lanterns, chanting sutras
  • Experiential: Peace during meditation; sense of connection to the community; gratitude
  • Institutional: Monasteries organize events; monks guide practice
  • Material: Temples, statues of the Buddha, prayer beads, lanterns, incense

Notice: different stories, different rituals, different buildings—but the same seven dimensions.

3. Explore

Model Activity: "I Do" Example

Example: The Islamic Hajj (pilgrimage to Mecca)

Which Smart dimensions does the Hajj express?

  • Narrative: Following in the footsteps of Prophet Muhammad and Abraham ✓
  • Doctrinal: One of the Five Pillars; expresses belief in absolute submission to Allah ✓
  • Ethical: Teaches humility and equality (all pilgrims wear the same white clothes) ✓
  • Ritual: The circling of the Kaaba, prayers, washing ✓
  • Experiential: Profound spiritual experience; feeling of connection to 2 billion other Muslims ✓
  • Institutional: Organized by Islamic authorities; requires planning and logistics ✓
  • Material: The Kaaba, sacred stones, the mount of Arafat, pilgrim's dress ✓

Insight: One practice can express all seven dimensions. That's the power of these dimensions—they're not separate boxes but interlocking parts of a whole system.

Your Turn: Match Examples to Dimensions

For each example below, identify which of Smart's dimensions it expresses. You can choose multiple.

1. Jewish Shabbat (weekly day of rest, Friday sunset to Saturday night)

Shabbat involves lighting candles, resting from work, special meals, prayer, and reflection. Families gather. It's both a commandment from the Torah and a cherished tradition.

Example answer: Narrative (Torah commands it), Ritual (candles, meals, prayer), Ethical (rest = respect for creation), Experiential (peace, family connection), Institutional (family and community participation), Material (candles, challah bread, wine)

2. Buddhist meditation (sitting in silence, watching the breath, letting thoughts pass)

Meditation is the core practice. It's taught by monasteries, recommended in the Eightfold Path, and produces calming experiences.

Example answer: Doctrinal (part of the Eightfold Path), Ritual (structured practice, often daily), Experiential (peace, mental clarity), Institutional (monks teach it in monasteries), Material (meditation cushions, temples)

3. Humanist wedding ceremony (couple exchanges vows, celebrant shares a poem about love and partnership, community witnesses)

No God is invoked, but the ceremony is meaningful and structured.

Example answer: Doctrinal (belief in human love and partnership as meaningful), Ethical (commitment to treat your partner well), Ritual (structured ceremony, vows), Experiential (joy, emotion), Institutional (celebrants are trained; communities support marriage), Material (rings, flowers, venue)
Key point: You might have identified the dimensions slightly differently—and that's fine. Religious Studies isn't a multiple-choice test. The skill here is recognizing patterns: Can you see how narrative, ritual, institutional, material, and experiential all show up together? That's the real learning.

What matters most: Did you spot that every one of these practices involves multiple dimensions, not just one? That's the insight Smart's framework reveals.
