The Problem of Evil Revisited

You learned about the Problem of Evil in Year 8. It remains the strongest objection to theism. If God is omnipotent (all-powerful), omniscient (all-knowing), and omnibenevolent (all-good), then:

  • He has the power to prevent evil
  • He knows evil exists and will occur
  • He would want to prevent evil if He is good
  • Yet evil and suffering exist abundantly

This logical contradiction troubles theists. Even attempted defences (free will, soul-making, skeptical theism) seem unsatisfying when confronted with horrors like childhood cancer, genocide, or natural disasters.

Scientific Explanations for Belief

Sigmund Freud (1856–1939) argued that God is a psychological projection. Humans invent God to comfort themselves in a hostile universe, much like a child invents an imaginary friend for comfort. Belief in God, Freud thought, was a neurosis — a coping mechanism rather than a grasp of truth.

Karl Marx (1818–1883) saw religion as a tool of oppression. He argued that the powerful use religion to comfort the poor with promises of heavenly rewards, keeping them compliant and accepting of earthly suffering. Religion is 'the opium of the people' — a drug that dulls the pain of exploitation.

Modern Neuroscience: Studies show that religious experiences correlate with activity in specific brain regions (temporal lobes, right parietal lobe). This suggests religious experiences are neural phenomena, not encounters with a transcendent God.

Scientific Atheism: The New Atheism

In the 21st century, scientists like Richard Dawkins, Sam Harris, and Daniel Dennett have made powerful cases that science makes God unnecessary. Drawkins's The God Delusion argues that:

  • Science, not God, explains natural phenomena
  • Evolution, not design, explains biological complexity
  • The burden of proof is on theists to prove God exists, not on atheists to prove He doesn't
  • Belief in God is irrational given the lack of evidence

The Absence of Evidence Argument

Philosopher Bertrand Russell famously argued that if an extraordinary claim (God exists) is made without extraordinary evidence, we should reject it. Russell pointed out:

  • No physical evidence: There is no scientific evidence for God — no fossils, no particles, no measurements that require God as an explanation
  • No agreed testimony: While believers report experiences of God, these are subjective and contradictory across religions
  • Hidden God: If God exists, He hides remarkably well. A clearer revelation would be more convincing
  • Inferior to natural explanations: Science explains what was once attributed to God (disease, weather, consciousness) through natural causes

Humanist Perspective: Meaning Without God

Humanists argue that you don't need God to have meaning, morality, or purpose. Instead:

  • Meaning is self-created: We make our own meaning through relationships, work, creativity, and service to others
  • Morality is rational: We can reason about what causes suffering and flourishing, and base ethics on that — not on divine commands
  • Community provides purpose: Belonging to communities of shared values and mutual care gives life purpose
  • This life matters: If this is our only life, it becomes more precious and motivating — we don't wait for an afterlife

Theist Response

Theists counter that the arguments for God (design, cosmology, experience) do provide evidence, even if not scientific proof. They argue that absence of physical evidence doesn't mean absence of God — God is spiritual, not physical. They also maintain that humanist ethics, while noble, lacks the ultimate grounding that only God provides.

Atheist Rejoinder

Atheists respond that these arguments are weak (evolution refutes design; logic doesn't prove causation; psychological explanations exist). They argue that if God existed and wanted to be known, the evidence would be overwhelming — not ambiguous arguments needing interpretation.
