Check your idea CLICK
Because the four situations aren't really the same kind of "future" — a fixed schedule, a plan you've already made with someone, something the evidence tells you right now, and a decision you're making as you speak all feel different, so English marks them with different forms. That's why choosing the right one matters more than just knowing "the future tense."
Today: five ways to talk about the future — will for decisions and opinions on the spot, going to for plans and for things the evidence already shows, the -ing form for fixed arrangements, the present simple for timetables, and one neutral way to ask about someone's plans — so you can pick the right one for the situation, not just any one that fits.

Deciding on the Spot: "I'll take the blue one"

For decisions made right now, as you speak, and for opinions and predictions with no hard evidence behind them.

Spot the pattern

Three things you might say without planning them in advance:

  • "Hmm, I'm not sure yet... I think I'll try the pasta."
  • "I bet this film will be terrible — the trailer looked awful."

Plans and What You Can See Coming: "going to"

One form, two very different jobs: a plan you already decided on, or a prediction based on evidence right in front of you.

Spot the pattern

  • "We're going to redecorate the kitchen this summer — we've been saving for months." (a decision already made)
  • "Look at those clouds — it's going to pour down any minute." (evidence you can see right now)
  • "I'm going to call the dentist tomorrow morning." (a decision already made)

What's the shared structure — and what are the two different reasons for using it?

Check the pattern CLICK
am / is / are + going to + base verb, for two separate reasons: (1) an intention — a plan or decision made before now, not on the spot, and (2) an evidence-based prediction — something you can see, hear, or already know for certain makes true right now. Same words, two different jobs — keep them apart in your head.
am / is / are + going to + base verb
→ an intention (decided already)  OR  an evidence-based prediction (visible now)

Arrangements, Timetables & Asking Nicely

For plans fixed with another person, for official schedules, and for asking about someone's plans without sounding pushy.

Spot the pattern

  • "I'm meeting Ana at six — we sorted it out yesterday." (a personal arrangement)
  • "We're flying to Lisbon on Thursday — the tickets are booked." (a personal arrangement)
  • "The film starts at eight." (a fixed schedule)

What's different about "The film starts at eight" compared with the other two?

Check the pattern CLICK
am / is / are + -ing is for a personal arrangement — a time and place you've already fixed with someone else. The present simple is for official timetables and schedules — trains, flights, cinemas, opening hours — where it doesn't matter who's involved, the schedule itself is fixed.
am / is / are + verb-ing → a fixed personal arrangement
present simple → a timetable or official schedule
One more form worth knowing: will be + verb-ing asks about someone's plans in a neutral way — it doesn't sound like a request, just a question about what's already going to happen. "Will you be needing the projector later?" sounds more natural than "Will you need the projector later?" when you're really checking if it's free.

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: both are grammatically fine, but "it's going to rain" is the more natural choice here because you have visible evidence right now — the clouds. "It will rain" sounds more like a general opinion or guess with nothing concrete in front of you.
