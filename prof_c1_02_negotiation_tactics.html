Six Moves at the Deal Table

Click each move to understand how it works. These are the tactics that shift the balance in your favour.

1. BATNA (Your Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement) +

Why it matters: Your power at the table comes entirely from what you can do if there's no deal. If your BATNA is weak, you have no leverage. If your BATNA is strong, you can walk away.

Weak BATNA: "If we don't do this deal, we're stuck."
Strong BATNA: "If we don't do this deal with you, we partner with your competitor or go it alone. Either way, we're fine."

The language: Never say your BATNA aloud unless you have to. Let them wonder. "We have other options being explored." That's often enough.

2. ZOPA (The Zone of Possible Agreement) +

Why it matters: A deal only happens in the range both sides will accept. If they want 60% and you want 50/50, there's overlap at 55%. But you don't know their bottom line — and they don't know yours. Your job is to make them think your bottom line is higher than it is.

You're comfortable at 52%. Don't tell them that. Start at 40% and negotiate toward 52%. They think 60% is their target, start at 70%, come down to 55%. You meet at 53%, you win.

The insight: The wider you make the range in your opening offer, the more room there is for the final deal to favor you.

3. Interest vs Position +

Why it matters: Position is what someone says they want. Interest is why they want it. Positions are rigid. Interests are negotiable.

Position: "We need 60% of revenue."
Interest behind it: "We need to show investors we're the dominant partner."
Alternative deal: Maybe they get 50% revenue but leading decision-making power. Same interest satisfied, different numbers.

Your move: When someone says "We need X", always ask "Why?" Find the interest. Then negotiate around that.

4. The Package Deal +

Why it matters: Never negotiate one issue at a time. If you haggle over revenue and lose, you've lost everything. If you package revenue, territory, decision rights, and timeline — you can trade.

They want 60% revenue. You say: "Let's talk about this as a package. Revenue, territory, governance structure, and what happens in year 3. We can be flexible on revenue if the governance and timeline work for us."

The benefit: You always have something to concede that doesn't hurt you much, and something to get that matters to you.

5. The Conditional Concession +

Why it matters: You never give something without getting something. The magic word is "if."

Don't say: "OK, we'll go to 58%."
Say: "If you can commit to [specific outcome], we could consider 58%. What would that look like from your side?"

Why it works: Every concession signals you have something left to give. If you give without getting, they think you have unlimited room to move.

6. The Principled Close +

Why it matters: When you reach an agreement, frame it as logical and fair, not as a victory for you.

"Based on everything we've discussed — your need for investor confidence, our need for operational control, and the market precedent we've looked at — a 55/45 split with joint governance seems like the fair arrangement. Both sides get something, neither loses everything."

The psychological shift: They stop thinking "Did we lose?" and start thinking "Is this fair?" Your frame becomes the reality.

The Partnership Negotiation

You are lead negotiator for a tech company seeking a distribution deal with a larger player. You need them to reach global markets. They don't need you — they can build this themselves, but slower. You have 8 minutes.

Your position

Ideal deal: 50/50 revenue split, you maintain product control, they handle distribution.

Fallback: 45/55 (them higher) if they commit to aggressive timeline and give you decision power on product roadmap.

Walk-away: Anything below 40/60 or loss of product control.

BATNA: You can bootstrap your own distribution or join a partner at 30% (bad, but walkable).

Reflection & Language Bank

What Makes a Good Negotiator

Not desperation. The moment they sense you need the deal, you've lost. Your BATNA has to be real — and they have to believe it.

Not aggression. The aggressive negotiator gets a deal — usually a bad one — and then the other side regrets it and walks away later.

Understanding. You win when you understand their interests better than they do. When you can say "What you really need is X, and here's how we can both get it."

Negotiation Language Bank

Opening: Set the Anchor
"We see this as a natural partnership. Our opening position is [X], which reflects [rationale]."
"Market precedent suggests [range]. We're proposing [your number] because..."
Understanding Their Interest
"Help me understand — when you say you need 65%, is it the revenue number itself or what it signals to your investors?"
"What's driving that requirement? What outcome are you trying to hit?"
Conditional Concession
"If you could commit to [specific thing], we'd be open to [concession]."
"If that timeline works, we can be flexible on [item]."
"We could go to [higher number] if [condition] is met."
Package Thinking
"Let's look at this holistically — revenue, territory, governance, timeline. We can be creative across all of those."
"One way to get you to 60% equivalent would be to adjust [other area]..."
Defending Your Position
"We can't go below [number] because [business reason]. But if we adjust [other item], there's room."
"That's not workable for us, but here's what would be..."
Principled Close
"Based on market precedent, our respective interests, and what we've discussed, [this split] feels like the fair arrangement."
"This gives you [what you wanted] and us [what we need]. Neither side is completely happy — which usually means it's fair."
