The Company That Bet Everything

How a state-owned oil and gas utility became the world's biggest offshore wind developer — and what happened next.

Today: you'll work through a transformation case that is still unresolved — and you'll sharpen the language executives use when they have to say something uncertain, unwelcome or not yet decided: it would appear that, there may be a case for, we're broadly on track.

  • When a long-term strategy runs into short-term losses, what actually decides whether a board holds its nerve?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Interest rates rose, steel and cable prices climbed, and permits took a year longer than planned. The whole sector was facing severe headwinds.
headwinds (n, plural) — external forces slowing you down. The opposite is tailwinds. Collocations: to face / encounter headwinds, macroeconomic headwinds.
2. The gas fields were still on the balance sheet at full value, but if the transition accelerated, nobody would ever pay to extract them. They risked becoming stranded assets.
a stranded asset (n) — an asset that loses its value before the end of its expected life, typically because markets, technology or regulation have moved. Central to every energy-transition conversation.
3. A wind farm costs billions before it earns a single euro, and almost all of that is borrowed. The business is extraordinarily capital-intensive.
capital-intensive (adj) — requiring very large up-front investment relative to labour. The key consequence: such businesses are acutely sensitive to the cost of capital.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: divest · write-down · pivot. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which verb means to sell off a business unit or holding, usually to concentrate on something else?
to divest (v) — "They divested the oil and gas division." Noun: divestment / a divestiture. Opposite: to acquire.
2. Which noun means an accounting reduction in the recorded value of an asset, recognising that it is worth less than the books say?
a write-down (n) — partial reduction; a write-off takes it to zero. The formal term in the accounts is an impairment. Verb: to write down an asset.
3. Which verb means to change strategic direction sharply while keeping the same organisation?
to pivot (v/n) — "The company pivoted to wind." Useful register note: common in strategy and start-up language, occasionally mocked as jargon — use it deliberately.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Losses mounted, and the board doubled down: it cancelled the hedging strategy and committed a further two billion. To double down is to…
  (a) reduce exposure by half   (b) increase your commitment to a position rather than retreat   (c) delay a decision
(b) — to double down (on something), originally from card play. Note the connotation: determined, and possibly reckless. "They doubled down on a failing strategy."
2. With hindsight, the contracts should have been index-linked. With hindsight means…
  (a) knowing now what could not have been known then   (b) after taking expert advice   (c) against the board's wishes
hindsight (n) — understanding an event after it has happened. With / in hindsight; hindsight bias = the illusion that it was obvious all along. Compare foresight.

D · Collocation & word building

1. Complete the term at the centre of this case: when interest rates rise, a company's cost of ___ rises with them. What does the term mean, and why does it matter more here than in most industries?
cost of capital — what it costs the business to fund itself through debt and equity. In a capital-intensive business, a two-point rise can destroy the economics of a project that was approved as viable.
2. English has a fixed phrase for a decision so large that failure would end the firm: a ___-the-company decision. Complete it, and give the everyday verb inside it.
a bet-the-company decision (also bet-the-farm). The verb: to bet. Related: to stake everything on, an all-in decision, to put the company on the line.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — A utility nobody admired

In 2008 the Danish state-controlled energy group DONG Energy — the name stood for Danish Oil and Natural Gas — was doing what it had been built to do. Roughly eighty-five per cent of its energy production came from fossil fuels: coal-fired power stations, oil fields, gas. It was competent, unloved, and squarely on the wrong side of the direction Europe was travelling in.

What the leadership proposed next was, on any conventional reading, irrational. They would invert the ratio: from eighty-five per cent fossil to eighty-five per cent renewable, and they would do it by building offshore wind farms — at that point one of the most expensive ways yet devised to generate a kilowatt-hour. The technology was immature, the supply chain barely existed, and the economics only worked with public subsidy. Every element of the plan required the company to become good at something it had never done.

Then the ground moved. The financial crisis hit, gas prices fell hard, and by 2012 the company was posting heavy losses and facing a credit downgrade. It needed capital urgently, and the capital it found came from an investor group led by Goldman Sachs. In Denmark the deal became a political scandal: a national asset, sold cheaply, to Wall Street. Part of the governing coalition walked out over it. A new chief executive arrived, cut hard, sold what did not fit, and kept the wind strategy intact. It is worth pausing on that: the transformation nearly died at the exact moment it was least defensible in public.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the company in 2008, and what did it propose to become?

2. What nearly killed the strategy, and what did that cost politically?

1. A Danish state-controlled oil, gas and coal utility, roughly 85% fossil. It proposed to invert that ratio to roughly 85% renewable, principally through offshore wind — a technology that was then immature and expensive.

2. The financial crisis and collapsing gas prices produced heavy losses and a funding emergency around 2012. The rescue capital came from a Goldman Sachs-led group, which caused a political crisis in Denmark and split the governing coalition.

Discuss:
  • The strategy survived because a new chief executive protected it while cutting everything around it. What does it take, practically, to defend a long-horizon bet during a liquidity crisis?

Part 2 — The textbook years

Then it worked. Offshore wind costs fell steeply through the 2010s — driven by larger turbines, industrialised installation, competitive auctions and the sheer accumulated experience of building the things — and the company that had committed earliest was positioned to take the largest share of the market it had helped to create. It listed on the stock exchange in 2016. In 2017 it sold its oil and gas business outright and renamed itself Ørsted, after the Danish physicist who discovered electromagnetism. The old name would have been an embarrassment.

The numbers were emphatic. The green share of production passed the eighty-five per cent target roughly two decades ahead of the original schedule. The share price multiplied. Ørsted became the world's largest developer of offshore wind, was ranked at the top of global sustainability indices, and turned into the case study — cited in business schools, in ministries, in the strategy decks of every incumbent wondering whether it could do the same thing.

The lesson everyone drew was clean and flattering: incumbents can transform; being early is a durable advantage; do the hard thing before you are forced to. What almost nobody said out loud was that the whole model rested on two conditions that had held for a decade and were treated as permanent — cheap capital, and costs that only ever fell.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did offshore wind costs fall so sharply, and why did that favour this company in particular?

2. What two assumptions was the celebrated model quietly resting on?

1. Larger turbines, industrialised installation, competitive auctions and accumulated build experience. Having committed earliest, the company had the capability and pipeline to capture the largest share of a market it had helped create.

2. A sustained low cost of capital, and input costs that would continue to fall. Both were treated as structural features rather than as conditions of a particular decade.

Discuss:
  • Which assumptions is your own plan resting on that nobody currently writes down because they seem permanent? Name two.

Part 3 — The reckoning

From 2022 the two conditions reversed at once. Central banks raised rates steeply; steel, cables, vessels and labour all inflated; interest rates and supply-chain costs collided with projects that had been bid years earlier at fixed prices. In a capital-intensive business, that combination is not an inconvenience — it is the whole economics of the project, undone after the price has been locked and before the asset earns anything.

In late 2023 the company cancelled two large offshore projects off the coast of New Jersey and took impairments running to tens of billions of Danish kroner. The share price fell by roughly three-quarters from its peak. American political risk compounded the arithmetic: permitting slowed, tax-credit conditions tightened, and offshore wind became a live target in US politics. The chief executive who had presided over the boom left. In 2025 the company came back to its shareholders for a very large emergency capital raise, and the Danish state — still the largest owner — had to decide, publicly, whether to put more money in.

What makes the case genuinely hard is that almost nothing in the underlying thesis has been refuted. Electricity demand is rising; Europe still wants domestic energy; offshore wind is still being built. The transformation was not wrong about the destination. It was wrong about the cost of getting there, and about how long the weather would hold. That is a distinction every executive making a fifteen-year commitment has to be able to explain — usually to people who will judge the decision by the share price this quarter.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Which two reversals struck simultaneously from 2022, and why is that combination particularly severe in this industry?

2. What is the distinction the final paragraph insists on?

1. Sharply higher interest rates and steep input-cost inflation. In a capital-intensive business with prices fixed years in advance, both hit before the asset generates any revenue, so the project's economics collapse without anything operational going wrong.

2. Between being wrong about the destination and being wrong about the cost and timing of reaching it. The strategic thesis largely survives; the financial assumptions did not.

Discuss:
  • If you sat on this board in 2024, what would you have said to a shareholder who asked, reasonably, whether the original bet had been vindicated or refuted?

Grammar — hedging & diplomatic distance

Everything difficult in this case had to be said out loud to a board, a regulator, a journalist or twenty thousand employees. At C1, the skill is not fluency — it is calibration: saying exactly how certain you are, and no more.

The four layers of hedging:
• Modal verbs: "Costs may rise further." · "That could prove expensive." · "It would appear that the assumptions were optimistic."
• Lexical verbs: appear, seem, tend to, suggest, indicate — "The data suggest a slowdown." · "Projects of this type tend to overrun."
• Adverbs & quantifiers: broadly, largely, somewhat, arguably, in the region of, on balance — "We're broadly on track."
• Impersonal framing: "There is a case for revisiting the schedule." · "It has been suggested that…" · "One could argue that…"
Diplomatic distance — removing the accusation from the sentence:
• Passive, agent deleted: "Mistakes were made in the tendering process." (compare: "Your team got the tender wrong.")
• Nominalisation: "There was a delay in the permitting." rather than "You delayed the permits."
• Past/continuous softening: "I was wondering whether we might revisit that." · "I'd been hoping to see the revised figures."
• Concessive opening: "I take the point entirely — that said, the schedule assumes…"

The reverse skill, equally important: knowing when to strip the hedges out. "This project will not deliver its approved return. I am recommending we stop it." Hedged language in a moment that requires commitment reads as evasion — and a board hears it as such.

1. Soften the statement

Rewrite each blunt sentence using the device in brackets. Write only the missing words.

1. Your cost assumptions are wrong. → The cost assumptions somewhat optimistic. (appear)

2. You delayed the decision by six months. → a six-month delay in reaching a decision. (impersonal + nominalisation)

3. We should cancel this project. → for reconsidering the project. (there is a case…)

1. would appear to be / appear to be   2. There was   3. There is a case

Notice that none of the three denies anything. They relocate the emphasis from the person to the situation.

2. Calibrate the certainty

Complete each sentence so that it expresses the degree of confidence described in brackets.

1. The write-down in the region of two billion. (likely, not confirmed — two words)

2. On the evidence available, the strategy sound; the timing was not. (a careful "yes, mostly" — two words)

3. Projects of this scale overrun by twelve to eighteen months. (a general pattern, not a promise — two words)

1. is likely to be / will probably be   2. remains broadly / was largely   3. tend to

"Tend to" is the executive's most useful verb: it states a pattern without committing you to any individual case.

3. The board answer — complete the response

A non-executive director asks: "Was the original decision a mistake?" Complete the reply so that it is honest, calibrated, and not evasive. Read it aloud when you finish.

"I take the question seriously. On the strategic thesis, the evidence (suggest) that we were right about the direction — demand is rising and the assets are being built. Where we (appear) to have been wrong is on the cost of capital, and there (be) a case for saying we should have index-linked the contracts. Projects of this type (tend) to absorb inflation badly. With (noun: knowing afterwards), we locked prices too early. What I (not / say) is that the strategy was wrong, and I (not / recommend) that we reverse it."

suggests · appear · is · tend · hindsight · am not saying · am not recommending

Read the last two clauses again. The hedging runs through the middle of the answer and stops dead at the end — which is precisely where a board is listening for a position.

Going deeper

A note on the figures: percentages and valuations here are rounded, and the situation is still developing — this is a live case rather than a closed one, which is exactly what makes it useful.

What the case actually tests. The comfortable reading of a transformation story is that courage was rewarded. This one refuses that shape. The strategic judgement — that electricity was going to decarbonise and that early scale in offshore wind would be valuable — has not been refuted by anything that has happened since. What failed was a set of financial assumptions so widely shared that almost nobody stated them: that capital would stay cheap, and that costs would keep falling. Every incumbent that copied the playbook copied those assumptions too, silently.

There is a second, harder lesson about sequence. A capital-intensive business commits its money years before it earns anything, and typically fixes its selling price at the moment of least information. That is a structural exposure, not a management error — but it can be hedged, indexed, staged or shared, and the question a board should have asked is not "do we believe in wind?" but "what happens to this project at seven per cent?"

And a third, about narrative. Having become the world's example of successful transformation, the company had to keep being that example. Reputational capital is not free: it makes retreat more expensive than it should be, and it attracts a shareholder base that bought the story rather than the cash flows.

Talk it through:
  • Separate the two judgements: the strategic thesis and the financial assumptions. Should a board be able to approve one while stress-testing the other — and does yours?
  • "What happens to this project at seven per cent?" Which of your current commitments would fail that question, and who is responsible for asking it?
  • The company became a symbol. Where has your organisation's public story made a sensible retreat harder than it needed to be?
  • German industry faces a version of this case in energy costs, electrification and Chinese competition. Is the lesson to move earlier, or to move in smaller, reversible steps?

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

You are on an investor call. An analyst says: "With respect, you've spent a decade telling us this was the defining strategic move of your generation. The share price says otherwise. Was the whole thing a mistake?"

Answer properly: concede what is genuinely conceded, distinguish the thesis from the assumptions, state what you have changed, and close with a position rather than a hedge. Then do it again in ninety seconds — the compression is the exercise.

Task C · Four rooms, one message (4 minutes)

Take one decision — real or invented — and deliver it four ways, one minute each. Notice how the hedging changes.

  • To the board: the full risk picture, including what you do not know.
  • To the workforce: honest, without alarming people who cannot act on the information.
  • To a journalist: nothing that becomes a headline you have to correct.
  • To a major customer or supplier: reassurance that survives being repeated back to you.
