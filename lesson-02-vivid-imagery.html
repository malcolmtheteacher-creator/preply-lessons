The man with the goat on his head

Imagine a morning in Rome, two thousand years ago. You cross the Forum on your way to a lesson. You pass a hundred things: stalls of olives, a woman arguing about the price of cloth, a boy with a basket, the same stone steps you climb every day. At the end of the day someone asks you what you saw. You can hardly say. It was all ordinary, and ordinary things slip straight through the mind.

Now imagine that halfway across the square a man comes towards you wearing a bright red wig, leading an elephant by a string, with a live goat balanced on his head. The goat is chewing a letter. You would remember him for years. You might not even need to try.

Why do you forget most of what you pass in the Forum?
Because it is ordinary. Ordinary things do not stand out, so the mind does not keep them.
Talk about it. What is the strangest thing you have ever seen in the street? How long have you remembered it?

The Roman author who made this point in a handbook on public speaking, written around 85 BC, is unknown. For centuries people thought it was Cicero. The handbook is called Rhetorica ad Herennium, and it says something very close to the Forum story. We remember little things, ordinary things, everyday things, it says, because nothing about them surprises us. But anything shocking, ugly, beautiful, funny, huge or unbelievable stays with us a long time.

So the advice for anyone who wants to remember is make the picture unusual. Do not hold a polite, realistic picture in your mind. Give it colour, make it move, make it louder and bigger than life, make it a little silly or a little shocking. The handbook adds that the pictures should be active: people doing things, not still lifes.

What does the Roman handbook say about ordinary things?
We forget them because they do not surprise us. Striking, strange, beautiful, funny or huge things are the ones we keep.
Talk about it. Why do you think a funny or shocking picture is easier to remember than a calm one?

People have been rediscovering this ever since. Modern memory trainers have turned the advice into a checklist you can use to build a picture on purpose. One popular version is a twelve-letter word, SMASHIN' SCOPE, and you will learn it in the next tab. Each letter is a way to make a picture richer: add a sense, add movement, add a joke, add a number.

There is a catch, and it is a good one. The picture has to be yours. A picture borrowed from someone else, however wild, does not hold as well as one you made, because making it is the work that fixes it in the mind. This lesson is where you start making them.

Why should you make your own pictures?
Making the picture is the work that fixes it in memory, so your own picture holds better than someone else's.
Talk about it. Describe, in two sentences, a picture you could use to remember the word Monday.

When the word is not a thing

You can picture an orange. You cannot picture justice or Brazil or lecture. For those you substitute: find something you can see that sounds like the word, or means something close to it, and picture that. Brazil sounds like bra; Russia sounds like rushing; lecture can be a person talking at a lectern. You will do a great deal of this in the rooms to come.

  1. Find the thing. A word you can see, or a substitute for it.
  2. Make it do something. Movement, a crash, an action.
  3. Add three tools from the checklist. Size, colour, sound, humour, a number.
  4. See it for a few seconds. Close your eyes. It is a picture only if you can see it.
Say it aloud. Describe the orange picture aloud, and add one more tool from the list that is not in the example.
