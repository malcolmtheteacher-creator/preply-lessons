Warm-up

Out loud, before anything else — don't worry about mistakes, just talk.

SPEAK

Think of a plan you made this week — a coffee, a lift somewhere, a dinner. How did you actually suggest it? Say the exact words you used.

Listen in on this plan being made

Jo: How about we grab a coffee Saturday morning?

Sam: I might be able to do that — depends on my sister's visit. Can I confirm Friday?

Jo: Sure. If she's not coming, we could try that new place near the cinema.

Sam: Deal. Unless something changes, let's say ten?

Look at what Sam does in four short lines. Three completely different grammar tools, all doing the work of pinning down one simple plan. Can you spot all three? Say them before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "I might be able to do that" — a hedge, honest about not being sure yet.
2. "If she's not coming, we could try..." — a conditional, negotiating what happens depending on the outcome.
3. "Unless something changes, let's say ten" — unless plus a suggestion, closing the deal.

This is why these tools matter: they ARE how real plans get made. Nobody just states a plan and hopes it works out.
Today: three tools for pinning down a plan — making a suggestion, negotiating it with if and unless, and being honest about how sure you are — so you can arrange something with someone and actually make it happen.

Shall We...?

Five different ways to float an idea without sounding like you're giving an order.

Spot the pattern

  • "Shall we try that new noodle place on Friday?"
  • "Why don't we just meet at the cinema instead of my place?"
  • "How about grabbing a coffee before the film?"

What's the shared job of these three? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
All three float an idea gently — an invitation to agree, not an instruction. Shall we and why don't we take a base verb straight after them (shall we try, why don't we meet). How about (and its twin what about) needs the -ing form instead: how about grabbing.
Shall we + base verb...?
Why don't we + base verb...?
We could + base verb...
How about / What about + verb-ing...?
Let's + base verb...
We could is the softest of the five — it just floats an option, no pressure. Let's is the most decisive — it sounds like you expect a yes.

"The queue for the new café's huge — we try the one round the corner instead?"

3. Turn this idea into a soft suggestion using we could: sit outside, since it's warm.

If We Leave at Six...

The grammar of reasoning a plan through out loud, before you agree to it.

Spot the pattern

  • "If we leave at six, we'll beat the traffic easily."
  • "We won't get a table unless we book ahead."
  • "If the weather's bad, we'll just stay in and order food instead."

What's the machinery, and what does unless actually mean? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
if / unless + present simple, ... will (or won't) + base verb. You're predicting the result of one option before you commit to it — perfect for talking a plan through. Unless already means "if...not" — "unless we book" = "if we don't book". Never stack a second negative on top of it.
If / Unless + present simple, ... will / won't + base verb
... will / won't + base verb ... if / unless + present simple
The trap: "unless we don't book" is wrong — unless is already negative. Just: unless we book.

Quick check

1. You're offering to collect a friend on Saturday, depending on whether they're free:

"If you're free Saturday, I you up around noon."

2. The film starts soon and you're both still getting ready:

"We the start of the film unless we leave now."

3. Combine using if:
Condition — you finish work early. Result — we can grab dinner before the shops close.

Now say it — talk the options through

SPEAK

Answer each with a real if or unless sentence, out loud:

  1. If it rains on the day, what's the backup plan?
  2. What happens if one of you is running late?
  3. Unless something changes, what time will you actually leave?
  4. If the first place is fully booked, what's plan B?
  5. What won't happen unless you book in advance?

I Can't Promise, But...

How to sound honest about a plan instead of vague or fake-certain.

Spot the pattern

  • "I might be able to come, but don't hold me to it."
  • "I should be free by seven, but plans keep changing."
  • "I can't promise anything, but I'll try."

How sure is each speaker, really? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
might be able to = genuinely not sure yet. should be = a confident guess, not a promise — this should has nothing to do with obligation. can't promise, but... = honest about the risk, followed by a real effort. All three protect you from breaking a promise you were never sure you could keep.
I might be able to + base verb (, but...)
I should be free / done / back... (a guess, not an order)
I can't promise, but I'll + base verb

Quick check

1. You'd like to come Saturday, but it depends on your sister's visit:

"I come Saturday, but I'm waiting to hear from my sister."

2. You're giving a confident guess about your evening, not a guarantee:

"I back by eight, but don't wait for me if I'm not."

Two futures that look similar but aren't

  • "I'm working late on Tuesday — I'll text you when I know."
  • "I'm seeing the dentist Thursday morning, so let's say afternoon."

Which future form is a diary fact, and which is a decision made on the spot?

Check the idea CLICK
am/is/are + -ing states a fixed arrangement — it's why you're not free. will is the on-the-spot reaction to that fact — "I'll text you" is decided as you speak, not booked in advance.
am / is / are + verb-ing → the fixed arrangement (why you're busy/free)
will + base verb → the decision made right now, reacting to it

The Negotiation

Everything together, out loud. This is the whole point of the lesson.

Round 1 — plan the weekend

Nothing is fixed yet: an entire free weekend, Saturday morning to Sunday evening.

Talk for two full minutes. Cover all three tools:

  • suggest things to do (shall we / how about / let's...)
  • negotiate times and options with if / unless / will
  • be honest with a hedge about anything you're not sure of
02:00

Round 2 — flip it: you can't have it your way

SPEAK

Now I'll make it difficult. I'm going to suggest three plans, one after another. Each time, refuse and immediately counter-offer — and use a different suggestion structure each time (why don't we / we could / how about + -ing / let's):

  1. First plan — refuse it, and counter-offer something else.
  2. Second plan — refuse again, with a real reason, and counter-offer.
  3. Third plan — refuse once more, then finally negotiate a time you'll both actually keep, using if or unless.

Round 3 — put it in writing

Write the 4–6 sentence message you'd send to lock in the weekend plan. Use: a suggestion, a conditional negotiation (if / unless), and a hedge (might be able to / should be / can't promise). The checker will tell you which it finds.

Real conversation never tells you which structure to use. Here everything's shuffled.
