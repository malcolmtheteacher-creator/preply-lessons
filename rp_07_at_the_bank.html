At the Bank

Your card died at the till yesterday. Then you found a charge on your statement — twice. One calm morning to put both right.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

Yesterday your card was declined at the supermarket till — cheerfully, loudly, and in front of a queue that suddenly had nothing better to look at than you. There was plenty in the account, so you have no idea why.

When you got home and opened the banking app to find out, you found something worse: a payment to a company you've never heard of — "NX Digital", forty pounds — and it's on there twice. Two problems, then, and one appointment at the bank this morning to sort them both out.

Behind the desk sits an adviser who has apologised on behalf of this bank a hundred times this month. They can genuinely fix things — lift blocks, raise disputes, move money — but only if you make it easy to say yes and hard to fob you off. The trick today isn't to shout. It's to stay pleasant, and completely immovable.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did a machine last let you down at the worst possible moment — a card declined, a phone dead, a ticket that wouldn't scan? How did you handle the audience?
  2. When a company gets something wrong, some people go quiet and some go straight to war. Where do you sit — and does "polite but firm" honestly get better results than either?

Who's who

You
The Customer 💳

Reasonable, unflappable — and nobody's pushover. You came to fix two things, and you're not leaving until both are done. Pleasantly.

The other voice
The Bank Adviser 🏦

Has said sorry for this bank all week. Can actually fix things — if you make saying yes the path of least resistance.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how the customer softens every sentence and yet never actually backs down.

AdviserMorning — have a seat. What can I do for you today?
YouMorning. I wonder if you could help me with something — two things, actually, and I'll try to keep it brief. My card was declined at the till yesterday, in front of a rather long queue, and I'm hoping you can shed some light on it.
AdviserOh, there's nothing worse — I'm sorry about that. Let me pull the account up for you. Right… I can see the card was blocked automatically. Our system flagged a payment as unusual: a large online purchase on Tuesday evening.
YouAh — that'll be the flights I booked, all perfectly genuine, I promise. I do see why the system got twitchy. That said, it would've been nice to get a text before you froze me out at the supermarket.
AdviserThat's a fair point, and I'll feed it back — we should have flagged it to you first. I can lift the block right now and send a code to your phone; that'll have you up and running in a couple of minutes.
YouThank you, that's a relief. While you've got the account open, though — there's a second thing, and this one I'm rather less relaxed about. There seems to be a charge I don't recognise. Forty pounds, to something called "NX Digital", on the 3rd.
AdviserLet me have a look… yes, I see it. Hmm. And actually — it's been charged twice. Same amount, same company, two minutes apart.
YouSo I've been charged twice for something I never bought once. I do appreciate these things happen — but you'll understand I'd like to get to the bottom of it, and get the money back.
AdviserCompletely understandable. It has the look of a duplicate from the merchant's side, but I can't rule out fraud either. My advice would be to raise a dispute on both charges and reissue your card, just to be safe.
YouThat sounds sensible — let's do that. Realistically, though, how long am I looking at to see the money again?
AdviserA provisional refund usually lands within five working days; the full investigation can take up to fifteen. I'll give you a reference number so you can chase it if it goes quiet.
YouI'd appreciate that. And could I get the key points in writing as well, just for my own records? Plus — if the fifteen days come and go with nothing, who exactly should I be asking for?
AdviserI'll email you a summary today, reference and all. If it does drag on, ask for the disputes team directly and quote that number — don't let anyone send you back to square one.
YouThat's exactly what I needed to hear. You've been genuinely helpful — which, after the week I've had, I honestly wasn't expecting. Thank you.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Why did the bank's system block the card — and how does the customer react to that reason? What do they accept, and what do they push back on?

2. The customer raises a second, bigger problem. What exactly is wrong with the "NX Digital" charge, and what two things does the adviser propose to put it right?

3. Before leaving, the customer asks for two specific things to protect themselves. What are they — and why do they matter more than the adviser's spoken promise?

1. The system flagged a large online purchase (the flights) as unusual and blocked the card automatically. The customer accepts the logic — "I do see why the system got twitchy" — but pushes back on the fact that nobody warned them before it failed embarrassingly at the till. They concede the point they can't win to win the one they can.
2. The forty-pound charge to "NX Digital" has been applied twice — same amount, same company, two minutes apart (a duplicate, or possibly fraud). The adviser proposes to (a) raise a dispute on both charges and (b) reissue the card, to be safe.
3. A summary of the key points in writing, and a reference number plus the name of who to ask for if the refund is late. They matter because a verbal promise vanishes — a paper trail and a reference turn "I'll sort it" into something the customer can actually chase and hold the bank to.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for getting a company to fix its own mistake — polite enough to keep them helping you, firm enough that they can't wriggle away. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚪 Opening calmly

Phrase 1

You've sat down annoyed, but you want to open in a way that keeps the adviser firmly on your side rather than braced for a fight. Which opener does that?

A. "Right, I've got a complaint to make."
B. "Someone at your bank has clearly messed up."
C. "I wonder if you could help me with something."
C — "I wonder if you could help me with something."
It casts the adviser as your ally, not your opponent — you're asking for help, not opening fire. You can still be firm later; you just don't start there.
Phrase 2

The block on your card makes no sense to you, and you want the adviser to explain it — without it sounding like an accusation. There's a warm idiom for "help me understand this": "I'm hoping you can ___ some ___ on it."

"I'm hoping you can shed some light on it."
"Shed light on" invites explanation rather than demanding a defence. It treats the problem as a shared puzzle you'll solve together — which makes the other person want to help.

🔍 Naming the problem — without the accusation

Phrase 3

You've found a charge you never made. A beginner snaps, "You've taken my money for no reason." Which version leaves room for an honest error — and is far harder to argue back against?

A. "You've taken my money for no reason."
B. "There seems to be a charge I don't recognise."
B — "There seems to be a charge I don't recognise."
"There seems to be…" and "I don't recognise" describe what you see instead of blaming them. Nobody can dispute what you recognise — so there's nothing to argue with, only something to fix.
Phrase 4

You want to state — plainly, but without heat — that your card was stopped and nobody told you why. Put the pieces in order:

and I'veany explanation.My card's been blocked,not been given
"My card's been blocked, and I've not been given any explanation."
Two flat facts, no adjectives, no anger — which makes it land harder than any outburst. "I've not been given…" quietly puts the missing explanation on them, without a single accusing word.
Phrase 5

Worse still: you were billed for the very same thing twice. State it in one clean sentence that begins with what you spotted: "I noticed I've been ___ ___ for the same transaction."

"I noticed I've been charged twice for the same transaction."
"I noticed…" is calm and factual — it reports evidence rather than launching a complaint. "For the same transaction" nails the problem precisely, so it can't be waved away as confusion on your part.

🧱 Firm but polite — holding your ground

Phrase 6

The adviser offers a sympathetic "these things happen." You want to accept the sympathy but absolutely NOT let the matter drop. What three-part move does both at once — concede, turn, then restate what you want?

"I appreciate these things happen, but I'd still like to get to the bottom of it."
"I appreciate…, but I'd still like…" concedes the small point to win the big one. You sound reasonable and immovable in the same breath — the exact tone that gets things fixed.
Phrase 7

You want to insist this gets solved today — but stay this side of hostile. Which line is firm without turning the adviser against you?

A. "I'm not leaving here until this is fixed."
B. "I'd like to get this resolved today, if at all possible."
B — "I'd like to get this resolved today, if at all possible."
A is a threat; B is a clear expectation wrapped in courtesy. "If at all possible" gives them room to agree without feeling cornered — and people help willingly when they don't feel bullied.
Phrase 8

The adviser starts to send you off to a department you've already tried. You need the polite phrase that signals "I'm still calm — but don't fob me off, because I've done this before." Begin: "___ ___, I've already explained this twice."

"With respect, I've already explained this twice."
"With respect…" is the polite red flag — it means you're keeping your temper but you've noticed you're being passed around. It draws a firm line while still sounding perfectly civil.

🤝 Fixing it — and protecting yourself

Phrase 9

The person in front of you simply can't authorise the refund. How do you ask for someone who can — without making it personal or making them feel useless?

A. "Get me your manager."
B. "Is there someone I could speak to who'd be able to authorise that?"
B — "Is there someone I could speak to who'd be able to authorise that?"
A humiliates the person and stiffens their resistance. B escalates the problem, not the argument — it treats them as your guide to the right person, so they help you climb rather than block the stairs.
Phrase 10

Everything's agreed — but spoken promises evaporate the moment you're out the door. Ask for the ONE thing that lets you chase this later if it all goes quiet.

"Could I get a reference number for this — and the key points in writing, just for my records?"
A reference number plus something in writing turns a promise into a paper trail. "Just for my records" makes the request sound routine and harmless — while quietly giving you everything you'd need to hold them to it.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole visit out loud using all ten, in order — calm opening → name the problems → hold your ground → get it fixed → protect yourself. When it flows without heat, you're ready for Your Turn.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your bank, your bad week. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer to keep the pressure of a real appointment behind you.

The Twist

🌩️ The refund that vanished

One more round — no script, no safety net. Fifteen working days have come and gone. No refund, no email, and no reference number that anyone on the phone can seem to find. You call the bank and get bounced — twice — and now a third person is gently suggesting that perhaps you authorised the charge after all. Start the timer and talk your way through it: stay maddeningly polite, but completely immovable. Establish the facts, refuse to be sent back to square one, ask for the disputes team by name, and don't hang up without a firm commitment AND a way to hold them to it. One rule: the second you lose your temper, they win — because a calm customer who simply will not go away is a bank's worst nightmare.

The Echo 🕰️

Six hundred years ago in Florence, a family called the Medici turned money-lending into an art — and poured the profits into a fair chunk of the Renaissance. They also invented the very tools you leaned on this morning: branches in every major city, letters of credit so merchants needn't carry a purse of gold, and the double-entry ledger — the bookkeeping trick that catches a charge billed twice.

Every time you check a statement, query a payment, or ask for something "in writing", you're using habits the Medici made ordinary. A bank, in the end, is just a promise written down carefully — which is exactly why you asked for that reference number.

They learned the hard way, too, what happens when a bank stops keeping careful books: their empire eventually collapsed under bad loans it should have caught. Your forty pounds was small by comparison — but the principle was identical. Always make them show you the ledger.
