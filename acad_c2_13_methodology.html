Writing Methodology Sections

Justifying research design and establishing scholarly credibility

10:00

The Distinction That Matters

Researchers often confuse methodology and method. Method refers to specific technical procedures: interview protocol, statistical test, coding scheme. Methodology refers to the philosophical and theoretical frameworks governing your research: your epistemological position, how you understand knowledge creation, what you consider evidence.

Why the Distinction Matters

Methodology sections should accomplish more than list procedures. They should articulate your philosophical positioning: you are not merely reporting what you did but justifying why this approach appropriately addresses your research question given your assumptions about how knowledge is created. This moves your methodology beyond technical competence to intellectual sophistication.

Philosophical Positioning

Consider your epistemological assumptions. Do you believe reality is objective, waiting to be discovered (realist epistemology)? Or do you understand reality as socially constructed, interpreted through human meaning-making (constructivist epistemology)? Your answer shapes everything: quantitative experiments suit realist assumptions; qualitative interviews suit constructivist assumptions. Yet many researchers never explicitly acknowledge these assumptions.

Paradigm Choice and Justification

Different disciplines embrace different paradigms. Positivist paradigms emphasize objectivity, generalizability, and causal mechanisms. Interpretive paradigms emphasize meaning-making and context-specific understanding. Critical paradigms emphasize power relations and social change. Your choice should be explicit and justified: not "I chose qualitative methods" but "I adopted an interpretive paradigm because understanding how individuals construct meaning requires access to their perspectives, which interviews enable in ways surveys cannot."

Methodology Vocabulary

Epistemology Philosophical framework specifying how knowledge is created and what counts as evidence.
Ontology Fundamental assumptions about the nature of reality; shapes methodological choices.
Paradigm Overarching framework (positivist, interpretive, critical) guiding research approach.
Positivism Paradigm emphasizing objective reality, empirical evidence, and universal laws.
Interpretivism Paradigm emphasizing meaning-making, context, and understanding human experience.
Reflexivity Critical examination of how researcher assumptions influence research design and findings.
Rigor Quality criteria ensuring trustworthy research; definitions vary across paradigms.
Mixed methods Research combining quantitative and qualitative data; requires justifying integration rationale.

The Register of Methodological Justification

Methodology sections demand a distinctive voice: assertive enough to justify your choices without defensive apologizing. You are explaining deliberate decisions, not excusing limitations.

Positioning Your Paradigm

Rather than declaring "I chose qualitative methods," explain: "This research adopts an interpretive paradigm, grounded in the epistemological position that understanding how individuals construct meaning around [phenomenon] requires access to their lived experience. Qualitative interviews enable this access by... Surveys, which assume respondents" experience can be represented through predetermined categories, cannot provide this understanding because..." This approach asserts your epistemological grounding while explaining what alternative approaches cannot accomplish.

Justifying Design Elements

Every methodological choice should be justified. Sample size reflects the research question (depth vs. breadth trade-offs) and saturation principles in qualitative work or power analysis in quantitative work. Data collection procedures connect to research questions (interviews answer "how" and "why" questions; surveys answer "how many" questions). Analysis procedures reflect the data type and research question.

Strong methodological positioning: "Following a pragmatist approach that recognizes multiple legitimate ways of knowing, this research employs mixed methods. Quantitative survey data address the question of prevalence (how widespread is this phenomenon?); qualitative interviews explore the question of mechanism (how does this phenomenon develop?). Integration occurs at the interpretation stage, where quantitative patterns are illuminated through qualitative explanation."

Transparency About Researcher Positionality

In interpretive research, acknowledge how you, as researcher, influence the work. "My background in [field/identity] shapes how I approach this work. I recognize [specific way this influences interpretation]. To minimize bias, I employed [specific strategy]." This reflexivity demonstrates intellectual maturity and enables readers to calibrate trust in your findings.

Building Your Methodological Argument

Draft a brief methodological justification for your research (or research you might conduct):

Your Methodology Framework

Epistemological grounding: What assumptions do you hold about how knowledge is created? Why?
Paradigmatic choice: Do you align with positivist, interpretive, critical, pragmatist, or other paradigms? Why is this appropriate for your research question?
Method justification: Why does your chosen methodology appropriately address your research question given your paradigmatic stance?
Design particulars: Sample size, data collection procedures, analysis approach—each justified by reference to research question and paradigm.
Quality assurance: How do you ensure rigor? Quality criteria differ across paradigms; specify yours explicitly.
Researcher reflexivity: How do your assumptions, identity, and positionality influence this work? What steps minimize bias?

Your Methodological Justification

Write 300-400 words justifying your research methodology. Focus on the epistemological and paradigmatic grounding, not just technical procedures.

Methodology as Scholarly Contribution

Strong methodology sections demonstrate intellectual sophistication. They show that you have thought carefully about how knowledge is created, that your design choices reflect this thinking, and that you can articulate complex epistemological positions clearly.

Mixed Methods and Methodological Innovation

Mixed-methods research—integrating quantitative and qualitative data—requires particularly nuanced methodological justification. You must explain not just why each method is appropriate but how you integrate them. Do quantitative findings generate hypotheses for qualitative exploration? Does qualitative analysis explain quantitative patterns? Rigorous mixed-methods research carefully specifies integration rationale.

Reflect: How explicitly have you articulated your epistemological grounding in previous research? What assumptions about knowledge creation guide your work? Have you ever felt defensive about your methodological choices? Use these reflections to strengthen your methodological positioning going forward.

The Bridge to Legitimacy

Methodology sections ultimately establish credibility. Readers need to understand not just what you did but why this approach appropriately answers your research question. When methodology is clearly justified, findings gain credibility even if some readers might have chosen differently. Conversely, poorly justified methodology undermines findings regardless of their statistical significance.
