Why Methodology Sections Often Fail

Many researchers write methodology sections that read like recipes: "Step 1: We recruited 50 participants. Step 2: We administered surveys. Step 3: We analysed the data using SPSS."

The reader is left asking: Why this sample size? Why surveys instead of interviews? Why SPSS instead of manual coding? A strong methodology section answers these questions before they're asked.

Your Turn For each major choice in your methodology (sample size, method, analysis technique), can you give one sentence explaining why it was necessary? If you can't, you need to add that justification.

Six Moves for Methodology Strength

1 Ontology and Epistemology in Plain Language +
Every methodology rests on assumptions about reality (ontology) and how we know things (epistemology). Name these openly—even if you don't use the technical terms.
Weak

"We used a qualitative approach."

Strong

"This study is grounded in a constructionist perspective: meaning isn't discovered but created through interaction. Therefore, quantitative surveys couldn't capture what we needed—how people actively construct meaning in workplace conversations. We required methods that preserve the interaction itself."

The Move

State your philosophical foundation → Show how it constrains/enables your choices → Connect it to your research question

2 Justifying Your Design Choice +
For each major methodological decision, show that you considered alternatives and chose this one for good reasons—not out of convenience.
Weak

"We conducted 30 interviews."

Strong

"We selected interviews over focus groups because our research question required understanding individual decision-making processes. Focus groups would have captured group dynamics but not the privacy needed for people to discuss sensitive workplace conflicts. With 30 interviews, we reached thematic saturation within our sector—further interviews produced no new codes."

The Move

Name the choice → Show the alternative you rejected → Give your reason → Show how you know when you've done enough

3 The Replication Standard +
A methodology section should be detailed enough that a competent researcher could replicate your study using your description. This is a gold standard for credibility.
Vague

"We analysed the data thematically."

Replicable

"We used Braun and Clarke's six-phase thematic analysis. Two researchers independently coded the first five interviews line-by-line using NVivo 12. We met weekly to resolve discrepancies, establishing shared definitions of codes before proceeding. Inter-coder reliability was 83%. We identified themes through iterative comparison, checking that each theme was supported by at least three distinct interview excerpts."

The Move

Name the framework → Give specific software and settings → Show how many people did it → Show how you checked your work

4 Positionality Without Oversharing +
Your identity shapes what you can see and what you might miss. Acknowledge this—but keep it scholarly, not autobiographical.
Too Personal

"I was an office manager for ten years, so I understand workplace dynamics intimately. This gave me insider knowledge."

Balanced

"As a former office manager, I held insider knowledge of workplace norms—an advantage for understanding tacit communication. However, this familiarity may have led me to overlook practices that seemed 'normal' to me. To mitigate this, we employed member checking: participants reviewed preliminary findings to highlight aspects I might have normalised."

The Move

Name your position → Show how it's an asset → Show how it's a liability → Show what you did about it

5 Limitations That Strengthen, Not Weaken +
Every study has limitations. Name them openly—and show that you designed around them. This is confidence, not weakness.
Apologetic

"Due to time constraints, we could only interview people in one region. This is a limitation."

Strategic

"We focused on one region to enable depth: intensive follow-up interviews with each participant across six months. Geographic generalisability is not the goal here—contextual understanding is. Our findings may not transfer to different economic contexts, which is an avenue for future research."

The Move

Name the limitation → Explain why you made that choice → Show what you gained by it → Point to what future work would need to do

6 Responding to the Methodology Reviewer +
When a reviewer asks for more methodology justification, they're asking you to make your thinking visible. In your response letter, do this strategically.
Weak Response

"We used interviews because they are a valid research method."

Strong Response

"The reviewer raises an important point about methodological justification. We have revised the methodology section to clarify our philosophical foundation: this study operates from a constructionist epistemology where meaning is co-created. This assumption is foundational to our choice of interviews over surveys. We have also added a paragraph explaining why interviews were the only method that could capture the interactional nature of workplace sense-making. These additions are marked in blue in the revised manuscript."

The Move

Acknowledge what they're really asking → Show where you've added justification → Be specific about changes → Use active language ("we have revised")

Revising Your Methodology Section

The reviewer is right: your methodology describes but doesn't justify. You need to go deeper—show the thinking behind every choice.

Test for Replicability

Now ask: Could someone else do this study using only my description? What's missing?

Replicability Checklist

PARTICIPANTS Can I recruit the same population using your description?
DATA COLLECTION Do I know exactly what you did, what tools you used, what you said?
ANALYSIS Would I end up with the same codes or themes following your process?
QUALITY CHECKS Did you describe how you ensured your findings are trustworthy?
Speak for 2 Minutes Walk through your revised methodology. What's the one piece of detail that makes your work replicable by someone else?

The move: Show philosophical/practical differences. Be honest if it was partly convenience.

Answer: "Excellent point. We have added a paragraph clarifying why thematic analysis fits our question better than grounded theory. Our goal is to map patterns in how people narrate conflict—not to build a new theory of conflict itself. Thematic analysis is explicitly designed for pattern identification. Grounded theory's iterative sampling and constant comparison would have served if we wanted to generate novel theory. We chose thematic analysis because it matched our research goal. Answer: "We agree that 'rigorous' is meaningless without detail.
