The Money You Lent

Two hundred pounds, four months ago, and not a word about it since. You don't need the money back. You need him to admit it exists.

The Scene 🎬

In March, Jonah was in a hole. He didn't ask twice and he didn't make a speech about it — he just said the number, two hundred, and looked at the table while he said it. You sent it that evening and you were glad to. He said "next month" and he meant it, and you believed him, and that was that.

It is now July. There has been no transfer, and — which is the odd part — no mention. Not a joke about it, not an "I've not forgotten", nothing. What there has been is two holiday photos: a white wall, a lizard, a very good-looking lunch. And a new pair of trainers, which you noticed, and which you would not have noticed in March.

You're not skint. That's what makes this so annoying. If you needed the money you'd have rung him in April and it would have been simple. Instead you've spent four months doing sums about somebody else's life, and he has absolutely no idea you're doing them. Jonah isn't a crook. He's just buried something uncomfortable in the garden and hoped it would rot down into nothing.

He's in your kitchen. He's got his keys in his hand. He's about to leave.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Have you ever lent money to a friend and then not mentioned it again? How long did you last before it started to bother you — and what, exactly, was bothering you?
  2. In your family or your country, is money between friends something you talk about openly, or is it one of the things people go quiet about? Which do you think does less damage?

Who's who

You
The One Who Lent It 💷

Fond of him, still. Not short of money, and not angry either — just tired of noticing things. You want one conversation, one date in a calendar, and your friend back.

The other voice
Jonah 🌵

Not dishonest. He was going to sort it, then it got embarrassing, then it got old, and now it's the kind of thing he can't raise without admitting he never raised it. So he says nothing, and posts a photo of a lizard.

It's a bit later, when You stop talking about money altogether and say the true thing instead: that the counting has started, and it's horrible. That's the line that lands, and everything useful happens after it.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Jonah's very first reaction isn't an apology and it isn't a refusal. What does he actually reach for, and what does that tell you about how ashamed he already is?

2. There's a moment where Jonah starts defending himself and then stops himself halfway through the sentence. What set him off, and what stopped him?

3. By the end Jonah has given two things and withheld one. What are they?

1. He reaches for busyness — he's been meaning to, work's been mad — which is an excuse rather than an apology, and he repeats "I have been meaning to" as though saying it twice makes it more true. People who feel caught out tend to explain before they apologise, so the scrambling is itself a sign he knows exactly what he owes.
2. The holiday set him off: as soon as it came up he started explaining who paid for Portugal. What stopped him was hearing himself do it — he cuts the sentence off and admits "you're not wrong", which is the first honest thing he says.
3. He gives an apology of a sort, and he gives two actual dates — half on the thirtieth, the rest the thirtieth after. What he withholds is whatever is genuinely wrong with him, which he says isn't money and promises to explain another time.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for asking someone you love for what they owe you. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Everything here depends on tone, because the words alone can sound either kind or cold. Keep your voice light and your face friendly, and — this is the hard bit — don't slow down and go serious when you say the number, because the moment you go solemn it starts to sound like a formal demand rather than a friend mentioning something. Say "two hundred" the way you'd say "the tickets". The lightness is what tells him you're not accusing him of anything.

🚪 Raising it at all

Phrase 1

He's got his keys in his hand and you're about to bring up something awkward. You need an opener that stops him leaving, warns him gently, and — crucially — shrinks the thing before he can inflate it. Which one does all three?

A. "We need to have a proper talk about something."
B. "Don't take this the wrong way — it's not a big thing."
C. "Before you go, I've got an issue I want to raise with you."
B — "Don't take this the wrong way — it's not a big thing."
To take something the wrong way is to hear an innocent remark as an attack, so asking someone not to take it the wrong way is a way of telling them in advance what your intention is, before they've had a chance to decide for themselves. A and C both announce that something serious is coming, which makes the other person defensive before you've said a word. B does the opposite: it makes the thing smaller, which is the only way a person will stay in the room for it.
Phrase 2

You're going to admit that you've been avoiding this conversation for months, and you're going to call yourself an idiot for it — which, oddly, makes it easier for him. Complete the chunk. The gap is a two-word phrasal verb meaning "delaying something you don't want to do": "I've been ___ ___ saying this, which is stupid."

"I've been putting off saying this, which is stupid."
To put something off is to keep postponing it, usually because it's unpleasant rather than because you're busy. The clever part is "which is stupid": you take the blame for the delay before he can be blamed for anything, so the conversation starts with you admitting a fault rather than with him being accused of one. It also quietly explains why you're raising something from four months ago, which otherwise looks strange.

🪶 Naming it without an accusation

Phrase 3

Now the number itself. You want a question that asks for information rather than for money — one that treats the debt as a shared situation you're both looking at, not a crime he committed. Put the pieces in order, after "That two hundred from back in March —":

witharewherethat?we
"That two hundred from back in March — where are we with that?"
Notice the "we". "When are you paying me back?" puts him on one side and you on the other, whereas "where are we with that?" is the sort of thing colleagues say about a job that's half done, and it treats the money as a task that's still open rather than a failing of his. Saying the amount and the month plainly also matters, because it shows you're not being coy and there's nothing to negotiate about the facts.
Phrase 4

He asks why you never said anything. The honest answer is complicated: it genuinely wasn't on your mind, and then suddenly it was, all the time. Which line admits that muddle instead of pretending to a tidier story?

A. "I'd honestly forgotten about it until I hadn't, if that makes sense."
B. "I've been waiting four months for you to bring it up."
C. "It's been on my mind every single day since March."
A — "I'd honestly forgotten about it until I hadn't, if that makes sense."
It's a strange little sentence — "forgotten until I hadn't" shouldn't really work — and that's exactly why it's believable, because that is genuinely how these things go. B and C are both accusations wearing different hats: one says he failed a test he didn't know he was taking, the other says he's been hurting you daily, and either one hands him a large bill of guilt. "If that makes sense" is the tag that lets you say something imperfectly worded without having to defend the wording.

🗓️ Refusing the vague promise

Phrase 5

He's just promised, sincerely, to sort it "soon" — and "soon" is what he said in March. You need to decline the vagueness without calling him a liar. Put the pieces in order:

have a date.I'd just rather"Soon" is fine —
"'Soon' is fine — I'd just rather have a date."
The first half accepts his word, which stops it being a fight, and the second half quietly refuses to accept it as an answer, which is the whole job. "I'd just rather" is about as soft as English gets — it's a preference, not a demand — and yet what you're asking for is completely specific and he can't really say no to it. A vague promise costs a person nothing, and that's exactly why it gets made; a date is the smallest thing you can ask for that actually changes something.
Phrase 6

You want him to know you'll take the money in pieces, at his speed, and that you are not going to become the person who rings him about it. One word fills the gap — normally it means running after someone, but here it means repeatedly contacting someone about money they owe: "Even a bit of it, whenever. I'm not ___ you."

"Even a bit of it, whenever. I'm not chasing you."
To chase someone for money is to keep ringing and emailing until they pay — it's what a company's accounts department does to you, and it's a slightly humiliating thing to have done to you by a friend. Saying "I'm not chasing you" names the thing he's afraid of and takes it off the table, and "even a bit of it, whenever" makes the offer real rather than polite, because a person who says "pay whenever you like" and then adds a condition wasn't offering anything.

💬 Saying the real reason out loud

Phrase 7

Here's the turn. Money isn't actually your problem — you're fine. Your problem is what four months of silence has done to you: you've started tallying up everything he spends. Which line says that, and doesn't make him the villain of it?

A. "You went to Portugal, though, didn't you."
B. "It's not the money. It's that I've started keeping score, and I hate it."
C. "It's fine. Honestly, forget it, it doesn't matter."
B — "It's not the money. It's that I've started keeping score, and I hate it."
To keep score is to count who's ahead — it comes from games, and that's the horror of it, because you've started playing a game against your own friend without telling him the rules. Notice that the ugly thing in that sentence belongs to you, not to him: you're the one keeping score and you hate it, so there's nothing for him to defend against, which is why he stops defending. A is the accusation you've been swallowing for months and it will start a row; C is a lie, and it's the lie that got you here. (Worth saying: if you were genuinely skint — broke, no money at all — this would be a much easier conversation, because then you could just say you need it.)
Phrase 8

Explain why you're raising it today rather than letting it run: you'd take one uncomfortable ten minutes over four more months of low-level resentment. Put the pieces in order:

every week.than think about itI'd rather havethis conversation once
"I'd rather have this conversation once than think about it every week."
It reframes the awkwardness as the cheap option, which it is — one bad ten minutes against months of quiet arithmetic — and it tells him something he didn't know, which is that this has been running in your head for a while. "I'd rather X than Y" is a useful shape for any awkward conversation, because it presents you as choosing the lesser of two unpleasant things rather than as someone who enjoys confrontation.

🤝 Putting the friendship back

Phrase 9

He's given you the dates. The money part is over — but a man who vanishes for four months over two hundred pounds might have something else going on, and this is the moment to ask, in a way that makes clear you're not being polite. In your own words: how would you ask? Say it out loud, then type it.

e.g. "Are you alright, though? Genuinely."
Two things are doing the work. "Though" hooks the question onto everything that's just been said, so it means "money aside — you, though. How are you?" And "genuinely" is there because "are you alright?" on its own gets an automatic "yeah, fine" and both of you know it; adding "genuinely" tells him you'd like the real answer, and gives him permission to give one. He still might not, and that's allowed — the offer is what matters.
Phrase 10

Last job: close it so completely that he can be in your kitchen tomorrow without thinking about any of this. Which line does that?

A. "Right, that's it done. Never mentioning it again."
B. "Okay, well, just don't forget this time, yeah?"
C. "So we're agreed — the thirtieth, and then the thirtieth after that."
A — "Right, that's it done. Never mentioning it again."
"Right" is the little British word that shuts a topic and opens the next one, and "that's it done" declares the matter finished rather than merely paused. The promise never to mention it again is the actual gift, because what he's been dreading isn't paying you — it's that this will now be a thing between you forever. B leaves a threat hanging in the air. C is technically sensible but it sounds like the end of a meeting, and you have just spent ten minutes carefully making sure this wasn't one.

The Twist

💸 The offer instead

One more round — no script. This time Jonah tells you the truth: he hasn't got it. Not now, not on the thirtieth, not in a month, and he's not going to insult you by inventing a date he already knows he'll miss. What he offers instead is the thing he's actually good at — the plastering, the accounts, the guitar lessons for your daughter, whatever it is in your version — for free, for as long as it takes to be worth two hundred. He means it. He might even be relieved. Now you have to answer, and both answers cost you something: say yes and the debt turns into a job with no end date, and you'll be counting hours instead of pounds; say no and you've just refused the only thing he has to give, which is a fairly brutal thing to do to a man who's finally being honest with you. Start the timer. Decide out loud, in front of him, and make him believe you meant it.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Lending to a friend is a test that neither of you agreed to sit, and the marking is secret. You hand the money over in about four seconds, feeling rather good about yourself, and only later do you find that you've quietly appointed yourself as someone with an interest in how they live — what they buy, where they go, whether they deserve it.

And the debt itself is rarely what breaks the friendship. Two hundred pounds is survivable; most things are. What isn't survivable is the silence around it, growing week by week, because the longer neither of you says it the more it costs to say, until eventually it's cheaper for both of you to just see a bit less of each other.

Which is why some people have a rule: if you can afford to lose it, give it, and if you can't afford to lose it, don't lend it. It sounds generous and it may be, but there's something a bit cowardly in it too — it's a way of never having the conversation at all.

Two bigger questions

  1. "Only lend what you can afford to give away." Is that wisdom, or is it just a way of avoiding an awkward conversation for the rest of your life? What would you actually do with a friend who asked for two hundred tomorrow?
  2. Jonah's silence made things worse for both of them, and yet it made perfect sense to him at the time. Why is it so much easier to say nothing? Think of something you've buried and hoped would go away — did it?
