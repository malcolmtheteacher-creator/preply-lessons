Question 1: What is Just War Theory, and what are its main criteria?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Just War Theory is a Christian teaching that war can be morally justified under strict conditions. Main criteria include: just cause (e.g., self-defence), right intention, legitimate authority, last resort, probability of success, and proportionality (response matches the harm). It contrasts with pacifism and allows defensive warfare while forbidding unnecessary violence.

Question 2: Explain the difference between Greater Jihad and Lesser Jihad in Islam.

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Greater Jihad is the internal spiritual struggle within oneself to live according to Islamic teachings and resist temptation. Lesser Jihad is the external struggle or physical effort to defend Islam and the Muslim community. Islamic teaching emphasises that the greater jihad (spiritual discipline) is more important than physical struggle. The word "jihad" means struggle or effort, not necessarily war.

Question 3: What is the Buddhist concept of ahimsa, and why is it central to Buddhist ethics?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Ahimsa is the Buddhist principle of non-harm or non-violence towards all living beings. It is central to Buddhist ethics because the Buddha taught that violence causes suffering (dukkha) for both the victim and the person acting violently. Ahimsa extends beyond not killing to include avoiding anger, hatred, and harsh words. Compassion (metta) and loving-kindness are seen as the antidote to violence.

Question 4: What is Dharam Yudh in Sikhism, and what makes it different from ordinary warfare?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Dharam Yudh (righteous war) is Sikh teaching that permits warfare as a last resort to defend faith, protect the innocent, and fight injustice. It differs from ordinary warfare because it is fought for religious freedom and justice, not for conquest, greed, or revenge. The Gurus taught that Sikhs must be both sant (saint—spiritually dedicated) and sipahi (soldier—willing to fight injustice). Dharam Yudh must be fought with honour and restraint.

Encounter: Synthesis of Teachings Across Traditions

The Unit's Central Question

Throughout Unit 10, we have asked: "Is religion a force for peace or conflict?" This is not a question with a simple yes/no answer. Each tradition has teachings promoting peace and, in certain contexts, allowing or justifying conflict. Your task is to understand these nuances and build an evidence-based argument.

Christianity's Approach to Peace and Conflict

Core Values on Peace

  • Jesus's teachings: "Love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44), "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9), and "Turn the other cheek" (Matthew 5:39) set an ideal of radical forgiveness and non-retaliation
  • The example of martyrs: Early Christian saints chose persecution and death rather than violence, modeling non-violent resistance
  • Pacifist traditions: Quakers, Mennonites, and other Christian denominations teach absolute non-violence as a religious commitment

Teaching on Justified Conflict

  • Just War Theory: Catholic and mainline Protestant traditions teach that under strict conditions—just cause, right intention, last resort, proportionality—defensive warfare can be morally permissible
  • Context matters: Self-defence and protection of the innocent are seen as obligations that may justify limited, proportional force

Synthesis: Christianity holds both an ideal of radical peace and a practical theology of justified defence. The tension between these reflects deep theological debate.

Islam's Approach to Peace and Conflict

Core Values on Peace

  • Islam means "submission" and "peace": The Arabic root connects to salaam (peace). Muslims greet each other with "Assalamu alaikum" (peace be upon you)
  • Greater Jihad: The internal spiritual struggle is emphasised as more important than physical effort. Living morally and resisting temptation is the primary struggle
  • Qur'anic teachings: "There is no compulsion in religion" (2:256) and "If they incline to peace, incline to it" (8:61) teach respect for others and preference for peace
  • Ummah (community): Muslims are taught to see all believers as one community and to work for collective welfare

Teaching on Lesser Jihad

  • Defence permitted: The Qur'an permits fighting against those who persecute believers: "Permission to fight is given to those being fought against" (22:39)
  • Rules of engagement: Shari'ah law sets strict rules: no killing of civilians, women, children, or monks; no destruction of property beyond necessity
  • Last resort: Warfare is permitted only when peaceful solutions have failed and Muslims face persecution

Synthesis: Islamic teaching prioritises peace and the spiritual struggle but permits defensive violence under strict ethical guidelines. The term "jihad" is widely misunderstood; it primarily means internal spiritual struggle.

Buddhism's Approach to Peace and Conflict

Core Values on Non-Violence

  • First Precept: "I undertake to refrain from killing living beings"—the foundation of Buddhist ethics
  • Ahimsa (non-harm): Non-violence extends to thought, speech, and action. Anger, hatred, and greed are seen as the roots of violence
  • Karma and consequence: Violence creates negative karma and perpetuates cycles of suffering. Compassion and loving-kindness (metta) are the antidotes
  • Dukkha (suffering): All violence causes suffering—to victims, perpetrators, and communities. The Buddha taught a path away from all forms of suffering

Historical Buddhist Approaches to Conflict

  • Monasticism: Traditional Buddhist monks are celibate and live in non-violent communities separated from warfare
  • Engaged Buddhism: Modern Buddhist activists use non-violent civil disobedience and advocacy to challenge injustice (e.g., Thich Nhat Hanh's peace activism in Vietnam)
  • Some Buddhist-majority societies: Historically, some Buddhist kingdoms used violence in warfare, showing a gap between ideal and practice

Synthesis: Buddhism teaches absolute non-violence as an ideal and a path to wisdom. Where Buddhist societies have engaged in warfare, this represents a failure to live up to Buddhist principles rather than a Buddhist justification for violence.

Sikhism's Approach to Peace and Conflict

Core Values on Peace and Justice

  • Kirat karo (honest living): Sikhs are taught to earn honestly and share wealth justly, promoting social peace through economic fairness
  • Langar (communal meal): Free meals for all, regardless of caste, religion, or status, embody equality and break down social divisions
  • Seva (selfless service): Serving others is a core value that promotes community harmony and social responsibility

Teaching on Dharam Yudh (Righteous War)

  • Sant (saint) and Sipahi (soldier): Guru Gobind Singh taught that Sikhs must be both spiritually devoted and willing to fight injustice
  • Conditions for warfare: Fighting is permitted only as a last resort to defend faith, protect innocents, and fight tyranny. It must be fought with honour, not for revenge or conquest
  • Historical context: Sikhs faced persecution from Mughal rulers and later British colonial rule. Armed resistance was seen as justified self-defence

Synthesis: Sikhism balances spiritual devotion with active justice-seeking. Peace is the ideal, but defending the vulnerable and resisting tyranny can justify proportional force.

What Connects These Traditions?

Shared Principles: All four traditions value peace as an ideal and teach compassion, justice, and respect for others. All limit violence to defence of self or community. All teach that violence has moral costs and consequences. All emphasise intention and restraint. None teaches aggression for greed, conquest, or revenge.
Where They Differ: Buddhism and Christian pacifism teach absolute non-violence. Christianity (Just War Theory), Islam, and Sikhism teach that defensive violence can be morally justified under strict conditions. The traditions differ on what constitutes justified cause, who can authorise it, and what rules apply.

Key Insight: Religion as Tool, Not Cause

The evidence suggests that religion is not inherently peaceful or violent. Religious teachings are interpreted by people in specific contexts. Religion can inspire peace and compassion — or be distorted to justify violence. The question "Is religion a force for peace or conflict?" depends on context, interpretation, and what other factors (political power, resources, grievances) are at play.

Comparison: Different Approaches to Violence

How do the four traditions compare in their approach to violence and conflict?

Christianity & Buddhism

Similar: Both teach love, compassion, and forgiveness as core values. Both have peaceful (pacifist and monastic) branches that reject violence entirely.

Different: Christianity (Just War) allows defensive violence under conditions. Buddhism teaches absolute non-violence as the path to wisdom and the end of suffering.

Islam & Sikhism

Similar: Both permit defensive warfare as a last resort to protect faith and community. Both teach that intention matters—fighting for justice is different from fighting for greed or revenge.

Different: Islam emphasises the greater jihad (internal spiritual struggle) as more important. Sikhism teaches the balance of Sant and Sipahi (spiritual devotion and willingness to fight injustice).

Christianity & Sikhism

Similar: Both teach justice and protection of the vulnerable as religious values. Both allow warfare under strict moral conditions.

Different: Christianity emphasises that violence is a last resort and brings moral burden. Sikhism teaches active resistance to tyranny as a religious duty, not just a permitted exception.

Buddhism & Islam

Similar: Both teach wisdom comes from overcoming anger and hatred. Both teach that actions have consequences (karma/accountability).

Different: Buddhism teaches non-violence as absolute. Islam permits defensive warfare when peaceful solutions fail. Buddhism focuses on individual spiritual liberation; Islam emphasises community protection.

Core Finding: All traditions value peace. Buddhism stands alone in teaching absolute non-violence. Christianity, Islam, and Sikhism share a common framework: peace is the ideal, violence is permitted only in defence, and strict moral limits apply. This reflects a shared commitment to justice alongside an acknowledgment of human conflict.

Evaluate: Weighing the Evidence

The Strongest Case for Peace

What is the most compelling evidence that religion is a force for peace?

Religious Teachings Promote Compassion

All four traditions teach love, compassion, and justice as core values. Jesus commanded "Love your enemies." Islam teaches respect for all humans as created in Allah's image. Buddhism teaches loving-kindness (metta) as the antidote to hatred. Sikhism teaches langar (sharing food with all) as an expression of equality. These teachings, taken seriously, inspire people to work for peace and justice.

Religious Figures Have Been Peacemakers

Many religious leaders and activists have used faith as motivation for peace: Martin Luther King Jr. (Christian), Gandhi (Hindu/universal), Thich Nhat Hanh (Buddhist), Muhammad Ali (Muslim), and countless community leaders. Their religious faith inspired them to work for justice non-violently. Religion can motivate moral courage and sacrifice for others.

The Strongest Case for Conflict

What is the most compelling evidence that religion has fuelled conflict?

Religion Has Been Used to Justify Violence

Groups like ISIS and historical movements like the Crusades claim religious justification for violence. When teachings are distorted or taken out of context, they can be used to cause enormous suffering.

Religious Identity Marks Divisions

The partition of India and Pakistan, Buddhist-Muslim violence in Myanmar, and sectarian conflict in the Middle East show how religious identity can become a flashpoint — whether religion is the true cause or a label for conflicts rooted in politics and power.

The Deeper Question

After examining both sides of evidence, the real insight is this: Religion is not inherently peaceful or violent. It is a tool that reflects the values and intentions of those who interpret and practice it. In the hands of people committed to compassion and justice, it promotes peace. In the hands of those seeking power or expressing hatred, it can justify violence. The same religious texts produce both peacemakers and terrorists, depending on how they are interpreted and in what context.

Key Terms from Unit 10

Just War Theory
Christian teaching that war can be morally justified if it meets strict criteria: just cause, right intention, legitimate authority, last resort, probability of success, and proportionality. Used primarily in Catholic and mainline Protestant traditions.
Greater Jihad
Islamic concept of the internal spiritual struggle within oneself to live according to Islamic teachings and resist temptation. Emphasised as more important than external struggle.
Lesser Jihad
Islamic concept of external struggle or physical effort to defend Islam and the Muslim community. Permitted only when peaceful solutions fail and Muslims face persecution.
Ahimsa
Buddhist and Hindu principle of non-harm or non-violence towards all living beings. Central to Buddhist ethics; extends to thought, speech, and action.
Metta
Buddhist practice of loving-kindness or goodwill towards all beings. Seen as the antidote to anger, hatred, and violence.
Dharam Yudh
Sikh concept of righteous war. Permitted as last resort to defend faith, protect innocents, and fight injustice. Must be fought with honour, not for conquest or revenge.
Sant and Sipahi
Sikh concepts taught by Guru Gobind Singh: Sant (saint—spiritually devoted) and Sipahi (soldier—willing to fight injustice). Sikhs should balance spiritual life with willingness to defend the vulnerable.
Langar
Sikh institution of a free communal meal for all, regardless of religion, caste, or status. Embodies equality and service.
Pacifism
Moral commitment to non-violence. Held by Quakers, Mennonites, and emphasised in Buddhist practice. Sees violence as always wrong.
Extremism
Holding extreme views and using violent methods to promote them. Religious extremism distorts core teachings on compassion and justice.
Central Learning: Religion is not inherently peaceful or violent. Whether it promotes peace or conflict depends on how believers interpret their teachings and what political, social, and economic forces shape their choices.
