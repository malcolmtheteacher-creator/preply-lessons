The Forbidden City

A palace built to keep the world out — and the seat of Chinese power for five centuries.

Today: you'll tell the story of a palace that ruled an empire, and discuss power, secrecy and who great buildings are really for — using relative and purpose clauses and the language of monarchy.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. For most of its history China was ruled by a single emperor, a supreme monarch who held absolute power over the whole country.
an emperor (n) — the male ruler of an empire; a monarch of the highest rank. (his realm: an empire)
2. A ruling family that passes power down through the generations is called a dynasty; the Ming and the Qing were the two great ones who lived here.
a dynasty (n) — a line of rulers from the same family, holding power over many generations.
3. The whole complex was a vast palace — hundreds of golden-roofed halls, courtyards and gates, built as a home and a stage for the throne.
a palace (n) — the grand official residence of a ruler. (a complex: a large group of connected buildings)
4. Around the palace ran a high red rampart and a wide moat, walls thrown up to keep ordinary people firmly on the outside.
a rampart (n) — a high, thick defensive wall around a fortress, castle or city.
5. Entry was strictly forbidden to the public — to step inside without permission could cost an ordinary person their life.
forbidden (adj) — not allowed; prohibited, often by a powerful authority. (verb: to forbid)
6. The emperor's many wives and companions, the concubines, lived their whole lives inside these walls, rarely if ever leaving.
a concubine (n) — historically, a woman who lived with a ruler as a secondary wife or companion within his household.
7. The only men allowed deep inside were the eunuchs, castrated servants who could be trusted around the imperial women.
a eunuch (n) — a castrated man, employed in many royal courts as a servant and official, especially around the ruler's household.
8. Life at court was governed by elaborate ritual: every bow, robe and procession followed rules that had not changed in centuries.

9. In 1912 the last emperor was forced to abdicate, giving up the throne and bringing two thousand years of imperial rule to an end.
to abdicate (v) — to formally give up the throne or one's position as monarch.
10. The palace eventually survived war and revolution to be turned into a public museum, where today millions of visitors walk through the once-sealed gates.
a museum (n) — a building where objects of historical, artistic or cultural value are kept and shown to the public.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A city built for one man

In the early 15th century, the Ming emperor Yongle decided to move the heart of his empire north to Beijing — and to build there a palace worthy of the ruler of all China. Work began around 1406, and for roughly fourteen years an army of labourers, said to number a million, raised an enormous complex of halls, courtyards and gates. Vast tree trunks were dragged from forests in the south, and great blocks of marble were slid across the winter ice. The palace was finished in 1420.

What they had built was extraordinary, and still is: the largest palace complex in the world, a walled rectangle of around 180 acres containing close to a thousand surviving buildings. Its grandest hall, the Hall of Supreme Harmony, remains one of the largest ancient wooden structures anywhere on earth. Everything was arranged along a single north–south line, with the emperor's throne at its symbolic centre — because this was not only a home, but a map of the cosmos, with the Son of Heaven sitting at the middle of the world.

And it was sealed off from that world. A high rampart and a wide moat enclosed it, and entry was forbidden to ordinary people on pain of death. That is the simplest meaning of the name we use in English: this was the Forbidden City, a place the public could not enter — and from which, for the most part, those inside could not freely leave.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who ordered the palace, roughly when was it built, and what scale of workforce did it take?

2. Why exactly was it called "forbidden"?

1. The Ming emperor Yongle ordered it; construction ran from around 1406 to its completion in 1420 (about fourteen years), using a workforce said to number a million.

2. It was sealed behind a high wall and moat, and ordinary people were forbidden to enter — to go inside without permission could cost them their life.

Discuss:
  • The whole vast complex was built to glorify and protect a single man. What does the sheer scale of it tell us about how that power wanted to be seen?

Part 2 — Life behind the walls

For around five hundred years, the Forbidden City was the seat of imperial power, home to twenty-four emperors of the Ming and then the Qing dynasties. But it was never a quiet house. Inside lived a whole hidden society of perhaps thousands of people — the imperial family, the empress, the emperor's many concubines, and the servants and officials who kept the machine running.

The inner courts, where the women of the court lived, were a world almost no ordinary man could enter. The exception was the eunuchs: castrated men who served as servants, messengers and watchdogs, and who could be trusted close to the imperial women precisely because they could father no rival heirs. Some rose to enormous influence, whispering in the emperor's ear; thousands of them lived and worked within the walls.

Over all of this lay an elaborate web of ritual. The colour of a robe, the height of a step, the exact way one bowed before the throne — everything was fixed by ceremony stretching back centuries. To outsiders the palace was a place of unimaginable splendour and mystery; to many who lived there it could be a gilded cage, a life lived entirely behind red walls, magnificent and confined in the same breath.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Roughly how long, and for which dynasties, was the Forbidden City the centre of imperial power?

2. Why were eunuchs, of all people, the men trusted in the inner court?

1. For roughly five hundred years it was the seat of power, home to twenty-four emperors of the Ming and then the Qing dynasties.

2. As castrated men they could father no children, so they posed no threat to the imperial bloodline and could be trusted around the emperor's concubines.

Discuss:
  • For the women and servants of the court, the palace was both a place of privilege and a kind of prison. Can great wealth and a beautiful home ever really be a trap?

Part 3 — The fall of the throne, the birth of a museum

By the early 20th century the imperial system was collapsing. In 1912 the last emperor, a small boy named Puyi who had taken the throne aged just two, was forced to abdicate — and with him ended more than two thousand years of imperial rule in China. Remarkably, the boy was at first allowed to go on living inside the palace, a phantom emperor in a country that no longer had one, until a warlord finally expelled him in 1924.

The following year the unthinkable happened: the gates that had been forbidden for five centuries were thrown open, and the palace became the Palace Museum, free for ordinary people to walk through at last. But survival was far from guaranteed. As Japanese invasion threatened in the 1930s, much of the priceless collection was packed into thousands of crates and carried across the country to keep it safe. Later, during the upheavals of the 20th century, the buildings themselves came under threat — and, by some accounts, were spared serious destruction only because troops were sent to guard the gates.

It endured. Today the Forbidden City stands at the very centre of Beijing, drawing many millions of visitors a year — one of the most visited museums on earth. A palace designed to shut the people out has become a place that exists, now, to let them in.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was the last emperor, and what ended in 1912 when he abdicated?

2. How did a sealed imperial palace become a public museum, and what threatened it in the 20th century?

1. The last emperor was Puyi, who had become emperor as a small child. His abdication in 1912 ended more than two thousand years of imperial rule in China.

2. After Puyi was expelled in 1924, the palace opened as the Palace Museum in 1925. Its treasures had to be evacuated ahead of the Japanese invasion, and the buildings themselves were later threatened during the century's upheavals but were protected and survived.

Discuss:
  • A building made to glorify absolute rulers is now a museum belonging to the public. Should we preserve the monuments of vanished powers — or do they keep old hierarchies alive?

The grammar this story runs on: relative & purpose clauses

To describe a place like this, you constantly add information to nouns — the palace WHICH took a million workers to build — and you explain why things were done — walls built IN ORDER TO keep the people out. Two tools do most of that work.

The two tools:
• Relative clauses — add information to a noun, using who (people), which / that (things), where (places): "the eunuchs who served the court," "a hall which is the largest of its kind," "the city where the emperor lived."
• Purpose clauses — say why something is done, using to, in order to or so that: "a moat dug to protect the palace," "walls built in order to keep people out," "rituals fixed so that nothing would change."

1. Relative clauses — join the two sentences

Combine each pair into one sentence using who, which, that or where.

1. Yongle was the emperor. He ordered the palace. → Yongle was the emperor ordered the palace.

2. The Hall of Supreme Harmony is a wooden building. It is one of the largest on earth. → The Hall of Supreme Harmony is a wooden building is one of the largest on earth.

3. The Forbidden City is the place. The emperors lived there. → The Forbidden City is the place the emperors lived.

1. who (a person) — Yongle was the emperor who ordered the palace.

2. which / that (a thing) — …a wooden building which is one of the largest on earth.

3. where (a place) — …the place where the emperors lived.

2. Purpose clauses — say why

Complete each sentence with to, in order to or so that. (More than one answer can be correct.)

1. A high wall and a wide moat were built keep ordinary people out.

2. The treasures were packed into crates and moved the Japanese army could not seize them.

3. Eunuchs served in the inner court protect the imperial bloodline.

1. to / in order to (+ keep — same subject, so a "to" form works)

2. so that (+ a new subject "the army", so we need "so that")

3. to / in order to (+ protect)

3. Put them together — describe the palace

Fill each gap with a relative word (who / which / that / where) or a purpose link (to / in order to / so that). Read it aloud once you've finished.

The Forbidden City is a palace took a million workers to build. It was the home of the emperors ruled China for five centuries. Around it ran a high wall, raised keep the public out. Inside lived the concubines and the eunuchs served them. Today it is a museum anyone can finally walk in.

which / that (the palace) · who (the emperors) · to / in order to (purpose) · who (the eunuchs) · where (a place — the museum)

Notice how the relative words pin extra detail onto each noun, while the purpose link answers the question "why?".

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. A palace built to keep the public out now exists to let them in. Is that a happy ending — or have we just turned imperial power into a tourist attraction?
  2. Life inside was magnificent but completely confined. Would you trade total freedom for total luxury? Where is the line?
  3. Many countries keep the palaces of rulers they have rejected. What do these places give us — pride, memory, warning — and is it worth the cost of maintaining them?
