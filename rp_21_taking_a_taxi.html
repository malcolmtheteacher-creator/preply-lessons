Taking a Taxi

A last train to catch, a stranger at the wheel, and a detour you didn't ask for. Time to trust — and to talk.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

It's late, it's cold, and the last train out of the city leaves in twenty-five minutes. You're across town with a heavy bag and no chance of walking it. Then a taxi swings to the kerb, the window rolls down, and a warm voice says, "Where can I take you?"

Everything now rests on a five-minute relationship with a total stranger. You need to name your destination, agree the way, and — when they suddenly turn off the route you expected — decide in a heartbeat whether to trust them or challenge them. Get the tone wrong and it's a tense, silent ride. Get it right and you're through the station doors with minutes to spare, both of you smiling.

At the wheel: a driver who's been on the road since before dawn, knows every back street and blocked bridge in the city, and has heard every nervous passenger say "are you sure this is the quickest way?" a thousand times.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last take a taxi — and did you chat the whole way, or stare out of the window in silence? Which do you prefer, and why?
  2. The driver turns off the road you expected and takes a route you don't recognise. Do you say something, or sit tight and trust them? What's the risk either way?

Who's who

You
The Passenger 🧳

In a hurry, a little on edge, watching the meter and the clock. You want to get there fast — without being the rude passenger who tells the driver how to drive.

The other voice
The Taxi Driver 🚕

On the road since dawn, unflappable, knows the city like the back of a hand. Has a shortcut for everything — and a story for every traffic jam.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't just follow where the passenger is going — watch how they question the detour without causing offence, and how they hand over the fare. That's the real skill.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the softeners, the way doubt is raised without blame, and the little flourish at the fare.

DriverEvening! Hop in out of that cold. Where can I take you?
YouThe main station, please — Waverley — and I'll be honest, I'm cutting it a bit fine for the last train, so quick as you safely can, if that's alright?
DriverLast train, is it? Leave it with me. Buckle up — I'll have you there with minutes to spare.
YouYou're a lifesaver. Busy night out there? You look like you've been at it since dawn.
DriverSince five this morning, love. It's the football — half the city's trying to get across town at once. Which is exactly why I'm going to swing us round the back of the park, if you'll trust me on it.
YouRound the back? I don't mean to tell you your job — but isn't the bridge usually the quickest way to the station?
DriverIt is, nine nights out of ten. But the bridge is one big car park right now — match traffic. Ten minutes sat dead still, meter ticking, going nowhere. My way's longer on the map but half the time tonight, I promise you.
YouFair enough — you'd know far better than I would. Lead on.
DriverThere — have a look at the bridge as we pass. See those brake lights? Not moved an inch since we set off. Told you. We'll be at the station in five.
YouI take it all back — you're a genius. Sorry for doubting you; it's just the train playing on my mind.
DriverNo harm done — everyone's a backseat driver when there's a train to catch. Here we are: Waverley, front entrance, and you've a good ten minutes going spare.
YouHonestly, brilliant. What do I owe you?
DriverMeter says eleven-forty.
YouCall it fourteen — the rest's for getting me here in one piece. Keep the change.
DriverVery kind, thank you. Now go on — run! And mind those wet steps. Safe trip!

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The driver deliberately avoids the road the passenger expected. Why — and what does the passenger notice a minute later that proves the driver was right?

2. The passenger clearly has doubts about the route, yet the driver never takes offence. How does the passenger word the objection so it lands as a question, not an accusation?

3. The meter reads eleven-forty, but the passenger hands over more than that. How much do they give — and what are the two things that extra money is quietly doing?

1. The usual road — the bridge — is completely gridlocked with match-day traffic, so the driver takes the longer-looking route round the park to save time. Moments later the passenger looks over and sees the bridge traffic hasn't moved at all — brake lights, dead still — which proves the shortcut was the smart call.
2. By disclaiming first and softening the challenge: "I don't mean to tell you your job, but isn't the bridge usually the quickest way…?" The opening line hands the driver their authority back, and turning it into a genuine question ("isn't it…?") invites an explanation rather than picking a fight.
3. They hand over fourteen against a fare of eleven-forty — so a tip of roughly two-sixty. The extra is doing two jobs at once: rounding the awkward figure up to a clean note, and thanking the driver for the speed and the safe arrival ("for getting me here in one piece").

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that get you across town and onto the train — with the driver on your side the whole way. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚕 Giving the destination

Phrase 1

You need the station fast — but you don't want to bark orders at someone about to do you a favour. Which line gets you moving quickest without setting the wrong tone?

A. "Station. Fast."
B. "Take me to the station and step on it."
C. "The station, please — I'm cutting it a bit fine, so quick as you safely can, if that's alright?"
C — "The station, please — I'm cutting it a bit fine, so quick as you safely can, if that's alright?"
"Cutting it a bit fine" explains your rush without panic; "quick as you safely can" asks for speed while handing them control of how; "if that's alright" makes it a request, not a command. You get the same speed — and a driver who's on your side.
Phrase 2

You only half-know the address — it's near a well-known spot, and the driver knows this city far better than you ever will. Finish the humble handover: "It's just off the High Street — you'll ___ ___ ___ ___ I do."

"It's just off the High Street — you'll know it better than I do."
Giving a landmark plus "you'll know it better than I do" hands the driver the expert's role — which relaxes them and gets you there. Cousins: "you're the expert", "you'd know the way better than me".

💬 A bit of small talk

Phrase 3

A little warmth goes a long way with someone who's been driving all day. Reorder this friendly opener:

at ityou've beenI imaginesince dawn,
"You've been at it since dawn, I imagine."
"Been at it" is a warm, idiomatic way to say "working hard", and tagging on "I imagine" invites a story without prying. It turns a silent cab into a conversation — and conversation is where goodwill comes from.
Phrase 4

The driver grumbles about the match-day traffic. You want to show you're listening and firmly on their side. Which reply sounds like a real human, not a recorded announcement?

A. "Yes. That is correct."
B. "I know exactly what you mean — it's chaos out there tonight."
B — "I know exactly what you mean — it's chaos out there tonight."
"I know exactly what you mean" signals you've really heard them, and adding your own little observation keeps the exchange alive. A is technically polite but oddly cold — it closes the chat down instead of warming it up.

🛣️ Questioning the route

Phrase 5

The driver turns off the road you expected and your stomach drops — is the meter about to run wild? You want to ask WITHOUT accusing them of taking the scenic route. Think: disclaim first, then a gentle question. What do you say?

"I don't mean to tell you your job, but isn't the bridge usually quicker?"
"I don't mean to tell you your job" disarms the challenge before you even make it, and phrasing your doubt as a question ("isn't it…?") asks for an explanation rather than picking a fight. You get your answer and keep the peace.
Phrase 6

Another gentle way to check the route hands the driver credit for knowing best. Put the pieces in order:

the quickest wayis thisdo you reckon?at this time of night,
"Is this the quickest way at this time of night, do you reckon?"
"At this time of night" quietly admits traffic changes by the hour, and "do you reckon?" invites their expert opinion instead of demanding a defence. It's a question a curious passenger asks — not a suspicious one.
Phrase 7

The driver explains the bridge is gridlocked and their way is faster. You were wrong — concede it warmly and gracefully. Finish: "Fair enough — you'd ___ ___ ___ than I would."

"Fair enough — you'd know far better than I would."
"Fair enough" accepts the answer without sulking, and "you'd know far better than I would" hands the expertise back gracefully. Backing down well is a skill — it turns a wobble of doubt into a moment of trust.

💷 The fare & a warm goodbye

Phrase 8

You've arrived with time to spare. Now to pay. Which question sounds friendliest — like the end of a good chat, not a cold transaction?

A. "How much?"
B. "State the fare."
C. "What do I owe you?"
C — "What do I owe you?"
Warm, natural and slightly generous-sounding, as though paying is a pleasure rather than a chore. A jokier version among friends is "What's the damage?" — but "What do I owe you?" is the safe, charming default anywhere.
Phrase 9

The meter reads eleven-forty. You want to round it up and hand it over generously — without counting out coins. Two classic moves: round the figure up with "___ ___ fourteen", then wave off the change with "Keep the ___." Fill both.

"Call it fourteen." / "Keep the change."
"Call it fourteen" rounds an awkward fare up to a clean number in a friendly, decisive way; "Keep the change" gifts the difference as a tip without any fuss. Together they turn payment into a small act of thanks.
Phrase 10

The driver got you there in time and in one piece. Which goodbye leaves them smiling as they pull away?

A. "Bye."
B. "Thank you for the transportation service provided."
C. "Cheers — you got me here in one piece. You're a star."
C — "Cheers — you got me here in one piece. You're a star."
"Cheers" is warm and easy; "in one piece" nods to the speed AND the safety; "you're a star" praises the person, not the service. A is flat; B sounds like a form letter. C is the one they remember.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole ride out loud using all ten, in order — destination → small talk → the detour and your doubt → backing down → the fare and the tip → goodbye. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

🌩️ The road that isn't there

One more round — no script, no safety net. You're halfway to the station when the driver rounds a corner and stops dead: the whole street is closed, blue lights, a diversion sign pointing the long way round. The last train leaves in twelve minutes and the meter is still running. Start the timer and talk your way through it: work the problem TOGETHER, not against each other — is there a back way? A drop-off point closer to the platform? Should you jump out and run the last stretch on foot? Agree a plan, and settle the fare fairly even though this wasn't the ride either of you booked. One rule: the second you snap at the driver or blame them for the road closure, you've lost the shortcut — and probably the train.

The Echo 🕰️

Long before meters and back streets, there was another driver you paid to get you across dangerous ground: the stagecoach driver of the American West. You climbed aboard, named a town a hundred miles off, and put your life in the hands of a stranger who knew every river crossing, every safe trail, and every place worth avoiding after dark.

You didn't tell them the route. You trusted the reins. And when you rolled into town in one piece, you thanked them like they'd saved your life — because, out there, they sometimes had.

Tonight's cab is the same old bargain, tamed for the city: a stranger, a hard road, and the quiet art of trusting the person at the wheel.

Two bigger questions

  1. To get where we're going, we hand control to a stranger — a driver, a pilot, a guide — and simply trust they know best. What makes you willing to do that? And what would make you suddenly stop trusting and speak up?
  2. The stagecoach driver was a local hero — everyone knew their name. Today's taxi driver is usually a stranger you'll never meet again. Have we lost something as these journeys became anonymous, or gained something? What?
