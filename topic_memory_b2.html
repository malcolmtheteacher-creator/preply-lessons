The Man Who Forgot Tomorrow

In 1953 a surgeon removed a small piece of a young man's brain, and by accident discovered what memory is made of. The true story of remembering — and of how much of yours never happened.

Today: you'll meet the patient who taught the world how memory works, and the researcher who proved how easily it can be rewritten — while mastering the tenses that every story is told in: he was tracing the star when he said he had never seen it before.
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • What is your earliest memory? How old were you — and how do you know it's yours and not a photograph or a family story?
  • Think of something you can do without thinking: cycling, driving, tying a knot. Could you explain it in words to someone who has never done it?
  • Have you and someone close to you ever remembered the same event completely differently? Who was right?

The vocabulary of this story

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. After the operation he could still describe his childhood perfectly, but nothing new ever stuck: every person he met was met for the first time, for ever. Doctors call this amnesia.
amnesia (n) — loss of memory. Two kinds matter here: retrograde (you lose the past) and anterograde (you can't build a future). Films almost always show the first; this story is about the second, which is far stranger.
2. Ask him what he had for lunch and there was nothing there — not a wrong answer, an empty one. The morning had already faded completely.
to fade (v) — to grow gradually weaker or dimmer until it disappears. Colours fade in sunlight; memories fade. Note the useful metaphor: memory is spoken of in English as a picture losing its colour, not a file being deleted.
3. A quarter of the volunteers described being lost in a shopping centre as small children — the crowd, the panic, the kind old man who helped them. The scenes were vivid, detailed, felt completely real, and not one of them had happened.
vivid (adj) — bright, clear and lifelike. A vivid memory, a vivid dream, a vivid description. The whole second half of this story turns on one fact: vividness feels like proof and is not.

B · Which word means…?

Three items: to recall · unreliable · a witness. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which one means a person who saw something happen and later describes it, often in court?
a witness (n) — an eyewitness; to call a witness; witness testimony. This is where the science stops being interesting and starts being dangerous: for centuries, courts treated a confident witness as close to proof.
2. Which one means to bring something back into your mind deliberately?
to recall (v) — to retrieve a memory on purpose. Slightly more formal than remember, and more precise: remember can be passive (it just arrives), recall is an act of effort — which, as part three explains, is exactly why it works.
3. Which one means not to be depended on; likely to be wrong?
unreliable (adj) — an unreliable witness, an unreliable car. Build the family: to rely on someone · reliable · reliability. The claim this lesson tests is whether "unreliable" is the fair word for human memory.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Memory does not play a recording back; each time you remember something, your brain reconstructs it from pieces — and files the new version. This implies that…
  (a) memories stay fixed once made   (b) remembering something often can change it   (c) only unimportant memories change
(b) — to reconstruct (v) — to build again from parts. The uncomfortable consequence: the memories you revisit most, the ones you are surest of, have been rebuilt most often — and are therefore the most edited.
2. Rereading your notes feels productive because the page grows familiar; testing yourself feels harder and works far better. The technique of deliberately pulling information out of your head is called retrieval practice. It suggests that the useful part of studying is…
  (a) putting information in   (b) taking information out   (c) how long you sit at the desk
(b) — retrieval (n) / to retrieve (v) — getting something back out. The effort of retrieving is what strengthens the memory. Everything in this lesson that asks you to answer before revealing is built on this one finding.

D · Word building & collocation

1. You know remember. Build the set: information you can put into words is ___ memory (adjective, from "declare") · skills your hands know but your mouth can't explain are ___ memory (adjective, from "procedure") · and the whole apparatus for holding recent information for a few seconds is ___-___ memory.
declarative memory · procedural memory · short-term memory. The first two names come straight out of this story — before 1953, nobody knew there was more than one kind.
2. Complete the forgetting set: a name that is nearly there but won't come is on the ___ of your ___ (idiom) · to lose a memory completely is for it to ___ ___ (two words) · and when a smell suddenly returns you to a place, the memory has been ___ ___ (verb + preposition).
On the tip of your tongue · to slip away (also fade away) · triggered by (or brought back by). Note the direction of all three idioms: memory is treated in English as something that moves of its own accord.

The story, in three parts

Part 1 — Henry

Henry Molaison was born in Connecticut in 1926, and by the time he was a young man his life had been taken over by epilepsy. The seizures had begun in childhood — a knock from a bicycle was blamed at the time, though nobody was certain — and they had grown steadily worse until he could not hold down work. In 1953, when he was twenty-seven, he was operated on by a neurosurgeon named William Scoville, who removed tissue from deep inside both temporal lobes, including most of a small seahorse-shaped structure called the hippocampus. The operation was experimental and Scoville had said so. It worked, in the narrow sense that the seizures improved. It also destroyed Henry's future.

When he woke, Henry could still talk, reason, joke and complete a crossword. His childhood was intact and his intelligence was undamaged. But from that morning onwards, nothing new was ever stored. He read the same magazine again and again with fresh interest. He was told of his father's death and grieved, and then was told again, and grieved again. A psychologist called Brenda Milner began studying him in the mid-1950s and went on doing so for the next fifty years; every single time she walked into the room, she was a stranger who had to introduce herself. Henry knew something was wrong. He described it once, with terrible clarity, as being like waking from a dream — every day alone, whatever sorrow or joy he might have had.

Then came the experiment that changed the science. Milner sat him down with a five-pointed star to trace, but he could only see his hand in a mirror, which turns the movement backwards and makes fools of everybody. Henry was hopeless on the first day, as everyone is. She brought him back the next day, and the day after. His accuracy improved steadily, exactly like a healthy person learning a new skill — and each morning, picking up the pencil, he assured her politely that he had never attempted anything like this before. His hands had been learning while he had been forgetting. In that one result, memory split in two: the memory of facts and events, which you can state, and the memory of skills, which you can only perform. They were not one faculty after all, and they did not live in the same place. Almost everything now known about human memory rests on a foundation that starts with Henry. He was known to the world only as "H.M." until his death in 2008; afterwards his brain was cut into more than two thousand thin sections, an operation that was broadcast live on the internet.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was removed in 1953, and what was the result?

2. What did the mirror-drawing test show, and why did it matter?

1. Tissue from both temporal lobes, including most of the hippocampus, to control severe epilepsy. The seizures improved — but Henry could never form a new lasting memory again. His past and his intelligence were untouched.

2. He got better at tracing a star in a mirror day after day while insisting each morning that he had never done it. His hands learned what he couldn't recall — proving memory is at least two separate systems: facts and events (statable) versus skills (only performable).

Discuss:
  • Henry was told of his father's death repeatedly and grieved each time. Would you tell him? What does your answer say about what memory is for?

Part 2 — The memory that makes things up

If Henry showed what memory is built from, a psychologist called Elizabeth Loftus spent her career showing what it is built out of — which turns out to be far less solid than anyone assumed. In an experiment in the 1970s, students watched a film of a car accident and were then asked how fast the cars had been going. The question was worded differently for different groups. Those who were asked how fast the cars were going when they smashed into each other estimated around forty-one miles per hour; those asked when the cars hit each other said around thirty-four. One verb, seven miles an hour. A week later they were asked whether they had seen any broken glass. The "smashed" group were more than twice as likely to say yes. There had been no broken glass in the film. The question had not merely influenced their answer; it had gone back into the memory and left something there.

Loftus then asked the harder question: could you install an entire event that never happened? In the mid-1990s she and a colleague recruited volunteers and, with the help of an older relative, presented each of them with four childhood stories — three true, one invented, in which the participant had been lost in a shopping centre at about five years old, had cried, and had been rescued by an elderly stranger. Roughly a quarter came to remember it. Not vaguely: they added details nobody had given them — what the man was wearing, how the shop had smelled, how frightened they had been. When it was explained afterwards that the event had never occurred, some of them argued. The memory was as vivid as any other memory they owned, because by then it was made of the same material.

This work has made Loftus enemies, and the reason is that it does not stay in the laboratory: it walks into courtrooms, where she has testified in cases that people feel very strongly about, and where the implications for those who have genuinely been harmed are painful and contested. So the honest summary matters. The finding is not that memory is worthless or that people who remember things are lying — most memory is broadly serviceable most of the time, which is why we survive. The finding is narrower and sharper: memory is reconstructive rather than reproductive, it is edited by every question that is asked about it, and — the part that should genuinely worry you — confidence and vividness are not evidence. A witness may be certain, detailed, entirely sincere and completely wrong, and there is no feeling from the inside that distinguishes that state from being right.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did changing one verb do — twice over?

2. What did the shopping-centre study show, and what is the careful conclusion?

1. "Smashed into" instead of "hit" raised speed estimates by around seven miles an hour — and a week later made people more than twice as likely to remember broken glass that was never in the film. The question edited the memory itself.

2. About a quarter of volunteers came to remember being lost in a shopping centre as a child — an invented event — and added rich details of their own. Conclusion: memory is reconstructive, editable by the questions asked about it, and confidence plus vividness are not evidence. Sincere and certain can still be wrong.

Discuss:
  • "There is no feeling from the inside that distinguishes being right from being wrong." What should a fair legal system do with that sentence?

Part 3 — The forgetting curve, and how to beat it

Long before either of them, a German psychologist named Hermann Ebbinghaus had decided to measure forgetting, and had done it the hard way: on himself. Because ordinary words carry meanings and associations that would contaminate the measurement, he invented thousands of meaningless syllables, learned lists of them by heart, and then tested how many survived after an hour, a day, a week. When he plotted the results in the 1880s he produced what is still called the forgetting curve: a steep collapse in the first hours, then a slower and slower decline. Most of what you learn today will be gone within days — and this is not a defect. A brain that kept everything would be unable to think at all, drowning in a detail it could never generalise from. Forgetting is not memory failing. It is memory doing its job, which is to throw away almost everything and keep what looks likely to matter.

The practical question, then, is how to change what "looks likely to matter". Ebbinghaus found the answer in his own data and a century of research has confirmed it. What defeats the curve is not more time at the desk and it is certainly not rereading — rereading is the most popular study technique in the world and one of the least effective, because familiarity feels exactly like knowledge and isn't. What works is retrieval: shutting the book and dragging the information out of your own head, which is unpleasant, feels like failing, and is the entire point. The effort of the search is what marks the memory as worth keeping. And the second half is timing — spacing those retrievals out, coming back just as the thing begins to slip, at wider and wider intervals. Difficulty at the moment of recall predicts strength later. Ease predicts nothing.

Which returns us to Henry, who could not do any of this, and to the small strange fact that his hands could. Every time you shut your eyes in this lesson and told the story back before pressing the button, you were running Ebbinghaus's experiment on yourself, and the reason it was uncomfortable is the reason it worked. There is a link, too, to the conversation elsewhere in this collection about attention: nothing can be retrieved that was never properly encoded, and nothing is properly encoded while your attention is somewhere else. A phone in the room does not merely interrupt the evening. It quietly decides which parts of it you will still own in ten years' time. That is the real stake of this topic — not trivia, not exams. What you attend to is what you will have been.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Ebbinghaus measure forgetting, and why is forgetting not a fault?

2. Why is rereading weak, and what beats it?

1. He invented thousands of meaningless syllables (so meaning couldn't help), learned them, and tested himself over hours, days and weeks — producing the forgetting curve: steep at first, then flattening. Forgetting is the brain discarding almost everything so that it can generalise; keeping everything would make thought impossible.

2. Rereading builds familiarity, which feels like knowledge and isn't. What works is retrieval — closing the book and pulling the information out — spaced at widening intervals, timed for just as it starts to slip. The difficulty is the mechanism, not a sign of failure.

Discuss:
  • "What you attend to is what you will have been." Look back at last week honestly: what will you still own from it in ten years?

Grammar — narrative tenses

This is a story about the past inside the past: an operation that had already happened, a skill that was quietly being learned, a memory that had been planted a week earlier. English has three tools for that, and using them accurately is the difference between telling a story and merely listing events.

The three tools:
• Past simple — the chain of events, in order. He woke up, he read the magazine, he forgot.
• Past continuous — the background, or a longer action interrupted by a shorter one. While she was studying him, he introduced himself again.
• Past perfect — the step further back: something completed before the moment you are describing. By 1953 the seizures had grown unbearable.
• Past perfect continuous — a longer activity that had been running up to that earlier moment. His hands had been learning for weeks.
The rule that actually matters: the past perfect is not "the very old past". It exists only to signal order when you break the sequence. If you tell events in the order they happened, past simple is enough:
"The seizures got worse, so he had the operation." — no past perfect needed.
"He had the operation because the seizures had got worse." — here the order is reversed, so English marks it.

The pair that changes meaning:
"When she arrived, he introduced himself." → she arrived, then he introduced himself.
"When she arrived, he had introduced himself." → impossible: the introduction happened first.
"When she arrived, he was introducing himself." → it was already in progress as she came in.
One story, three completely different films.

1. Choose the tense

2. While Henry the star, his hands were quietly learning.

4. She realised that somebody the wording of the question.

2. Which film are you describing?

Say what actually happened in each version, in order, before revealing.

a. When I got to the station, the train left.

b. When I got to the station, the train had left.

2. A week later the volunteers remembered broken glass, because they had heard the word "smashed". (or: The volunteers who had heard "smashed" remembered broken glass a week later.)

3. He tested himself on the syllables that he had invented. (Note: "He invented the syllables and then tested himself" is also perfectly good — in order, so no past perfect required.)

Going deeper

A note on the record: Henry Molaison was a real patient, studied for over half a century and known publicly as "H.M." only until his death in 2008. Elizabeth Loftus's experiments are real, widely replicated, and genuinely contested where they touch legal cases — this lesson keeps both the finding and the objection on stage. Ebbinghaus's curve is from the 1880s and has been confirmed many times since.

What the story actually teaches. First, the accident lesson. Nobody set out to discover that memory has separate systems; a surgeon was trying to stop seizures and destroyed something whose function was unknown because it had never been possible to see what it did. Much of neuroscience is built on damage like this — knowledge bought with somebody's life, by people who did not consent to be a discovery. That is worth saying plainly rather than smoothing over: Henry gave more to science than almost any researcher in it, and he was never able to know that he had.

Second, the confidence lesson, which is the practical one. The most useful sentence in this whole conversation is that vividness and certainty are produced by the brain independently of accuracy. You have no internal instrument for telling a true memory from a rebuilt one — none, at all — and yet every argument you have ever had with a partner or a sibling about "what actually happened" has been conducted as though you did. This does not mean nobody can be trusted. It means the appropriate posture towards your own certainty is curiosity rather than defence.

Third, the encoding lesson, which connects this conversation to the one about attention. Memory is not a passive recording device that catches whatever is in the room; it keeps a version of what you actually attended to, rebuilt afterwards. That makes attention the gate through which the past is admitted. Time spent half-present does not merely feel thin at the moment — it will be thin permanently, because there will be nothing there to reconstruct. Which turns a topic about brains into a topic about how you intend to spend Saturday.

Talk it through:
  • Henry could not consent in any meaningful way to fifty years of study, and the knowledge gained has helped millions. Was it right? What would you have required?
  • If confidence is not evidence, how should courts, journalists and families actually behave differently tomorrow? Give one concrete change for each.
  • Is a happy false memory harmful? Consider a family story everyone tells that you now suspect is untrue — would you correct it?
  • Across this collection: attention is captured, choices are engineered, evenings vanish — and now the bill arrives as a past you can't reconstruct. Is that one argument or four separate ones?
