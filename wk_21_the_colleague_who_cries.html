The Colleague Who Cries

Two desks away, someone has just gone to pieces in front of the whole floor. There's a meeting in ten minutes and nobody knows where to look. You're the one who gets up.

The Scene 🎬

It's a Thursday, twenty to eleven, and the office is doing what it always does — printer, kettle, somebody laughing on a call. Then, two desks away, Marta Ilves puts her hand over her mouth and starts to cry. Not quietly. The kind that surprises the person it's happening to.

Nobody moves. Two people find something urgent on their screens. Someone half-stands and then sits down again. This is the thing about crying at work: everybody wants to help and almost nobody knows how, because the office is the one place where feelings are supposed to have been left at the door, and there's a meeting at eleven, and the whole room can hear.

So you get up. And now you have about four seconds to work out what you're going to say — knowing that "Are you all right?" will get you "I'm fine", and that the wrong kind of kindness, the loud, fussing kind, will make her wish you'd stayed in your seat.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Has anyone ever cried in front of you at work — or have you? What did people actually do, and did it help or make it worse?
  2. Some workplaces treat tears as a private emergency to be tidied away; others barely blink. What are they like where you work — and does the language you'd use change depending on which kind of place it is?

Who's who

You
The One Who Got Up 🚪

You're not her closest friend and you're not her manager. You just happen to be the person who noticed and didn't look away. You've got no training for this, and about four seconds to decide.

The other voice
Marta Ilves 💻

Sits two desks from you. Eleven years here, and the one everybody goes to when something's broken. She has never once lost it in front of anybody, and she has just lost it in front of everybody.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Notice that almost nothing here is clever. It's short, plain and unhurried, and that's exactly what makes it work.

MartaSorry. Sorry, I'm — give me a second. I'm fine, I'm honestly — sorry.
YouCome on — let's go and stand outside for a minute.
MartaNo, I've got the — the eleven o'clock, I need to get the deck across to Priya, I can't just—

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Marta apologises again and again, and not once does the other person tell her she has nothing to be sorry for, or ask her if she's all right. Both of those sound kind. Why are they avoided — what would each of them have cost her?

2. There is one question that lets Marta avoid explaining herself without having to admit she doesn't want to. What was it, roughly — and what does it do that "Are you OK?" can't?

3. Marta is offered the chance to go home and she can't decide. What does the other person do with that — and why is it better than waiting for her to make her mind up?

1. Telling her she has nothing to be sorry for is still a small argument, so she has to answer it — and arguing is the last thing she has energy for. "Are you all right?" is worse, because there's only one polite answer to it and she'll give you that answer, which is "I'm fine", and then you're both stuck. Shutting the apology down flatly, and then simply not being in a hurry, asks nothing of her at all.
2. The offer of a coffee and no questions, put as a choice against talking. It hands her a way of saying "not now" that doesn't sound like a refusal, because both options are equally acceptable and she only has to pick one. "Are you OK?" gives her nothing to pick — it just asks her to perform being fine.
3. The big decision is dropped and a smaller one is offered instead: something specific that can be taken off her today. Someone in that state can't weigh up going home, so making her choose is another job for her to do, whereas handing over one meeting is small enough to say yes to — and it helps her immediately.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten things to say to someone who's crying at work. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick, and this is a conversation you won't get to plan.

Say it like you mean it. Almost everything here depends on tone rather than words, and the tone you want is flatter and more ordinary than you think. Warmth in this situation sounds like calm, not like softness — a low, unhurried voice, the same one you'd use to say the printer's jammed. If you go gentle and breathy, you tell her that something terrible is happening and that she is now a delicate object, which is precisely what she's dreading. Short sentences, plainly said, with actual gaps between them.

🚪 Getting her out of the room

Phrase 1

She's at her desk with the whole floor pretending not to look. You want her out of there — but she has no energy left for decisions. Which one moves her without asking her to choose anything?

A. "Do you want to go outside for a bit?"
B. "Come on — let's go and stand outside for a minute."
C. "Maybe go and get some air, and come back when you're ready."
B — "Come on — let's go and stand outside for a minute."
It's a statement, not a question, so there's nothing for her to weigh up, and "let's" means she isn't being sent anywhere on her own — you're going too. "For a minute" makes it small enough to agree to. A looks kind but hands her a decision at the exact moment she can't make one, and she'll say no out of habit. C sends her off alone to be seen walking out, which is the bit she's frightened of.
Phrase 2

Outside now. She's stopped crying and started apologising, over and over, and every apology makes her feel worse. Two of these try to reassure her. One just closes it. Which is which — and which do you want?

A. "It's fine, it's fine, honestly, don't worry, it's completely fine."
B. "Don't apologise. Honestly."
C. "There's really no need to be sorry — everybody has days like this, and nobody thinks any less of you."
B — "Don't apologise. Honestly."
Three words and it's finished. The instruction takes the whole business off the table rather than discussing it, and "honestly" on its own at the end means you're not just being polite. A and C are both reassurance, and reassurance is a conversation — the longer you spend proving she needn't be embarrassed, the more it sounds like something worth being embarrassed about. C also promises her what other people think, which you can't actually know.

⏳ Taking the hurry out of it

Phrase 3

She starts to speak, stops, breathes, starts again. She's very aware that you're standing there and that it's nearly eleven. Complete it — three words, and the first one is a verb: "___ ___ ___. I'm not in a rush."

"Take your time. I'm not in a rush."
The first half gives her permission and the second half proves it, because "take your time" on its own can be said impatiently and often is. Naming your own state rather than hers is the trick worth stealing: you're not telling her how to feel, you're just telling her there's no clock, which is a fact she can believe.
Phrase 4

She's calmer. You have no idea whether she wants to explain or wants everyone to stop looking at her, and "do you want to talk?" only offers the one door. Say it out loud: offer her both ways out in a single question, so that either answer is an easy yes.

"Do you want to talk about it, or do you want a coffee and no questions?"
Because both halves are offered in the same breath, neither one is the right answer, so she can take the coffee without it feeling like she's shut you out. The phrase "and no questions" is what does the work — it says out loud that you won't push, which is the thing she can't ask for herself. Add "either's fine" and she'll believe you.

🤐 The room, and what you'll say to it

Phrase 5

She keeps glancing back at the office. It isn't the crying that's frightening her — it's Monday, and what forty people will already know by then. One word in each gap: "I'm not going to say ___ to ___."

"I'm not going to say anything to anyone."
Say it before she has to ask, because asking would mean admitting she doesn't trust the floor, and she has to work with these people tomorrow. "Anything to anyone" is deliberately total — no exceptions, no "well, obviously your manager". Only say it if it's true, and then hold it, including in the twist later on, where you'll find out what it costs.
Phrase 6

You'd like to know roughly what you're dealing with, but she's in no state to tell the whole story and you've no right to it anyway. Which question costs her the least — one word to answer, and no guessing on your part?

A. "What's happened?"
B. "Is it work, or is it something else?"
C. "Is it something at home?"
B — "Is it work, or is it something else?"
It sorts the situation into two boxes and lets her point at one, which she can manage even when talking is beyond her, and it tells you what you actually need — whether this is something you can fix or something you can only sit beside. A asks for the whole story and she'd have to build it. C guesses at her private life and puts her in the position of correcting you.

🧯 Making it survivable

Phrase 7

She's told you what's happened, and then apologised for it — she thinks she should be coping better than this. Put it in order:

wouldanybody.Thatfloor
"That would floor anybody."
To floor someone is to knock them flat on the ground, from boxing, so the picture is of a person hit so hard their legs go — which is why it's the right word for something that has stopped her functioning rather than merely upset her. "Anybody" is the important half: it moves her from "I'm weak" to "this is heavy", and it does it in four words, without a speech about how strong she normally is.
Phrase 8

She wants to leave and can't — not because anyone would stop her, but because she'd have to walk past everybody and explain herself, and that's beyond her. Say it out loud, in three short sentences: ask, give her permission, and then hand her the cover story so she doesn't have to invent one.

"Do you need to go home? Because you can. I'll say you're with a client."
The three sentences do three separate jobs, and it falls apart if you drop any of them: the question, then the permission, then the lie that makes the permission usable. Most people stop after the second and wonder why she stays at her desk — the real obstacle was never the rule, it was the walk past forty people. Notice it's "I'll say", not "you could say"; you're doing it, not giving her homework.

🤝 Today, and tomorrow

Phrase 9

She can't decide about going home, so you drop it and offer something smaller. You don't want to help her with her work — you want to remove some of it. The verb is "take": "Is there anything I can take ___ ___ today?"

"Is there anything I can take off you today?"
To take something off someone means to lift a job off them completely, so that it stops being theirs — which is different from helping, because helping still leaves them in charge of it. "Anything" and "today" keep the offer small and bounded, so she isn't accepting a favour she'll owe you for, and "is there anything" is easier to answer than "what can I do", which makes her manage you.
Phrase 10

You're both about to go back in. Put it in order — the practical promise first, then, as the last thing she hears, the permission:

tomorrow.be fine.I'll check inYou don't have to
"I'll check in tomorrow. You don't have to be fine."
To check in on someone is to make brief contact just to see how they are, with no other purpose, and naming the day turns kindness into an appointment — which is the difference between meaning it and saying it. The second sentence is there because she will otherwise spend tonight preparing a performance for tomorrow, and letting her off that in advance is the most useful thing you can give her, which is why it goes last.

🔑 Make them yours

Pin three of these to people you actually work with, and say each answer out loud until it comes without thinking. You won't get a warning when this happens.

Think of a real colleague who'd hate being seen upset. Where in your building would you take them, and what exactly would you say to get them there?

Your workload this week — what could you genuinely lift off someone else, today, without asking permission from anybody?

The cover story. What's the ordinary, boring reason someone might be away from their desk where you work — the one nobody ever questions?

Your Turn 🎭

Now you're the one standing up. Say every answer out loud FIRST — this is a conversation you'd have on your feet, so keep talking even when it isn't tidy. Use the timer: in the real thing you get about four seconds, not four minutes.

Round 1 — you're the one who got up 🚪

Another Thursday, the same two desks, a different piece of bad news. Marta speaks — you answer, out loud, without looking back at the phrases. Flat voice. Short sentences. Gaps.

Round 2 — now you're Marta 💻

Swap seats. You're the competent one who's just gone at their desk in front of everybody, and a colleague you don't know that well has come over. Play her properly: she doesn't want this, she says she's fine, she apologises too much, and she is not going to make it easy. The Twist

🎯 The promise, ten minutes later

Marta wouldn't go home. She said she was fine, she went back in and she sat down, and before she did she took hold of your sleeve and said: "Don't tell anyone. Promise me. Especially not Dan." You promised. Dan is your manager. It's now 11:02, and Dan has stopped you in the corridor: "What's going on with Marta? She's just cancelled the client call, that's twice this week, and finance are asking me about the handover. Is there something I need to know?" He isn't being unkind — he's asking a fair question, and he can tell you know the answer. Start the timer and have that conversation out loud. You can keep the promise and let Dan think Marta's slipping, or you can break it and she finds out by lunchtime. You cannot do both, and "I'd rather not say" is itself an answer that tells him something. Decide, in the corridor, in real time — and then say what you'd say to Marta about it afterwards.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I got her away from her desk without asking her to make a decision about it.
  • I stopped the apologising in under five words, and didn't argue with her about it.
  • I left real silence — gaps where I said nothing and let her fill them.
  • I offered her something specific and practical, not "let me know if you need anything".
  • I said what I'd do about the rest of the office, and I said something about tomorrow.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one to fix — run the twist again and get it in.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

People remember who came over. Not what was said — almost nobody can quote it afterwards — but they remember, for years, which colleague stood up and which ones found something interesting on their screens.

The reason so few people stand up isn't coldness. It's that everyone's waiting to think of the right thing to say, and there isn't one, and by the time they've finished looking for it the moment has gone and it would be strange to go over now.

Which is the useful part of today: none of these ten phrases is clever. They're small, flat and ordinary, and that's the whole point — you don't need to be good at this. You need to be the one who gets up.

Two bigger questions

  1. Marta asks you to tell nobody, and your manager then asks you a fair question that you know the answer to. Whose interests are you actually serving when you keep that promise — and is there a point at which staying silent about a colleague stops being loyalty and starts being a way of protecting yourself from an awkward conversation?
  2. An office where nobody ever cries is usually described as professional. What is that word really doing there — and what does a workplace get, and what does it quietly pay, for the rule that feelings are left at the door?
