The Flow State

B2 Level · When Time Disappears

Have you ever lost track of time?

Sometimes we get so absorbed in something that an hour passes and feels like five minutes. Psychologists call this "flow" — and it might be one of the most enjoyable experiences a human being can have.

Reading: The Psychology of Flow

In the 1970s, a Hungarian psychologist called Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi noticed something strange. When he interviewed artists, athletes, surgeons and chess players about their best moments at work, they all described the same experience — in almost the same words.

They said that time seemed to disappear. They felt completely absorbed in what they were doing. The action felt effortless, even when it was extremely difficult. They forgot about themselves, their hunger, their phone, their problems. Afterwards, they felt tired but deeply happy. He gave this experience a name: flow.

According to Csikszentmihalyi, flow happens when three conditions come together. First, the activity must be challenging enough to demand your full attention — but not so difficult that you panic. Second, you need a clear goal: you have to know what "doing well" looks like. Third, you need fast feedback, so you can see immediately whether you are getting closer to that goal.

Interestingly, his research showed that people experience flow far more often at work than during free time. Watching television, for example, almost never produces flow, even though most of us choose it when we want to relax. The activities that make us happiest are usually the ones that ask something of us.

The modern problem, of course, is interruption. Every notification, message and tab cuts the connection between us and what we are doing. Some studies suggest it can take more than twenty minutes to return to deep concentration after a single distraction. Flow, it seems, is a state we have to protect.

1. What surprised the psychologist about his interviews?

Show Answer
People with very different jobs (artists, athletes, surgeons, chess players) all described their best moments in almost identical words.

2. What three conditions does flow need?

Show Answer
A challenging activity (but not impossible), a clear goal, and fast feedback on how you are doing.

3. According to the text, why is television a strange choice for relaxation?

Show Answer
Because it almost never produces flow — the activities that actually make us happiest are the ones that demand something from us.

4. Why is the modern world bad for flow?

Show Answer
Constant interruptions and notifications break our concentration. Returning to deep focus after a single distraction can take more than twenty minutes.

Past simple vs. past continuous

When we tell flow stories ("I was so absorbed I didn't notice the rain"), we naturally mix two tenses: the past continuous for the long, ongoing activity, and the past simple for the short event that interrupts it.

Past continuous

The background — what was happening over a period of time.

I was working on a problem...

Past simple

The interruption — a single, completed event.

...when my phone rang.
