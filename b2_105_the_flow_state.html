The Flow State

B2/C1 Level · When Time Disappears

Have you ever lost track of time?

Occasionally we become so utterly engrossed in what we're doing that an hour slips by and feels like five minutes. Psychologists call this state of intense, almost unselfconscious absorption "flow" — and it may well be one of the most rewarding experiences a human being can have.

Think about this:

1. When was the last time you completely lost track of time? What were you doing, and what eventually pulled you out of it?

2. Some people insist they feel most alive when they are under enormous pressure. Does that strike you as paradoxical, or does it make perfect sense?

3. What is the difference, in your view, between being merely busy and being genuinely absorbed?

Reading: The Psychology of Flow

In the 1970s, the Hungarian-American psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi stumbled upon something quite striking. When he interviewed artists, athletes, surgeons and chess players about the very best moments of their working lives, they all described an oddly similar experience — often in almost identical language.

Time, they said, seemed to dissolve. They had been utterly engrossed in what they were doing; the action had felt deceptively effortless, even when the task itself was punishingly difficult. They had forgotten about themselves — their hunger, their phones, their problems. Afterwards, they felt drained but profoundly satisfied. He coined a term for the experience: flow.

According to Csikszentmihalyi, flow tends to emerge when three conditions converge. First, the activity must be sufficiently challenging to command your full attention — but not so demanding that you slip into anxiety. Second, you need a clear goal, so that you know precisely what "doing well" looks like. Third, you need rapid, continuous feedback, allowing you to sense, almost instinctively, whether you are inching closer to that goal.

What is striking, however, is that his research suggests people enter flow far more frequently at work than during their leisure time. Television, by contrast, almost never produces it — despite being the default many of us reach for when we want to switch off. The activities that genuinely satisfy us, it seems, are precisely the ones that ask something of us.

The modern threat, of course, is interruption. Every notification, every blinking tab, severs the delicate thread between us and what we are doing. Some studies estimate that it can take well over twenty minutes to return to deep concentration after a single distraction. Flow, in other words, is a fragile and increasingly endangered state — one we have to actively protect.

1. What did Csikszentmihalyi find surprising about the interviews he conducted?

Show Answer
People in radically different fields (artists, athletes, surgeons, chess players) described their peak working moments in almost identical language — suggesting a universal psychological state rather than a profession-specific one.

2. Summarise the three conditions that allow flow to emerge.

Show Answer
A task that is challenging but achievable (just within reach), a clearly defined goal, and continuous, rapid feedback that allows you to sense progress in real time.

3. Why does the writer describe television as a "strange" choice for relaxation?

Show Answer
Because it almost never produces flow, despite being the activity people instinctively reach for when they want to unwind. Counter-intuitively, the activities that genuinely satisfy us tend to be those that demand something of us.

4. In what sense is flow "endangered" today?

Show Answer
Constant digital interruptions sever the connection between us and our task. Reclaiming deep concentration after a single distraction can take well over twenty minutes — meaning flow now has to be deliberately defended rather than passively enjoyed.

5. Inference: why might the phrase "deceptively effortless" be a particularly accurate description of flow?

Show Answer
Because the experience feels easy from the inside, even though the task itself may be objectively very demanding. The skill, the difficulty and the effort are all real — what disappears is the conscious sense of struggle.

Narrative tenses + cleft sentences for emphasis

When we tell flow stories, we layer three past tenses — and we often use cleft sentences to highlight what mattered most.

Past continuous

The background — an action in progress.

I was working on a tricky problem...

Past simple

The interruption — a single, completed event.

...when my phone rang.

Past perfect / continuous

An earlier action that explains the moment.

By then I had been working for four hours straight without realising it.

Cleft sentences: putting the spotlight on what mattered

Cleft sentences are a hallmark of fluent C1 narrative. They split a sentence in two so you can place real emphasis on the key element.

  • What-cleft: What surprised me most was how quickly the time vanished.
  • It-cleft: It was the silence in the flat that finally pulled me out of it.
  • The thing/reason-cleft: The reason I love that work is that I genuinely lose myself in it.

Useful C1 chunks for telling a flow story

  • I'd been utterly engrossed in...
  • I had no idea how long I'd been at it...
  • The next thing I knew, ...
  • It felt deceptively effortless, even though...
  • What pulled me out of it was...
  • It wasn't until ... that I realised...
  • I was so absorbed I didn't even register...
