The Egg

Andy Weir (2009) — a man dies, meets God, and learns who every other person really is

Easier version available: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Read in three parts

Andy Weir wrote this very short story in 2009 and put it online for free; it has been read and argued over by millions since. This is a graded retelling of his idea in three movements. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The whole story is a slow reveal — do not skip ahead.

Part 1 — After the crash

You died on the way home. It was a car accident — nothing too dramatic, but fatal all the same. You left behind a husband and two children. There was no pain. The paramedics tried their best to save you, but it was no use. Your body was so badly broken that, honestly, you were better off this way. And then you met me.

"What happened?" you asked. "Where am I?"

"You died," I said, plainly. There seemed little point in dressing it up.

"There was… a truck, and it skidded…"

"There was," I agreed. "You needn't worry about the details now."

You looked around. There was nothing — just the two of us, and an emptiness that did not feel empty. "Are you God?" you asked. "You must be God." I smiled. "You catch on quickly," I said.

Pause and discuss:
  • The story is told in the second person — "you died." How does being called "you" change the way you read it?
  • God is gentle and matter-of-fact, not grand or frightening. Why might Weir choose to write God this way?
  • The first thing the woman wants is the details of the crash. The last thing God wants to discuss is the crash. What does that gap tell us is coming?

Part 2 — The wheel of lives

You asked what came next — heaven, or hell, or nothing. "Nothing so simple," I told you. "You'll be reincarnated. You'll go back and live another life." You frowned. "So I'll come back as something else? A new person?" "Yes," I said. "A new baby, in a new time, with no memory of this one — or of any of the others."

"How many times have I done this?" you asked. "Many times," I said. "Many, many times. And each life has been a different person." You thought about that. "Then I might have lived… centuries ago? I could have been someone in the ancient world?" "You could have been, and you were," I said. "But here is the part you haven't guessed yet. Time, where I am, doesn't work the way it does for you. The lives don't have to come in order."

Pause and discuss:
  • God says the lives "don't have to come in order." Take a moment with that. What does it open up?
  • The woman keeps reasoning toward the truth one careful step at a time. Why does Weir make her work it out rather than simply telling her?

Part 3 — Every soul is one soul

"Every person who has ever lived," I said, "is you. Every person who will ever live is you too. You are every human being who has ever walked the Earth." You sat in silence as it settled. "I am… everyone?" "Now you're starting to see," I said. "Every time you wronged someone, you were wronging yourself. Every act of kindness you ever did, you did to yourself. Every joy and every cruelty, given and received, has been given and received by you alone."

"But why?" you asked. "Why all of this?" "Because one day you will become like me," I said. "This — the whole universe — is an egg. It was made for you. You are not yet finished. You are growing, life by life, into something that cannot be born until it has lived every human life there is. When you have been each of them, and learned what each of them had to teach, you will be ready to hatch." You stared at me. Then you went on to your next life. And I went about my work.

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • "Every act of kindness you ever did, you did to yourself." Read that again. If you fully believed it, what would change tomorrow morning?
  • The universe is "an egg" made to grow one soul to maturity. Is that a comforting idea, a lonely one, or both at once?
  • The story ends with no triumph and no reunion — just "you went on to your next life." Why end on something so quiet after so vast a revelation?
  • Your turn: The idea is beautiful but unprovable. Does its truth matter, or is it enough that it changes how you treat the next stranger?

The grammar this story teaches itself

The whole story is the woman working something out — moving from guess to certainty. That is exactly what modals of deduction do. We use must for what we are sure is true, can't for what we are sure is false, and might / may / could for what is possible — in the present and in the past.

1. Deduction about the present — must / can't / might

must = I'm sure it's true. can't = I'm sure it's false. might / may / could = it's possible. ("You must be God." / "This can't be real." / "It might be true.")

1. "You know everything and we are nowhere — you be God." (certain, true)

2. "I feel no pain at all; I still be alive." (certain, false)

3. "If time runs differently here, I have lived in any century." (possible)

4. "He is so calm about it — this be the first time he has done this." (certain, false)

1. must

2. can't

3. might / may / could

4. can't

2. Deduction about the past — must have / can't have / might have

For deductions about the past, add have + past participle. ("I must have lived thousands of lives." / "You can't have been only one person." / "I might have been someone in the ancient world.")

1. "If I am everyone, I (be) the man who hurt me in my last life." (certain)

2. "She remembers nothing of her past lives, so she (be, certain-negative) told about them while she lived them." (certain, false)

3. "He says many lives — I (live) in Rome, or I might not have." (possible)

4. "Every kindness I received, I (give) to myself." (certain)

1. must have been

2. can't have been

3. might have lived / may have lived / could have lived

4. must have given

3. Speculating about the future — the unknowable

For the future we lean on will / won't (confident prediction), is likely to, and may well. Fill each gap with a natural choice.

1. "You be reincarnated," God says with total certainty.

2. "One day you well become something like me." (strong possibility)

3. "Until you have lived every life, you be ready to hatch." (negative certainty)

1. will

2. may / might

3. won't / will not

What Weir does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are how a story barely a thousand words long stays with people for years.

1. Second person — "you died"

By telling the story as "you," Weir implicates the reader directly. You are not watching a character receive this news; you are receiving it. The technique collapses the distance between reader and protagonist — which is the whole point of a story about everyone being one person.

2. The slow reveal — dialogue as discovery

Weir withholds the central idea and lets the woman deduce it through questions. We arrive at "you are everyone" at the same speed she does, so the revelation feels earned rather than announced.

3. The plain God — understatement of the sacred

God speaks in calm, almost conversational English. Weir undercuts our expectation of thunder and majesty, and the smallness of the voice makes the bigness of the idea land harder by contrast.

4. The single metaphor — the egg

The whole cosmology rests on one homely image: an egg. Weir compresses an entire theology into a thing you could hold in your hand, and the title reframes the universe as something small, warm and in the process of becoming.

Spot-the-technique — quick MCQ

Weir writes "You died on the way home" instead of "A woman died on the way home." What does the second-person choice achieve?

Speaking tasks

Do all four. Each one builds on the last. The timer is for your benefit — it forces you to fill the time rather than escape it. Use modals of deduction and effect verbs from the previous tabs.

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · Retell the story in your own words (3 minutes)

Before we discuss it, tell the story back to me as if I had never read it — in your own words, not line by line. Aim for about eight to ten sentences.

As you go, use at least five words from the Vocabulary tab and keep the events in order using the modals of deduction from the Grammar tab. These beats will help you: a man dies in a car crash and meets God · God explains the universe and time are not what he thought · he learns he has lived, and will live, every human life there has ever been · every person he ever met was also him · he is a soul maturing across all of humanity (the turn: "You are every human being who ever lived").

Task B · Work It Out Aloud (4 minutes)

Role-play the woman in the empty space, reasoning her way toward the truth — but think out loud, using modals of deduction at every step. "If there's no pain, I can't still be alive… You know everything, so you must be… If time runs differently, I might have lived…"

Use at least four different modals (must, can't, might, must have, can't have…). Let the certainty grow as you go.

Task C · The Stranger Who Was You (4 minutes)

Think of one specific stranger you encountered recently — someone you barely noticed, or someone who annoyed you. Now tell their story as if "The Egg" is true and that person was a past or future version of you. Speculate about their life: what they must have been going through, what they might have wanted, what you can't have known by looking.

Build the whole monologue on past modals of deduction ("She must have been exhausted… he might have just lost someone…"). Use at least three effect verbs from Tab 3.

Task D · Does It Have to Be True? (4 minutes)

The story is unprovable. For two minutes, argue that this doesn't matter — that an idea which makes you kinder to strangers is worth holding whether or not it's literally true. For two more, push back: argue that believing comforting things we can't verify is exactly the habit we should resist, and that the value of empathy shouldn't depend on a cosmic story.

Use the distancing language from Tab 1 ("Whether or not it's true…") and at least one modal of speculation about the future.

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. If you genuinely believed every person you met was you, would you behave better — or would the idea lose its force precisely because it's too big to feel?
  2. The story borrows reincarnation but bends it into something new. Does dressing a moral lesson in a cosmic story make it more powerful, or does it let us off the hook by making ethics depend on metaphysics?
  3. God calls the universe an egg "made for you." Is a universe built to grow a single soul a beautiful idea or a lonely one?
  4. Compare this with "How Much Land Does a Man Need?" — both are short stories about what a human life is for. What would Tolstoy's Pahóm make of Weir's egg?
