"I'm going to visit my sister" — decided before, already arranged (tickets booked last week). "I'll get it!" — decided right at that second, the moment the phone rings. English uses two different forms depending on when you decided.
Today: will for decisions you make right now — offers and promises — and going to for plans you decided before, and for predictions when you can see the signs right now. So you can offer to help without thinking twice, talk about your real plans, and warn people about what you can see coming.

Deciding Right Now: "I'll help you"

For decisions, offers and promises you make at the exact moment you're speaking — not before.

Spot the pattern

Three things people say without planning them first:

  • "You look busy — I'll carry those bags for you."
  • "I haven't got any cash on me — don't worry, I'll pay you back tomorrow."
  • "The phone's ringing — I'll get it!"

In all three, did the speaker plan this before, or decide it right at that moment? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Right at that moment — that's the key to will. No planning, no arrangement, just a decision, offer or promise made as you speak.
will / 'll + base verb (never "will to")

Plans You've Already Made: "We're going to visit my parents"

For plans you decided before now — already in your head, or already arranged.

Spot the pattern

Three plans, all decided before today:

  • "We're going to visit my parents this weekend — we planned it last week."
  • "I'm going to start a new job next month."
  • "They're going to move to a bigger flat in the spring."

What's different about when these were decided, compared with the last tab? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
All three were decided before now — days, weeks, even months earlier. That's when we use going to: am/is/are + going to + base verb.
am / is / are + going to + base verb
Don't drop the "am/is/are" — "I going to visit" isn't a complete sentence in English. You always need I'm / she's / we're + going to.

Reading the Signs: "It's going to rain"

For predictions — when you can see or hear something right now that tells you what's coming.

Spot the pattern

Three predictions, each based on something happening right now:

  • "Look at those clouds — it's going to rain."
  • "She's driving so fast — she's going to have an accident!"
  • "The gate's closing and we're still running — we're going to miss the train."

What do all three speakers have in front of them, right now, that tells them what's coming? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Evidence they can see or hear right now — clouds, dangerous driving, a closing gate. "Going to" isn't a guess out of nowhere; it's a prediction based on something happening in front of you. Same structure as plans: am/is/are + going to + base verb.
am / is / are + going to + base verb
(evidence you can see or hear right now)
Careful with "will" here: "it will rains" is wrong twice — after "will" you never add -s, and plain "will" doesn't point at evidence you can see right now the way "going to" does.

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: "I'm meeting Ana at six" is present continuous — a fixed arrangement with an exact time. "I'm going to meet Ana sometime this week" uses "going to" — a general plan without a fixed time yet. Both talk about the future, but one is more definite than the other.
