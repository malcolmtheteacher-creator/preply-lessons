Understanding Teacher Feedback

B1 Intermediate Academic English

⏱️ Time: 5:00

Types of Feedback

Teachers give feedback to help you improve. Learning to understand it helps you develop academically.

Common Feedback Phrases

"Well done" / "Excellent"

Positive feedback praising good work. Means this section meets or exceeds expectations.

"Good point, but..." / "Consider..."

Mixed feedback. Acknowledges something good, then suggests improvement.

"Needs development" / "Unclear"

Constructive criticism. The idea needs more work or explanation.

"Evidence?" / "Citation needed"

Points out missing support. You've made a claim but haven't proven it.

"Off-topic" / "Not relevant"

Indicates the section doesn't relate to your main argument.

"Proofread carefully" / "Grammar"

Points out language or spelling errors. Easy to fix in revision.

Correction Codes and Abbreviations

Teachers often use shorthand codes. Understanding these saves time and helps you improve.

Common Correction Codes

  • Sp — Spelling error
  • Gr / G — Grammar mistake
  • AWK — Awkward phrasing; sounds unnatural
  • Ww / W — Wrong word choice
  • P / Punct — Punctuation error
  • ^ — Something is missing; add a word
  • Ref — Reference unclear or missing
  • Tense — Wrong verb tense
  • ? — Meaning unclear
  • Org — Organisation or structure needs work

Responding to Feedback

Don't Get Discouraged

  • Feedback is about your work, not you as a person
  • Teachers want to help you improve
  • Mistakes are how we learn

What to Do with Feedback

  • Read all comments carefully
  • Look for patterns (do you always make the same mistake?)
  • Ask for clarification if you don't understand
  • Make changes in future work based on feedback
  • Track your progress—mark improvements

Interpreting Feedback

What does each piece of feedback mean? How should you respond?

Example 1

Comment on your essay: "Good introduction, but needs more evidence in paragraph 3."

What does this mean?

Meaning: Your introduction is strong. However, your paragraph 3 makes claims without supporting them with facts, studies, or examples. What to do: Find credible sources to support the points in paragraph 3. Add citations.

Example 2

Correction code marked on your sentence: "Tense" with an arrow pointing to "He is studying for three years."

Meaning: The verb tense is wrong. "Is studying" (present continuous) doesn't match the context. What to do: Change to "He has been studying for three years" (present perfect) to show a period from past until now.

Example 3

Comment: "This is off-topic. How does this relate to your main argument?"

Meaning: The paragraph or section doesn't connect to your thesis. It may be interesting but doesn't support your main point. What to do: Either remove this section or add a sentence explaining how it relates to your argument.

Example 4

Comment with question mark: "Smith argues that exercise improves mood?"

Meaning: The sentence is unclear. Does Smith say this? Is he correct? What's your point? What to do: Clarify the statement. Example: "According to Smith's research, exercise improves mood by releasing endorphins."
