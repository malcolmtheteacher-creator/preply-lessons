The Limits of Language

In which the most gifted philosopher of the twentieth century writes two masterpieces—the second destroying the first—and concludes that the deepest things cannot be said

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951) wrote two of the most important philosophical works of the twentieth century. The first, the Tractatus, claimed that language mirrors reality, that philosophy's job is logical analysis. The second, the Investigations, destroyed this view, claiming language is a game with many uses, not a mirror of reality. Throughout, he maintained: the deepest things—ethics, aesthetics, the meaning of life—lie beyond what language can express. Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent.

I. Before We Begin

What can language do? What can it not do?

♦ ♦ ♦

Wittgenstein will argue something radical: philosophy has assumed language can say everything. But it cannot. The most important things—beauty, meaning, ethics, the mystical—cannot be said. They can only be shown. This is not a weakness. It is a recognition of language's true nature.

II. The Story — From Picture to Game

Ludwig Wittgenstein was born in Vienna in 1889. He was the son of a wealthy industrialist. He was brilliant, intense, spiritually tormented. He served in World War I, wrote the Tractatus in a prisoner-of-war camp, then gave away his inheritance and worked as a schoolteacher.

The Tractatus: Language as Picture

Wittgenstein's first masterpiece claimed that language is fundamentally a picture of reality. Words stand for things. Sentences are combinations of words that picture facts. The job of philosophy is logical analysis—to clarify the relationship between language and reality.

Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent. — Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus

This famous final sentence is key. The deepest matters—ethics, aesthetics, the meaning of life—cannot be said. They transcend the limits of language. Philosophy must recognize this boundary and stop trying to speak about the unspeakable.

The Investigations: Language as Game

Decades later, Wittgenstein rewrote his thinking. Language is not one thing. It is a multiplicity of language-games. A child learning to speak, a scientist describing an experiment, a poet writing a sonnet, a person praying—all are using language in different ways. There is no single function of language. There are many.

Consider the word "game." What do chess, tag, peek-a-boo, and poker have in common? There is no single essence. They share "family resemblances"—overlapping similarities, not a common core.

Language is the same. Words don't get their meaning from standing for things. They get their meaning from use—from the form of life they're part of.

III. The Ideas

Idea 1: The Limits of Language (Tractatus)

Language can picture facts. But the deepest truths cannot be said.

What cannot be said: ethics, aesthetics, the mystical, the meaning of life. These are not because they are unclear. They are because language itself has a boundary. The most important things lie beyond it.

Idea 2: Language Games (Investigations)

Language has no single essence. It is many games with different rules.

Meaning comes from use. A word means what it does in the language-game it's part of. To understand language, study how it's actually used—not in isolation, but in the context of a form of life.

Idea 3: The Mystical Remains

Even in the later work, the deepest matters remain beyond language.

Show, don't tell. The parable, the poem, the silence—these can gesture toward what cannot be said. But saying itself has limits.

VII. Final Retrieval

1. The Three Dates

1921 — Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus

1953 — Philosophical Investigations (posthumous)

1951 — Wittgenstein's death

2. Two Views of Language

Compare the Tractatus and the Investigations:

Tractatus: Language pictures reality. Meaning = standing for things. Philosophy = logical analysis

Investigations: Language is many games. Meaning = use in form of life. Philosophy = description of usage

3. The Limits of Language

What cannot be said?

Ethics, aesthetics, the meaning of life, the mystical. These transcend language. They can be shown but not said.
