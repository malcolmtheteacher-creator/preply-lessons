The Vocabulary of Workforce Pressure

Explain each term aloud before clicking the card. Then use it to interpret one figure from the case.

vacancy rateThe proportion of authorised roles that are currently unfilled.
time to hireThe time between opening a vacancy and securing an accepted offer.
attritionThe gradual loss of employees when people leave and are not immediately replaced.
skills mixThe combination of different qualifications and capabilities within a team.
rotaA schedule showing who works which shift or period.
contingent labourTemporary, agency, freelance, or contract workers used when permanent capacity is insufficient.
capacity bufferExtra staffing or time deliberately kept available for sudden peaks or disruption.
retention leverA change intended to reduce departures, such as better scheduling, progression, pay, or support.

Cause, Symptom or Amplifier?

Classify each item, but allow for overlap. The point is to defend the classification, not merely attach a label.

Long time to hire · unpredictable rotas · rising overtime · weak induction · sudden demand growth · increasing absence

Possible analysis: demand growth and long hiring times may be causes; rising overtime and absence may be symptoms that later amplify the problem; unpredictable rotas and weak induction may be both causes and amplifiers. A defensible answer depends on sequence and evidence.

What data would separate correlation from causation here? Propose three questions for an exit interview or staff survey.

From Assertion to Analysis

Strong decision language does three jobs: it identifies the current diagnosis, exposes an assumption, and makes the trade-off visible.

DIAGNOSE
The immediate bottleneck appears to be…
DISTINGUISH
That points to a problem with … rather than …
TEST
The case for this rests on the assumption that…
TRADE OFF
That may stabilise …, but only at the cost of…
SEQUENCE
In the short term, we should …, while in parallel…
WARN
Unless we address …, we risk merely…

Choose two phrases and complete them using the fictional case. Then challenge the assumption in each statement.

Which Version Can Survive a Challenge?

Version A: “We need more people. The obvious solution is to recruit immediately. This will solve the staffing crisis.”

Version B: “The vacancy rate makes recruitment unavoidable, but recruitment alone is unlikely to stabilise the service. Unless we also reduce avoidable attrition and shorten induction, we risk filling posts at roughly the same rate as experienced staff leave.”

Before revealing the analysis, identify every feature that makes Version B more defensible. Is it also more persuasive? Explain the difference.

Version B acknowledges the need to recruit but limits the claim, distinguishes hiring from stabilisation, identifies two linked mechanisms, and states a foreseeable risk. Its caution makes the recommendation easier to defend under questioning.

Reframe the Claim

Transform each blunt claim into a calibrated C1 recommendation. Speak first; use the text box only to capture the strongest version.

  1. Agency workers are too expensive.
  2. The rota is causing people to leave.
  3. Training new staff will take too long.
  4. Management must offer higher salaries.

Contingent labour may be difficult to sustain financially, although limited cover could protect essential capacity while permanent recruitment catches up.

The exit pattern appears consistent with dissatisfaction about the rota, but we would need shift-level and interview data before treating scheduling as the primary cause.

Training will delay the immediate benefit, yet failing to invest in the skills mix may leave the organisation permanently dependent on scarce specialists.

The case for higher salaries rests on pay being a decisive cause of attrition; if scheduling and progression matter more, a pay-only response may be poorly targeted.

Pressure-Test the Plan

Respond to each challenge without abandoning the logic of the plan. Acknowledge what is valid, defend what remains sound, and identify what would need to change.

“Your plan spends money on retention without proving that people are leaving for preventable reasons.”

A strong response concedes the evidence gap, explains why limited retention action may still be justified, and proposes a measure or checkpoint before further spending.

“Temporary cover is expensive. Why not tolerate lower capacity until permanent staff arrive?”

A strong response compares the visible cost of temporary cover with the less visible cost of delay, overload, error, reputational damage, or further attrition.

“Rota redesign sounds efficient on paper, but what if the new pattern causes more resignations?”

A strong response treats implementation as a testable intervention, proposes consultation or a pilot, and states a clear stop condition.

2. Explain the difference between a cause, a symptom and a trade-off. Give one example of each.

A cause helps produce the problem; a symptom is evidence or a consequence of it; a trade-off is what must be sacrificed or risked when choosing one response over another.

3. Rephrase “This plan will fail” in two more calibrated ways.

“The plan is unlikely to succeed unless…” / “The plan may relieve the immediate pressure, but it does not yet address…” / “Its success rests on an assumption that has not been tested.”
