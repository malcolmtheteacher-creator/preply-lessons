MINDS WORTH MEETING · C1 · 50 min

Forough Farrokhzad

The Iranian poet who dared to write as a woman who feels, desires and refuses to be silent — and paid for that honesty with her reputation, her son and very nearly her place in the canon.

You talk first — before you read anything

This is a talking lesson, not a reading one. So before a single fact, the floor is yours.

Start here · 2 minutes

Look at her name and the line under this lesson's title: “a woman who feels, desires and refuses to be silent.” What kind of writer do you imagine she was? What might it have cost a woman to write that way? And have you come across her at all — the poems, the film, the life — what pulled you in, or kept you away? Just talk.

Now meet her — one beat at a time

Three short facts. After each one, you do the talking.

Forough Farrokhzad was born in Tehran in 1934. She married at sixteen, had a son, and divorced a few years later — and under the customs of the time, lost custody of him. That loss was not a footnote; it became one of the open wounds running through everything she wrote.

Over to you:
  • She lost her son for refusing to stay inside the expected role. How might a wound like that change the kind of work an artist makes — and what would you never be able to write around?

She began publishing very young, and the frankness of her early collections — “The Captive”, “Rebellion” — caused a scandal. Here was a woman writing openly about female desire, the body, loneliness and longing, in a mid-century Iran that expected a respectable woman to stay silent on exactly those subjects. She was attacked, dismissed, gossiped about. She kept writing.

Over to you:
  • Her early poems were called scandalous simply because a woman wrote them. Do you think the shock was really about the content — or about who was speaking?

By her thirties she had outgrown the scandal and become a major artist. Her 1964 collection “Another Birth” is among the finest modern Persian poetry, and she had moved into film, directing the short documentary “The House Is Black” in 1962, shot in a leper colony — now a landmark of Iranian cinema. In 1967 she was killed in a car accident, aged thirty-two. After the 1979 revolution her work was suppressed for years.

She wrote, in effect, of setting her hands down into the dark soil of the earth so that one day she might grow green again — speaking of her own renewal as something planted, inevitable, alive.

Over to you:
  • A free female voice was suppressed years after she had died. Why would a dead poet's words still feel dangerous to people in power? What does that tell you about her?

The big idea — and you do the thinking out loud

Underneath the scandal and the beautiful images is one steady demand. I will hand it to you in small pieces. After each piece, you talk — I will mostly listen.

Farrokhzad's central act was deceptively simple: she wrote as a woman who was a full human being — one who desires, doubts, grieves and wants freedom, and has every right to say so out loud. In a culture that allowed women to be daughters, wives and mothers but not openly themselves, that was radical.

Put yourself there

She simply spoke from inside a woman's experience as if it were obviously worth hearing — and dared anyone to say it was not. Why do you think that small act was treated as dangerous, then and in places now? What would it take from you to speak that plainly about your own inner life?

That is why her image of “another birth” matters so much. She is not writing about being born once, biologically, into the role society assigns you. She is writing about being born again, on your own terms — choosing your own life, your own voice, your own self, even when the cost is rejection. Rebirth, for her, is an act of will, not of nature.

Over to you:
  • She frames freedom as a “rebirth” you choose, not one you are given. Have you ever had to choose a version of yourself the people around you did not want? What did it cost?

Here is the uncomfortable part. Her honesty was not free. It cost her respectability, it was tangled up with the loss of her son, it drew constant attack, and after her death it got her work banned for years. Her poems never pretend the freedom was painless — they hold loneliness and longing right next to defiance. She is not selling liberation as a happy ending.

Her work insists, again and again, that a window is enough — one act of seeing, one act of speaking truthfully — to break the silence a whole society has agreed to keep.

Take a side · 3–4 minutes

“Authenticity — saying the true thing in your own voice — is always worth what it costs.” Argue for it or against it, and bring real examples: from art, from public life, from your own world. Is there a point where the price is too high, or where honesty turns reckless toward the people tied to you? Build your case, then I will push back and you defend it.

The work itself

Her voice changed and deepened across her short life. Glance at the table, then tell me which one you would actually read or watch next — and why that one.

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that she would have written very differently had she lived longer.
  • It might well be that the scandal protected her work as much as it harmed it.
  • I suspect the truth is somewhere in between.
  • You could argue that the loss of her son shaped every poem that followed.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say her honesty was a political act in itself.
  • What strikes me most is how calmly she claimed the right to speak.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, her voice feels more urgent now than in the 1960s.

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure I fully buy that.
  • I take the point, but doesn't that assume…?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…

A · The Price of Honesty (3–4 min)

Farrokhzad told the truth about her inner life and lost her reputation, and was entangled in the loss of her son, for doing it. Imagine you could only be fully honest — about your feelings, your choices, your real self — by accepting a real cost in your own world: a relationship, a job, a community's approval.

Argue one side: is being authentic always worth the price, or is there a point where staying quiet is the wiser, kinder choice? Build a real case — give an example from your own life or world, raise the strongest objection to your own view, then answer it.

B · Whose Voice Threatens Us? (3–4 min)

Her work was banned after the revolution; in her lifetime it was simply called scandalous. A free, honest personal voice clearly unsettled people in power.

Take today's world — social media, censorship, "cancel culture", state control of art. Whose honest voices do we still try to silence, and why? Pick one real example and make the case: is the silencing ever justified, or never?

C · Your "Another Birth" (4–5 min)

Farrokhzad's great image is being born again on your own terms — choosing a self the world did not hand you. Your turn: describe a real or imagined "another birth" of your own. A moment, a decision, or a future choice where you would step out of the role expected of you and become someone you chose to be.

Set it up, walk me through what you would have to leave behind, and land the idea underneath it in one sentence. I will then push back — name the cost, ask if it is worth it — and you defend it.
