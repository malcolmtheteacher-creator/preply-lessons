“I’m Fine”

Four days of one-word answers, a flat that's gone very quiet, and a person you love telling you nothing's the matter. Tonight you're going to ask properly.

The Scene 🎬

It started on Tuesday, or possibly Monday — you've stopped being able to tell. Alex comes in, hangs the coat up, answers everything in about four words, and spends the evening on their phone at the other end of the sofa. Not slamming doors. Nothing you could point at. Just gone slightly out of the room while still being in it.

You've asked twice already. Both times: I'm fine. And you've let it go, because pushing felt worse — but four days of this is starting to get into everything, the washing-up, the telly, the space in the bed. Somewhere underneath there's a question you'd rather not ask out loud, which is whether the thing that's wrong is you.

So tonight, with the plates done and the kitchen quiet, you're going to ask a third time. Properly. And then — this is the hard bit — you're going to accept whatever comes back, including nothing.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When something's bothering you, are you a say-it-immediately person or a go-quiet-for-days person? Where do you think you learnt that — and does it work for you?
  2. Has anyone ever got you to talk when you'd decided you weren't going to? What did they actually do? And what does someone do that makes you shut down completely?

Who's who

You
The One Still Asking 🫖

Worried, a bit fed up, and quietly afraid the answer involves you. You want the truth more than you want a peaceful evening — but you don't want to interrogate the person you live with.

The other voice
Alex 🤐

Your partner. Monosyllabic since Tuesday and insisting nothing's the matter. Not being cruel — genuinely can't find the words yet, and half-convinced that saying it out loud will make it real.

The Conversation 💬

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Early on, one line lands badly and Alex bites back at it. What does the other one do in their very next breath, and why does the conversation survive it?

2. Alex answers the question “have I done something?” twice — once quickly, once with a small change to it. What was the change, and what does the other one do about it?

3. Alex never actually says what's wrong. Why isn't that a failure? Give two things the other one got, even so.

1. The remark about “fine” sounds like a dig, and Alex hears it as one. Instead of defending the line or explaining that it was a joke, the other one apologises immediately, admits it came out wrong, and then says the plain version of what they actually meant — four days, nine words. Repairing it that fast is what keeps the conversation open, because Alex is left with nothing to argue against.
2. First it's “it's not you”, then it's “it's not really you” — which is a much smaller no. The other one notices the word, says it back once, and then leaves it alone rather than pouncing on it, and instead promises not to be angry tonight, which is the thing standing between Alex and the truth.
3. They got a category (work, sort of, partly) and they got a time (the weekend), which turns an open silence into something with an end. They also stopped asking before Alex had to push them away, so the door is still open and Alex isn't defending it.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for getting someone to talk who has decided not to. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Everything here depends on speed and volume rather than words. The same question, fired off fast and a bit loud, is an interrogation; said slowly, quietly, while you're both looking at something else, it's an offer. Drop your voice, leave the silences alone instead of filling them, and let your questions trail off rather than land — because a person who feels chased will say “fine” forever.

🚪 Opening the door

Phrase 1

You want to start by saying what you've actually noticed, rather than telling them what they're feeling or asking a question they can bat away with one word. Which opening does that?

A. “What's the matter with you at the moment?”
B. “You've been off all evening. What's going on?”
C. “You're clearly in a mood again.”
B — “You've been off all evening. What's going on?”
To be “off” is to be not quite yourself — a bit flat, a bit distant — and it's deliberately mild, which is why it's usable. A puts the fault in them (“the matter with you”) and C tells them what they're feeling and adds “again”, so both invite an argument rather than an answer. B works because the first half is a fact they can't really deny, and the second half is wide open, so they'd have to make an effort to say nothing.
Phrase 2

Later, you offer them a choice of three explanations instead of one big question — and you keep the third one deliberately small and easy, so they can take it and stop. Complete the chunk: “Is it me, or is it work, or is it just one of ___ ___?”

“…or is it just one of those days?”
“One of those days” means a day where nothing in particular has gone wrong and everything is annoying anyway, which is an answer nobody has to justify. Offering three options is easier to answer than “what's wrong?”, because they only have to point rather than explain, and putting the harmless option last means the question doesn't feel like a trap.

🪞 Not letting “fine” stand

Phrase 3

They've said the F word. You want to point out, lightly, that the word is being asked to cover an enormous amount. Put the pieces in order:

a lot of“Fine”there.is doingwork
“‘Fine’ is doing a lot of work there.”
If a word is “doing a lot of work”, it's being made to carry far more meaning than it can hold — one small word covering four days of something. It's dry and it's affectionate, and it says I heard you and I don't believe you without calling anyone a liar. Fair warning, though: it's a clever line, and clever can land as a dig, which is exactly what happens in the conversation you read.
Phrase 4

You need them to know that a difficult conversation isn't the thing you're afraid of — being shut out is. Which line says that?

A. “I'd honestly rather a row than the silence.”
B. “Fine. Don't tell me then.”
C. “Can you please just talk to me?”
A — “I'd honestly rather a row than the silence.”
A “row” is an argument, and the whole point of the line is that you're volunteering for one — you're telling them the worst outcome they're protecting you from is one you'd happily take. B is sulking dressed up as giving up, and C is a plea, which puts the pressure back on them. A takes the pressure off, because it removes the reason people go quiet in the first place.

🛡️ Making it safe to say

Phrase 5

You want to say that you'll take the awkwardness of the truth over the comfort of not knowing. Which line puts it that way round?

A. “You can tell me anything, you know that.”
B. “I'd rather you told me and it was awkward.”
C. “Just tell me — I can take it.”
B — “I'd rather you told me and it was awkward.”
A and C are both promises that it'll be fine, and the person going quiet knows perfectly well that it might not be, so the promise doesn't reach them. B is the only one that admits the conversation could go badly and chooses it anyway, which is much more believable — and being believed is the entire job of this phrase.
Phrase 6

The unasked question underneath all of this is whether you're the cause. Ask it straight, and give them permission to say yes. Complete it: “Have I done something? Just say if I ___.”

“Have I done something? Just say if I have.”
The short answer echoes the auxiliary from the question — have I done something, just say if I have — which is how English avoids repeating the whole verb. It also does something braver than it looks, because most people ask this hoping to be told no, and the second half is you saying you'd genuinely take a yes.
Phrase 7

They're braced for you to react badly, and that's what's keeping them quiet. Reassure them about right now — but honestly, without pretending you'll never mind at all, because they won't believe that. Say it out loud, in your own words, before you reveal.

“I'm not going to be cross. I might be, later, but not now.”
“Cross” is the mild, everyday British word for annoyed, and the strength of the line is the middle bit, because “I might be, later” is the part nobody says. A promise never to mind anything is worthless and both of you know it; a promise about tonight only is small enough to be true, so it gets believed.

⏳ Not rushing them

Phrase 8

They can't get near it because they think they have to explain it properly first. Take that requirement away. Which line does?

A. “Start at the beginning and take me through it.”
B. “Have a think and come back when you know what you want to say.”
C. “You don't have to have it all worked out to tell me.”
C — “You don't have to have it all worked out to tell me.”
To have something “worked out” is to have made sense of it and be able to explain it in order. A demands exactly that, and B sounds kind but quietly sets homework, so both leave them waiting until they're ready — which, with a feeling they don't understand yet, is never. C is the only one that lets them arrive with a mess.
Phrase 9

They've asked for time. Give it, and make clear that the offer doesn't expire. Complete the chunk: “Take your time. I'm not ___ ___.”

“Take your time. I'm not going anywhere.”
Literally it means you're staying in the room; underneath, it means you're staying, full stop — the patience is not a limited offer that runs out if they use too much of it. It's short, which matters, because a long reassuring speech here would be its own kind of pressure.

🚶 Knowing when to stop

Phrase 10

They're not going to say it tonight. Stop asking — but leave the door open so that stopping isn't the same as giving up. Complete it: “Alright — I'll leave it. But come and ___ ___, yeah?”

“Alright — I'll leave it. But come and find me, yeah?”
To “leave it” is to drop the subject, and saying it out loud is what makes it a gift rather than a sulk, because they can hear you choosing to stop. Then “come and find me” hands them the next move, and the “yeah?” on the end asks for a small yes, so you leave with something rather than nothing. Stopping before they have to push you away is what keeps you welcome next time.

🔑 Make them yours

These aren't only for the person you live with — they work with a friend who's gone quiet, a colleague who's clearly had a week, a teenager, a parent. Tie three of them to someone real, so you'll reach for them when it matters. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

“You've been off all evening. What's going on?” — who in your life has gone a bit quiet lately? Say what you've noticed, plainly, without diagnosing them.

“I'm not going to be cross. I might be, later, but not now.” — what's the reaction someone might be afraid of from you? Make them a promise small enough to be true.

“Alright — I'll leave it. But come and find me, yeah?” — think of a time you kept asking when you should have stopped. How would you close it now?

The Twist

😬 It's you

One more round, no script. You've done it all beautifully — the promise, the patience, the door left open — and it works. Alex takes a breath and says: “Alright. It's you. It's been you for about a month, and I didn't say anything because I knew you'd do exactly this — be lovely about it — and then I'd never get to be angry.” Every reassurance you made has just been cashed in at once. You promised you wouldn't be cross tonight, and now you'd quite like to be. Start the timer. Stay in the conversation you asked for: keep asking, keep listening, and get them to say the rest of it — because there's more, and it's worse. One rule: no defending yourself until they've finished. If your first move is “that's not fair”, you've lost it.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said what I'd noticed, without telling them what they were feeling.
  • I asked whether it was me — out loud — and I'd have taken a yes.
  • I made them a promise that was small enough to be believable.
  • I let a silence sit there instead of filling it or asking again straight away.
  • I stopped asking before they had to push me away, and left the door open.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

“I'm fine” is one of the most useful sentences we have, and almost none of its uses are honest. It's the thing you say to the colleague at the sink, to your mother on the phone, to the person you live with when the real answer would take an hour you haven't got.

Which leaves the other person with a small, awkward decision, over and over: believe it, or ask again. Ask too often and you're chasing someone round their own kitchen. Never ask twice and you can live beside a person for years and miss the whole thing.

There isn't a technique that gets the truth out of someone who isn't ready. There's only making it as easy as you can — no anger tonight, no need to explain it well, no deadline — and then stopping, and staying in the room. Sometimes that's the weekend's worth of waiting. Sometimes it's a lot longer.

Two bigger questions

  1. Where's the line between caring enough to ask again and refusing to let someone be private? Who gets to decide where it is — the asker or the one being asked?
  2. Some people are raised to say what's wrong the moment it's wrong; others are raised to sort it out alone and mention it afterwards, if ever. Which were you? And what happens when someone from one of those households ends up living with someone from the other?
