Dad's New Partner

Your dad is happier than he's been in years, and you're pleased for him, and there's still something sitting in your chest that you haven't said. Nobody's done anything wrong. That's rather the problem.

The Scene 🎬

Your mum died a year and a half ago. Since about March, there has been Marion — and Marion is, by any fair measure, lovely. She remembers things. She laughs at your dad's jokes, the terrible ones, the ones he's been telling since 1994 and which nobody had laughed at for a very long time. Your dad has started ironing his shirts again.

And you are pleased. You genuinely are. But there's a pair of her reading glasses on the arm of your mum's chair, and a second mug on the draining board, and every time you come round something small has quietly moved. None of it is Marion's fault. None of it is anybody's fault. You just feel a bit winded, and you've been smiling through it for four months, and your dad has started asking — twice now, lightly, not lightly at all — whether you like her.

So today you've come round early, while she's out at the shops, to say the thing you can't quite put into words. It isn't a complaint. There's nothing to fix. You're going to hand him a feeling that isn't even fair, about the person it's about, and somehow do it without asking him to give up a single thing that's making him happy.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Is there such a thing as a "right" amount of time before someone starts again after losing a partner — and who on earth gets to decide it? Has anyone ever decided it for you?
  2. Think of a feeling you've had that you knew, even while you were having it, wasn't fair on anybody. Did you ever say it out loud? What happened — or what stopped you?

Who's who

You
The One Who's Been Smiling 🙂

Pleased for him and thrown by it, both at once, and quite aware that only one of those is easy to say. You want to be honest without landing him with a problem, because there isn't one — there's just you, catching up.

The other voice
Dad 🍳

Widowed eighteen months, happier than you've seen him in years, and quietly braced for you to take it away from him. He keeps asking if you like her, which means he's already half decided you don't.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Nothing gets solved in it, and nothing is meant to. Watch how the awkward thing gets said: clumsily, on purpose; without blaming Marion; without asking him to change anything at all. That's the whole skill.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the moments where the speaker stops, restarts, and says it worse than they'd planned. Those are the moments doing the work.

DadOh — you're early. She's only nipped out for milk and that, she'll be back in ten minutes or so. Kettle's just boiled if you—
YouYeah. Yeah, in a bit. Dad — have you got five minutes, before she gets back?
Dad…Right. Nobody's ever said that to me and it's been good news. Go on. What have I done?
YouNothing. You haven't done anything, that's — look. I've been practising this in the car all the way over and it came out wrong about nine times, so I'm just going to — I'd rather say this badly than not say it at all. Alright?

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Dad guesses what's coming and gets it completely wrong. What does he assume, and how does the reply both correct him and make itself believable?

2. There's one moment where Dad gets defensive. What word sets him off — and what does the speaker do next that stops it turning into a row?

3. After all that, only one actual request gets made in the whole conversation. What is it, and why is it easy for Dad to say yes to?

1. He assumes it's Marion — that his child doesn't like her — and says so before he can be stopped. The reply denies it flatly and then, instead of leaving it as a polite denial, gives a small specific reason for liking her: she laughs at his jokes, which nobody had done for a long time. A detail that precise is much harder to disbelieve than a compliment, which is why he lets it go.
2. "Quicker." He hears it as a judgement on his timing and pushes back, because eighteen months felt like nine years from inside it. What rescues it is that the speaker agrees with him first — twice — and then moves the whole problem onto themselves, so there's nothing left for him to defend.
3. Not to be asked to call Marion anything yet. It's easy to agree to because it's tiny, it's temporary, it costs him nothing he actually wants, and it arrives attached to a promise that this won't be forever.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for saying the thing that isn't fair, to the person it's about, without asking them to change. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. This one doesn't want your best voice. Polished is the enemy here — if it sounds prepared, he'll hear a case being made against him and start defending himself. So let it be a bit rough: stop, start again, leave the sentence half finished, drop your volume rather than raising it. And go slower than feels comfortable. The silences are where he catches up with you.

🚪 Finding the moment

Phrase 1

You're in the hall, coat still on, and she'll be back from the shops before long. You need him sitting down and listening, without making him think somebody's died. Which opener does that?

A. "Dad, we need to talk about Marion."
B. "Have you got five minutes, before she gets back?"
C. "So — are we going to talk about all this, or not?"
B — "Have you got five minutes, before she gets back?"
A names her before he's even sat down, so he starts the conversation already defending her. C is a challenge rather than a question, and he'll hear that coming from the other end of the hall. B asks for a small, ordinary amount of time — five minutes is nothing, so it's easy to give — and the second half quietly explains the timing: you want to say this out of her hearing, which is a kindness to her as much as to him.
Phrase 2

You rehearsed this in the car and every version was worse than the last. Rather than pretend you've got it sorted, you tell him plainly that you're going to make a mess of it and you're saying it anyway. Put the pieces in order:

say itthan notI'd ratherat all.say this badly
"I'd rather say this badly than not say it at all."
"I'd rather X than Y" is how you show someone you've weighed two options and chosen the less bad one, which here means you've already thought about staying quiet and decided against it. Admitting up front that it's going to come out wrong buys you enormous room: every clumsy sentence afterwards is covered, so he listens for what you mean rather than judging how you said it.

💬 Saying the unfair thing

Phrase 3

He's jumped in and decided you don't like her. You need to knock that down instantly — and, more than that, you need him to actually believe you, because everything else you say depends on it. Which answer works?

A. "It's not that I don't like her."
B. "She seems lovely."
C. "I like her, actually. That's the honest answer."
C — "I like her, actually. That's the honest answer."
A is built out of two negatives, so what he hears is the door being left open a crack. B is what you say about somebody's new sofa — polite, weightless, and he'll hear the shrug in it. C says the good thing straight out, and then puts a label on it, because "that's the honest answer" tells him you're not just being nice to him. Since the whole conversation is going to ask him to trust you, you spend the first sentence being worth trusting.
Phrase 4

Now the actual difficulty — and it isn't her, it's the speed. Say it as the small thing it is, not the accusation it could become. Put the pieces in order:

that's all.a bit quickerIt's justthan I was expecting,
"It's just a bit quicker than I was expecting, that's all."
Look at how much of this phrase is busy making itself smaller: "just", "a bit", and then "that's all" at the end, which closes the subject before he can inflate it. And "than I was expecting" is the important move — it makes this a fact about your expectations rather than a verdict on his timing, so there's nothing there for him to argue with.

🛡️ Keeping her out of it

Phrase 5

He's heard "quicker" as a criticism and he's bristling. You have to take Marion out of this completely, and one clean denial won't do it — he's not in a mood to be convinced first time. Complete it. The gap is one word you have already used in the sentence, said again on its own:

"It's got nothing to do with her, honestly. ___."

"It's got nothing to do with her, honestly. Nothing."
Said once, it's a denial and he can hear it as politeness. Said twice — the second time alone, with a full stop either side of it — it stops being a sentence and becomes a door closing. This is a very ordinary trick in spoken English: repeat one word on its own when you need somebody to stop wondering. And it matters that the word is "nothing", because she has to leave this conversation completely innocent, or he'll spend the rest of it protecting her from you.
Phrase 6

So whose problem is it? You've admitted it isn't hers and it isn't the timing. Now say the real thing — the ordinary, slightly embarrassing thing that happens to you when you walk through the front door of that house — and then, in the same breath, say who it belongs to. Say it out loud in your own words first. What would you say?

e.g. "I keep half expecting Mum to walk in, and that's mine to deal with, not yours."
"Half expecting" is exactly right for a feeling you know is nonsense while you're having it — half of you knows, and half of you is still listening for the door. Then "that's mine to deal with, not yours" hands the problem back to yourself before he can pick it up, which is what stops the whole thing being a complaint. You've given him something true about you and asked him for absolutely nothing, and that combination is very hard to be defensive about.
Phrase 7

He's been watching your face for four months, waiting for you to tell him he's not entitled to any of this. Say the permission out loud — and make the second half sound like you mean it, not like you're reciting it. Complete the chunk by adding one word between "I" and "know", which presses down on the verb:

"You're allowed to be happy. I ___ know that."

"You're allowed to be happy. I do know that."
English lets you drop "do" in front of an ordinary verb purely to lean on it, and that lean carries a meaning of its own: it answers something the other person hasn't said out loud. "I know that" is information. "I do know that" says I can hear what you're worried about, and you're wrong to worry. He never accused you of forgetting he's allowed to be happy — he didn't have to. That's what the "do" is for.

🕰️ Asking for one small thing

Phrase 8

He keeps nudging you towards a word for her — a family word, a big one — and you're nowhere near it. Ask him to stop nudging, without making it sound like a refusal that lasts forever. Put the pieces in order:

call herjust notCould youanything yet?ask me to
"Could you just not ask me to call her anything yet?"
Every word here is working to keep this small. "Could you just" makes it a favour rather than a condition; the vague "anything" means you never have to say the word out loud, which spares you both; and "yet" is the one that saves it, because it turns a refusal into a delay. Notice too what you're asking him to stop doing — the asking, not the wanting. He can have the hope. You just can't have it offered to you every Sunday.
Phrase 9

You've just asked him to wait. Now tell him what he's waiting for, so the delay doesn't sound like a polite no. Which one does that?

A. "I'll get used to it eventually, I suppose."
B. "Give me a bit of time and I'll get there, I promise."
C. "I just need some space with all this."
B — "Give me a bit of time and I'll get there, I promise."
"I'll get there" treats the feeling as a journey you're already walking — not there yet, definitely going. A is resigned rather than willing, because "I suppose" tells him you're bracing yourself for something you don't want, which is worse than saying nothing. C asks him to stay away from you, which is the opposite of what you mean. B is the only one that names an ending, and a man who's been waiting four months for your face to change needs to hear that there's an ending.
Phrase 10

Last thing. He's started tidying his own happiness away where you can't see it — moving her glasses, going quiet when she rings. Tell him to stop, and make it about his life rather than about your permission. Which line does the most work?

A. "Don't feel like you have to hide her from me."
B. "You don't have to worry about me."
C. "I don't want you creeping round your own life because of me."
C — "I don't want you creeping round your own life because of me."
To "creep round" is to move about quietly hoping nobody notices you — which is a fine way to behave in somebody else's house at two in the morning, and a terrible way to live in your own. That's the whole picture, and it's the picture that does it: he'll recognise himself in it long before he'd accept the criticism plainly. A only covers Marion, and he's hiding more than Marion. B is so general that he can nod along and change absolutely nothing.

🔑 Make them yours

These aren't only for a dad and a Marion. They're for any time you have to hand someone a feeling that isn't fair — and want them to keep their life exactly as it is afterwards. Pin three of them to something real, so you'll actually reach for them when it comes. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I'd rather say this badly than not say it at all." — something you've been rehearsing and not saying. What is it, and who to?

"It's got nothing to do with her, honestly. Nothing." — a time someone took your difficulty personally when it wasn't about them at all. How would you have taken them out of it?

"Give me a bit of time and I'll get there, I promise." — something you're not ready for yet, but expect to be. Ask for the time.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your hall, your coat, your ten minutes before the front door goes. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer — this isn't a conversation you get to draft.

The Twist

😬 The time you asked for doesn't exist

One more round — no script. You've said your piece, he's taken it beautifully, and you're four minutes from a cup of tea and a nice afternoon. Then he says: her flat's gone, the lease is up in five weeks, and he's been meaning to ask her to move in here. Into this house. And he hasn't asked her yet, because — and he says this looking straight at you — he wanted to know what you thought first. Start the timer. The bit of time you just asked for has turned into five weeks, and he's handed you a veto you never wanted and can't give back. Say something. One rule: you don't get to say "whatever you want, Dad" and you don't get to say no.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said the good thing about her first, with a real reason attached — not just "she seems nice".
  • I never once made the difficulty her fault, and I said so more than once.
  • I named my own feeling plainly, and made it clear it was mine to carry.
  • I asked for one small, temporary thing — not a change to how he lives.
  • He came out of it still allowed to be happy, and knowing I meant it.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Most of the hard conversations we rehearse in the car are about something someone did wrong. This one isn't, and that's what makes it hard. There's no case to put, no apology to accept — just a feeling you didn't choose, about a person who has done nothing but be kind.

The temptation is to say nothing at all, on the grounds that it isn't fair. But unsaid feelings don't sit still. They come out as a flat voice on the phone, a Sunday you're suddenly busy, a face he keeps checking. He'd read all of that anyway. He was already reading it.

So you say it badly, and you say it early, and you take care to ask for almost nothing — because the point was never to change his life. The point was that he stops having to guess what you're thinking, and you stop having to hold your face still on a Sunday.

Two bigger questions

  1. Is honesty always a gift? Some feelings genuinely do fade if you sit on them — so how do you tell in advance which ones will pass quietly and which ones will leak out sideways for years? Have you ever got that judgement wrong in either direction?
  2. Grown children often behave as though a widowed parent's happiness needs their approval — and parents often behave as though it does too. Where does that come from, and is it love, or is it something a bit more possessive wearing love's coat?
