Warm-up — Show, Don't Tell

The single most common piece of advice given to new poets is: show, don't tell. It is also the most misunderstood. Today we unpack what it actually means.

Look at two ways of saying the same thing:

Telling — abstract
My grandmother was very sad in her last years. She felt useless and forgotten.
Showing — concrete
My grandmother sat at the window with a thimble on her finger, looking at it for hours.

Both lines mean roughly the same thing. But only the second one MAKES YOU SEE HER. The first one is a label on an experience; the second one IS the experience.

Why "showing" works

Because the reader's mind does the work. When you write "she was sad," the reader hears a word. When you write "she sat at the window with a thimble on her finger," the reader BUILDS THE IMAGE inside their head — and feels the sadness as their own conclusion. That conclusion lands harder than any label.

Concrete images give the reader something to do. Abstract statements ask them to passively accept what you've decided.

The three jobs of an image in poetry

  1. To compress. One concrete image can carry the weight of three abstract sentences.
  2. To carry meaning sideways. The image of the thimble doesn't just describe — it suggests her labour, her domestic life, her smallness. A good image always means more than it says.
  3. To anchor feeling. Emotions are slippery. A specific physical object pins the feeling to the world so the reader can feel it too.

Today's eight tools are the ways poets build images that do all three jobs at once.

Vocabulary — Eight Image Tools

Tap each card. Three you've met before (metaphor, simile, image — recap). Five are new and take you further.

Concrete image
Tap for definition
A specific physical thing — something you could touch, see, smell. The opposite of an abstract feeling.
e.g. "a tin of pins" (concrete) vs "her loneliness" (abstract).
Sensory imagery
Tap for definition
Language that appeals to the five senses — sight, sound, touch, smell, taste. Strong poems use more than just sight.
e.g. "the smell of bread" (smell), "the lid felt cold" (touch).
Metaphor
Tap for definition
Saying one thing IS another (recap from Lesson 1). The strongest direct comparison.
e.g. "The box was a small museum."
Simile
Tap for definition
A comparison using "like" or "as" (recap from Lesson 1). Softer than a metaphor.
e.g. "a thimble like a tiny crown."
Extended metaphor
Tap for definition
A single metaphor sustained across several lines, a stanza, or the whole poem. The strongest poems usually have one.
e.g. the sewing-box-as-museum running through today's reading.
Personification
Tap for definition
Giving human qualities to a non-human thing — an object, an idea, a feeling, an animal.
e.g. "Inside the box LIVED everything she had not done."
Symbol
Tap for definition
An object in the poem that stands for something larger than itself.
e.g. the sewing box symbolises a life of unfulfilled possibility.
Conceit
Tap for definition
An especially elaborate or surprising extended metaphor — one that takes a small idea and pushes it to an unexpected place.
e.g. "half-finished sentences in cotton and silk" — unfinished embroidery as unfinished thoughts.

Reading — "My Mother's Sewing Box"

Read aloud, twice. The whole poem is built on one central image — a sewing box — and then keeps finding new metaphors for that one object.

My Mother's Sewing Box
The box was a small museum,
shelves of buttons in glass jars,
a tin of pins like silver rain.
She lifted the lid like opening a country.
The ribbons spilled out, the colours
of countries she had not been to:
emerald India, gold Morocco, red Spain.
Inside lived everything she had not done.
Inside lived everything she had not been.
She would lift a thimble like a tiny crown
and place it on her finger
and look at it for a long time.
I think the box was a window
no one had bothered to open.
I think the box was a small museum
where all the visitors were ghosts.
When she died we found
threads still threaded through her needles —
half-finished sentences in cotton and silk,
each one ending in a knot, then nothing.

What's in this poem?

The eight image tools — where they appear
  • Concrete images — buttons, pins, ribbons, a thimble, threads, needles. Things you can see and touch.
  • Sensory imagery — sight ("emerald India, gold Morocco, red Spain"), touch (the thimble on her finger), implicit smell of old fabric.
  • Metaphor — "The box was a small museum", "the box was a window", "half-finished sentences in cotton and silk." The box IS each of these things — no "like" or "as".
  • Simile — "a tin of pins like silver rain", "like opening a country", "a thimble like a tiny crown". Each uses "like" to compare.
  • Extended metaphor — the box-as-museum runs through the whole poem. It appears in stanza 1 and returns in stanza 4 ("a small museum where all the visitors were ghosts"). That return is what makes it EXTENDED.
  • Personification — "Inside LIVED everything she had not done." Things can't live — that's a human verb. The poem gives the box's contents a life of their own.
  • Symbol — the box stands for the mother's life of unfulfilled possibility. We never have to say "she was sad and unfulfilled" because the box says it for us.
  • Conceit — the closing image: unfinished embroidery as unfinished sentences. A small visual detail (threads in needles) pushed into a bigger idea (her unfinished thoughts, unfinished life).

Discussion

  • Why a sewing box? What would change if the poem was about her wardrobe, or her desk, or her shoes?
  • The poet calls the contents "everything she had not done" and "everything she had not been". Why phrase it as what she didn't do, instead of what she did?
  • What does the final image ("half-finished sentences in cotton and silk") suggest about her death?
  • The poem never says the words "sad", "lonely", or "regret". How does it make us feel those things anyway?

Stretch — name the device

"Half-finished sentences in cotton and silk, each one ending in a knot, then nothing." — what device is THIS?

This is a conceit — a particularly inventive extended metaphor. The poet takes a small visual detail (unfinished threads in needles) and stretches it into a big idea (her unfinished thoughts, unfinished life). It's a metaphor that earns its existence by surprising you.

Conceits are not just clever — they're the moments when a poem lifts above its starting material. Many famous poems are built on one conceit.

Your Turn — Write an Extended Metaphor

Pick an OBJECT that belonged to a person you knew well. A tool. A bag. A book. A cup. A piece of clothing. Anything specific.

The trick: today you don't write about the PERSON. You write about the OBJECT — and let the object carry everything you'd want to say about the person.

Writing timer
12 minutes.
12:00
Stanza 1 — Open with a metaphor for the object
"The [object] was a [unexpected larger thing]" — and then fill in 2–3 concrete details about what it looked or felt like.
Stanza 2 — Sensory imagery + personification
Use at least TWO senses (sight, sound, touch, smell). And give the object — or something inside it — a HUMAN verb (live, breathe, wait, remember, forget).
Stanza 3 — Return to the metaphor (extend it)
Echo the opening metaphor in a new way. Use the phrase "I think the [object] was…" twice. Try TWO different new metaphors for the same object.
Stanza 4 — Closing conceit
Pick ONE small physical detail about the object and stretch it into a bigger idea. The detail should be specific. The bigger idea should land hard.

Read it aloud. The object should now carry the weight of a person.

Free Write — One Central Image

You've used a scaffold built on an extended metaphor. Now drop the scaffold but keep the principle: one CENTRAL image that holds the whole poem.

Your prompt
Write a 4–6 stanza free-verse poem about a TIME IN YOUR LIFE — not a person, not a place, but a period (early childhood, a year you remember vividly, a season of change). Build the whole poem around ONE central image that comes back at least twice.

Try to use: at least one metaphor, at least one simile, at least one piece of personification, and at least three concrete sensory details.
Free-write timer
15 minutes.
15:00

After you've written

  1. Underline your central image every time it appears. If it only appears once, it's not yet extended — push it.
  2. Cross out any line where you TELL the reader how to feel ("it was sad", "I loved it"). Replace with a SHOWING detail.
  3. What's your final image? The last image in a poem stays with the reader longest. Make it earn that place.
