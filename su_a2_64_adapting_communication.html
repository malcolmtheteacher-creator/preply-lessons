Nicky and Alex's Meeting

Nicky is a Senior Accounting Manager from a direct communication culture. Alex is a Senior Accountant from an indirect communication culture. Read their conversation. What goes wrong?

Nicky:
Give me the Icarus project update. Where are we on schedule?
Alex:
Well... as you know... we do have a deadline approaching. The team has been quite busy, and, um... maybe they need more time.
Nicky:
That's not clear. Is it on schedule or not? Yes or no?
Alex:
I see... well, we have some challenges. It's complicated. The situation is... evolving.
Nicky:
No, that is not possible. Get it done this week, please.
What went wrong? Click to reveal analysis
The Communication Clash:

Nicky uses direct communication—quick, clear questions expecting yes/no answers. Nicky values efficiency and straight answers.

Alex uses indirect communication—hints, context, softening language. Alex is trying to be respectful and build understanding, not just deliver bad news.

The result: Nicky thinks Alex is avoiding the truth or is unclear. Alex thinks Nicky is rude and not listening to the context. Both feel frustrated.

Why This Matters

Both Nicky and Alex are doing their job well. They just have different cultural communication styles. Understanding this difference helps you communicate better in international workplaces.

Adapting Your Communication Style

Good communicators can switch styles depending on who they are talking to. Let's practice.

Exercise 1: Direct vs. Indirect Approaches

For each situation, what's the best way to communicate? Think about whether being direct or indirect works better.

Situation 1: Your project is late.

Your boss wants an update on a project that won't be finished on time. What do you say?

Direct: "The project will be two weeks late because my team had other urgent work to do."
Indirect: "We have been very busy with the other request. The situation is... complicated right now."
Which is better? Click to see

It depends on your boss's style. If your boss is direct (like Nicky), use the direct answer. Your boss wants clear information and solutions. If your boss is indirect (like Alex), you can use more softening language, but include the key facts. The best answer includes honest information plus respect for the person you are talking to.

Situation 2: You disagree with a decision.

Your manager made a decision you think is wrong. How do you respond?

Direct: "That decision is not good. Here is a better approach."
Indirect: "That's interesting. I was wondering if we could also think about another approach? Maybe it could work well too."
Which is better? Click to see

Again, it depends. Some managers want direct feedback. Others need to hear ideas gently so they don't feel attacked. A skilled communicator can adapt: "I understand your thinking. I wonder if we could also consider X because it might help with Y." This is respectfully direct—you are clear about your idea, but you also show respect.

Exercise 2: Time Orientation

Different cultures have different relationships with time. Let's explore two approaches:

Fixed Time Orientation

⏰ Time is money. Schedules matter. "The meeting is at 2pm, not 2:10pm."

Common in Northern Europe, North America, East Asia

Flexible Time Orientation

🤝 Relationships matter more than the clock. "We will finish when we are done."

Common in Latin America, Middle East, Africa

Read this text message conversation between Alexis and Sascha about planning a team lunch:

Alexis (fixed time):
Let's meet at 12:30 pm on Friday at Mario's restaurant. Can you confirm?
Sascha (flexible time):
Friday sounds good! I'll let you know closer to the time what time works. Maybe 12 or 1? We'll see how the morning goes.
Alexis:
But I need to book the table. What time exactly?
Sascha:
Don't worry! We can figure it out. It will be fun no matter what time. Flexibility is good, right?
What's the misunderstanding? Click to analyze

Different time orientations: Alexis needs a fixed time to make a restaurant reservation. Sascha thinks planning too far ahead with exact times is too rigid. Sascha wants to stay flexible. Neither person is wrong—they just have different cultural values about time.

Better approach: Alexis could explain the restaurant needs a reservation. Sascha could agree to a specific time to help Alexis plan. When you work with different time orientations, communication and respect go both ways.

Key Takeaway

The best communicators are not just direct or indirect, fixed or flexible. They adapt to the person they are talking to. Understanding different communication styles helps you work better with people from different cultures.
