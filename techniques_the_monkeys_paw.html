The Monkey's Paw

W. W. Jacobs (1902) — a dried little talisman grants three wishes, and charges a price for every one of them

Read in three parts

On a wet, windy night, a soldier home from India brings the White family a strange souvenir: a little mummified paw that an old fakir charged with a spell, so that three separate men might each have three wishes from it. The whole story is a warning about getting what you ask for. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The horror is not in what you see — it is in what the door never quite opens to reveal.

Part 1 — The visitor and the paw

Without, the night was cold and wet, but in the small parlour of Laburnam Villa the blinds were drawn and the fire burned brightly. Father and son were at chess; the former, who possessed ideas about the game involving radical changes, putting his king into such sharp and unnecessary perils that it even provoked comment from the white-haired old lady knitting placidly by the fire.

There was a sudden and unlooked-for visitor, Sergeant-Major Morris, a tall, burly man, beady of eye and rubicund of visage. He had been twenty-one years in the army. As the visitor finished his glass and set it down, he sighed, and the old man took it up and refilled it.

"What was that you started telling me the other day about a monkey's paw or something, Morris?"

"Nothing," said the soldier hastily. "Leastways nothing worth hearing."

Morris took the little paw from his pocket. It was nothing but a little paw, dried to a mummy. He held it out, and Mr. White took it.

"It had a spell put on it by an old fakir," said the sergeant-major, "a very holy man. He wanted to show that fate ruled people's lives, and that those who interfered with it did so to their sorrow. He put a spell on it so that three separate men could each have three wishes from it."

"If you can have your three wishes," said the old man, eyeing him keenly, "why do you keep it?"

The soldier regarded him in the way that middle age is wont to regard presumptuous youth. "I have," he said quietly, and his blotchy face whitened.

"And did you really have the three wishes granted?" asked Mrs. White.

"I did," said the sergeant-major, and his glass tapped against his strong teeth.

"And has anybody else wished?" persisted the old lady.

"The first man had his three wishes. Yes," was the reply; "I don't know what the first two were, but the third was for death. That's how I got the paw."

Suddenly he flung it upon the fire. White, with a slight cry, stooped down and snatched it off.

"Better let it burn," said the soldier solemnly.

"If you don't want it, Morris," said the other, "give it to me."

"I won't," said his friend doggedly. "I threw it on the fire. If you keep it, don't blame me for what happens. Pitch it on the fire again, like a sensible man."

The other shook his head and examined his new possession closely.

Pause and discuss:
  • The fakir's whole purpose was "to show that fate ruled people's lives, and that those who interfered with it did so to their sorrow." Is the paw a gift, a trap, or a test? Defend your reading.
  • Morris has had his wishes, kept the paw, and now begs the Whites not to take it — yet he hands it over. Why doesn't he simply destroy it himself?
  • Mr. White snatches the paw back off the fire. What does that small, instinctive movement already tell you about human nature — and about how this story will end?

Part 2 — The first wish and its cost

After their visitor had departed, the family discussed the paw. Mr. White, half-ashamed, half-curious, held it up.

"If you only cleared the house, you'd be quite happy, wouldn't you?" said Herbert. "Well, wish for two hundred pounds, then; that'll just do it."

His father, smiling shamefacedly at his own credulity, held up the talisman, as his son, with a solemn face, sat down at the piano and struck a few impressive chords.

"I wish for two hundred pounds," said the old man distinctly.

A fine crash from the piano greeted the words, interrupted by a shuddering cry from the old man. His wife and son ran toward him.

"It moved," he cried, with a glance of disgust at the object as it lay on the floor. "As I wished, it twisted in my hand like a snake."

They sat for a while, but nothing happened, and they went to bed with no harm done. In the brightness of the wintry sun next morning over the breakfast table the old man laughed at his fears. There was an air of prosaic wholesomeness about the room which it had lacked the night before.

But Herbert went off to his work, and in the afternoon Mrs. White noticed a man standing outside, a well-dressed stranger who at last opened the gate. She brought him in. He gazed at her furtively, and seemed ill at ease.

"I was asked to call," he said at last, and stooped and picked a piece of cotton from his trousers. "I come from Maw and Meggins."

The old lady started. "Is anything the matter?" she asked breathlessly. "Has anything happened to Herbert? What is it?"

"I'm sorry —" began the visitor.

"Is he hurt?" demanded the mother wildly.

The visitor bowed in assent. "Badly hurt," he said quietly, "but he is not in any pain."

"Oh, thank God!" said the old woman, clasping her hands. "Thank God for that! Thank —"

She broke off suddenly as the sinister meaning of the assurance dawned upon her and she saw the awful confirmation of her fears in the other's averted face.

"He was caught in the machinery," said the visitor at length, in a low voice.

"Caught in the machinery," repeated Mr. White, in a dazed fashion, "yes."

The visitor coughed, and rising, walked slowly to the window. "I was to say that Maw and Meggins disclaim all responsibility," he continued. "They admit no liability at all, but in consideration of your son's services they wish to present you with a certain sum as compensation."

"How much?" asked the old man.

"Two hundred pounds," was the answer.

Unconscious of his wife's shriek, the old man smiled faintly, put out his hands like a sightless man, and dropped, a senseless heap, to the floor.

Pause and discuss:
  • The wish is granted — to the exact pound — but as compensation for Herbert's death. Why does Jacobs make the sum match so precisely? What is the effect of that arithmetic?
  • The visitor's careful corporate language — "disclaim all responsibility," "admit no liability," "in consideration of your son's services" — clashes with a mother's terror. What is Jacobs doing by contrasting the two voices?
  • "As I wished, it twisted in my hand like a snake." Where, in this part, do you find earlier signs that the wish would arrive in a way the family never intended?

Part 3 — The night, the knocking and the third wish

The old people buried their dead, and came back to a house steeped in shadow and silence. It was a week after that the old woman woke suddenly in the night and reached out a hand for her husband, who was not in bed.

"The paw!" she cried wildly. "The monkey's paw!"

He started up in alarm. "Where? Where is it? What's the matter?"

"I want it," she said quietly. "You've not destroyed it?"

"It's in the parlour, on the bracket," he replied, marvelling. "Why?"

"I only just thought of it," she said hysterically. "Why didn't I think of it before? Why didn't you think of it?"

"Think of what?" he questioned.

"The other two wishes," she replied rapidly. "We've only had one. Go and get it quickly, and wish our boy alive again."

The man sat up in bed and flung the bedclothes from his quaking limbs. "Good God, you are mad!" he cried, aghast. "He has been dead ten days, and besides he — I would not tell you else, but — I could only recognize him by his clothing. If he was too terrible for you to see then, how now?"

"Bring him back," cried the old woman, and dragged him towards the door. "Do you think I fear the child I have nursed?"

He went down in the darkness, and felt his way to the parlour, and then to the mantelpiece. The talisman was in its place. He raised his hand. "I wish my son alive again," he cried, and trembling, threw it from him.

He sat staring at the window until a cold wind made him shiver. The candle-end, which had burnt below the rim of the china candlestick, was throwing pulsating shadows on the ceiling and walls, until, with a flicker larger than the rest, it expired. The old man, with an unspeakable sense of relief at the failure of the talisman, crept back to his bed.

A minute or two afterwards a knock came so quiet and stealthy as to be scarcely audible. He held his breath. The knock was repeated. He jumped out of bed.

"What's that?" cried the old woman, starting up.

"A rat," said the old man, in shaking tones — "a rat. It passed me on the stairs."

His wife sat up in bed listening. A loud knock resounded through the house.

"It's Herbert!" she screamed. "It's Herbert!"

She ran to the door, but her husband was before her, and catching her by the arm, held her tightly.

"What are you going to do?" he whispered hoarsely.

"It's my boy; it's Herbert!" she cried, struggling mechanically. "I forgot it was two miles away. Let go. I must open the door."

"For God's sake don't let it in," cried the old man, trembling.

"You're afraid of your own son," she cried, struggling. "Let me go. I'm coming, Herbert; I'm coming."

There was another knock, and another. The old woman with a sudden wrench broke free and ran from the room. Her husband followed to the landing, and called after her appealingly as she hurried downstairs. He heard the chain rattle back and the bottom bolt drawn slowly and stiffly from the socket. Then the old woman's voice, strained and panting.

"The bolt," she cried loudly. "Come down. I can't reach it."

But her husband was on his hands and knees groping wildly on the floor in search of the paw. If he could only find it before the thing outside got in. A perfect fusillade of knocks reverberated through the house, and he heard the scraping of a chair as his wife put it down in the passage against the door. He heard the creaking of the bolt as it came slowly back, and at the same moment he found the monkey's paw, and frantically breathed his third and last wish.

The knocking ceased suddenly, although the echoes of it were still in the house. He heard the chair drawn back, and the door opened. A cold wind rushed up the staircase, and a long loud wail of disappointment and misery from his wife gave him courage to run down to her side, and then to the gate beyond. The street lamp flickering opposite shone on a quiet and deserted road.

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • We never see what was at the door. Jacobs ends on "a quiet and deserted road." Would the story be more frightening, or less, if he had described what stood outside? Why?
  • The father uses the third wish to send away the very thing his wife begged back to life. Was that an act of love, of cowardice, of mercy — or all three at once?
  • The mother's "long loud wail of disappointment" is the last sound in the story. She gets nothing; she is even denied her grief's small hope. Why does Jacobs leave her, and us, with that?
  • Your turn: "Be careful what you wish for." Have you ever got exactly the thing you asked for — and found the cost hidden inside it? Tell that story.

Grammar — Modal Perfects: should have / needn't have / might have

This is a story made of looking back. After Herbert's death, every reader thinks the same thing: they should never have wished. That impulse — to judge a past action, to regret it, or to guess at what really happened — is exactly what modal perfects are for. The pattern is always modal + have + past participle, and it lets us talk about the past we can no longer change.

1. Regret & criticism — should have / shouldn't have / ought to have

should have / shouldn't have + past participle says a past action was a mistake, or that something good was not done: "They should never have wished on the paw." / "Mr. White shouldn't have snatched it off the fire." ought to have + past participle is a slightly more formal twin of should have: "He ought to have listened to Morris." 2. Wishing on the paw was a terrible mistake. They at all. (regret — negative)

What Jacobs does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are the reason a story from 1902 can still raise the hairs on a reader's neck — and why so many horror films since have borrowed its shape.

1. Foreshadowing — the warnings planted from the start

The disaster is announced long before it lands. The fakir designed the paw to prove "those who interfered with fate did so to their sorrow"; Morris says flatly "Better let it burn," "don't blame me for what happens." Jacobs foreshadows and plants these warnings so that, on a second reading, the whole tragedy is already visible in Part 1. The pleasure — and the dread — is that the characters cannot hear what we can.

2. The rule of three — three wishes, rising dread

Three wishes; three knocks that build; three members of the family. The number three gives a folk tale its shape, and Jacobs structures the whole story around it. Each wish escalates the horror: a trivial sum of money, then a death, then a thing at the door. The pattern conditions us to expect a third beat — and then weaponises that expectation, because the third wish does not fix anything; it only sends the horror back into the dark.

3. The unseen, offstage horror — the terror you are never shown

We never see Herbert's mangled body ("I could only recognize him by his clothing"). We never see what knocks at the door. We never learn what stood on the road. Jacobs withholds the monster and so makes the reader's own imagination do the frightening. By keeping the horror offstage, he implies something worse than any description could deliver. The empty road at the end is the proof: nothing is shown, and it is unbearable.

4. Suspense and pacing — the knocking and the search for the paw

In the final pages Jacobs slows time to a crawl. The candle gutters; a quiet knock, then a louder one, then "a perfect fusillade"; the bolt draws back "slowly and stiffly"; the husband gropes on his hands and knees for the paw. He intercuts the wife rushing to open the door with the husband racing to make the wish, and stretches the seconds so that we feel every one. The pacing tightens the screw until it snaps — and the wish lands one heartbeat before the door does.

Task A · Retell the story in your own words (3 minutes)

Before we discuss it, tell the story back to me as if I had never read it — in your own words, not line by line. Aim for about eight to ten sentences.

As you go, use at least five words from the Vocabulary tab and, when you look back on what the family did, use modal perfects from the Grammar tab ("They should never have wished…", "He must have suffered…"). These beats will help you: Sergeant-Major Morris brings a cursed talisman and warns the Whites to let it burn · they wish for two hundred pounds · the money arrives as compensation for their son Herbert's death in the machinery · the grieving mother makes the father wish the boy alive again · something knocks at the door, and the father uses the last wish before they open it onto an empty road.

Task B · Regret and "if only" (4 minutes)

The whole story is a machine for regret. Tell me about a real decision — yours or one you watched closely — that, looking back, was clearly the wrong move. Take it in three steps:

  1. what was decided, and what seemed sensible about it at the time;
  2. what actually went wrong as a result;
  3. what, with hindsight, should have been done instead.

Build it on modal perfects of regret and criticism ("We should have…", "I shouldn't have…", "They ought to have…") and at least one of unnecessary action ("I needn't have worried so much, because…").

Task C · Tempting fate (3 minutes)

"Be careful what you wish for." Pick a moment when you, or someone you know, got exactly the thing you wanted — the job, the move, the relationship, the win — only to find a hidden price inside it.

Talk me through what you wished for, how it arrived, and the cost that came folded into the gift. Was the price worth paying? Use at least two effect verbs from the Technique tab and one past-speculation modal ("Looking back, it must have been…", "I might have known that…").

Task D · A decision you'd take back (4 minutes)

The father's last wish is, in effect, an undoing — he takes something back. Imagine you were granted one wish to reverse a single past choice in your own life or your country's recent history. Describe the choice, then make the wish out loud.

Now think like Jacobs: what would be the hidden cost of getting your undoing granted? What might go wrong in a way you never intended? Speculate with could / might / must / can't have ("It might have fixed X, but it could have…") and judge the original choice with should have / ought to have.

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The fakir's lesson was that fate cannot be cheated and "those who interfered with it did so to their sorrow." Do you believe some doors are better left closed — or is that just a comfortable excuse not to act?
  2. We never see what was at the door. Why do you think the unseen frightens us more than the thing we are shown? Where else — in films, in life — have you felt that?
  3. The father makes the third wish to spare his wife a horror she begged for. Was he right to overrule her grief? Who should decide such a thing?
  4. If the paw were real and offered you three wishes, knowing each one is granted with a cruel twist, would you ever use it — and what single wish would you risk?
