Encounter: Religious Attitudes to Contraception

Christianity

Roman Catholic: Artificial contraception is forbidden.

"The Pope's 1968 encyclical Humanae Vitae (Paul VI) reaffirmed the Church's teaching that contraception is immoral because it severs the 'unitive and procreative' aspects of sexual intercourse."

Natural Family Planning: Catholics are encouraged to use methods like the rhythm method (tracking fertility cycles) to space pregnancies naturally.

Protestant churches: Generally accept contraception within marriage as a responsible choice.

Islam

Contraception is generally permitted if both husband and wife agree, based on the principle of maslaha (preventing harm and promoting wellbeing).

The Qur'an does not explicitly forbid contraception. Many Islamic scholars support family planning as compatible with Islam, especially when it protects a mother's health or prevents family poverty.

Some scholars prefer natural methods (withdrawal, fertility awareness) over medical ones, but modern practice varies widely.

Judaism

Judaism generally accepts contraception, especially for health reasons.

The Pronatalist Tradition: "Be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28) expresses the Jewish value on children and family. However, modern Jewish practice is flexible — contraception is used to protect women's health and allow couples to plan families.

Different streams have different rules: Orthodox Judaism has more restrictions, while Reform Judaism emphasises personal choice.

Humanism

Contraception is a personal choice and a human right.

  • Access to contraception is essential for women's freedom, education, and economic independence
  • Family planning reduces poverty and improves health outcomes
  • Decisions about reproduction should be made by individuals, not governments or religious authorities

The Global Impact of Contraception Access

Where contraception is available, we see:

  • Higher rates of girls' education
  • Women entering the workforce
  • Lower maternal mortality rates
  • Reduced child poverty
  • Slower population growth in developing countries
