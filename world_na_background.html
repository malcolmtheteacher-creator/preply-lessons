North America: Two Neighbours, One Continent

To many outsiders, "North America" just means "America". In reality it is a continent where the USA and Canada share a long border, a lot of culture — and some strong differences.

The stereotype: "Americans and Canadians are basically the same people." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A continent and two neighbours

Geographically, North America also includes Mexico, Central America and the Caribbean, but in everyday English the phrase usually means the USA and Canada. The two share a land border of about 8,900 kilometres, often called the longest international border in the world. Canada, which is the second-largest country on Earth by area, has only about 41 million people — roughly one-eighth of the US population of around 340 million.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How long is the US–Canada border?

2. How does Canada's population compare with the USA's?

1. About 8,900 km.

2. It is about one-eighth.

Discuss:
  • How does a small neighbour live next to a giant?

Part 2 — Languages and history

The USA became independent from Britain in 1776, after a revolution. Canada, by contrast, separated gradually and peacefully, and the British monarch is still its head of state. Canada has two official languages, English and French; about one in five Canadians speaks French as a first language, most of them in Quebec. The USA had no official language at federal level for most of its history, and tens of millions of Americans speak Spanish at home.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Canada become independent?

2. What are Canada's official languages?

1. Gradually and peacefully.

2. English and French.

Discuss:
  • How does a revolution shape a country's identity?

Part 3 — So close, so different

Americans and Canadians watch many of the same films, eat in the same chains and follow many of the same sports. Yet surveys regularly show Canadians defining themselves partly by what they are not: American. Canadians often point to public health care, a less individualistic political culture and stricter gun laws, while Americans often stress personal freedom and self-reliance. Both are generalisations — opinions vary a lot inside each country.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What do the two countries share?

2. How do Canadians often define themselves?

1. Films, chains, sports (any).

2. Partly as "not American".

Discuss:
  • Is it fair to define yourself by what you are not?

Grammar — Relative Clauses

To pack a lot of information into one sentence — what a place is, who lives there — we use relative clauses.

How it works:
• who for people, which for things, where for places, whose for possession.
• Non-defining clauses add extra information with commas: "Brussels, which hosts the EU, is multilingual."
• Defining clauses identify which one — no commas.

Going deeper

From far away, the USA and Canada look almost identical, and American media dominate the region, so outsiders often merge them into one. Canadians, living next to a country nearly ten times their size, are especially sensitive to small differences — which is why calling a Canadian "American" is one of the quickest ways to cause offence.

Worth noticing: Use "whose" for possession (a country whose head of state…), and "where/when" for places and times.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Ask where people are from — and never assume. If you're unsure whether someone is American or Canadian, ask "Where are you from?" instead of guessing. Learn a few differences (head of state, health care, French in Quebec) — people notice and appreciate it.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Why do outsiders mix the two up?
  • Can a small country keep its culture next to a giant?
  • Which neighbour of your country do you most resemble?
