To Build a Fire

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Nothing on the trail had changed that you could point to, yet an intangible sense of menace hung over the silent forest — felt rather than seen.
intangible (adj) — not able to be touched or clearly defined; felt rather than seen.
2. A grey pall of freezing fog lay over the valley all day, so heavy that even noon felt like dusk.
a pall (n) — a dark covering or gloomy atmosphere that spreads over everything.
3. Alone on the ice, days from any help, the traveller finally understood his own frailty — how quickly a strong body fails when the air itself is against it.
frailty (n) — weakness; the state of being easily damaged or broken.
4. Off the packed path the snow was chest-deep, and within three steps he was floundering — arms swinging, boots finding nothing solid to stand on.
to flounder (v) — to struggle clumsily to move or stay upright; to struggle helplessly.
5. The lake looked solid enough to cross, but the ice near the reeds was treacherous — thin, hidden, and quietly waiting.
treacherous (adj) — dangerous because the danger is hidden; not to be trusted.
6. Every gust sent a faint agitation through the pines — a trembling almost too small to see — and shook loose little showers of powder snow.
agitation (n) — a small disturbance or shaking; also, a state of anxious excitement.
7. The temperature fell by imperceptible degrees; no single hour felt colder than the last, yet by evening the air burned any skin it touched.
imperceptible (adj) — so small or gradual that it cannot be noticed.
8. The overloaded sled struck a hidden ridge and capsized, tipping a whole week's supplies out into the snow.
to capsize (v) — to turn over or tip out its load (a boat, a sled — or a snow-laden branch).
9. The silence of the North was oppressive — it pressed down on the camp like a weight that none of them could lift.
oppressive (adj) — weighing heavily on the mind or body; hard to bear.
10. Warm at last beside the stove, the old dog drowsed, sliding gently in and out of a comfortable sleep.
to drowse (v) — to be half asleep; to drift towards sleep.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The man who was not afraid

Day had broken cold and grey, exceedingly cold and grey, when the man turned aside from the main Yukon trail and climbed the high earth-bank. It was a clear day, and yet there seemed an intangible pall over the face of things, a subtle gloom that made the day dark. This fact did not worry the man. He was without imagination. He was quick and alert in the things of life, but only in the things, and not in the significances.

Fifty degrees below zero meant eighty-odd degrees of frost. Such fact impressed him as being cold and uncomfortable, and that was all. It did not lead him to meditate upon his frailty as a creature of temperature, and upon man's frailty in general. Fifty degrees below zero was to him just precisely fifty degrees below zero. That there should be anything more to it than that was a thought that never entered his head. And yet his spit had crackled in the air before it could reach the snow — undoubtedly it was colder than fifty below. In fact, it was seventy-five below zero.

At the man's heels trotted a dog, a big native husky. The animal was depressed by the tremendous cold. It knew that it was no time for travelling. Its instinct told it a truer tale than was told to the man by the man's judgment. The dog did not know anything about thermometers. Possibly in its brain there was no sharp consciousness of a condition of very cold. But the brute had its instinct.

The old-timer on Sulphur Creek had been very serious in laying down the law that no man must travel alone in that country after fifty below. Well, here he was. He had had the accident of no company; he was alone; and he was getting along fine. Those old-timers were rather womanish, some of them, he thought. All a man had to do was to keep his head, and he was all right.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does London tell us the man lacks — and why does it matter more than the cold?

2. What was the old-timer's rule, and what does the man privately think of it?

1. He is "without imagination." He can register the fact of seventy-five below, but not what it means for a fragile human body — so he cannot fear what he cannot picture.

2. Never travel alone in that country after fifty below. The man scoffs at it — he thinks such caution "womanish" and that keeping his head is all a man needs.

Discuss:
  • The dog and the man walk the same trail. What does each of them "know" — and whose knowledge is worth more out here?

Part 2 — The wet feet

He was following a little-travelled trail, and the creeks he knew were frozen solid. But under the snow were hidden pools of water, treacherous springs that never quite froze. To break through meant wet feet, and wet feet at fifty below was no laughing matter. And then it happened. At a place where there were no signs, where the soft, unbroken snow seemed to advertise solidity beneath, the man broke through. It was not deep. He wet himself halfway to the knees before he floundered out to the firm crust.

He was angry, and cursed his luck aloud. He had hoped to get into camp with the boys by six o'clock, and this would delay him an hour, for he would have to build a fire and dry out his foot-gear. He knew there must be no failure. He remembered the advice of the old-timer on Sulphur Creek, and smiled. The old-timer had been very serious in laying down the law: after fifty below, a man should travel with a partner. Well, here he was; he had had the accident; he was alone; and he had saved himself.

He gathered dry twigs and grasses, and dead branches, and built his fire under a spruce tree. Working carefully from a small beginning, he soon had a roaring fire, and he sat on the snow and began to thaw the ice from his moccasins. The blood of his body was like the blood of the dog, and, like the dog, he wanted to bury himself in the snow. But he had a fire, and the fire was climbing higher, and he was safe.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What hidden danger does the trail hold, and what happens to the man?

2. How does he react to the accident — and to the memory of the old-timer's advice?

1. Under the snow lie treacherous springs that never quite freeze. He breaks through where the snow looked solid and soaks himself halfway to the knees — a mortal danger at that temperature.

2. He is merely angry — he treats it as an hour's delay. And he smiles at the old-timer's warning, because he believes he has just proved he can save himself alone.

Discuss:
  • He remembers the warning — and smiles. Why is "I saved myself alone" exactly the wrong lesson to take from this accident?

Part 3 — The tree

It was his own fault, or rather his mistake. He should not have built the fire under the spruce tree; he should have built it in the open. But it had been easier to pull the twigs from the brush and drop them directly on the fire. The tree carried a weight of snow on its boughs, and each time he had pulled a twig he had communicated a slight agitation to the tree — imperceptible to him, but sufficient to bring about the disaster.

High up in the tree one bough capsized its load of snow. This fell on the boughs beneath, capsizing them, and the process spread until it involved the whole tree. It grew like an avalanche, and it descended without warning upon the man and the fire — and the fire was blotted out! Where it had burned was a mantle of fresh and disordered snow.

The man was shocked. It was as though he had just heard his own sentence of death. Perhaps the old-timer on Sulphur Creek was right. If he had only had a trail-mate, he would have been in no danger now. He tried to build a new fire with fingers he could no longer feel; the tiny flame, his one hope of life, he smothered by his own clumsiness. A fear of death, dull and oppressive, came to him: it was a matter of life and death, with the chances against him.

And then he ran — ran until he stumbled and fell, and could rise no more. He grew calm, and entertained in his mind the conception of meeting death with dignity. Then he drowsed off into what seemed the most comfortable and satisfying sleep he had ever known.

The dog sat facing him and waiting. It whined softly, but the man remained silent. Later it crept close and caught the scent of death; it bristled and backed away. A little longer it delayed, howling under the cold stars. Then it turned and trotted up the trail in the direction of the camp it knew, where were the other food-providers and fire-providers.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What destroys the fire — and whose fault is it?

2. How does the story end for the man, and for the dog?

1. Snow, capsizing from the spruce boughs he himself had shaken by pulling twigs. It is entirely his own mistake — he built the fire under the tree instead of in the open.

2. The man panics and runs, falls, then grows calm and decides to meet death with dignity, drifting into sleep. The dog catches the scent of death and trots on towards the camp — and the other food-providers and fire-providers.

Discuss:
  • The whole disaster turns on one small, ordinary choice — a fire under a tree instead of in the open. What does that tell us about how catastrophes really happen?

Grammar — Concession & Contrast

This story lives in a gap between two truths: it is deadly cold, and yet the man is not afraid. The dog knows, whereas the man does not. Holding two clashing ideas in one breath is the grammar of concession and contrast — and English gives you several tools for it, each with its own shape.

Concession — "this is true, BUT so is that":
• although / even though / though + a full clause (subject + verb): "Although the day was clear, a pall of gloom lay over it." "Even though he'd been warned, he set out alone."
• despite / in spite of + a noun, pronoun or -ing form — never a bare clause: "Despite the old-timer's warning…", "In spite of being soaked…". To attach a clause, add the fact that: "despite the fact that his feet were wet".
Contrast — turning between two ideas:
• however / nevertheless open a new sentence (followed by a comma): "The fire was dead. Nevertheless, he forced himself to stay calm."
• yet / and yet link inside a sentence, with a literary edge: "It was a clear day, and yet a subtle gloom made it dark."
• whereas / while set two things directly side by side: "The man trusted his judgment, whereas the dog trusted its instinct."

1. Concession — pick the linker that fits the shape

Complete each sentence with although, even though, despite or in spite of. The cue tells you what kind of structure follows the gap.

1. the old-timer's warning, he set out alone. (+ noun)

2. the day was clear, a strange gloom lay over it. (+ full clause)

3. He felt no fear being days from any help. (+ -ing form)

4. He kept walking, the dog plainly wanted to stop. (+ full clause, mid-sentence)

1. Despite / In spite of (a noun follows, so no clause-linker is possible)

2. Although / Even though ("Even though" stresses the surprise a little more)

3. despite / in spite of (the -ing form behaves like a noun)

4. even though / although (a full clause follows: "the dog… wanted…")

2. Contrast — choose the word for the job

Complete each with however, nevertheless, whereas or yet. The cue in brackets describes the job the word does.

1. The man had judgment and a thermometer. , the dog had something far older — instinct. (formal opener for a new sentence, + comma)

2. The man read the cold as a number, the dog felt it as danger. (sets the two directly side by side)

3. He knew wet feet could kill, he treated the accident as a lost hour. (short link inside the sentence, slightly literary)

4. The fire was dead and his fingers were wooden. , he forced himself to stay calm. (formal: "in spite of that")

1. However (Nevertheless also fits — both open the sentence and take a comma)

2. whereas (while also works — a direct side-by-side contrast)

3. yet ("but" would work too; "yet" carries more weight)

4. Nevertheless (the strongest "despite all that" — However also fits)

Going deeper — take the idea further

You have the story and the words. Now push the ideas outward — argue, disagree, change your mind. Do all of this out loud. There are no right answers here, only good reasons.

Useful language for building an argument: "What's striking is…", "You could argue that…", "Although X, Y…", "…, whereas…", "That may be true, but…".
1 · An indifferent universe

Nature in this story is never cruel — never angry, never vengeful. It simply does not notice the man at all. Which is more frightening: a world that hates you, or one that is utterly indifferent to you? And what do we get wrong when we talk about "conquering" a mountain or "taming" the wild?

2 · Instinct vs intellect

The dog cannot read a thermometer — and the dog is right. What does instinct "know" that reasoning doesn't? Where should a modern person trust the older signal — and where would instinct lead us badly astray? Build a case for each side, then say where you'd draw the line.

3 · Why warnings fail

The old-timer's rule was free, simple and correct — and it was scoffed at. Why do people ignore warnings? Pride? Optimism? Something about how warnings are delivered? Think of examples from history, business or film where "it won't happen to me" won the argument — and say what would make a warning impossible to ignore.

4 · Modern Yukons

Every year, well-equipped people walk past clear warnings — into the mountains, out to sea, up Everest in a queue. Our phones and gear have made us far safer than the man with his matches; have they also made us more like him? What would an old-timer of our age warn people about — and would anyone listen?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The man's fatal flaw is not ignorance but "no imagination." Is imagination a survival skill? What can't you protect yourself from if you can't picture it?
  2. The dog survives by fear; the man dies by confidence. Where in modern life does confidence earn more reward than caution — and is that wise?
  3. London refuses to mourn the man — the story ends on the dog, feeling nothing. What would be lost if it ended in grief instead?
