SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B2/C1 · 50 min

To Build a Fire

Jack London (1908) — a man walks into seventy-five degrees of frost, certain he can handle it. Nature has no opinion about that.

Read in three parts

London gives us a man with one fatal flaw — no imagination. He can read a thermometer but not what it means. Watch how the story keeps the man's confidence and the reader's dread on two separate tracks, and notice who, in the end, has the better judgement: the man, or the dog trotting at his heels.

Part 1 — The man who was not afraid

Day had broken cold and grey, exceedingly cold and grey, when the man turned aside from the main Yukon trail and climbed the high earth-bank. It was a clear day, and yet there seemed an intangible pall over the face of things, a subtle gloom that made the day dark. This fact did not worry the man. He was without imagination. He was quick and alert in the things of life, but only in the things, and not in the significances.

Fifty degrees below zero meant eighty-odd degrees of frost. Such fact impressed him as being cold and uncomfortable, and that was all. It did not lead him to meditate upon his frailty as a creature of temperature, and upon man's frailty in general. Fifty degrees below zero was to him just precisely fifty degrees below zero. That there should be anything more to it than that was a thought that never entered his head.

At the man's heels trotted a dog, a big native husky. The animal was depressed by the tremendous cold. It knew that it was no time for travelling. Its instinct told it a truer tale than was told to the man by the man's judgment. The dog did not know anything about thermometers. Possibly in its brain there was no sharp consciousness of a condition of very cold. But the brute had its instinct.

The old-timer on Sulphur Creek had been very serious in laying down the law that no man must travel alone in that country after fifty below. Well, here he was. He had had the accident of no company; he was alone; and he was getting along fine. Those old-timers were rather womanish, some of them, he thought. All a man had to do was to keep his head, and he was all right.

Pause and discuss:
  • London tells us plainly that the man "was without imagination." Why is that, and not the cold, the real danger in this story?
  • The dog and the man walk the same trail. What does each of them "know" — and whose knowledge is worth more here?
  • The man calls the cautious old-timer "womanish." What does that one word tell you about him — and how does it set up everything that follows?

Part 2 — The wet feet

He was following a little-travelled trail, and the creeks he knew were frozen solid. But under the snow were hidden pools of water, treacherous springs that never quite froze. To break through meant wet feet, and wet feet at fifty below was no laughing matter. And then it happened. At a place where there were no signs, where the soft, unbroken snow seemed to advertise solidity beneath, the man broke through. It was not deep. He wet himself halfway to the knees before he floundered out to the firm crust.

He was angry, and cursed his luck aloud. He had hoped to get into camp with the boys by six o'clock, and this would delay him an hour, for he would have to build a fire and dry out his foot-gear. He knew there must be no failure. He remembered the advice of the old-timer on Sulphur Creek, and smiled. The old-timer had been very serious in laying down the law: after fifty below, a man should travel with a partner. Well, here he was; he had had the accident; he was alone; and he had saved himself.

He gathered dry twigs and grasses, and dead branches, and built his fire under a spruce tree. Working carefully from a small beginning, he soon had a roaring fire, and he sat on the snow and began to thaw the ice from his moccasins. The blood of his body was like the blood of the dog, and, like the dog, he wanted to bury himself in the snow. But he had a fire, and the fire was climbing higher, and he was safe.

Pause and discuss:
  • The man is angry at the accident, and treats it as an inconvenience — an hour's delay. What should he be feeling instead, and why doesn't he?
  • He remembers the old-timer's warning — and smiles. He has just proved (he thinks) that he can save himself alone. Why is that exactly the wrong lesson to take?
  • He builds the fire under a spruce tree. Nothing seems wrong. Why does London slow right down here — what is he asking you to notice?

Part 3 — The tree

It was his own fault, or rather his mistake. He should not have built the fire under the spruce tree. He should have built it in the open. But it had been easier to pull the twigs from the brush and drop them directly on the fire. Now the tree under which he had done this carried a weight of snow on its boughs. No wind had blown for weeks, and each bough was fully freighted. Each time he had pulled a twig he had communicated a slight agitation to the tree — an imperceptible agitation, so far as he was concerned, but an agitation sufficient to bring about the disaster.

High up in the tree one bough capsized its load of snow. This fell on the boughs beneath, capsizing them. This process continued, spreading out and involving the whole tree. It grew like an avalanche, and it descended without warning upon the man and the fire, and the fire was blotted out! Where it had burned was a mantle of fresh and disordered snow.

The man was shocked. It was as though he had just heard his own sentence of death. He looked at the pile of snow which had been the fire, and he became very serious. Perhaps the old-timer on Sulphur Creek was right. If he had only had a trail-mate he would have been in no danger now. He tried to build a new fire with fingers he could no longer feel; the tiny flame, his one hope of life, he smothered by his own clumsiness. A certain fear of death, dull and oppressive, came to him. It was no longer a mere matter of freezing his fingers and toes; it was a matter of life and death with the chances against him.

And then he ran — ran until he stumbled and fell, and could rise no more. He grew calm. He entertained in his mind the conception of meeting death with dignity. Then the man drowsed off into what seemed to him the most comfortable and satisfying sleep he had ever known.

The dog sat facing him and waiting. It whined softly, then flattened its ears down in anticipation of being chidden by the man. But the man remained silent. Later, it howled loudly. And still later it crept close to the man and caught the scent of death. This made the animal bristle and back away. A little longer it delayed, howling under the stars that leaped and danced and shone brightly in the cold sky. Then it turned and trotted up the trail in the direction of the camp it knew, where were the other food-providers and fire-providers.

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • The whole tragedy turns on one small, ordinary choice: he built the fire under a tree. Not a monster, not a villain — a twig. What is London saying about how disasters actually happen?
  • The dog feels no grief. It waits, catches the scent of death, and simply trots off to the next fire-provider. Why does London end on the dog, and not the man?
  • Nature here is never once described as cruel — only indifferent. Which is more frightening: a hostile universe, or one that simply does not notice you?
  • Your turn: The man dies of confidence, not cold. When have you seen someone ignore a warning because they were sure the rules didn't apply to them — and what happened?

The grammar this story runs on: concession & contrast

The whole story lives in a gap: it is bitterly dangerous, and yet the man is not afraid. The dog knows, whereas the man does not. That gap — saying two things that pull against each other — is the grammar of concession and contrast. English gives us three different tools for it, and they take different shapes.

1. Concession before a clause — although / even though / though

These join two full clauses (each with a subject and verb): "Although the cold was extreme, the man was not worried." Put the linker at the start of the less important idea. Complete each with the right linker.

1. the thermometer read fifty below, he felt no real fear. (concession, most neutral)

2. He walked on the dog clearly wanted to stop. (concession, mid-sentence)

3. he was quick and alert, he had no imagination at all. (concession, stronger — "…as this may be")

1. Although (or Though — both fit; "Even though" is a little stronger)

2. although / even though / though (any of the three works mid-sentence)

3. Even though (the strongest of the three — it stresses the surprise)

2. Concession before a noun or -ing — despite / in spite of

These are followed by a noun, a pronoun, or an -ing form — NOT a full clause: "Despite the warning…" / "In spite of being wet…". To follow them with a clause you must add "the fact that". Fill each gap.

1. the old-timer's warning, he travelled alone. (+ noun; one word)

2. of the danger, he built the fire carelessly. (two words; + noun)

3. He kept moving despite that his feet were soaked. (the linker that turns "despite" into a clause)

1. Despite (despite + noun — no "of")

2. In spite ("In spite of" + noun — three words in total)

3. the fact ("despite the fact that + clause")

3. Contrast between two sentences — however / nevertheless / yet / whereas

These signal a turn between two ideas. However / nevertheless start a new sentence (with a comma): "…; however, the dog knew better." Yet / and yet link within a sentence. Whereas / while set two things directly side by side. Choose the best word for the effect in brackets.

1. The man trusted his judgment. , the dog trusted its instinct. (a formal sentence-opener + comma)

2. He was clever with facts, he could not read what they meant. (short link inside the sentence: "…, ___ he could not")

3. The dog wanted to burrow into the snow, the man kept walking. (direct side-by-side contrast)

1. However (However, + clause — the neutral formal choice; "Nevertheless" also fits)

2. yet (…, yet he could not… — "but" also works; "yet" is a touch more literary)

3. whereas (whereas / while — puts the two reactions directly against each other)

What London does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves. Together they turn a simple accident into one of the great warnings in American literature.

1. Naturalism — the indifferent universe

Nature in this story is never cruel; it is simply indifferent. The cold has no plan, no malice, no interest in the man at all. London strips the world of comfort and meaning, and lets pure cause and effect play out: wet feet, a tree, gravity, snow. The horror is that the universe does not even notice a man die. This is naturalism — humans as small creatures subject to forces far larger than their will.

2. Dramatic irony & foreshadowing — we know before he does

From the first page, London signals the ending while keeping it from the man. We are told he lacks imagination; we are shown the dog's dread; we hear the old-timer's warning — and then we watch the man smile at it. Every reassurance he gives himself tightens our dread, because we understand what he refuses to. The gap between his confidence and our knowledge is dramatic irony.

3. Man versus dog — instinct against intellect

London juxtaposes the reasoning man with the instinctive animal, and quietly awards the victory to instinct. The man has thermometers and judgment; the dog has an ancient, wordless caution — and it is the dog that survives. By ending on the animal trotting calmly to the next camp, London deflates human pride: our intellect, without humility, is no match for a wolf's inherited fear.

4. Repetition & the accumulation of small errors

Notice how the disaster is built from tiny, ordinary things: a pulled twig, an unseen tremor, a bough. London accumulates small causes until they tip into catastrophe — the avalanche of snow is really an avalanche of little mistakes. The plain, repetitive sentences mirror the plodding steps of a man walking, unknowing, towards his end.

Speaking tasks

Do all four. Each builds on the last. Use the concession-and-contrast structures and the effect verbs from the previous tabs. The timer is for your benefit — fill the time rather than escape it.

Task A · Retell the story (3 minutes)

Tell the story back to me as if I had never read it — in your own words, about eight to ten sentences. Use at least five words from the Vocabulary tab, and hold the two tracks together with concession and contrast ("Although he was warned…", "…, whereas the dog…"). Beats to help you: a man walks alone into extreme cold, sure he can handle it · his dog senses danger he cannot · he breaks through hidden ice and gets wet feet · he builds a life-saving fire — under a tree · snow falls and puts it out · his fingers freeze, he panics, then accepts death · the dog trots off to find the next fire.

Task B · The warning that was ignored (4 minutes)

The man dies because he was sure the rules didn't apply to him. Tell me about a real time — you, or someone you watched — when a clear warning was brushed aside. Take it in three steps: (1) what the warning was, and who gave it; (2) why the person ignored it (pride? impatience? "I know better"?); (3) what actually happened. Use at least one concession clause ("Even though they were told…") and one contrast ("…, however, …"), plus two effect verbs.

Task C · Instinct vs intellect (3 minutes)

London gives the victory to the dog's instinct over the man's reasoning. Talk me through a decision where your gut and your logic disagreed. Which did you follow — and which turned out to be right? Build it around contrast: "My reasoning said X, whereas something in me said Y."

Task D · Describe an indifferent place (4 minutes)

London makes the Yukon terrifying not by making it cruel, but by making it not care. Describe a place you have been that felt completely indifferent to you — a vast landscape, a huge city, the sea, an empty office at night. Make me feel its size and its lack of interest in you. Use "however" and "and yet" to hold the beauty and the coldness of it together in the same breath.

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The man's fatal flaw is "no imagination." Is imagination really a survival skill? What can't you protect yourself from if you can't picture it?
  2. Nature is indifferent, not evil. Which frightens you more — a world that hates you, or one that simply doesn't notice you? Why?
  3. The dog survives by fear; the man dies by confidence. Where, in modern life, does confidence get more reward than caution — and is that wise?
  4. If the man had one trail-mate, he would almost certainly have lived. What does the story say about the price of going it completely alone?
