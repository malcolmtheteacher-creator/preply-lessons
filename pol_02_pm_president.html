The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. France has a semi-presidential system.
semi-presidential (adj) — describing a system with both a directly elected president and a prime minister answerable to parliament. Example: “France has a semi-presidential system.”
2. Cohabitation forced President Mitterrand to work with a rival government.
cohabitation (n) — in France, a period when the president and the prime minister belong to opposing camps. Example: “Cohabitation forced President Mitterrand to work with a rival government.”
3. The president may dissolve the National Assembly.
dissolve (v) — to officially end a parliament so that new elections can be held. Example: “The president may dissolve the National Assembly.”
4. A motion of censure can bring down the government in France.
censure (n) — a formal vote of disapproval that can force a government to resign. Example: “A motion of censure can bring down the government in France.”
5. Poland has a bicameral parliament made up of the Sejm and the Senate.
bicameral (adj) — having two separate chambers in a parliament. Example: “Poland has a bicameral parliament made up of the Sejm and the Senate.”
6. A three-fifths majority in the Sejm can override a presidential veto.
override (v) — to cancel a veto or decision with a stronger vote. Example: “A three-fifths majority in the Sejm can override a presidential veto.”
7. The House of Representatives can impeach a US president.
impeach (v) — to accuse a leader formally of serious wrongdoing, which can lead to removal. Example: “The House of Representatives can impeach a US president.”
8. The president claimed a mandate to change the pension system.
mandate (n) — the authority to govern that is claimed after winning an election. Example: “The president claimed a mandate to change the pension system.”

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Two classic models

In a parliamentary system such as the UK, voters elect Members of Parliament, and the leader of the party that can command a majority becomes prime minister. The prime minister can be replaced without a general election: the UK had three in 2022 alone. If the House of Commons passes a vote of no confidence, the government must resign or call an election. In a presidential system such as the USA, executive and legislature are elected separately for fixed terms. The president is chosen indirectly by 538 electors in the Electoral College (270 are needed to win), which has twice this century produced a winner who lost the national vote. A president serves four-year terms, limited to two, and can be removed only by impeachment: the House accuses, but the Senate needs a two-thirds majority to convict. No president has ever been removed this way.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How can a UK prime minister lose their job?

2. What is needed to remove a US president?

1. By losing a vote of no confidence, or by being replaced by their party.

2. Impeachment by the House and a two-thirds majority in the Senate to convict.

Discuss:
  • Which model gives voters more control over a leader, in your view?

Part 2 — France: a president who governs, sometimes

France's Fifth Republic, created in 1958, is the classic semi-presidential system. The president is elected directly by voters in a two-round election, now for five years, and can serve two consecutive terms. The president appoints the prime minister, chairs the cabinet, leads foreign and defence policy and may dissolve the National Assembly, the lower chamber. However, the prime minister and government are answerable to that Assembly, which can force them out through a motion of censure. If the president's camp loses the parliamentary election, the result is cohabitation: in 1986, 1993 and 1997 the president had to appoint a prime minister from the opposition, and domestic policy shifted to the prime minister. The five-year term and a change in the election calendar have made cohabitation less likely since 2002. The French parliament is bicameral: the National Assembly is elected directly by voters, whereas the Senate is elected indirectly by local officials, and if the two disagree, the Assembly usually has the last word.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is cohabitation?

2. Which chamber has the last word in France?

1. A period when the president and prime minister come from opposing camps.

2. The National Assembly.

Discuss:
  • Is a president who can dissolve parliament too powerful? Why or why not?

Part 3 — Poland: the same label, a different balance

Poland's 1997 constitution also created a semi-presidential system, but a weaker presidency. The president is directly elected for five years, with a limit of two terms, and nominates the prime minister, yet the government must win a vote of confidence in the Sejm, the 460-seat lower house, and can be removed by it. The president cannot dissolve the Sejm freely, but only in narrow cases such as a failure to pass the budget on time. The main presidential weapon is the veto: the president can block a bill or refer it to the Constitutional Tribunal, and the Sejm can override a veto only with a three-fifths majority, which governing coalitions often lack. The Senate, with 100 members elected by constituency, can amend bills, but the Sejm can reject its changes. Since 2023, a government led by Donald Tusk has had to work with a president from a rival camp, and the veto has become a central political tool.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How can the Polish Sejm override a presidential veto?

2. Why is the Polish president weaker than the French one?

1. With a three-fifths majority.

2. Because the president cannot freely dissolve the Sejm and the government answers to the Sejm.

Discuss:
  • What risks does a divided executive create, and what advantages might it have?

Grammar — The Passive — Who Is Chosen by Whom

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
Passive: subject + be + past participle.
• Present: “The French president is elected every five years.”
• Past: “The 1997 constitution was adopted after a referendum.”
• After a modal: “A veto can be overridden by a three-fifths majority.”
• Present perfect: “The Sejm has been dissolved only in rare cases.”
• Add the person doing the action with by only if it is important: “The government can be dismissed by the Assembly.”

2. The National Assembly can (dissolve) by the president.

3. A veto in Poland can (override) by a three-fifths majority in the Sejm.

1. is elected

2. be dissolved

3. be overridden

1. In 1986 a prime minister from the opposition (appoint) by President Mitterrand.

2. The Senate in Poland (elect) by constituency, not by the Sejm.

3. A government in France can (force out) by a motion of censure.

1. was appointed

2. is elected

3. be forced out

Going deeper

The belief that “Americans elect the leader, Britons do not” misses the more interesting point: each system makes a different bet on where the risk lies. Presidential systems protect against sudden changes, but can produce deadlock when president and legislature disagree, and the leader is difficult to remove. Parliamentary systems make governments easy to remove, but a leader can change without voters having a say. Semi-presidential systems try to combine direct election with accountability to parliament, and they succeed best when the president and parliamentary majority belong to the same camp. When they do not, the outcome depends on the constitution, from French cohabitation, in which power shifts towards the prime minister, to Polish gridlock, in which vetoes and counter-vetoes can stall legislation.

Worth noticing: Passive: be + past participle. Use it when the person doing the action is unknown, obvious or less important than the person or thing affected: “the president is elected every five years”.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Do not stop at the label. When someone says a country is “presidential”, ask three questions: who is elected directly, who can dismiss whom, and what happens when they disagree? The answers tell you more than the system's name.
