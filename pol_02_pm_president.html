The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The prime minister answers questions in parliament every week.
prime minister (n) — the leader of the government in a parliamentary system. Example: “The prime minister answers questions in parliament every week.”
2. The president signs the bill into law.
president (n) — an elected leader of a republic. Example: “The president signs the bill into law.”
3. The Electoral College decides the winner, not the national vote.
Electoral College (n) — the group of state representatives who formally choose the US president. Example: “The Electoral College decides the winner, not the national vote.”
4. The party needs a majority to pass its laws easily.
majority (n) — more than half of the votes or seats. Example: “The party needs a majority to pass its laws easily.”
5. The cabinet meets every Tuesday morning.
cabinet (n) — the group of senior ministers who lead government departments. Example: “The cabinet meets every Tuesday morning.”
6. A US president can serve at most two four-year terms.
term (n) — the length of time a leader is allowed to serve. Example: “A US president can serve at most two four-year terms.”
7. The president may veto a bill that Congress has passed.
veto (v) — to officially refuse to approve a law. Example: “The president may veto a bill that Congress has passed.”
8. If it loses a vote of confidence, the government must resign.
confidence (n) — support from parliament that a government needs to stay in office. Example: “If it loses a vote of confidence, the government must resign.”

The stereotype: “In America people elect the leader; in Britain they don't.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Who do voters really choose?

In the United Kingdom, voters elect Members of Parliament in their local area. The leader of the party that can command a majority in parliament becomes prime minister. Voters never write the prime minister's name on a ballot paper, and a party can replace its leader between elections: the UK had three prime ministers in 2022. In the USA, by contrast, voters choose electors in each state, and these electors formally choose the president.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who becomes prime minister in the UK?

2. What did the UK show in 2022?

1. The leader of the party that can command a majority in parliament.

2. That a prime minister can be replaced without a general election.

Discuss:
  • Is it important that voters choose the leader directly?

Part 2 — Two separate powers

In the US system the president and Congress are elected separately and can belong to different parties. The president cannot dissolve Congress, and Congress cannot easily remove the president. A US president serves four-year terms and is limited to two. In the UK, the prime minister sits in parliament and needs its confidence: if a majority votes against the government, it must go. An election must be held at least every five years.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What limits a US president's time in office?

2. What happens if a UK government loses a vote of confidence?

1. Four-year terms, and a maximum of two.

2. It must resign, and there may be an election.

Discuss:
  • Which system gives the leader more power, in your view?

Part 3 — Somewhere in between

Not every country fits neatly into one box. In France the president is directly elected by voters, yet also appoints a prime minister who needs the support of parliament. This is often called a semi-presidential system. The details differ, but the question is always the same: who can choose the leader, and who can remove them?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How is the French president chosen?

2. What is the key question in every system?

1. By direct vote of the voters.

2. Who can choose the leader, and who can remove them?

Discuss:
  • Would a mixed system be a good compromise?

Grammar — The Passive — Who Is Chosen by Whom

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
Passive: subject + be + past participle.
• Present: “The president is elected every four years.”
• Past: “The law was signed yesterday.”
• After a modal: “A leader can be removed by parliament.”
• Future: “A vote will be held next week.”

1. Choose the right form

Complete each sentence about Prime Minister or President?.

1. In the USA the president (elect) every four years.

2. In the UK a new prime minister can (choose) by the governing party.

3. The law (sign) by the president yesterday.

1. is elected

2. be chosen

3. was signed

2. A US president can (remove) by Congress in an impeachment.

3. A vote of no confidence (hold) next week.

2. be removed

3. will be held

Going deeper

The belief that “Americans elect the leader, Britons do not” is partly right and partly wrong. British voters do decide who governs, but through parliament. Americans do vote for president, but through the Electoral College, and in the past the person with the most votes nationally has lost. Both systems were designed at different times for different worries: the US system for dividing power, the British system for making governments accountable to parliament.

Worth noticing: Passive: be + past participle. Use it when the person doing the action is unknown, obvious or less important: “the president is elected every four years”.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Use the right word. When you talk about another country's politics, check whether it has a president, a prime minister or both, and whether the person was chosen directly by voters. A wrong guess can sound like a basic misunderstanding.
