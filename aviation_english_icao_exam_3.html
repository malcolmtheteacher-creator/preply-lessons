Where you are now

You have the shape of the test, the weather codes, and the standard words of the radio. This lesson is the part that decides whether you sit at Level 4 or push into Level 5: the moments when something goes wrong, and the moment afterwards when someone asks you what happened.

What separates Level 4 from Level 5

Level 4 handles the routine
Clear on familiar work — but slows down, hesitates or loses accuracy when the situation turns unusual.
Level 5 handles the unusual
Stays fluent when it gets complicated: reports, explains, recommends, and keeps the grammar under control.
Level 4 finds a word
Gets round a missing word, but with visible effort and long pauses.
Level 5 goes round it smoothly
Paraphrases so naturally the listener barely notices anything was missing.
Level 4 answers
Responds to what is asked, mostly correctly.
Level 5 manages
Checks, confirms, corrects the other speaker, and takes charge of the exchange.
The idea that runs through this whole lesson: in an emergency nobody wants beautiful English. They want what is happening, where, how bad, and what you want them to do — in that order, at that speed. Say those four things and you sound like a professional in any language.

Emergency words — two levels of trouble

Aviation has exactly two calls for trouble, and the difference between them is not politeness. It is how close the aircraft is to being lost.

MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY — distress. Grave and imminent danger; the aircraft needs immediate assistance. Everything else on the frequency stops.

PAN PAN, PAN PAN, PAN PAN — urgency. A serious problem, but no immediate danger to life. Priority, not silence.

Each call is spoken three times, then: who you are, where you are, what the problem is, what you intend to do, and what you need.
“MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY. Speedbird one-two-three. Engine fire, number two engine.
Position three-five miles south-east of the field, descending through eight thousand feet.
Request immediate return, runway two-seven, and full emergency services on the ground.
One-eight-nine souls on board, fuel remaining two hours.”

Notice the order: the call → who → what is wrong → where → what they want. Nothing is decorated. Nothing is explained twice.

The words you will hear around an emergency

to declare an emergencyTo formally announce that you are in trouble and need priority.
souls on boardThe total number of people on the aircraft — crew and passengers.
to divertTo go to a different airport instead of the planned one.
to holdTo fly a waiting pattern in the air until you can land.
to go aroundTo abandon the landing, climb away, and try again.
an overrunWhen an aircraft goes off the far end of the runway.
an excursionWhen an aircraft leaves the runway surface — off the side or the end.
a bird strikeA collision between an aircraft and a bird.
to stand downTo cancel the alert — the emergency is over.
be advised…Take note of this important information (a warning, not a request).
all stationsA message for everyone on the frequency at once.
to blank / to obscureWhen cloud, snow or dust hides something from view.
A cabin passenger is seriously ill. The aircraft is flying normally, but the crew wants priority to land quickly. Which call?

The state of the runway

Weather on its own does not stop an aircraft. What stops it is what the weather left on the runway — and describing that precisely is one of the most valuable things you can do in English.

What is on the surface

standing waterMore than 3 mm of water lying on the surface — a risk of aquaplaning.
slushHalf-melted snow and water — heavy, and it drags on the wheels.
dry snowLoose, powdery snow that blows away easily.
wet snowSnow that sticks together when you squeeze it — heavier, more slippery.
compacted snowSnow pressed into a hard, solid layer by traffic or its own weight.
iceA hard frozen layer — the worst braking of all.
to aquaplaneWhen the tyres ride on water and lose all grip on the surface.
braking actionHow well an aircraft can slow down on the surface.
contaminationAnything covering the runway that should not be there.
to grit / to sandTo spread grit or sand for grip.
to plough / to clearTo push snow off the surface with a machine.
a snow bank / a driftA pile or ridge of snow at the side of the runway.

How bad the braking is

Braking action is reported on a fixed scale. The words matter more than the numbers — a pilot decides on these six phrases.

CodeYou sayWhat it means for the pilot
6DryNormal braking, no contamination.
5GoodBraking is normal; directional control is normal.
4Good to mediumSlightly reduced — noticeable, but manageable.
3MediumClearly reduced braking; control needs care.
2Medium to poorSignificantly reduced; landing distance grows a lot.
1PoorBraking is badly reduced and control is uncertain.
0Less than poorUnreliable braking. The runway should be closed.

Report the surface (60 seconds)

Snow fell all night and stopped an hour ago. The runway has been ploughed down the middle, but the outer thirds are still covered in about two centimetres of wet snow, and there are snow banks along both edges. Temperature is minus one and falling. Report it, and say what you recommend.

01:00
Something like: “Tower, runway condition report. The centre third has been ploughed and is wet; the outer thirds are covered with approximately two centimetres of wet snow. Snow banks along both edges. Temperature minus one and falling, so I expect the surface to freeze over the next hour. Braking action reported medium. I'd recommend gritting the outer thirds and a further inspection within thirty minutes.”

Telling the story afterwards

When the emergency is over, someone asks you what happened. This is where grammar decides your score — because now you need past tenses in the right order and reported speech.

Three tenses, three jobs:
Past simple — the events, one after another: “The pilot declared an emergency and turned back.”
Past continuous — the background, already in progress: “It was snowing heavily at the time.”
Past perfect — what happened before that: “The temperature had dropped below zero an hour earlier.”
Reported speech — one step back:
“I am losing airspeed” → He reported that he was losing airspeed.
“I have lost an engine” → She said she had lost an engine.
“We will divert” → They said they would divert.
“Is the runway clear?” → He asked whether the runway was clear. (No question order, no question mark.)

Put the verb in the right past form. Type your answer, then check.

1. By the time the aircraft landed, the fog (close) in completely.

2. While I (take) the eight o'clock observation, the tower called me about the wind shear.

3. The pilot said he (would / will) need a full emergency response.

4. She asked me whether the braking action (be) still medium.

5. We issued a special report because the visibility (fall) below the minimum ten minutes earlier.

1. had closed  ·  2. was taking  ·  3. would  ·  4. was  ·  5. had fallen
1 and 5 go one step further back because they happened before the main event. 2 is the background action already in progress. 3 and 4 step back because the words are being reported.

When the word won't come

Every candidate loses a word. The examiner is not listening for a perfect vocabulary — they are listening for what you do in the second after it disappears. Freezing costs you a level. Going round it cleanly can gain you one.

Four ways round any missing word:
Category — “It's a kind of cloud that…”
Function — “It's the machine we use for clearing snow.”
Opposite — “It's the opposite of taking off.”
Example — “Like when the wind suddenly changes direction near the ground.”
The bridging phrases that buy you a second: “How can I put this…” · “I don't know the exact word, but…” · “What I mean is…” · “In other words…” · “The thing we use for…” · “Let me put it another way.” Every one of them keeps you talking. Silence does not.

Before you finish — be honest with yourself

  • I gave the emergency information in order: what, where, how bad, what I need.
  • I named the contaminant and its depth, not just “bad weather”.
  • My story used past simple, past continuous and past perfect in the right places.
  • I reported what someone said with the tense stepped back correctly.
  • When a word disappeared, I went round it in under two seconds — no silence.
  • I asked someone to repeat instead of guessing what I had heard.
Any box you couldn't tick is your target for next time — and it's exactly what the examiner is listening for.
