The mystery of the two voicemails

The same person leaves two voicemails in one hour:

To her landlord: Good afternoon, this is a message regarding the boiler in flat four. I would appreciate a call back at your earliest convenience.
To her sister: it's me (.) boiler's dead AGAIN (.) I'm fuming (.) ring us back yeah
SPEAK Same person, same boiler, same hour. List everything that changed — words, grammar, tone, even the pet name for herself. Then the real question: which voicemail is the REAL her?
Trick question — both are. Every speaker owns a wardrobe of styles and switches by situation. The switching itself (the concept name is register, and the act is style-shifting) is a skill, not a deception. Identity is the whole wardrobe.

The seven concepts

1 · Idiolect
One individual's unique language fingerprint — their pet phrases, favourite constructions, comic habits, everything. No two people share one. In Section A you are essentially describing two idiolects and what built them.
2 · Sociolect
The shared language of a social group — a class, a profession, a friendship group, a generation. Gamers, nurses, skaters and lawyers each carry one. Your idiolect is partly a stack of your sociolects.
3 · Dialect
The vocabulary and grammar of a region: "canny" in Newcastle, "cob" for a bread roll in the Midlands, "we was" in many regions. Dialect = WORDS and GRAMMAR.
4 · Accent
The SOUNDS of a region or group — what transcripts capture in /slashes/. You can speak standard dialect in a strong accent, and vice versa. Never confuse the two in an essay; examiners notice.
5 · Register
The formality level a situation demands — and the skill of moving between levels (style-shifting). A register CLASH inside one text (formal word in a chatty sentence) is nearly always deliberate and always worth analysing.
6 · Mode
Speech vs writing (Lesson 1's engine) — with a middle ground: texting, posts and scripted talks blend the two.
7 · Accommodation
Speakers adjust TOWARD their listener (convergence) to build closeness, or AWAY (divergence) to mark distance — the researcher attached is Howard Giles. An interviewee mirroring the host's chattiness is converging.

Prestige — the concept that wins essays

Why doesn't everyone just switch to the "poshest" English available? Because language runs on two currencies:

Overt prestigeCovert prestige
The open, official status of standard English and "received" accents — the currency of interviews, courtrooms, news desks.The hidden status of NOT talking that way — loyalty to your roots, group membership, authenticity. The currency of belonging.

A famous footballer keeps his home-city accent on national TV. A politician suddenly drops her g's ("fightin' for you") at a rally. A successful comedian still calls tap water by its childhood nickname. All three are spending covert prestige — and any of the three could appear in your exam data.

SPEAK Your turn: think of a real public figure who keeps a strong regional voice despite fame. What does the accent BUY them? And what might they lose if they smoothed it away?
It buys authenticity, community loyalty, distinctiveness — covert prestige. Smoothing it away risks sounding like everyone else and betraying the group that made them. In exam voice: the speaker's retained regional features carry covert prestige, performing loyalty to origins that fame has not dissolved.

From memory

1. All seven concepts, one line each.

Idiolect (personal fingerprint) · sociolect (group language) · dialect (regional words/grammar) · accent (regional sounds) · register (formality level + shifting) · mode (speech/writing) · accommodation (adjusting toward or away from the listener — Giles).

2. Overt vs covert prestige — one example each.

Overt: standard English in a job interview. Covert: keeping "we was" or a strong accent among your own people — the status of loyalty.

3. Dialect vs accent in one sentence.

Dialect is regional words and grammar; accent is regional sound — and a speaker can have either without the other.
