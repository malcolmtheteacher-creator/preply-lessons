The Ulysses Contract

B2 Level · Why it's smart to make rules for yourself in advance

Rules you set for yourself

Two and a half thousand years ago, the Greek hero Ulysses had himself tied to the mast of his ship so that he could hear the famous song of the Sirens without jumping into the sea. He didn't trust his future self to resist that song. Today, most of us do the same thing, on a smaller scale — we delete apps that waste our time, we set a rule about no phone during deep work, we lock our savings away, we promise people we'll do something so that we can't easily back out. Each of these is what philosophers now call a Ulysses contract. The interesting question is no longer whether to use them. It's which ones, and against what.

Speak · first reactions

Answer each in two or three full sentences. Take your time.

  1. What is one rule you have already set for yourself — about work, about your phone, about food, about sleep? Why did you set it?
  2. Have you ever made a rule and broken it the same week? What happened?
  3. Modern apps and websites are designed to be hard to put down. Is willpower still enough to handle them — or do we need rules?
  4. Some people think that setting rules for yourself shows weakness. Others think it shows that you know yourself well. What do you think?
Speak · pick a side

Below are four claims. Pick the one you most want to defend, and defend it for two minutes. Then pick the one you find most uncomfortable, and try to defend it anyway.

  • (a) "The smartest people don't fight temptation. They remove the option."
  • (b) "If you really wanted to change, you would just change. Rules are an excuse."
  • (c) "A team without clear rules is a team that argues about everything, every week."
  • (d) "In the modern world, willpower is not enough — we need structure."

Eight chunks to put to work

a Ulysses contract A rule or decision you make in advance, to stop your future self from doing something you know you shouldn't.
to commit yourself in advance To decide now, while you're thinking clearly, what you will (or won't) do later.
decision fatigue When the quality of your decisions drops later in the day, after you've made a lot of small ones.
to lock something in To make a plan, decision or rule firm and difficult to change later.
a hard rule A clear rule you have decided not to negotiate, no matter how you feel in the moment.
willpower The strength to make yourself do something difficult — useful but limited, and easy to run out of.
to stick to (a rule / a plan) To follow it consistently, even when it's tempting to break it.
to second-guess yourself To start doubting a decision after you've made it — often a sign the rule is working, not failing.

One paragraph. Then we talk.

Read this aloud, slowly. It's here to set up the conversation — not to be studied to death.

The Norwegian philosopher Jon Elster, writing in 1979, called these arrangements Ulysses contracts — after the Greek hero who had himself tied to the mast of his ship so that he could safely hear the dangerous song of the Sirens. The basic idea is simple: instead of relying on willpower at the moment of temptation, you commit yourself in advance, while you are still thinking clearly. Today, this matters more than ever. We work inside conditions Elster could only partly imagine — apps designed to compete for our attention, calendars filled up by other people, and important decisions that arrive at the end of long, tiring days. Decision fatigue is real, and it is unfair to expect a tired late-afternoon version of you to make the same quality of choices as your fresh morning self. The smarter move is not to try harder; it is to lock things in earlier — a hard rule about no meetings before 10am, a clear rule about no email after dinner, a deliberate plan to stick to. A well-designed Ulysses contract takes the pressure off your future self, and the moment you start second-guessing yourself is usually a sign that the contract is doing its job — not a sign that it was wrong.

Phrasing · read aloud, with a small pause at every comma. The sentences are long; chunk them.

Two quick questions before we open it out:

1. In one sentence: what is the main argument the writer is making?

Show Answer
In modern conditions, with constant distractions and decision fatigue, it is smarter to make rules in advance than to rely on willpower in the moment.

2. Why does the writer say that second-guessing yourself is "a sign that the contract is doing its job"?

Show Answer
Because the whole point of a Ulysses contract is to hold you when your future self wants to give in. The discomfort of doubting yourself is the moment the rule is actually being tested — and being useful. If you never felt that, the rule probably wasn't needed.

No writing. Pick the question that pulls you most, and run with it.

  2. The writer talks about apps designed to compete for our attention. Which apps win that competition with you most often, and what would a good rule against them look like?
  3. Is there a Ulysses contract you've seen work brilliantly for someone you respect — and what was it?
  4. Most teams have at least one unwritten rule that everyone follows but nobody ever wrote down. Is that a strength or a weakness? Give a real example.

Modal + perfect infinitive (talking about the past)

When we talk about Ulysses contracts — rules we made, rules we didn't make — we often look back at decisions. English uses a very useful pattern for this: modal verb + have + past participle. Get this clean, and your past-talk will sound twice as fluent.

The form: modal + have + past participle. Always have, never has. Not "could done", not "should did" — always could have done, should have done. The auxiliary have is what carries the past.

should have / shouldn't have

Looking back at a decision with regret or mild criticism.

I should have set that rule a year ago.

We shouldn't have made the decision so late in the day.

could have / couldn't have

Something was possible (or impossible) in the past — but didn't happen.

I could have locked the plan in last week. I didn't.

You couldn't have known at the time.

might have / must have

Guessing about the past. Might have = maybe. Must have = I'm sure.

She might have stuck to the rule that day — I'm not sure.

He must have been exhausted by 6pm.

Speak · oral transformation drill

Your teacher reads a flat sentence. You reformulate it on the spot, aloud, using the modal indicated. Listen for the rhythm: modal + have + past participle. Don't write. Each item has a model below.

1. Flat: "I didn't set a clear rule about meetings. Now I'm regretting it."   →   use should have

Show Answer
I should have set a clear rule about meetings.

2. Flat: "We had the chance to lock the schedule in last month. We didn't."   →   use could have

Show Answer
We could have locked the schedule in last month.

3. Flat: "Maybe she stuck to her own rule yesterday — I'm not sure."   →   use might have

Show Answer
She might have stuck to her own rule yesterday.

4. Flat: "He was very disciplined yesterday — I'm sure he kept to the plan."   →   use must have

Show Answer
He must have kept to the plan.

5. Flat: "I made the rule too strict, and now I regret it."   →   use shouldn't have

Show Answer
I shouldn't have made the rule too strict.

6. Flat: "If we hadn't made the rule, we wouldn't have finished on time."   →   rebuild with the second half first: "Without that rule, we …"

Show Answer
Without that rule, we wouldn't have finished on time.

Three speaking tasks

Time remaining: 3:00
Task A · a rule you set (3 minutes)

Tell the story of one rule you set for yourself — about work, sleep, phone, food, anything. Cover:

  1. What was the rule, exactly?
  2. Why did you set it? What was failing without it?
  3. How did you make sure you stuck to it — what was the mechanism?
  4. Would you set the same rule again, or change it?

Use at least three vocabulary chunks from Tab 2 and at least two modal-perfect forms (should have / could have / would have) from Tab 4.

Task B · argue back (3 minutes)

A friend says to you:

"All these rules and apps and plans — it's a bit weak, isn't it? If you really wanted to focus, you'd just focus. You shouldn't need a rule to stop you doing what you already know you shouldn't do."

Argue against this for three minutes. Be concrete about modern conditions — phones, notifications, decision fatigue. Use at least two modal-perfect forms ("He could have said the same thing twenty years ago, but…").

Task C · looking back (3 minutes)

Think of a project, a piece of work, or a decision in your own life where you wish you had set a clear rule earlier. Describe it using mostly modal-perfect forms:

  1. What happened?
  2. What rule should you have set, and when?
  3. If you had set it, what could have gone differently?
  4. What rule have you set since — or what rule are you about to set, because of that experience?

Aim to use should have, could have, and would have at least once each.

Final open discussion

  1. What's a rule almost everyone you know should have, but doesn't?
  2. Some companies have very strict rules; others almost none. From your experience, which kind works better, and why?
  3. How would you teach a younger person — a teenager, a new colleague — to think in terms of rules they set for themselves, without sounding boring or strict?
  4. If you imagine yourself five years from now, which rule do you most hope present-you will have set this year?
