Formal written English favours nouns over verbs and hides the personal "I/we" behind neutral phrasing — it reads as a fact being reported, not a person complaining. That's the engine behind most formal style: turn the action into a thing, and choose the word family member that fits the sentence exactly.
Today: turning verbs and adjectives into formal nouns, building whole word families from one root, and converting between informal and formal register — so you can write emails, reports, and messages that sound exactly as formal (or as relaxed) as the situation needs.

Turning Actions into Things: Nominalisation

Formal writing prefers nouns to verbs — it makes the sentence sound like a fact, not a story about people doing things.

Spot the pattern

Three pairs — the same idea, spoken and written:

  • informal"We decided to close the branch." → formal"The decision to close the branch was taken."
  • informal"Prices rose sharply last quarter." → formal"There was a sharp rise in prices last quarter."
  • informal"We're worried the plan might fail." → formal"There is concern about the failure of the plan."

Before you check: what happens to the verb (decided, rose, worried/fail) in each formal version?

Check the pattern CLICK
The verb (or adjective) becomes a noun — decide → decision, rise → rise, worried → concern, fail → failure — and a "support verb" like was taken, there was, or there is carries almost no meaning on its own. The person who decided, or who is worried, often disappears altogether.
verb/adjective → noun + support verb
decide → the decision (was taken / was made)
fail → the failure (occurred / was noted)

From clause to noun phrase

"The council will review the policy next month."
→
"A review of the policy will take place next month."

"Review" stops being an action the council performs and becomes a thing that simply happens — the council can even be left out.

One Root, Many Forms: Word Families

At this level you don't just know a word — you know its whole family, and you pick the exact member the sentence needs.

Spot the pattern

The root ANALYSE across four forms:

analyse (verb)→ analysis (noun)→ analytical (adjective)→ analyst (person)

"The team will analyse the results" → "Her analysis was thorough" → "a highly analytical mind" → "she works as an analyst".

Before you check: what job does each ending (-yse, -ysis, -ytical, -yst) do?

Check the pattern CLICK
Same root, four jobs: -yse makes the verb (the action), -ysis makes the noun (the process/result), -ytical makes the adjective (describing something), -yst makes the person who does it. Two other endings do similar work across many roots: -ise/-ify turn adjectives/nouns into verbs (modern → modernise, pure → purify), and prefixes over-, under-, mis-, re- change a verb's meaning without changing its form (estimate → over/under/mis/re-estimate).
-ise / -ify → makes a verb (modern → modernise, simple → simplify)
over- / under- / mis- / re- + verb → changes the meaning of the action

Same Meaning, Different Voice: Shifting Register

The same true statement can be dressed for a text message or a formal letter — the facts don't change, only the words and the distance.

Spot the pattern

Three matched pairs:

  • informal"Sorry, we messed up your order." → formal"We apologise for the error in processing your order."
  • informal"The heating's broken again." → formal"The heating system has malfunctioned once more."
  • informal"We need more info before we can help." → formal"Further information is required before assistance can be provided."

What changes each time — just single words, or the sentence shape too?

Check the pattern CLICK
Both: informal words get swapped for more precise Latinate ones (messed up → error, broken → malfunctioned, info → information), and the sentence often moves to the passive so no one has to say who is at fault. Choosing the exact word matters as much as the grammar — "error" is more precise than "mistake" in a complaint letter, and "malfunctioned" is more precise than "broken" for a machine.
vague/informal word → precise formal word
active, personal ("we messed up") → passive, impersonal ("an error occurred")

From casual complaint to formal one

"You guys never called us back about the leak."
→
"No response was received regarding the reported leak."

"You guys never called" (personal, informal) becomes "no response was received" (impersonal, formal) — the fact is identical.

2. Say it out loud: what's the difference between "the decision" and "to decide" in a sentence — which part of speech is each, and which one sounds more formal in a report?

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: "to decide" is a verb — an action someone performs ("we decided to close it"). "the decision" is a noun — the same idea turned into a thing that can simply exist or "be taken", with no person attached. The noun form is the one favoured in formal reports.
