The Interview

You want this job. The CV got you in the door — now there's a person across the table, a handful of hard questions, and forty minutes to sound like the obvious choice, in English.

The Scene 🎬

You've done the hard part. Your application stood out from a hundred others, and now you're sitting in a bright meeting room — or looking into a webcam — for the interview you actually wanted. This isn't a job you'd settle for. It's the one you drove home thinking about. Which is exactly why your heart is going a little fast.

And you know the shape of what's coming, because every interview shares the same handful of hard moments. It opens with the deceptively simple "So, tell me about yourself" — a question with no right answer and a hundred wrong ones. Somewhere in the middle comes the one everyone dreads: "What's your greatest weakness?" Then the behavioural turn — "Tell me about a time you handled a difficult situation" — where a vague answer sinks you and a good story floats you. And at the end, the quiet test most people fail: "So, do you have any questions for us?"

None of these reward the loudest voice or the biggest adjectives. The candidate who wins the room is the one who can sell themselves without sounding arrogant, own a weakness without falling apart, and answer "tell me about a time…" with a real story instead of a shrug. In your own language you could do all of this. Today's job is to carry that same calm, evidenced confidence into English.

Across the table: an interviewer who is friendly but paying close attention — not trying to catch you out, but genuinely weighing whether you're the person they've been looking for.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of the last interview you sat in — in any language. Which single question threw you most, and what did you wish afterwards you'd said instead?
  2. When you talk about your own strengths, do you tend to undersell yourself or oversell? Where's the line, for you, between confidence and arrogance — and does it move when you switch into English?

Who's who

You
The Candidate 💼

You genuinely want this role, and you're right for it. Today's job is to prove that with evidence, not adjectives — to sell yourself warmly, own a weakness honestly, and tell a real story well, all while staying composed.

The other voice
The Interviewer 🗒️

Warm, curious, and listening hard. They're not out to trip you up — they're deciding whether you're the one. Every question is really the same question: should we hire you?

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Notice how the candidate answers every question with something concrete — a number, a moment, a story — rather than a string of flattering adjectives.

InterviewerThanks for coming in. So — to start us off — tell me about yourself.
YouThank you. In a sentence, I'm someone who takes messy, half-organised processes and makes them run smoothly. For the last four years I've done exactly that in logistics, and this role looks like the same work on a bigger stage — which is why I applied.
InterviewerNice and clear. You mention you're good at organising. Can you give me a sense of what that looks like in practice?
YouOf course. Rather than just say I'm organised, let me give you an example. When I joined my current team, orders were tracked across three spreadsheets and nothing matched. I built one shared system everyone actually used, and within six months our late deliveries dropped by about a third. That's what "organised" means for me — fewer things falling through the cracks.
InterviewerThat's exactly the kind of thing we need. Let me ask the harder one, then — what would you say is your greatest weakness?
YouHonestly, my weakness is that I find it hard to delegate — I've always assumed it's faster to do it myself. And here's what I'm doing about it: on my last two projects I deliberately handed whole pieces to junior colleagues, kept my hands off, and coached instead. It was uncomfortable at first, but the work got done and they grew. I'm not fully cured, but I'm a lot better than I was.
InterviewerI appreciate the honesty. Tell me about a time you had to deal with a difficult situation at work.
YouLet me walk you through a specific example. Last year a key supplier pulled out three days before a major shipment — the situation was, frankly, a small crisis. What I did was call every alternative supplier I could find, split the order across two of them, and keep the client updated hourly so they were never in the dark. The result: the shipment went out one day late instead of two weeks late, and we kept the client. I learned that in a crisis, over-communicating is worth more than a perfect plan.
InterviewerThat's a strong example. We're nearly at time — do you have any questions for us?
YouI do, thank you. Can I ask what success looks like in this role after a year? I want to understand what "doing this job well" actually means to you.
InterviewerGreat question. A year in, you'd have our regional operation running on one system and the error rate down — much like what you just described, in fact.
YouThen this sounds like exactly the kind of problem I enjoy. Thank you for your time today — I've come away more interested in the role, not less, and I'd be glad to take it further.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Asked to prove they're organised, the candidate doesn't pile on more adjectives — they do one specific thing instead. What is it, and why does it land harder than saying "I'm very organised and efficient"?

2. On the weakness question, the candidate names a real flaw rather than a fake one. Yet it doesn't hurt them. What's the second half of their answer that turns the weakness into a point in their favour?

3. The "tell me about a time…" answer follows a hidden three-part shape. What are the three parts — and why is that shape so much stronger than just describing the problem?

1. They give a concrete example with a result — one messy system replaced by one shared one, late deliveries down by about a third. Evidence beats adjectives every time: anyone can claim to be organised, but a number and a story are hard to argue with and easy to believe. They let the fact do the boasting for them.
2. The "here's what I'm doing about it" half. Naming a genuine weakness shows self-awareness; showing the concrete steps you're taking to fix it shows maturity and drive. Together they read as confidence, not confession — the weakness becomes proof that you grow.
3. Situation → action → result: the crisis (supplier pulled out), what they personally did (split the order, over-communicated), and how it ended (shipment saved, client kept). The shape works because it moves past "here was a problem" to "here's what I did and what it achieved" — which is the only part an interviewer actually wants.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the hardest moments of any interview — the opening pitch, the weakness question, the story, the close. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. These phrases only work if they carry warm confidence — not arrogance, not apology. Keep the pace steady; a candidate who slows down sounds sure of themselves, while one who rushes sounds nervous. And smile as you speak, even on a call: an interviewer can hear a smile, and it turns "I'm good at this" from a boast into an offer. Confident, warm, unhurried — that's the whole delivery.

🎤 The opening pitch — "tell me about yourself"

Phrase 1

"So, tell me about yourself." You don't want to recite your whole CV — you want one crisp line that frames who you are. There's a four-word opener that forces you to be short and sharp: "___ ___ ___, I'm someone who…"

"In a sentence, I'm someone who…"
Promising "in a sentence" forces you to distil yourself to one clear idea — and interviewers love a candidate who can be concise under pressure. Cousins: "The short version is…", "If I had to sum myself up…"
Phrase 2

You've given your one-line pitch. Now you want to connect it straight to this job, so it doesn't sound generic. Which follow-on ties your background to the role best?

A. "Anyway, that's basically me, I suppose."
B. "…and this role looks like the same work on a bigger stage, which is why I applied."
C. "I need a job and this one seemed fine."
B — "…this role looks like the same work on a bigger stage, which is why I applied."
It bridges from who you are to why you're sitting there, showing you've thought about their job, not just your own story. A trails off and wastes the moment; C sounds like you'd take any job going. Cousins: "…which is exactly why this role caught my eye."

📊 Evidence, not adjectives

Phrase 3

You've claimed a strength and the interviewer wants proof. Instead of piling on more praise for yourself, you pivot to a concrete example. Complete the chunk: "Rather than just say I'm organised, let me ___ ___ ___ ___."

"Rather than just say I'm organised, let me give you an example."
This one line does the whole job of this lesson: it drops the adjective and reaches for evidence. Swap "organised" for any strength. Cousins: "Let me show you rather than tell you…", "Here's what that looked like in practice…"
Phrase 4

An interviewer half-hears "I'm a strong communicator" fifty times a day and believes it none of those times. In your own words, how could you make that same claim believable — what would you add? (Say a full version out loud.)

e.g. "I'd rather show you than tell you — last quarter I presented our results to the board and turned a nervous room into a yes." / "A number might say it better: I cut our reply time to clients from two days to two hours."
The move is always the same: replace the adjective with a moment, a number, or a result. "Strong communicator" is a claim; "I turned a nervous board into a yes" is proof. Reach for the smallest concrete detail — it's worth ten adjectives.

🪞 The weakness question

Phrase 5

"What's your greatest weakness?" A fake answer ("I work too hard") is transparent and a real one left bare is risky. The trusted move names a genuine flaw and the action you're taking. Complete it: "Honestly, my weakness is X — and ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ about it."

"Honestly, my weakness is X — and here's what I'm doing about it."
The first half earns trust by being real; the second half turns the flaw into evidence that you grow. Never stop at the weakness — the "what I'm doing about it" is the part that actually wins the point. Cousins: "…and I've been actively working on it by…", "…it's a work in progress, but here's the progress."
Phrase 6

Which of these is the safest, strongest way to answer the weakness question?

A. "Honestly? I don't really have any weaknesses I can think of."
B. "I find it hard to delegate — and here's what I've been doing to get better at it."
C. "My weakness is that I'm a perfectionist and I care too much."
B — a real weakness plus the work you're doing on it.
A sounds arrogant and evasive — everyone has weaknesses, and pretending otherwise is a red flag. C is the classic humblebrag; interviewers hear it constantly and it reads as dodging the question. B is honest, specific, and shows growth — which is the whole point of the question.

📖 Telling a story — situation → action → result

Phrase 7

"Tell me about a time you handled a difficult situation." You want to signal that a proper, specific story is coming — not a vague generalisation. Put it in order:

walk youa specificlet meexamplethrough
"Let me walk you through a specific example."
"Walk you through" promises a clear, step-by-step story, and "specific" tells the interviewer you're about to give the real thing, not a generalisation. Then follow the shape: situation → action → result. Cousins: "There's one moment that captures this…", "Let me give you a concrete case."
Phrase 8

Mid-story, you want to move cleanly from the problem to what you personally did about it — the most important part. There's a simple two-word hinge that does it: "___ ___ was, I split the order across two suppliers…"

"What I did was, I split the order across two suppliers…"
"What I did was…" pulls the spotlight onto your action, which is exactly what the interviewer is listening for — not the drama of the problem, but your part in solving it. Pair it with a closer: "The result was…". Cousins: "So my move was…", "The step I took was…"

🙋 Your questions & the close

Phrase 9

"Do you have any questions for us?" is a test, and "No, I think you've covered everything" fails it. You want a question that shows ambition and makes them picture you in the job. Complete it: "Can I ask what ___ ___ ___ in this role after a year?"

"Can I ask what success looks like in this role after a year?"
It's forward-looking and confident — it quietly puts you in the job, a year down the line, doing it well. And their answer tells you exactly what they'll judge you on. Cousins: "What would a great first six months look like?", "How will you know this hire worked out?"
Phrase 10

The interview's ending and you want to close warmly — leaving them with your interest, not just a flat "thanks". In your own words, how would you sign off so you sound keen but not desperate? (Say a full version out loud.)

e.g. "Thank you for your time — I've come away more interested in the role, not less, and I'd be glad to take it further." / "This has only made me keener; I'd really welcome the next step."
A warm, specific close leaves a strong last impression — "more interested, not less" is honest enthusiasm without begging. Avoid both the flat "okay, thanks, bye" and the over-eager "I really, really need this job." Keen and composed wins.

The Twist

🎯 The curveball you didn't prepare

One more round — no script, no warning. You've settled into a rhythm when the interviewer leans back and asks the question you didn't rehearse: "So — why should we not hire you?" (or, if you'd rather: "What would your worst-ever boss say about you?"). Start the timer and handle it live. Buy your second calmly, stay honest — don't pretend there's no answer — and turn it into a strength without sounding rehearsed or slippery. The goal isn't a perfect reply; it's to stay composed, keep it real, and come out sounding like someone secure enough to be honest about themselves. One rule: no freezing, and no fake "I have no flaws" answer — meet it head-on.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

It helps to remember what an interview actually is. It's not an interrogation, where one side holds all the power and the other simply survives. It's a two-way audition: they're deciding whether they want you, and you're deciding whether you want them. The candidate who walks in asking "will they pick me?" shrinks; the one who walks in also asking "is this the right place for me?" stands taller — and, paradoxically, becomes far more attractive to hire.

That's also why evidence beats adjectives so reliably. Anyone can call themselves hardworking, reliable, a great communicator — and interviewers have heard those words until they've stopped hearing them at all. A single specific moment, though — the number you moved, the crisis you steadied, the system you built — is yours alone and impossible to fake. Adjectives ask to be believed; evidence simply is believed.

And there's a quiet truth hiding in the weakness question. The person who can look you in the eye and say "here's what I'm not yet good at, and here's how I'm working on it" is showing you something no boast ever could: that they're secure enough to be honest about themselves. Owning a weakness well doesn't read as weakness — it reads as confidence. The people most afraid to admit a flaw are usually the ones least sure of their strengths.

Two bigger questions

  1. If an interview really is a two-way audition, what should you be trying to find out about them — and how would asking those questions change the way you come across?
  2. We're often taught to hide our weaknesses and lead with our strengths. When does that instinct help you, and when does it quietly cost you trust — in an interview, and in the rest of working life?
