Rosenhan's pseudopatients: true or false?

In 1973, the psychologist David Rosenhan published 'On Being Sane in Insane Places'. Here are six claims about the study and the debate that followed. Decide which are true.

1. Later investigations confirmed that every detail of Rosenhan's account was accurate.
False. Critics, including a journalist who examined Rosenhan's papers, raised serious doubts about parts of the study.
2. Some real patients suspected that the pseudopatients were not ill, even when the staff did not.
True. Many patients said things like 'You're not crazy.'
3. The hospital staff never wrote notes about the pseudopatients' behaviour.
False. Staff wrote notes, and ordinary behaviour such as writing notes was recorded as a symptom.
4. Once admitted, they were diagnosed with only mild, short-term problems.
False. Seven were diagnosed with schizophrenia, and they stayed 19 days on average.
5. The average stay was about 19 days, ranging from 7 to 52.
True. They were discharged with the label 'in remission'.
6. Rosenhan and seven others, all healthy, went to psychiatric hospitals saying they heard voices, and they were all admitted.
True. Seven received a diagnosis of schizophrenia.

The evidence, in three steps

Before each part, make a prediction out loud. Then read, and check yourself.

Part 1 — Eight pseudopatients

Before you read: How reliable do you think the diagnoses of different clinicians are when they see the same person?

In 1973, the psychologist David Rosenhan published 'On Being Sane in Insane Places' in the journal Science. He and seven others, all psychologically healthy, presented themselves at twelve different psychiatric hospitals in five US states, complaining of a single symptom: hearing a voice that said words such as 'empty', 'hollow' and 'thud'. Once admitted, they acted normally and said that the voice had stopped.

All were admitted. Seven received a diagnosis of schizophrenia, one of manic-depressive psychosis. Stays lasted between seven and fifty-two days, about nineteen on average, and all were discharged with a diagnosis of 'in remission', never as healthy. Rosenhan reported that the staff interpreted ordinary behaviour through the label: a nurse recorded that a pseudopatient's note-taking was 'writing behaviour'. By contrast, a number of real patients suspected the pseudopatients of being researchers or journalists.

Part 2 — The second experiment

Before you read: In the second part of the study, staff were told to look for fake patients, but none were sent. What do you predict happened?

In a second part of the study, Rosenhan told staff at a research hospital that, over the next three months, one or more pseudopatients would seek admission. Each member of staff was asked to rate new patients on how likely they were to be impostors. Of 193 patients, 41 were judged to be impostors by at least one member of staff. In fact, no pseudopatients had been sent.

The paper had a large impact. It was cited as evidence that psychiatric diagnosis was unreliable and context-dependent, and it contributed to the movement away from the loosely defined diagnoses of the time. The 1980 edition of the American diagnostic manual introduced explicit criteria, designed to make diagnoses more consistent between clinicians. Rosenhan's study is often listed among the factors that prompted the change.

Part 3 — The investigation

Before you read: What is the difference between a diagnosis that is reliable and one that is valid?

Critics had questioned the study from the start. The psychiatrist Robert Spitzer argued that the pseudopatients had presented a symptom that would, in a real patient, justify admission, and that the result showed hospitals behaving cautiously more than being unable to tell sane from insane. The diagnosis of 'in remission' could be read as a sign of care rather than failure.

In 2019, the journalist Susannah Cahalan published a book after examining Rosenhan's archives and trying to trace the pseudopatients. She found that she could identify only a few of them, that some details in the published account did not match his own notes, and that Rosenhan had perhaps shaped his data. The study's reputation has since declined, but its main questions remain: how reliable are diagnoses, how much do labels shape perception, and who has the authority to decide what is normal?

Indirect and softened questions

In sensitive conversations, direct questions can sound blunt. Indirect questions are more polite and give people room to answer.

Could you tell me…? / Would you mind telling me…? + statement word order. Could you tell me where you live? (not where do you live).
I was wondering whether / if… — I was wondering whether you have been sleeping well.
Yes/no questions: use if or whether. Do you know if the clinic is open?
Word order after the opening phrase is the same as a statement.

Reliable or valid?

Five situations. Say whether each is mainly a question of reliability (consistency) or validity (does the label match something real), or both, then phrase one question you would ask using an indirect question. Check the guidance last.

1. Two clinicians give different diagnoses for the same person.Could you tell me…
Reliability. A question of consistency. Structured interviews and explicit criteria reduce disagreement.
2. A diagnosis is consistent between clinicians, but it does not predict treatment response.I was wondering whether…
Validity. Agreement is not enough; the category may not match a real difference.
3. A clinician writes 'hostile' about a client who is simply frightened.Would you mind telling me…
Interpretation. A label that goes beyond the observation; asking 'What did you see and hear?' separates fact from interpretation.
4. A client asks, 'Will this label follow me?'Do you know if…
Stigma. Honest, careful answers: describe what the diagnosis is for, who can see it and how it can change.
5. A culture treats a behaviour as normal that another considers a symptom.Could you tell me…
Cultural context. Diagnosis depends on norms; clinicians need to ask how the person and their community understand the experience.
