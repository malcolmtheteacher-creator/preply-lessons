Marie Curie

The woman who named radioactivity — and paid for it with her life.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of a scientist who broke every rule about what a woman could do — and discuss the price of discovery — using relative and purpose clauses and the language of science and ambition.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. As a young woman she was barred from the university in her own city — the doors were simply closed to her because she was female.
to be barred (from) (v, often passive) — to be officially prevented from entering a place or joining something.
2. Some materials give off invisible rays all by themselves; she invented a word for this strange property and called it radioactivity.
radioactivity (n) — the giving-off of energy as rays or particles from the unstable core of certain atoms.
3. To find the new element, she had to boil down tonnes of rock and isolate a tiny, pure speck of it from everything else.
to isolate (v) — to separate one pure substance out from a mixture so you have it on its own.
4. For four years they worked in a leaking, freezing shed, and her perseverance never wavered, no matter how slow the results.
perseverance (n) — the quality of continuing to try hard, despite difficulty or how long it takes.
5. Discovery is never quite finished; every answer she found simply opened a fresh frontier of questions no one had ever asked.
a frontier (n) — the edge of what is known; the furthest point that knowledge has so far reached.
6. She refused to patent her methods, choosing instead to share them freely for the benefit of all humanity.
benefit (n) — an advantage or good thing that helps people; (v) to do good to / to gain from.
7. The same rays that could destroy a tumour could also harm healthy cells, so the discovery was a true double-edged sword.
a double-edged sword (idiom) — something that has both a good side and a dangerous or harmful side.
8. When war broke out she did not stay safely in the lab; she chose to mobilise her science, driving X-ray units to the front line.
to mobilise (v) — to organise people or resources and put them into active service, especially in wartime.
9. Year after year the radiation built up in her body, an invisible toll that no one yet understood how to measure.
a toll (n) — the cost or damage that something causes, especially over a long time.
10. Long after her death her achievements still shape medicine and physics — an enduring legacy that has not faded in a hundred years.
enduring (adj) — lasting a very long time; continuing in strength without weakening.

  • Using any three of the words, predict: what kind of life are we about to read about, and what might it cost the person who lived it?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A door that would not open

She was born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw in 1867, at a time when Poland did not appear on any map — her country had been swallowed by the Russian Empire, and even her language and history were forbidden in school. She was brilliant, hungry to learn, and completely barred from the path her mind deserved: the University of Warsaw did not admit women.

So she learned in secret. Maria joined what students called the "Flying University" — illegal evening classes that moved from flat to flat so that the authorities could never catch them. To reach the real laboratories of Paris, she made a pact with her sister: Maria would work as a governess and send money so that Bronisława could study medicine first, and in return her sister would later support her. For years she taught other people's children by day and studied physics alone by night.

In 1891, at last, she boarded a train for Paris and enrolled at the Sorbonne. She was so poor that she sometimes fainted from hunger at her desk, yet she finished top of her class in physics. There she met a quiet, gifted scientist named Pierre Curie, who shared both her laboratory and, soon, her life. When they married she chose to be known as Marie Skłodowska-Curie — keeping the Polish name of the country that had refused to teach her.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why couldn't Maria study at university in Warsaw, and how did she get an education anyway?

2. What deal did she make with her sister, and where did it finally take her?

1. The University of Warsaw did not admit women (and Poland was under Russian rule, which restricted Polish learning), so she studied secretly at the illegal "Flying University," whose classes moved location to avoid the authorities.

2. She worked as a governess to fund her sister Bronisława's medical studies in Paris first, on the understanding that her sister would later help her — which eventually let Maria study physics at the Sorbonne.

Discuss:
  • She was kept out of education simply because she was a woman, and she found a way around it. What does her story suggest about how much talent the world has wasted by closing doors?

Part 2 — The shed and the new elements

In 1896 another scientist, Henri Becquerel, noticed that uranium gave off mysterious rays on its own. Marie made this puzzle her own. Measuring the rays with great care, she realised the energy came from inside the atoms themselves — a genuinely revolutionary idea — and she invented a word for the phenomenon: radioactivity. Then she went further. Studying a black ore called pitchblende, she found it was far more radioactive than its uranium alone could explain. Something unknown had to be hidden inside it.

To prove it, Marie and Pierre worked for four years in a freezing, leaking shed, stirring boiling cauldrons of ore by hand to isolate the tiny amounts of new material it contained. Their perseverance was rewarded twice over. They announced one new element and named it polonium, after Poland — a quiet act of patriotism on the world stage. Then they found a second, far more powerful one, which glowed faintly in the dark: radium.

In 1903 the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded for the work on radioactivity. At first the committee planned to honour only Becquerel and Pierre; Pierre protested until Marie was named alongside them, and so she became the first woman ever to win a Nobel Prize. When Pierre was killed in a street accident in 1906, she took over his professorship — the first woman to teach at the Sorbonne — and pushed on alone. In 1911 she won a second Nobel Prize, this time in Chemistry, for isolating pure radium. To this day she remains the only person to have won Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Marie realise about where the rays came from, and what two new elements did the Curies discover?

2. What makes her two Nobel Prizes so remarkable?

1. She realised the energy came from inside the atoms themselves (and coined the word "radioactivity"). Studying pitchblende, the Curies isolated two new elements: polonium (named after Poland) and radium.

2. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize (Physics, 1903), and with her Chemistry prize in 1911 she became the only person ever to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

Discuss:
  • She named her first element "polonium" after a country that, at the time, did not officially exist. Why might a scientist use a discovery to make a political point?

Part 3 — The price of the light

Radium seemed like magic. It glowed; it could shrink tumours; for a while the public even put it in toothpaste and face cream. But it was a double-edged sword, and Marie had handled it with her bare hands for decades, carrying glowing tubes in her pockets simply to admire their light. When the First World War came, she refused to sit it out. She mobilised her science, designing mobile X-ray units — soldiers nicknamed them the "petites Curies," the little Curies — and driving them to the front herself, where they helped surgeons locate bullets and shrapnel in over a million wounded men.

All the while, the radiation was taking its toll. The very rays she had given the world were quietly destroying her own body. In 1934 she died of aplastic anaemia, a disease of the blood almost certainly caused by a lifetime of exposure to the materials she loved. She had never sought to grow rich from her work — she refused to patent the process for producing radium, insisting it belonged to everyone.

Her legacy proved enduring beyond anything she could have imagined. Radiotherapy still treats cancer today; her name marks elements, institutes and hospitals across the world; in 1995 she became the first woman laid to rest in the Panthéon in Paris for her own achievements. And there is one strange, haunting echo: her notebooks are still so radioactive that they are kept in lead-lined boxes, and anyone who wishes to read them must sign a waiver. A century on, her work has not stopped giving off light.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Marie use her science during the First World War?

2. What finally killed her, and what makes her notebooks famous today?

1. She designed and drove mobile X-ray units (the "petites Curies") to the battlefront, helping surgeons find bullets and shrapnel in over a million wounded soldiers.

2. She died in 1934 of aplastic anaemia, caused by decades of radiation exposure. Her notebooks remain so radioactive that they are stored in lead-lined boxes and readers must sign a waiver.

Discuss:
  • The very discovery that made her famous also killed her, and she gave it away for free. Does that make her a hero, a cautionary tale, or both?

The grammar this story runs on: relative & purpose clauses

A biography constantly adds extra information about people and things, and explains why someone acted. Two structures do almost all of that work — and the story above is full of both.

The two tools:
• Relative clauses add information about a noun, using who (people), which / that (things), whose (possession) and where (places): "the scientist who discovered radium," "a shed where they worked for four years."
• Purpose clauses answer "why?" — they express the goal of an action, using to + infinitive, in order to, or so that + clause: "she worked in order to isolate radium," "classes moved so that the police could not catch them."

1. Relative clauses — join the two sentences

Combine each pair into one sentence using who, which, that, whose or where.

1. She studied at the Flying University. The classes there moved from flat to flat.
→ She studied at the Flying University classes moved from flat to flat.

2. Marie met Pierre Curie. He shared her laboratory.
→ Marie met Pierre Curie, shared her laboratory.

3. They worked in a shed. The shed was freezing and leaked.
→ They worked in a shed was freezing and leaked.

1. ...the Flying University, whose classes moved... (possession — the classes belong to the university)

2. ...Pierre Curie, who shared her laboratory. (a person)

3. ...a shed which / that was freezing and leaked. (a thing)

2. Purpose clauses — answer "why?"

Complete each sentence so that it explains the goal of the action. Use to, in order to, or so that.

1. The classes kept changing location the authorities could not catch them.

2. Marie boiled tonnes of ore isolate a tiny amount of radium.

3. She drove X-ray units to the front surgeons could find bullets in the wounded.

1. so that the authorities could not catch them (a new subject + clause → "so that")

2. to / in order to isolate a tiny amount (same subject → infinitive)

3. so that surgeons could find bullets (different subject → "so that")

3. Put them together — one sentence, both clauses

Fill each gap with the relative word or purpose link the meaning needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

Marie Curie, won two Nobel Prizes, refused to patent the process radium could belong to everyone. She named the element polonium after the country she was born, and she handled the materials would one day kill her understand them better.

who (person) · so that (purpose, new subject) · where (place) · which / that (thing) · to / in order to (purpose, same subject)

Notice how relative clauses pack in extra detail about a noun, while purpose clauses tell you the reason behind the action.
