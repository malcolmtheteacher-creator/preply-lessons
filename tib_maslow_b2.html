The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — The boy in the library

Abraham Maslow was born in Brooklyn, New York, in 1908, the eldest of seven children. His parents were Jewish immigrants from Kyiv, in what was then the Russian Empire. The family was poor, and the streets could be unfriendly to Jewish boys. Maslow later described his childhood as lonely and unhappy, and his relationship with his mother as very difficult.

He found his escape in public libraries, where he read everything he could. At university, he fell in love with psychology. He studied at the University of Wisconsin, where he worked with the psychologist Harry Harlow, who studied monkeys. Watching the animals, Maslow began to wonder which needs come first, and which only matter once others have been met. The question stayed with him for the rest of his life.

Most psychologists of the time studied what goes wrong with people — illness, fear, strange behaviour. Maslow grew more interested in a different question: what do healthy people need in order to grow?

1. From Kyiv, then part of the Russian Empire.

2. Which needs come first, and which only matter once other needs are met.

3. Most studied what goes wrong with people; Maslow wanted to know what healthy people need to grow.

Part 2 — Five sets of needs

In 1943, in the middle of the Second World War, Maslow published a paper called “A Theory of Human Motivation”. In it, he described five groups of needs. First come the needs of the body: food, water, sleep, warmth. Next comes safety: shelter, order, a life without constant danger. Then love and belonging: family, friends, a group. Then esteem: respect from others and for yourself. And finally, what he called self-actualisation: becoming the fullest version of yourself.

His key idea was that a need we have satisfied stops motivating us. A person who is starving thinks only about food; a person who is well fed begins to care about safety, then friendship, and so on. But Maslow was careful. He said that the levels overlap, that most people are partly satisfied on several levels at once, and that the order is not the same for everyone.

To find out what the top level looked like, he studied people he admired, such as Abraham Lincoln, Eleanor Roosevelt and Albert Einstein. They seemed, he wrote, to be creative, independent and deeply connected to others.

3. What did Maslow say about the order of the levels?

1. Body needs, safety, love and belonging, esteem, self-actualisation.

2. It stops motivating us.

3. That the levels overlap, people are partly satisfied on several at once, and the order isn't the same for everyone.

Part 3 — Someone else's triangle

In his 1943 paper, and in everything he wrote later, Maslow never drew a pyramid. The famous triangle seems to have appeared first in 1960, in a business magazine article called “How Money Motivates Men” by a consulting psychologist, Charles McDermid. McDermid wanted to help managers get the most effort from their workers at the lowest cost, and a pyramid was a perfect picture for a training room.

Managers loved it. Within a few years, the pyramid was in textbooks all over the world, and most people assumed Maslow had drawn it. But the neat shape made his ideas more rigid than he meant them to be. It suggested that you must complete one level before you can climb to the next, which is exactly what Maslow had warned against.

Maslow himself kept developing his theory. Before he died in 1970, he wrote about a level beyond self-actualisation: self-transcendence, meaning living for something bigger than yourself. Critics still argue about the model. Some point out that people living through war or poverty often put friendship, faith and meaning first. Yet the pyramid survives, perhaps because it is so easy to draw.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Who first drew the pyramid, and in what kind of publication?

1. Charles McDermid, a consulting psychologist, in a 1960 business magazine article.

2. It made the ideas more rigid — it suggested you must complete each level before moving up, which Maslow didn't say.

3. Self-transcendence: living for something bigger than yourself.

Discuss:

How the tool works

The pyramid puts the most basic needs at the bottom and growth at the top. Read it as a rough guide, not a staircase: the levels overlap, and different people put them in a different order.

Using it, step by step

  1. Choose a person or a group — yourself, a team, a class, a community.
  2. Go up the levels and ask, for each: how well is this need met?
  3. Look for the lowest level with a real gap. Problems higher up are hard to solve while that gap is open.
  4. Plan something for that level — and remember that meeting higher needs, like belonging, can also help people through hard times.

Try it yourself

Where the tool breaks

Not a staircase. People don't finish one level before starting the next — Maslow said so himself.

Culture changes the order. In many societies, family and belonging come before personal achievement.

Hard to test. Researchers have found only partial support for a fixed order of needs.

Talk it through:
  • Would you change the order of the levels for yourself? How?
  • Why might managers have liked the pyramid so much in 1960?

Grammar — verb + -ing or to + verb

Talking about needs and motives means using verbs like want, avoid, hope, enjoy — and each one is followed by a particular form. A few verbs take both, with a change in meaning.

Verb + to + verb: want, need, hope, decide, plan, agree, refuse, manage, afford, expect, learn.
“We decided to rent a smaller flat.”  “She refused to sign the contract.”
Verb + -ing: enjoy, avoid, finish, mind, keep, consider, suggest, practise, can't stand, give up.
“He keeps checking his phone.”  “Would you mind closing the window?”
Both, with a different meaning:
stop doing (end an activity) vs stop to do (pause in order to do something): “We stopped to buy petrol.”
remember doing (a memory of the past) vs remember to do (not forget a task): “Remember to lock the door.”
try doing (an experiment) vs try to do (make an effort): “Try turning it off and on again.”
After a preposition, always -ing: “She's good at listening.”  “I'm tired of waiting.”
