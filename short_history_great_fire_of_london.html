The Great Fire of London

How a baker's oven became four days that remade a city.

Today: you'll tell the story of a disaster that destroyed a city and the calmer one that rebuilt it — using narrative tenses and the language of fire, panic and recovery.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The buildings were packed so tightly together that a single flame could leap from one roof to the next and start a wider blaze.
a blaze (n) — a large, fierce, very bright fire.
2. With the wind pushing it forward, the fire began to spread, reaching streets that had been perfectly safe an hour before.
to spread (v) — to grow and move outwards, covering a larger and larger area.
3. The dry summer had left the timber houses parched — as light and thirsty as old paper, ready to catch at the smallest spark.
parched (adj) — extremely dry, with all the moisture gone.
4. Many roofs were coated in pitch, a black, sticky, tar-like substance that kept out the rain but burned ferociously.
pitch (n) — a thick, dark, sticky substance like tar, used for waterproofing; highly flammable.
5. The leaders hoped to create a firebreak — a deliberate gap with nothing left to burn — so the flames would run out of fuel and stop.
a firebreak (n) — a cleared strip of land or empty space made to stop a fire from advancing.
6. Faced with an emergency, the official simply hesitated, unsure whether to act, while the precious minutes slipped away.
to hesitate (v) — to pause or delay because you are uncertain about what to do.
7. To make the gaps, they used gunpowder to blow up whole houses, bringing them crashing down before the fire could reach them.
gunpowder (n) — an explosive powder; here, used to demolish buildings deliberately.
8. By the time it was over, much of the medieval city lay in ruins — blackened walls and rubble where streets had stood.
ruins (n, pl) — the broken, destroyed remains of buildings.
9. Once the danger had passed, the slow work to rebuild the city could finally begin, brick by brick.
to rebuild (v) — to build something again after it has been destroyed or damaged.
10. A tall stone column was raised as a monument — a public reminder, in stone, of everything the city had lost and survived.
a monument (n) — a statue, column or building put up to remember a person or event.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A spark in Pudding Lane

It began, as great disasters often do, with something small. In the early hours of Sunday 2 September 1666, a fire broke out in the bakery of Thomas Farriner on Pudding Lane, near the River Thames. Farriner was a respected baker who supplied bread to the Royal Navy, and that night his ovens had not been fully put out. Smoke filled the upstairs rooms; Farriner and his daughter escaped across the rooftops, but a frightened maid would not follow, and she became one of the first to die.

On its own, a house fire was nothing unusual in London. But the conditions that night were close to perfect for catastrophe. The summer had been long and dry, and the city's wooden houses were parched. They leaned over the narrow streets, almost touching, their roofs coated in pitch. And a strong, dry wind was blowing from the east. Within an hour the flames had taken hold of the neighbouring houses and a riverside full of warehouses stuffed with oil, tar, timber and brandy — and the blaze began to spread.

The Lord Mayor, Sir Thomas Bloodworth, was woken and brought to the scene. The obvious answer was to pull down houses in the fire's path at once, before it could grow. But Bloodworth hesitated. Tearing down homes was expensive and unpopular, and he could not see the danger. Famously dismissive — he is said to have sneered that a woman could put the fire out — he went back to bed. By the time anyone grasped what was happening, the chance to stop it early had gone.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where and how did the fire start, and why was a small fire able to become a huge one?

2. What decision did Sir Thomas Bloodworth get wrong, and why did it matter?

1. It started in Thomas Farriner's bakery on Pudding Lane in the early hours of 2 September 1666, from ovens that weren't fully put out. A long dry summer, parched wooden houses crowded close together with pitch-covered roofs, riverside warehouses full of flammable goods, and a strong east wind let it spread fast.

2. He hesitated instead of pulling down houses early to make firebreaks; he dismissed the danger and went back to bed, so the one chance to stop the fire while it was small was lost.

Discuss:
  • Bloodworth failed to act because the threat didn't look serious yet. Why is it so hard for people in charge to take a small problem seriously before it becomes a disaster?

Part 2 — Four days of fire

For four days the fire ruled the city. Driven by the wind, it moved relentlessly westward, jumping from roof to roof and street to street, far faster than anyone could fight it. London had no fire brigade — only neighbours with leather buckets and hand-pumps that were useless against a blaze on this scale. Panic took hold. People fled with whatever they could carry, clogging the gates and crowding onto boats on the Thames, while the sky turned the colour of dull copper and burning fragments rained down across the river.

Among those watching was Samuel Pepys, a senior naval official whose diary would become the most vivid record we have. He hurried to warn the King, climbed a church tower to see the scale of it, and watched the pigeons circle their burning homes until their wings caught fire. With the danger closing on his own house, he and a neighbour dug a pit in the garden and buried their most precious things — Pepys famously saving his wine and a prized Parmesan cheese from the flames.

At last the authorities did what should have been done on the first night. With the King and his brother now directing the effort, sailors used gunpowder to blow up whole rows of houses, creating firebreaks the flames could not cross. Then, on the fourth day, the wind eased. Starved of fuel and no longer fanned forward, the great blaze finally began to die down — leaving the heart of London in smoking ruins.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the city so unable to fight the fire in those four days?

2. What two things finally brought the fire under control?

1. There was no fire brigade — only neighbours with leather buckets and weak hand-pumps — and the wind drove the fire from roof to roof faster than anyone could fight it, while panic spread.

2. Sailors used gunpowder to blow up rows of houses and create firebreaks, and on the fourth day the wind eased, so the fire ran out of fuel and died down.

Discuss:
  • Pepys buried his wine and his cheese. If you had minutes to save a few possessions from a fire, what would you choose, and what does that say about what we really value?

Part 3 — Out of the ashes

The numbers were staggering. Around 13,200 houses were gone, along with 87 parish churches, the Royal Exchange, and the old St Paul's Cathedral, whose lead roof had melted and run down the streets in rivers. Tens of thousands of Londoners were left homeless, camping in the fields around the city. And yet — strangely — the official records list only a handful of deaths. Historians suspect the true number was higher, since the poor often went uncounted and the heat was fierce enough to leave no trace; but by the standards of such a catastrophe, the loss of life was remarkably small.

In the shock that followed, people looked for someone to blame. Rumours spread that foreigners or Catholics had started the fire deliberately, and an innocent Frenchman, Robert Hubert, made a false confession under pressure and was hanged for a crime he could not have committed. It was a grim reminder of how fear turns a frightened public against outsiders.

But out of the ruins came something extraordinary. The architect Sir Christopher Wren was given the task of rebuilding, and over the following decades a new London rose in brick and stone instead of timber — most gloriously his masterpiece, the new St Paul's Cathedral. Near the spot where it all began, Wren and Robert Hooke raised a towering stone column, simply called the Monument, which still stands today. The disaster had remade the city; the echoes of those four days are built into London's streets even now.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. The fire destroyed a vast amount — so why is the recorded death toll surprising?

2. What changed about London because of the fire, and who led the rebuilding?

1. Around 13,200 houses, 87 churches and old St Paul's were destroyed and tens of thousands were left homeless, yet only a handful of deaths were officially recorded — surprisingly few, though historians think the real figure was probably higher because the poor went uncounted.

2. The city was rebuilt in brick and stone instead of timber; Sir Christopher Wren led the rebuilding, including the new St Paul's Cathedral, and the Monument was raised near where the fire began.

Discuss:
  • After the fire, a frightened public turned on outsiders and an innocent man was hanged. Why do disasters so often make people look for someone to blame rather than something to fix?

The grammar this story runs on: narrative tenses

A disaster story like this needs three past tenses working together. Get them right and your storytelling sounds effortless; get them wrong and the timeline collapses.

The three tools:
• Past simple — the main events, in sequence: "a fire broke out," "the wind eased."
• Past continuous — the background, the longer action in progress: "the fire was spreading while people slept."
• Past perfect — a step further back, something that happened before another past event: "by the fourth day the flames had destroyed most of the city."

1. Past simple vs past continuous — event against background

Use the past continuous for the longer action already in progress, and the past simple for the shorter event that interrupts or sits inside it.

1. While most of the city (sleep), the fire quietly (take) hold of Pudding Lane.

2. The blaze (still / spread) westward when the wind finally (ease) on the fourth day.

3. As Pepys (watch) from the tower, burning fragments (rain) down across the river.

1. was sleeping / took (background in progress / the event)

2. was still spreading / eased (longer action interrupted by a sudden change)

3. was watching / rained

2. Past perfect — the step further back

When you are already telling the past, and you reach back to something earlier still, use had + past participle.

1. By the time Bloodworth arrived, the fire (already / reach) the riverside warehouses.

2. The houses burned so fiercely because the long dry summer (leave) them parched.

3. When the rebuilding began, the old St Paul's Cathedral (be) reduced to ruins.

4. They saved Pepys's wine because he (bury) it in the garden the night before.

1. had already reached

2. had left

3. had been

4. had buried

3. Put them together — narrate a paragraph

Fill each gap with the tense the meaning needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

In the early hours, Farriner (sleep) above his bakery when smoke (wake) him. His ovens (not / go) out properly, and a dry wind (blow) hard from the east. Within an hour the flames (reach) the next street, and by morning the fire (become) impossible to stop.

was sleeping (background) · woke (event) · had not gone (earlier cause) · was blowing (background) · reached / had reached (the next event) · had become (the result by that point)

Notice how the three tenses layer the time: the long background, the sudden events, and the things that had already happened before.
