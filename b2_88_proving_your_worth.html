Language for Presenting Your Case

When you need to convince someone that you are qualified for a role, these phrases help you sound confident and professional.

Talking about experience

"I have extensive experience in [area] — over [X] years."

"Throughout my career, I have consistently demonstrated [skill/quality]."

"My track record shows that I can deliver results at a senior level."

Talking about qualifications

"My qualification is equivalent to [local standard] and has been formally recognised by [body]."

"I completed my training at [institution], which is accredited by [organisation]."

"I have undergone additional training in [area] to meet the requirements."

Expressing frustration professionally

"I understand the process, but I feel my experience should speak for itself."

"It can be disheartening when you have to re-prove something you have already achieved."

"I am fully committed to completing whatever steps are needed, but I do find it frustrating."

What would you say?

Read each situation carefully. All the options are reasonable — but which one is the most effective for the context, and why?

Situation 1

You are in a formal review meeting. The panel says: "We can see you have been in this role for some time. Help us understand why you feel ready for the next step."

a) "I have extensive experience in this area — over seven years — and I have consistently demonstrated that I can work at a senior level."

b) "Throughout my career, I have consistently demonstrated strong analytical skills, and my track record shows I can deliver results independently."

c) "I have undergone additional training in leadership and project management to ensure I meet the requirements for this role."

Which response best answers the panel's question? What is each option missing?

All three are professional, but they answer different questions. (b) is arguably the strongest here because the panel asked why you feel ready — (b) addresses capability and evidence directly. (a) leads with time served, which is less persuasive than results. (c) is good but focuses on preparation rather than readiness. The ideal answer would combine elements of all three. How would you merge them?

Situation 2

Your manager tells you informally: "Between us, I think you are more than capable. But the validation process is mandatory and there is nothing I can do to speed it up."

a) "I appreciate your support. It can be disheartening when you have to re-prove something you have already achieved, but I understand it is part of the process."

b) "I understand the process, but I feel my experience should speak for itself. Is there any way to fast-track this?"

c) "I am fully committed to completing whatever steps are needed. Could you advise me on the best way to approach the next stage?"

Your manager is an ally here. Which response makes the best use of that relationship?

This is nuanced. (a) is emotionally honest and builds rapport — your manager already agrees with you, so acknowledging the frustration is natural. (b) pushes back, which is fine, but the manager already said they cannot speed it up — so it risks sounding like you were not listening. (c) is the most strategic — it turns the conversation towards actionable next steps. There is no single right answer. What would you prioritise: emotional honesty, assertiveness, or strategy?

Situation 3

In a formal interview for the senior role, the panel asks: "Your qualification was obtained in a different country. How can you assure us it meets the standards we require?"

a) "My qualification is equivalent to the local standard and has been formally recognised by the relevant professional body."

b) "I completed my training at a highly respected institution, and I have since gained over five years of experience working within your system."

c) "I have undergone the full accreditation process, and I would also point to my track record — the results I have achieved here demonstrate that my training was more than adequate."

Which response would you trust most if you were on the panel? What makes it convincing?

(c) is the most complete — it addresses the formal requirement (accreditation) AND provides evidence (track record). (a) is factually correct but relies entirely on paperwork. (b) sidesteps the direct question about standards and pivots to experience, which could seem evasive. The strongest answers combine formal recognition with practical evidence. How would you answer this question about your own qualifications?
