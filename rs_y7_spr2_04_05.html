A Different Kind of Practice

Buddhism is different from many other religions. There is no God to pray to, no commands to follow, no holy laws to obey. Instead, the heart of Buddhism is meditation — training your mind. But what does "training your mind" mean? Let's find out.

Quick Experiment

Try this: Sit completely still for 30 seconds. Don't move. Don't speak. Just notice what your mind does. Does it stay quiet? Does it jump from thought to thought? Does it get bored? That's what meditation is about — noticing and training your mind.

Buddhist Meditation: The Core Practice

What is Meditation?

Meditation is the central practice in Buddhism. It's not prayer (talking to a god), and it's not reading a holy text. It's training your mind. Through meditation, Buddhists work toward enlightenment (understanding the true nature of reality) and reducing suffering.

The Main Types of Meditation

Samatha Meditation

Calm meditation. You focus on ONE thing — usually your breath. You watch your breath going in and out, in and out. The goal is samadhi: deep concentration. Like training a muscle. When your mind wanders (and it will), you gently bring it back to the breath.

Vipassana Meditation

Insight meditation. You observe your thoughts, feelings, and sensations WITHOUT reacting to them. You notice them like clouds passing in the sky — you see them, but you don't grab onto them or push them away. This is how you see reality as it truly is, which is the path to enlightenment.

Metta Bhavana

Loving-kindness meditation. You systematically send good wishes toward yourself, someone you love, a neutral person, someone you find difficult, and finally all beings. The goal is to develop compassion and reduce hatred or anger.

Zazen (Zen Meditation)

Sitting meditation. You sit in a precise posture, often facing a wall, with minimal instruction. Some Zen groups use koans — paradoxical riddles like "What is the sound of one hand clapping?" — to provoke insight.

Is Meditation Worship?

Click to reveal: Do Buddhists worship?

Buddhists don't pray TO a god. But in many Buddhist traditions, people DO make offerings at shrines: flowers, incense, and candles before a Buddha rupa (a statue of the Buddha). However, these are NOT worshipping the Buddha as a god. The Buddha rupa is a reminder of the goal — enlightenment. It's like a focal point, similar to how a Christian might look at a cross, not to worship the cross itself, but to remember Christ and their faith.

Buddhist Shrines and the Buddha Rupa

The Buddha Rupa

A Buddha rupa is a statue or image of the Buddha. In many Buddhist homes and temples, a shrine includes a Buddha rupa, flowers, candles, and incense. The Buddha himself taught that meditation was more important than worshipping him. But his image can serve as a focus and reminder.

Meditation and Modern Science

Here's something remarkable: modern neuroscience has discovered that regular meditation physically changes the brain. Brain scans show that people who meditate have:

  • Increased grey matter in areas responsible for attention, emotion regulation, and self-awareness
  • Lower levels of stress hormones like cortisol
  • Improved focus and attention span
  • Greater emotional resilience

Mindfulness in the UK: Mindfulness (which comes directly from Buddhist vipassana meditation) is now taught in over 2,000 UK schools. The NHS recommends it for people with recurring depression. This shows that Buddhist practices have benefits beyond religion — they have practical, measurable effects on health.

Meditation vs. Prayer: Key Differences

Prayer

Who it's to: A god or deity. Purpose: Asking for help, expressing gratitude, seeking guidance. Process: Usually involves words (spoken or thought). Outcome: Hoped-for answer or blessing.

Meditation

Who it's to: No one — it's a solo practice. Purpose: Train the mind, reduce suffering, gain insight. Process: Focusing on breath, observing thoughts, or developing compassion. Outcome: Greater peace, clarity, and understanding.

Key Terms

  • Meditation: Training the mind through focused concentration or mindful observation.
  • Samatha: Calm meditation — focusing on the breath to achieve deep concentration.
  • Vipassana: Insight meditation — observing thoughts and feelings without reacting.
  • Mindfulness: Being fully present and aware of your thoughts, feelings, and surroundings.
  • Zazen: Zen sitting meditation, often with minimal instruction.
  • Metta bhavana: Loving-kindness meditation — sending goodwill to yourself and others.
  • Mantra: A repeated word, phrase, or sound used in meditation.
  • Sangha: The Buddhist community of monks, nuns, and lay followers.
  • Vihara: A Buddhist monastery or temple.
  • Buddha rupa: A statue or image of the Buddha.

Activity 2: Prayer or Meditation?

Read each description. Is it closer to prayer (talking to God) or meditation (training the mind)? Some might be debatable — that's the point!

Description 1

"I sat quietly, watching my breath go in and out, and noticed my worry slowly fading away."

Answer: Meditation. This is mindful observation of the breath.

Description 2

"I asked God to help my friend get better from her illness."

Answer: Prayer. This is asking a deity for help.

Description 3

"I sat in the temple and felt peaceful looking at the statue of the Buddha."

Answer: Debatable. It could be meditation (focusing on peacefulness) or a form of devotion (feeling connected through the image).

Description 4

"I said 'thank you' over my food before eating."

Answer: Prayer (or close to it). This is expressing gratitude, which is part of prayer.

Description 5

"I spent 20 minutes observing my angry thoughts without trying to fight them or believe them."

Answer: Meditation. This is vipassana (insight meditation).

Description 6

"I repeated 'May all beings be happy and free from suffering' over and over."

Answer: Meditation (specifically metta bhavana). It's structured mind training, even though words are used.

Evaluating the Statement

Discuss Both Sides

"Meditation is more useful than prayer because it has scientific evidence behind it."

✓ FOR (Agree)

Neuroscience proves meditation changes the brain, reduces stress, improves focus. These benefits are measurable and universal — they work whether you're religious or not. Prayer relies on faith; meditation relies on brain biology.

✗ AGAINST (Disagree)

Prayer also has benefits: it brings comfort, community, hope, and meaning. Many studies show prayer reduces anxiety and increases wellbeing. Using "scientific evidence" as the only measure of value is too narrow. Religion isn't just about measurable outcomes — it's about connection and purpose.
