SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B1/B2 · 50 min

The Lumber Room

Saki's story of a boy in disgrace, a locked room, and an aunt who is certain she is right.

Today: you'll tell the story of a boy who is punished for telling the truth — and you'll stop confusing bored with boring, fascinated with fascinating, for good.
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • Have you ever been punished for something you didn't do — or been right about something and not believed? What happened?
  • Why do some adults find it so hard to admit that a young person was right?
  • What's the most interesting place you were not allowed into as a child?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four short activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. Nobody spoke to him at breakfast, he was not allowed to go with the others, and everyone made a point of looking past him. He was in disgrace.
disgrace (n) — the state of having lost everyone's approval because of something you did. "He was in disgrace for a week."
2. The room at the end of the corridor was always locked. Old furniture, rolled-up carpets and boxes nobody had opened for years were stored in it. It was the lumber room.
a lumber room (n) — a room used for storing old furniture and things no longer wanted. (Today we'd more often say a box room or a storage room.)
3. She did not shout. She simply announced, in a flat voice that allowed no argument, that the matter was closed. Her tone was completely self-satisfied.
self-satisfied (adj) — too pleased with yourself and your own rightness. Near neighbours: smug, superior.

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: treat · punishment · tapestry. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means something enjoyable that you arrange especially, often as a reward?
a treat (n) — "The trip to the beach was a treat." Also a verb: to treat someone to something.
2. Which word means something unpleasant done to you because you did something wrong?
punishment (n) — from the verb to punish. "The punishment was to stay at home."
3. Which word means a heavy cloth picture, woven in colours, hung on a wall?
a tapestry (n) — a picture made by weaving coloured threads, usually hung on a wall.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. The aunt was determined to catch him going into the garden, and watched the gate for an hour. Determined means…
  (a) having decided firmly and refusing to give up   (b) confused   (c) extremely tired
(a) — determined (adj) = firmly decided, not going to stop. Noun: determination.
2. She climbed onto the edge of the water tank, slipped, and found she could not get out. She was trapped. Trapped means…
  (a) embarrassed   (b) unable to escape from a place   (c) soaking wet
(b) — trapped (adj) = caught somewhere with no way out. "The cat was trapped on the roof."

D · Word building & word partners

1. You know to forbid. Complete: "The garden was ___ ground" (past participle as adjective), and the noun in "the ___ on going outside".
forbidden ground · the ban on going outside. Also useful: out of bounds = a place you are not allowed to enter.
2. One verb fits all three: to ___ a rule · to ___ a promise · to ___ the silence. And the noun for a short rest between lessons?
break — break a rule / a promise / the silence. The noun: a break.

Now use them — out loud

Talk it through before we read:
  • Using disgrace, forbidden, determined and trapped, predict what happens between a clever boy and a very certain aunt.
  • Which of today's words describe people, and which describe places or things? Sort them out loud.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The frog in the bread and milk

At breakfast, Nicholas announced that there was a frog in his bread and milk, and refused to eat it. Older and wiser people told him that such a thing was impossible and that he was talking nonsense. He went on repeating it calmly. The remarkable part, which nobody considered, was that there really was a frog in his bread and milk. He had put it there himself, and he felt he was in the best possible position to know.

The sin of taking a frog from the garden and putting it into a bowl of bread and milk was bad enough. Being right about it in front of adults who had explained that he could not possibly be right was very much worse. That afternoon the other children were taken to the sands at Jagborough as a treat, and Nicholas was to stay at home. The aunt who had organised everything explained the arrangement carefully, so that he would understand exactly what his wickedness had cost him.

She was not really an aunt at all — she was somebody else's aunt who had appointed herself to the position. She was quite certain that Nicholas would spend the afternoon trying to get into the gooseberry garden, which was strictly forbidden, so she spent much of it standing where she could watch the small door that led into it, glancing up from her gardening every few minutes. Nicholas had no intention of going anywhere near the gooseberry garden, but he made two or three careful approaches to the door, which kept her at her post for over an hour.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Nicholas say at breakfast, and what was surprising about it?

2. What is his punishment, and what does the aunt spend the afternoon doing?

1. That there was a frog in his bread and milk. The adults told him it was impossible — and he was right, because he had put it there himself.

2. He is left at home while the other children are taken to the beach. The aunt guards the door of the gooseberry garden for over an hour, convinced he is trying to get in.

Discuss:
  • He is punished less for the frog than for being right in front of adults who said he was wrong. Does that ring true to you?

Part 2 — Behind the locked door

While the aunt guarded the gooseberry door, Nicholas quietly took the key he had been studying for weeks and let himself into the lumber room. It was a place of legend. Nobody was allowed in; questions about it were never answered; and therefore it had become, in his imagination, a room of wonders. For once, the reality was better than the imagining.

The room was crowded with things that had been put away rather than thrown away. There was a tapestry on a frame, showing a hunter who had just shot a stag with an arrow. It would have been an easy picture to understand, except that in the bushes behind him four wolves were coming through the trees. How many arrows did the hunter have left? Could he climb the tree beside him, and would the wolves be able to reach him there? Nicholas stood in front of it for a long time, working out what would happen next.

There was more: candlesticks shaped like snakes, a teapot in the form of a china duck, a sandalwood box packed with brown paper, and, best of all, a large book full of coloured pictures of birds — a mandarin duck, a toucan, a heron — birds he had never seen and had no name for. He was completely absorbed. And then, from the garden, he heard his aunt calling his name.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Nicholas get into the lumber room, and why is the room so exciting to him?

2. What is happening in the tapestry, and what questions does it raise for him?

1. With a key he had been quietly studying for weeks, while the aunt watched the wrong door. The room is forbidden and mysterious, so his imagination has filled it with wonders — and for once reality is even better.

2. A hunter has shot a stag, but four wolves are coming through the trees behind him. Nicholas wants to know how many arrows are left and whether the hunter could escape up the tree.

Discuss:
  • He doesn't just look at the tapestry — he asks what happens next. What is he actually doing, and why does the story spend so long on it?

Part 3 — "You are the Evil One"

The aunt had gone into the gooseberry garden to hunt for him, slipped on the wet edge of the rainwater tank, and could not climb out. Her voice came up thin and cross, ordering him to fetch the ladder from beside the cherry tree.

Nicholas walked into the garden and looked at her. "Am I allowed to be in the gooseberry garden?" he asked. "I told you not to," she said, from the tank. "Now bring the ladder." "Will there be strawberry jam for tea?" he asked. "Certainly," she said. "Why do you ask?" "Because," said Nicholas, "you told me this morning there was none — and there are four jars of it in the cupboard. You know it and I know it. So you are not my aunt. You are the Evil One, pretending to be her." And he walked away, explaining pleasantly that since she had told him only last week that there was no such thing as the Evil One, he could hardly be blamed for believing in one now.

She was found, eventually, by the kitchen maid who came out for parsley. Tea that evening was silent and heavy. The trip to Jagborough had been a disaster: the tide was fully in, so there had been no sand to play on at all, and one of the children had spent the whole afternoon in boots that were too tight. The aunt sat in the frozen silence of someone who has been badly treated in a way she cannot explain to anyone. And Nicholas said nothing either, because he was busy: he was still deciding whether the hunter, with two arrows left and the light going, would get away from the wolves.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What has happened to the aunt, and what does Nicholas ask her?

2. How does he justify walking away — and why is the tea silent?

1. She has slipped into the rainwater tank and cannot get out. He asks whether he is allowed in the garden, and whether there will be strawberry jam for tea.

2. She says there will be jam, though that morning she said there was none — so, he announces, she must be the Evil One pretending to be his aunt. He adds that she herself told him the Evil One does not exist. Tea is silent because the beach trip failed, the aunt has been humiliated, and Nicholas is lost in the tapestry.

Discuss:
  • He beats her using her own rules and her own words. Is that fair play, or is he as cold as she is?

Grammar — -ing and -ed adjectives

This story is full of them: a fascinating room and a fascinated boy; a bored child and a boring afternoon. Mix them up and you say something very different from what you meant.

The rule, in one line:
• -ing describes the thing — what it does to you: "The film was terrifying."
• -ed describes the person — how you feel: "I was terrified."

So: "The lesson was confusing, so the students were confused." The cause takes -ing; the feeling takes -ed.
The classic mistake: "I am boring" means you send other people to sleep. You almost certainly mean "I am bored".
The common pairs: interesting/interested · surprising/surprised · exciting/excited · frightening/frightened · disappointing/disappointed · exhausting/exhausted · annoying/annoyed · embarrassing/embarrassed · amazing/amazed.

What follows them: -ed adjectives usually take by, with or at: "amazed by the view", "annoyed with my brother", "surprised at how easy it was". And -ing adjectives can take intensifiers: absolutely fascinating, utterly exhausting, slightly annoying.

1. Choose the right ending

Write the correct form of the adjective in brackets.

1. The lumber room was (fascinate) — far better than Nicholas had imagined.

2. Nicholas was completely (absorb) in the picture of the hunter and the wolves.

3. The trip to the beach was (disappoint), because the tide was in and there was no sand.

1. fascinating (the room causes the feeling)

2. absorbed (the boy feels it)

3. disappointing (the trip causes the feeling — the children were disappointed)

2. Both halves

Complete each sentence with the two forms of the same adjective.

1. (embarrass) Sitting in a water tank calling for help is — no wonder she was .

2. (annoy) The boy's polite questions were deeply , and his aunt grew more and more .

3. (exhaust) Guarding a door for an hour in the sun is , and she came in .

1. embarrassing / embarrassed   2. annoying / annoyed   3. exhausting / exhausted

Same word, two jobs: the situation gets -ing, the person gets -ed.

3. Retell the afternoon — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the correct form, then read the whole paragraph aloud.

The other children expected an (excite) afternoon at the sea, but the tide was in and they came home (bore) and cross. Nicholas, left at home, found something far more (interest) than a beach. He was (amaze) by a book of coloured birds, and (absorb) by a picture of a hunter in danger. His aunt, meanwhile, spent an (exhaust) hour guarding the wrong door, and ended the day (humiliate) in a water tank.

exciting · bored · interesting · amazed · absorbed · exhausting · humiliated

Check each one: is it the thing (-ing) or the person (-ed)?

Going deeper

Saki published this in 1914. He was raised, after his mother's death, by two strict aunts in England while his father worked abroad — and aunts in his stories are almost never kind. He turns that childhood into comedy, but the comedy has an edge you can feel.

The heart of it. The story sets two kinds of mind against each other. The aunt's world runs on rules, and she is so certain of them that she guards a door nobody wants to open. Nicholas's world runs on curiosity: he asks what happens next, invents questions nobody has set him, and reads a picture like a story. Notice what the lumber room actually contains — dull objects that adults have decided are finished with, which turn out to be a treasure house the moment somebody looks properly. And notice the ending. Saki does not give Nicholas a victory speech. He gives him something better: at the miserable tea table, the boy is not thinking about his aunt at all. He is somewhere else entirely, with a hunter, two arrows and four wolves.

Talk it through:
  • Why does the story make the aunt's punishment so undignified — a water tank, a kitchen maid, no apology from anyone?
  • The lumber room is full of "useless" things. What does the story suggest about who decides what is worth keeping?
  • Nicholas wins by using the aunt's own words against her. Is that clever, cruel, or simply what she taught him?
  • The last paragraph moves away from the argument and into the tapestry. Why end there — what would be lost if the story ended with the aunt in the tank?

Speak

This is the heart of the lesson — do all of it out loud. The timer is there to keep you going: fill the time with detail rather than racing through it.

Useful language: lean on today's adjectives — it was fascinating / I was fascinated, absolutely exhausting, slightly annoyed with, amazed by. For opinions: "The thing is…", "What I don't get is…", "To be fair to her…".

Task A · Tell the whole story (4 minutes)

Tell The Lumber Room in your own words. Move through the arc: the frog at breakfast → the punishment and the beach trip → the aunt guarding the wrong door → the key and the forbidden room → the tapestry and the book of birds → the voice from the water tank → the jam, the Evil One → the silent tea.

Use at least four -ing / -ed adjectives and at least five key words (disgrace, lumber room, self-satisfied, treat, punishment, tapestry, determined, trapped, forbidden, break a rule).

Task B · Should he have fetched the ladder? (4 minutes)

Take a side and argue it properly. He left an adult stuck in a water tank to make a point. Was that a fair reply to how he had been treated, or did he go too far? Give three reasons, deal with the strongest objection to your view, and say what you would have done standing in that garden.

Then argue the opposite side for one minute.

Task C · Rules, rooms and being right (4 minutes)

Talk about being told what to do — and about places that fire your imagination.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Describe a room, building or place that fascinated you as a child. Use at least three -ing adjectives.
  • A rule you think is pointless — and how you would explain that to the person who made it.
  • Invent your own lumber room: five objects in it, and the story behind one of them.
  • Look at the tapestry problem: a hunter, two arrows, four wolves, one tree. Tell us what happens next.
  • Should adults ever admit to a young person that they were wrong? What stops them?
  • Describe the most boring afternoon you can imagine, then the most exciting — carefully, using both adjective forms.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Nicholas is punished for being right. What does the story say about how adults defend their authority when it slips?
  2. Both the aunt and the boy spend the afternoon on something useless. Why does the story treat one of them as ridiculous and the other as free?
  3. Is imagination something you have, or something you practise? Where did Nicholas learn to look at a picture like that?
  4. Before you go — from memory: make one sentence with an -ing adjective and one with the matching -ed adjective about this story — and explain to someone learning English why "I am boring" is a dangerous sentence.
