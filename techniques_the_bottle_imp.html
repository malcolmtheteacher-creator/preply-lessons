The Bottle Imp

Robert Louis Stevenson's fable of a bottle that grants any wish — and the terrible rule that comes with owning it.

Now — the words for today's story

1. Inside the glass, a small imp curled and shifted like a living shadow — a mischievous little spirit that seemed to watch everyone who came near.
an imp (n) — a small, mischievous devil or spirit, often thought to cause trouble.
2. The old man swore the bottle would grant any wish its owner asked for — money, health, love, whatever the heart desired.
to grant (v) — to give someone what they ask for, especially a wish or a request.
3. There was a strict condition attached to the sale: the bottle had to be sold on for less than its owner had paid, or not at all.
a condition (n) — a rule that must be obeyed for something to happen or remain true.
4. If he died still owning the bottle, the old man warned, his soul would be damned — lost to hell, with no way back.
damned (adj) — condemned to hell; spiritually lost forever.
5. Keawe felt a stab of dread — a cold, heavy fear of what would happen if he could never pass the bottle on.
dread (n) — deep fear or anxiety about something that may happen.
6. The whole affair was a devil's bargain: extraordinary power in exchange for the risk of eternal ruin.
a bargain (n) — a deal or agreement; here, an exchange with a hidden or terrible cost.
7. Each time the bottle changed hands, its price had to diminish — falling from fifty dollars, to ten, to a single coin.
to diminish (v) — to become smaller, fewer or less over time.
8. As the price fell to almost nothing, selling the bottle became a plight from which there seemed no escape.
a plight (n) — a difficult, dangerous or unhappy situation that is hard to get out of.
9. Kokua would not stand by and watch her husband suffer; she was ready to sacrifice herself to save him.
to sacrifice (v) — to give up something precious, even your own safety, for the sake of someone or something else.
10. In the end, only a reckless man — one who cared nothing for his own soul — could take the bottle off their hands.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The bottle and its rule

Keawe was a poor but clever Hawaiian sailor. One day, wandering through the fine houses of San Francisco, he met a sad-faced old man who showed him a strange white bottle with something living moving inside it. "An imp lives in this bottle," the man said, "and it is the shadow you can see. Whoever owns the bottle can have anything they wish for — money, ships, houses, whatever they desire."

But there were conditions, and they were terrible. The owner could never simply throw the bottle away or destroy it; it always came back. If a man died while he still owned it, his soul would be damned to burn in hell forever. And there was one more rule, stranger than the rest: the bottle could only be sold for cash, and always for less than the owner had paid for it. Sell it for more, and it would return to you at once.

The old man was desperate to be rid of it. Keawe, tempted and half-doubting, bought the bottle for fifty dollars. He tested it, wished the money back into his pocket — and there it was. The imp was real. Keawe now owned the most dangerous object in the world, and the price he could sell it on for had already begun to fall.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What could the owner of the bottle wish for, and what were the two worst conditions of owning it?

2. What was the strange rule about selling the bottle on?

1. The owner could wish for anything (money, houses, ships). But the bottle could never be destroyed, and if you died still owning it, your soul would be damned to hell.

2. It could only be sold for cash, and always for less than you had paid for it — sell it for more and it came straight back.

Discuss:
  • Keawe bought the bottle knowing the rules. What is it about a wish granted instantly that makes people ignore the price?

Part 2 — The wish, the loss, and the falling price

Keawe wished for a beautiful house, and it appeared, exactly as he had imagined it. Frightened of the bottle now, he quickly sold it to a friend for forty-five dollars — a small loss — and walked away free. The friend sold it on for less, and so it passed from hand to hand, its price diminishing with every sale, each new owner shedding it as fast as they could.

Time passed, and Keawe fell in love with a lovely young woman named Kokua. His happiness was complete — until he discovered he had caught leprosy, a disease that would cost him his home, his love and his future. Only the bottle could cure him. In dread, he set out to find it again, tracing it from owner to owner. When at last he found it, the price had already fallen frighteningly low: it now cost only two cents.

Here was the trap in its full horror. Keawe could buy the bottle, wish himself well, and marry Kokua. But two cents is almost the smallest coin there is. To sell the bottle on, he would have to find a buyer willing to take it for one cent — and after that, no sale would be possible at all, for there is no coin smaller than one. Whoever held the bottle when the price could fall no further would keep it forever, and be damned. Keawe bought it, was cured, married Kokua — and carried the secret of his coming damnation like a stone in his chest.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Keawe have to go looking for the bottle again after he had got rid of it?

2. Why was the low price of two cents so terrifying rather than convenient?

1. He had caught leprosy, and only the bottle's wish could cure him and let him marry Kokua.

2. Because the price must always fall when it is sold. At two cents, there was almost no room left — soon no one could buy it for less, and the last owner would be damned forever.

Discuss:
  • The bottle's power and its danger are the same thing: the wish. Why does Stevenson make salvation and ruin come from a single object?

Part 3 — The sacrifice and the escape

Kokua saw her husband's secret misery and forced the truth from him. Rather than let Keawe be lost, she thought of a way out. In the French islands of Tahiti, they could use centimes — coins smaller than an American cent. There, the bottle could still be sold for less. But someone had to be brave enough, or foolish enough, to take it.

Kokua made a secret plan of her own. She hired an old man to buy the bottle from Keawe for four centimes, then bought it back from that man herself for three — taking the curse quietly onto her own soul so that her husband would go free. Keawe, discovering what she had done, was horrified: now he was saved but his beloved wife was damned in his place. So he in turn arranged, through a go-between, to buy it back from her for two centimes. Each of them was trying to sacrifice themselves for the other, and the price crept lower and lower toward the point of no return.

The bottle was now at one centime — the very last step. Then, in a waterfront tavern, Keawe met a wild, hard-drinking old boatswain, a reckless man who laughed at the idea of hell and said he was bound for it anyway. To him, a bottle that granted wishes and cost only one centime was pure profit. He bought it, cackling, and refused point-blank to sell it on. Keawe and Kokua walked home into the warm night, free at last — their souls saved, the bottle and its imp gone with a man who simply did not care.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Kokua try to save Keawe, and how did he respond?

2. Who finally took the bottle for good, and why was he the only possible buyer?

1. In Tahiti she secretly had the bottle bought back down to a lower price and took the curse onto herself. When Keawe found out, he bought it back again to save her instead.

2. A reckless old boatswain who believed he was already going to hell and did not care. Because he refused to sell it on, the curse ended with a man who had nothing to lose.

Discuss:
  • The lovers are saved only by someone who has given up on his own soul. Is that a happy ending, an uncomfortable one, or both?

Grammar — Conditionals (zero, first, second)

The whole story turns on a single word: if. If you own the bottle, you can have any wish. If you die with it, you are damned. If you can't sell it for less, you keep it forever. English has three main conditional patterns for talking about rules, likely futures and imaginary situations — and this story uses all three.

The three conditionals, side by side:
• Zero conditional — if + present simple, present simple. A fact or a rule that is always true: "If you own the bottle, the imp grants your wishes." Use it for the bottle's unchanging rules.
• First conditional — if + present simple, will + base verb. A real, likely future: "If Keawe sells it cheaply, he will be free." Use it for real choices and their probable results.
• Second conditional — if + past simple, would + base verb. An unreal or imaginary situation: "If I owned that bottle, I would sell it at once." Use it for hypothetical or unlikely situations.
Feel the difference:
• Zero = a law of the world; it is simply how the bottle works.
• First = a genuine possibility in the future; it really might happen.
• Second = imagining; it is not real, or is very unlikely. The past-tense if-clause is the signal ("if I had…", "if she were…").

Going deeper — take the idea further

You have the story and the words. Now push the ideas outward — argue, disagree, change your mind. Do all of this out loud. There are no right answers here, only good reasons.

Stevenson wrote The Bottle Imp in 1891, drawing on an old European folk tale but setting it among the islands of the Pacific where he spent his last years. Its power is a piece of pure mathematics: a price that must always fall, and a smallest coin that cannot fall further. The horror is not a monster but a rule — one that turns every owner into a gambler racing the arithmetic. That is why it still unsettles: it feels less like a fairy tale than a trap you could actually reason your way into.
Useful language for building an argument: "What's unsettling is…", "You could argue that…", "It's not X that frightens me, it's Y…", "On one level… but on another…", "That may be true, but…".
1 · The price that can only fall

The whole terror of the bottle is one rule: it must always sell for less. Why is a shrinking price so much more frightening than, say, a fixed one? Think about how the story turns a simple sum into a countdown to damnation. Can you name a real situation — a debt, an auction, a game — where the maths itself becomes the threat?

2 · Would you take the bottle?

Imagine the bottle is offered to you today for ten dollars. You could wish for anything. Would you buy it — and if so, what would you wish for first, and how quickly would you try to sell it on? Now imagine it costs only two cents. Does your answer change completely? Use the second conditional as you argue: "If I bought it, I would…".

3 · Love as sacrifice

Keawe and Kokua each secretly try to damn themselves to save the other. Is this the highest form of love, or is there something almost reckless in it — two people racing to destroy themselves? Would a marriage really survive knowing that one of you had been willing to go to hell for the other? Where else in life do people quietly take on a burden so the person they love never has to know?

4 · Saved by a man with nothing to lose

The lovers are rescued only by a drunken sailor who believes he is damned already and simply does not care. It is an uncomfortable kind of happy ending — their salvation rests on his ruin. Does the story let Keawe and Kokua off too easily? And what does it suggest about who ends up carrying the risks that careful, respectable people manage to escape?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The bottle grants every wish yet ruins nearly everyone who holds it. Is Stevenson warning us about magic, or about wanting things too much?
  2. Keawe and Kokua are saved only because someone else is willing to be damned. Can an ending be "happy" if it depends on another person's ruin?
  3. The story survives because of its arithmetic, not its imp. Why do the coldest, most logical traps make the most frightening stories?
