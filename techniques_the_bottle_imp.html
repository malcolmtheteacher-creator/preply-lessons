The Bottle Imp

Robert Louis Stevenson's tale of a bottle that grants every wish — and damns the man who dies still holding it.

Need an easier version? Read this story at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll retell a haunting bargain — a bottle that can buy you anything, on one deadly condition — and you'll master the grammar that makes rules and consequences precise: the zero, first and second conditionals, the very machinery the bottle runs on.
Warm up — a few discussion questions before the words. Talk each one through out loud; there are no right answers yet, only your first instincts.
Before we begin:
  • If you could have absolutely anything you wished for, but there was a hidden catch, what one question would you want answered before you agreed?
  • Is there such a thing as a "free" gift? When someone offers you something that seems too good to be true, what do you usually do?
  • Would you rather own something wonderful and dangerous, or nothing and be safe? Why?

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The old sailor swore the strange imp inside the glass was a real spirit — a small, mischievous devil that would do whatever its owner asked.
an imp (n) — a small mischievous devil or spirit, often one that causes trouble or plays tricks.
2. He struck a bargain with the merchant: the ring in exchange for the horse, and both men shook hands on the deal.
a bargain (n) — an agreement between people about what each will give or do; also, something bought cheaply.
3. The priest warned that anyone who sold his soul would be damned — condemned to suffer forever, with no way back.
damned (adj) — condemned to eternal punishment; doomed, with no hope of being saved.
4. There was one strict condition attached to the loan: he had to repay every penny before the year was out, or lose the house.
a condition (n) — a rule or requirement that must be met before something else can happen.
5. She was tormented by the thought of what she had done — the guilt kept her awake, gnawing at her night after night.
to torment (v) — to cause someone great suffering, especially in the mind, over a long time.
6. Blinded by greed, he kept demanding more — more gold, more land — until the wanting itself had swallowed him whole.
greed (n) — a strong, selfish desire for more of something, especially money or possessions, than one needs.
7. The contract had a cruel catch: the money was free, yes, but only until the day you needed it most, when it would vanish.
a catch (n) — a hidden difficulty or disadvantage in something that otherwise seems good.
8. He made a solemn vow at her bedside — a promise so heavy that breaking it would have felt like a second death.
a vow (n) — a serious, solemn promise, often made with deep feeling or before others.
9. As the price fell to a single small coin, the deal grew impossible — no one could sell it for less, because there was nothing less to offer.
impossible (adj) — unable to be done or to happen; not able to exist.
10. In an act of pure sacrifice, she gave up her own safety to save him, asking for nothing in return.
a sacrifice (n) — the giving up of something valuable, or of one's own comfort or safety, for the sake of someone or something else.

  • Which of these words feel dangerous, and which feel noble? Sort them out loud and say why greed and sacrifice end up on opposite sides.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The bottle and its terrible rules

There was a man of Hawaii named Keawe — poor, but brave and clever — who sailed to San Francisco to see the wide world. Wandering the fine streets, he stopped before a beautiful house and fell into conversation with its sad-eyed owner, an old man who lived there quite alone. "All this," said the old man, "I bought with the bottle you see on my shelf." It was a small, round bottle, white like milk, with changing rainbow colours moving in its glass.

Inside the bottle, the old man explained, lived an imp that would grant its owner anything he desired — love, money, cities, ships, whatever the heart wished for. But the bargain came with a catch, and the catch was dreadful. The bottle could never be given away; it could only be sold, and always for less money than the owner had paid for it. And there was one final, terrible condition: if a man died still owning the bottle, he was damned, and his soul would burn forever.

Keawe listened, half laughing, half afraid. To prove it was no joke, the old man let him try the bottle — and Keawe wished his money back, and there it was in his hand. Tempted by the beautiful house and all it stood for, Keawe bought the bottle for fifty dollars. Only when the deal was done did the old man smile with relief and hurry away, free at last of the thing he had carried so long.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What can the imp in the bottle do for its owner?

2. What are the two strict rules about selling the bottle, and what happens to a person who dies still owning it?

1. It grants the owner anything they wish for — money, love, houses, ships, whatever the heart desires.

2. It can only be sold, never given away, and always for less than the owner paid. If a person dies still owning it, they are damned and their soul burns forever.

Discuss:
  • The old man was clearly relieved to be rid of the bottle. Why do you think he still sold it, rather than simply destroying it or throwing it into the sea?

Part 2 — The wish, the price, and the fear

Keawe used the bottle carefully. He wished for a fine house on the island, and it was built, exactly as he had imagined it. For a while he was happy — but the thought of the bottle's rule sat in him like a cold stone. He resolved to sell it and be safe, and he did sell it, for a little less than he had paid, to a friend. Free of it, Keawe fell in love with a beautiful young woman named Kokua, and asked her to marry him. She said yes, and his joy was complete.

Then, on the eve of his wedding, disaster struck: Keawe discovered a mark of leprosy on his skin, a disease that in those days meant exile and slow ruin. Only one thing could save him and his marriage — the bottle. He hunted it down across the islands, following the trail of buyers, each of whom had sold it on for less. When at last he found it, the price had fallen frighteningly low: it stood at just two cents. Keawe understood the trap at once. If he bought it for two cents, he could only sell it for one; and after one cent, there was no smaller coin. Whoever bought it last would be damned. Desperate to be cured and to marry Kokua, he bought it anyway.

The imp cured him, and he married. But now the old dread returned, sharper than ever. He was well, he was loved — and he was almost certainly doomed, for how could he ever sell a bottle for less than one cent? Kokua saw the shadow on her new husband and, little by little, drew the whole terrible truth out of him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Keawe, who had freed himself of the bottle, feel he had to buy it back?

2. Why was buying it at two cents such a frightening trap?

1. He caught leprosy on the eve of his wedding, and only the bottle's imp could cure him and save his marriage to Kokua.

2. He could only sell it for less than he paid — and below one cent there is no smaller coin, so whoever owned it last could never sell it and would be damned.

Discuss:
  • Keawe knew the danger and bought the bottle anyway, to be cured and to marry. Was that a foolish choice, a brave one, or simply a human one?

Part 3 — Love, and the last sale

Kokua would not let her husband face damnation alone. She had an idea. In another country, she reasoned, they used a coin worth less than a cent — the French centime. If they sailed to Tahiti, the bottle could be sold for four centimes, then three, then two, then one, and each sale would be lawful. So they crossed the ocean together, full of hope. But in Tahiti no one would buy such a wicked thing; the couple's story frightened everyone away, and the bottle stayed in Keawe's hands.

Then Kokua made a secret, selfless plan. In the dark, she paid an old man to buy the bottle for four centimes and then sell it straight back to her — so that she, and not her husband, would hold it at the deadly price. She made her sacrifice quietly, telling Keawe nothing, ready to be damned in his place out of love. But Keawe soon discovered what she had done, and was overcome that she should suffer for him. Now it was his turn: he found a way to buy it back from her, and once more the bottle was his, at a price from which there was no escape.

At the very end, chance — or mercy — intervened. A hard-drinking old sailor, careless of his soul and certain he was already lost, agreed to take the bottle off Keawe's hands for the final coin. "I'm bound for hell anyway," the old man laughed, and would not sell it on. So Keawe and Kokua were freed, their love the thing that had carried them through, and they lived out their days in peace — while the last owner sailed away, holding a bottle that could never be sold again.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Kokua's secret plan, and why did she make it?

2. How were Keawe and Kokua finally set free from the bottle?

1. She secretly arranged to buy the bottle at the deadly low price herself, so that she would be damned instead of her husband — an act of love and sacrifice.

2. An old sailor, who believed his soul was already lost, agreed to take the bottle for the last coin and refused to sell it on, leaving the couple free.

Discuss:
  • Both Keawe and Kokua tried to take the damnation onto themselves to spare the other. What does the story seem to say about what love is willing to do?

Grammar — conditionals (zero / first / second)

The whole story runs on if. "If you die owning it, you are damned." "If I sold it for less, I would be safe." Conditionals are how English talks about rules, real futures, and imagined ones — exactly the machinery of the bottle's bargain. Three types cover almost everything.

The three conditionals, side by side:
• Zero conditional — if + present simple, present simple. For facts and rules that are always true: "If you die owning the bottle, your soul burns forever." The bottle's law never changes, so it takes the zero conditional.
• First conditional — if + present simple, will + base verb. For a real, likely future: "If Keawe sells the bottle, he will be safe." It is genuinely possible.
• Second conditional — if + past simple, would + base verb. For unreal, unlikely or imagined situations: "If the price fell below one cent, no one would be able to buy it." He imagines a situation and its consequence.
Feel the difference:
• Zero = an always-true rule (cause → automatic effect). Both clauses in the present.
• First = a real possibility in the future. Present in the if-clause, will in the result.
• Second = imagined or contrary to fact. Past in the if-clause, would in the result — but it is not about the past; it is a hypothetical present or future.

1. Which conditional? Choose zero, first or second

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence with the correct form.

1. If you die owning the bottle, your soul forever. (an unchanging rule)

2. If Keawe the bottle tomorrow, he will be safe. (a real, likely future)

3. If the price below one cent, no one would be able to buy it. (imagined, unreal)

1. burns (zero conditional — a rule that is always true)

2. sells (first conditional — present simple in the if-clause)

3. fell (second conditional — past simple for an imagined situation)

2. Fix the form

Each sentence uses the right idea but the wrong verb form. Write the corrected verb.

1. If Keawe sold the bottle, he (will) finally feel safe. (second conditional)

2. If you (will heat) water to 100°C, it boils. (zero conditional)

3. If Kokua buys it at the last price, she (would be) damned in his place. (first conditional)

2. heat (zero conditional: present simple in both clauses — never "will" after if)

3. will be (first conditional: if + present simple → will + base verb)

3. Complete the bargain — fill the paragraph

Fill each gap with the correct conditional form of the verb in brackets. Read it aloud once you're done.

The rule is simple and never changes: if a man (die) still holding the bottle, his soul is lost. Keawe thinks it through. "If I (sell) it tomorrow for less than I paid, I will be free," he tells himself — that much is really possible. But then the darker thought comes: "If the price (be) already just one cent, no one (buy) it from me" — and that imagined trap is the one he most fears.

dies (zero — an unchanging rule) · sell (first — a real future possibility) · were / was (second — imagined) · would buy (second — the imagined consequence)

Notice the shift: a fixed rule (zero) → a real plan (first) → a feared, imagined situation (second).

Going deeper

Stevenson wrote The Bottle Imp in 1891, drawing on an old European folk tale but setting it among the islands of the Pacific where he spent his last years. On the surface it is a spooky fable; underneath, it is a sharp little study of desire and its price. The bottle gives you everything — and the moment you have it, you start counting the cost of keeping it.

The falling-price trap. The genius of the story is its mathematics. Every owner can escape only by finding someone poorer, or more foolish, or more desperate, to take the bottle for a little less. As the price drops toward a single coin, the pool of possible buyers shrinks to almost nothing — and greed, which drove people to buy the bottle when it was cheap, is exactly what makes it unsellable at the end. The thing that promises freedom becomes a cage.
Talk it through:
  • The bottle grants any wish, yet almost every owner ends up desperate to be rid of it. What is Stevenson suggesting about getting everything you want?
  • The story turns on price — each sale must be for less. Why do you think Stevenson made the danger increase precisely as the bottle got cheaper? What does that say about greed?
  • Kokua and Keawe are each willing to be damned to save the other. Is their love the real "magic" of the story, stronger than the bottle itself? Or is it just luck that saves them?
  • The bottle is finally taken by a man who believes he is already lost, so its threat means nothing to him. Is that a satisfying ending, a troubling one, or both? What would you have done, holding the bottle at one cent?
