What Are Sacraments?

A sacrament is a sacred action or ceremony that uses physical signs (water, bread, wine, oil, etc.) to convey God's grace (unearned help and blessing) to believers. Sacraments are outward signs of inward spiritual change.

Key idea: God uses the material world (water, bread, wine) to touch the spiritual world (grace, forgiveness, transformation). The physical and spiritual are connected.

Seven Sacraments (Catholic & Orthodox)

Catholics and Orthodox Christians recognize seven sacraments:

  1. Baptism: Initiation into the Church; washing away sin
  2. Confirmation (Chrism): Anointing with oil; strengthening by the Holy Spirit
  3. Eucharist: Communion; body and blood of Christ
  4. Reconciliation (Penance): Confession and forgiveness of sins after baptism
  5. Anointing of the Sick: Oil for the seriously ill or dying
  6. Marriage: Sacramental union of husband and wife
  7. Holy Orders: Ordination of priests and deacons

Why seven? These cover all the important moments and transitions in Christian life, from birth to old age.

Two Sacraments (Most Protestants)

Most Protestant churches recognize only two sacraments, instituted directly by Jesus:

  1. Baptism: Commanded by Jesus (Matthew 28:19)
  2. Eucharist/Communion: Commanded by Jesus ("Do this in remembrance of me")

Why only two? Only these two were explicitly commanded by Jesus. Protestants see these as ordinances (practices to observe) rather than sacraments (means of grace). The grace comes from faith, not the ritual.

Other Protestant practices: Some have confirmation, but it's not sacramental—just a personal commitment ceremony. Weddings and ordinations are important but not seen as means of grace.

Quakers and the Salvation Army

Quakers (Religious Society of Friends): Believe the entire life is sacramental—all actions can convey God's grace if done with intention. They don't practice water baptism or Eucharist, believing that internal transformation matters more than external rituals. The "Inner Light" (direct communion with God) is what matters.

The Salvation Army: Also doesn't practice water baptism or Eucharist. They focus on service to the poor, social action, and practical Christianity. They believe "sacraments" are living a life of obedience and service to God.

Key difference: For most Christians, sacraments are special actions. For Quakers and Salvationists, all of life can be sacramental if lived spiritually.

Why Do Sacraments Matter to Christians?

Sacraments are central to Christianity because they:

  • Connect to God's grace: Sacraments are believed to deliver God's grace directly. You don't have to be spiritually mature or perfectly faithful—the sacrament itself conveys grace. This is why even infants can be baptized.
  • Use the physical to reach the spiritual: God doesn't just work in minds and hearts; He works through the body. Water, bread, wine, oil—the material world carries spiritual meaning.
  • Mark important transitions: Baptism marks entry into faith; Eucharist marks weekly or frequent renewal; Marriage marks a life transition; Holy Orders marks a calling. Sacraments give shape and meaning to the faith journey.
  • Build community: Sacraments are usually done with others—in church, with witnesses, with a priest or minister. They're not private activities. They connect the individual believer to the Church.
  • Recall Jesus's actions: Sacraments point back to what Jesus did (washing feet, breaking bread, anointing with oil). They're remembrances and re-enactments of His ministry.
  • Promise God's presence: In a sacrament, God promises to be actively present and working. The believer isn't alone; God is there in the ritual.

Different emphasis by tradition:

  • Catholic/Orthodox: Sacraments are means of grace—they actually deliver grace through their performance
  • Protestant: Sacraments are signs and seals—they signify grace (like a seal on a letter) but the grace comes from faith, not the ritual
  • Quaker/Salvationist: All of life is potentially sacramental—external rituals matter less than inward transformation and active service

Evaluate (12 marks)

Question: "Sacraments are essential for salvation." Evaluate this statement with reference to different Christian views.

Guidance:

  • For (Catholic/Orthodox view): Sacraments are means of grace instituted by Jesus; baptism removes original sin; the Eucharist sustains spiritual life; without sacraments, grace doesn't reach you
  • For (Protestant critique): Only faith saves, not rituals; sacraments are good practices but not essential; Quakers and Salvationists have salvation without traditional sacraments; the Reformation challenged the idea that priests control grace through sacraments
  • Nuance: "Salvation" itself is understood differently. If you mean being right with God, Protestants say faith alone suffices. If you mean the whole spiritual journey, sacraments are important even if not technically "essential."
  • Conclusion: Different Christian traditions disagree fundamentally. For Catholic/Orthodox, yes—essential. For Protestants, good but not essential. For Quakers, no—living spiritually is what matters.
