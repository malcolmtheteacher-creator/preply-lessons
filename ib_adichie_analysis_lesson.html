📚 The Danger of a Single Story

Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie | TED Talk, 2009 | IB Analysis Lesson

Part 1: Understanding Context

Before We Read

Author: Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie (born 1977, Nigeria)

Text Type: TED Talk (Technology, Entertainment, Design conference speech)

Date: July 2009

Audience: Global, educated, English-speaking audience interested in ideas

Purpose: To persuade the audience that limiting people/cultures to single narratives is dangerous

Quick Discussion Question: Before we read, have you ever been stereotyped? Has someone assumed something about you based on one aspect of who you are (where you're from, your appearance, your background)? How did it make you feel?

The Text - First Reading

Read this excerpt from "The Danger of a Single Story" carefully. As you read, underline or note any moments that surprise you, interest you, or confuse you.

1I'm a storyteller. And I would like to tell you a few personal stories about what I like to call "the danger of the single story." I grew up on a university campus in eastern Nigeria. My mother says that I started reading at the age of two, although I think four is probably close to the truth. So I was an early reader, and what I read were British and 5American children's books.

6I was also an early writer, and when I began to write, at about the age of seven, stories in pencil with crayon illustrations that my poor mother was obligated to read, I wrote exactly the kinds of stories I was reading: all my characters were white and blue-eyed, they played in the snow, they ate apples, and they talked a lot about the weather, how 10lovely it was that the sun had come out.

11Now, this despite the fact that I lived in Nigeria. I had never been outside Nigeria. We didn't have snow, we ate mangoes, and we never talked about the weather, because there was no need to.

14What this demonstrates, I think, is how impressionable and vulnerable we are in the face of 15a story, particularly as children. Because all I had read were books in which characters were foreign, I had become convinced that books by their very nature had to have foreigners in them and had to be about things with which I could not personally identify.

18Now, things changed when I discovered African books. There weren't many of them available, and they weren't quite as easy to find as the foreign books.

20But because of writers like Chinua Achebe and Camara Laye, I went through a mental shift in my perception of literature. I realized that people like me, girls with skin the colour of chocolate, whose kinky hair could not form ponytails, could also exist in literature. I started to write about things I recognized.

24Now, I loved those American and British books I read. They stirred my imagination. They 25opened up new worlds for me. But the unintended consequence was that I did not know that people like me could exist in literature. So what the discovery of African writers did for me was this: It saved me from having a single story of what books are.

28I come from a conventional, middle-class Nigerian family. My father was a professor. My mother was an administrator. And so we had, as was the norm, live-in domestic help, 30who would often come from nearby rural villages. So, the year I turned eight, we got a new house boy. His name was Fide. The only thing my mother told us about him was that his family was very poor. My mother sent yams and rice, and our old clothes, to his family. And when I didn't finish my dinner, my mother would say, "Finish your food! 34Don't you know? People like Fide's family have nothing." So I felt enormous pity for 35Fide's family.

36Then one Saturday, we went to his village to visit, and his mother showed us a beautifully patterned basket made of dyed raffia that his brother had made. I was startled. It had not occurred to me that anybody in his family could actually make 39something. All I had heard about them was how poor they were, so that it had become 40impossible for me to see them as anything else but poor. Their poverty was my single story of them.

42Years later, I thought about this when I left Nigeria to go to university in the United States. I was 19. My American roommate was shocked by me. She asked where I had 44learned to speak English so well, and was confused when I said that Nigeria happened to 45have English as its official language. She asked if she could listen to what she called my "tribal music", and was consequently very disappointed when I produced my tape of Mariah Carey.

48She assumed that I did not know how to use a stove.

49What struck me was this: She had felt sorry for me even before she saw me. Her default 50position toward me, as an African, was a kind of patronizing, well-meaning pity. My roommate had a single story of Africa: a single story of catastrophe. In this single story, there was no possibility of Africans being similar to her in any way, no possibility of feelings more complex than pity, no possibility of a connection as human equals.

61But I must quickly add that I too am just as guilty in the question of the single story. A few years ago, I visited Mexico from the U.S. The political climate in the U.S. at the time was tense, and there were debates going on about immigration. And, as often happens 64in America, immigration became synonymous with Mexicans. There were endless stories 65of Mexicans as people who were fleecing the healthcare system, sneaking across the border, being arrested at the border, that sort of thing.

67I remember walking around on my first day in Guadalajara, watching the people going to work, rolling up tortillas in the marketplace, smoking, laughing. I remember first feeling 69slight surprise. And then, I was overwhelmed with shame. I realized that I had been so 70immersed in the media coverage of Mexicans that they had become one thing in my mind, the abject immigrant. I had bought into the single story of Mexicans and I could not have been more ashamed of myself.

73So that is how to create a single story, show a people as one thing, as only one thing, over and over again, and that is what they become.

75Stories matter. Many stories matter. Stories have been used to dispossess and to malign, but stories can also be used to empower and to humanize. Stories can break the dignity of a people, but stories can also repair that broken dignity.

82I would like to end with this thought: That when we reject the single story, when we realize that there is never a single story about any place, we regain a kind of paradise.

Part 2: Analyzing Rhetorical Techniques

The Rhetorical Triangle

When analyzing persuasive texts (like speeches), we look at how the writer/speaker appeals to the audience:

ETHOS (Credibility)

The speaker establishes trustworthiness and authority

PATHOS (Emotion)

The speaker appeals to the audience's emotions

LOGOS (Logic)

The speaker uses reason and evidence

Let's analyze the key techniques Adichie uses. Click each card to reveal the analysis:

1. ANECDOTE (Personal Stories)
What is it? A short personal story used to illustrate a point
Examples in the text:
  • Writing stories with white, blue-eyed characters (lines 6-10)
  • Fide's family and the beautiful basket (lines 36-40)
  • Her American roommate at university (lines 42-52)
  • Her own prejudice about Mexicans (lines 67-71)
EFFECT (SO WHAT?): Anecdotes create pathos (emotional appeal). They make abstract ideas CONCRETE and RELATABLE. We feel sympathy for young Adichie who couldn't see herself in books. We understand stereotyping through specific examples, not just theory. This makes her argument more PERSUASIVE.
Now you analyze: Which anecdote is most effective? Why? How would the speech be different if she only used abstract statements without personal stories?
2. REPETITION ("single story")
What is it? Deliberately repeating key words or phrases to emphasize the main idea
Count the phrase "single story" in the text:
  • Line 27: "saved me from having a single story"
  • Line 40: "Their poverty was my single story of them"
  • Line 51: "a single story of Africa: a single story of catastrophe"
  • Line 61: "guilty in the question of the single story"
  • Line 71: "the single story of Mexicans"
  • Line 73: "how to create a single story"
  • Line 82: "reject the single story"
EFFECT: The repetition creates RHYTHM and makes the phrase MEMORABLE. By the end, "single story" is embedded in our minds. This is the exact phrase Adichie wants us to remember and use. The repetition also creates UNITY - it ties all her different anecdotes together under one concept.
Now you analyze: Why do you think she uses "single story" instead of "stereotype" or "prejudice"? What's the difference in connotation?
3. ETHOS (Establishing Credibility)
What is it? Building trust and authority with the audience
Examples:
  • Personal experience: "I grew up on a university campus" - educated background
  • Self-awareness: "I must quickly add that I too am just as guilty" (line 61)
  • Honesty: Admitting her own prejudice about Mexicans
  • Cultural authority: Speaks as both African and someone who lived in America
EFFECT: By ADMITTING her own mistakes, Adichie seems HONEST and TRUSTWORTHY. She's not preaching from a position of superiority - she's saying "I've done this too." This makes the audience more receptive to her message. She has credibility from BOTH perspectives (being stereotyped AND stereotyping others).
Now you analyze: How would the speech be different if she only talked about being stereotyped, but never admitted to stereotyping others?
4. STRUCTURE (From Personal to Universal)
What is it? The way she organizes her speech - moving from specific to general
The Structure:
  1. Personal childhood experience (books, Fide) - pathos
  2. Being stereotyped (roommate) - pathos
  3. Admitting her own prejudice (Mexico) - ethos
  4. Universal truth ("how to create a single story") - logos
  5. Call to action ("reject the single story") - all three appeals
EFFECT: She starts with EMOTION (her childhood) to hook us, builds CREDIBILITY (admitting fault), then moves to LOGIC (explaining the pattern). This structure takes us on a JOURNEY from individual experience to universal truth. It feels natural, not like a lecture.
5. PARALLEL STRUCTURE & ANTITHESIS
What is it? Using similar grammatical patterns and contrasting ideas
Example from lines 75-76:
"Stories have been used to dispossess and to malign, but stories can also be used to empower and to humanize. Stories can break the dignity of a people, but stories can also repair that broken dignity."
EFFECT: The parallel structure creates RHYTHM - it sounds almost musical. The ANTITHESIS (contrast between harm and help) shows the POWER of stories in both directions. This makes her final appeal more urgent: we must choose to tell many stories, not single ones.
Now you analyze: Read that quote aloud. Can you hear the rhythm? How does the balanced structure make it more memorable and powerful?
6. INCLUSIVE PRONOUNS ("we" / "us")
What is it? Using "we" and "us" instead of "you" to create unity
Example from lines 14-15:
"What this demonstrates, I think, is how impressionable and vulnerable we are in the face of a story"

Example from line 82:
"when we reject the single story, when we realize... we regain a kind of paradise"
EFFECT: She could have said "you are vulnerable" or "you should reject." But "we" creates SOLIDARITY - she's including herself. It's not a lecture; it's an invitation to join her. This makes the audience feel part of the solution, not just the problem.
7. TONE (Conversational, Honest, Gentle)
What is it? The speaker's attitude conveyed through word choice and style
Evidence of tone:
  • "my poor mother was obligated to read" - gentle humor
  • "I must quickly add" - conversational
  • "I could not have been more ashamed of myself" - honest vulnerability
  • "I would like to end with this thought" - gentle, not preachy
EFFECT: The tone is ACCESSIBLE and HUMBLE, not angry or accusatory. She laughs at herself ("my poor mother"). She admits shame. This makes the audience RECEPTIVE rather than defensive. If she were angry or preachy, people might resist her message.
Now you analyze: How would the speech be different if she sounded angry or accusatory? Why is the gentle tone more effective for her purpose?

Part 3: Close Reading Practice

Let's do close analysis of specific passages. Read each extract carefully, then discuss the questions.

Passage 1: The Opening (Lines 1-5)

"I'm a storyteller. And I would like to tell you a few personal stories about what I like to call 'the danger of the single story.'"
Analysis Questions:
  1. Why does she start by identifying herself as "a storyteller"? How does this establish ethos?
  2. What's the effect of saying "I would like to tell you" (direct address to audience)?
  3. She says "a few personal stories" - why emphasize "personal"?
  4. Why does she say "what I like to call" - what does this suggest about the phrase "single story"?

Passage 2: The Realization (Lines 36-40)

"Then one Saturday, we went to his village to visit, and his mother showed us a beautifully patterned basket made of dyed raffia that his brother had made. I was startled. It had not occurred to me that anybody in his family could actually make something."
Analysis Questions:
  1. What's significant about the detail "beautifully patterned basket"? Why not just "basket"?
  2. Why does she say "I was startled"? What emotion does this convey?
  3. What's the effect of "It had not occurred to me"? What does this reveal about single stories?
  4. Analyze "could actually make something" - what assumptions does "actually" reveal?

Passage 3: The Roommate (Lines 49-52)

"What struck me was this: She had felt sorry for me even before she saw me. Her default position toward me, as an African, was a kind of patronizing, well-meaning pity."
Analysis Questions:
  1. Why "even before she saw me"? What does this timing reveal?
  2. Analyze "default position" - what does this metaphor (from computers) suggest?
  3. Why "patronizing, well-meaning pity" - how do these three words work together?
  4. What's the effect of saying "as an African" rather than "as Chimamanda" or "as me"?

Passage 4: Creating Single Stories (Line 73)

"So that is how to create a single story, show a people as one thing, as only one thing, over and over again, and that is what they become."
Analysis Questions:
  1. Why does she use "how to" - like an instruction manual? What's the effect?
  2. Analyze the repetition: "one thing, as only one thing" - why repeat?
  3. What's the effect of "over and over again" - why not just "repeatedly"?
  4. What does "that is what they become" suggest about the power of stories?

Passage 5: The Ending (Lines 82-83)

"That when we reject the single story, when we realize that there is never a single story about any place, we regain a kind of paradise."
Analysis Questions:
  1. Why "regain" instead of "gain"? What does this suggest we've lost?
  2. Why "a kind of paradise" - not just "paradise"? What does "a kind of" do?
  3. What is the "paradise" she's referring to? What does this metaphor suggest?
  4. How does this ending connect back to the opening? (Look at structure)

Part 4: IB Paper 1 Practice

IB Paper 1 Reminder

In Paper 1, you'll receive an UNSEEN text (similar to this one) with a guiding question. You have 2 hours 15 minutes to write an analysis (aim for 1000-1300 words).

You need to analyze:

  • WHAT the text is about (summary/understanding)
  • WHO the audience is and WHAT the purpose is (context)
  • HOW the writer creates meaning (techniques + effects)
  • HOW EFFECTIVE it is (evaluation)

Practice Guiding Question

"Explore how Adichie uses language to persuade her audience in this speech."

Planning Your Response (10 minutes)

Before you write, plan using this checklist:

Sample Introduction

Chimamanda Ngozi Adichie's 2009 TED Talk, "The Danger of a Single Story," addresses a global, educated audience about the dangers of reducing people and cultures to limited narratives. Through a carefully structured speech that moves from personal anecdotes to universal truths, Adichie persuades her audience that "single stories" create stereotypes that "rob people of dignity." She achieves this persuasive purpose primarily through the use of personal anecdotes, strategic repetition, and the establishment of credibility through honest self-reflection. By combining emotional appeal (pathos), logical structure, and ethical authority (ethos), Adichie creates a compelling argument for seeking diverse narratives.

What makes this introduction effective?

  • ✅ Identifies context (speaker, date, text type, audience)
  • ✅ States the purpose clearly
  • ✅ Previews main techniques to be analyzed
  • ✅ Answers the question directly

Your Task

Write a full Paper 1 analysis (1000-1300 words) answering the guiding question.

Structure:

  1. Introduction (see example above)
  2. Body Paragraph 1: Anecdotes (pathos)
    • Technique: Personal stories
    • Examples: childhood books, Fide, roommate, Mexico
    • Effect: Creates emotional connection, makes abstract concrete
  3. Body Paragraph 2: Repetition
    • Technique: "single story" repeated 7+ times
    • Effect: Memorable, creates unity, emphasizes central concept
  4. Body Paragraph 3: Ethos & Structure
    • Technique: Admitting her own prejudice, personal to universal structure
    • Effect: Builds trust, avoids preachiness, creates journey
  5. Body Paragraph 4: Language choices
    • Technique: Inclusive pronouns, parallel structure, gentle tone
    • Effect: Creates solidarity, rhythm, accessibility
  6. Conclusion: Overall effectiveness - how techniques work together to persuade

IB Assessment Criteria (What examiners look for)

  1. Understanding (Criterion A): Do you understand what the text is about and its context?
  2. Analysis (Criterion B): Do you explain HOW techniques create meaning? (Not just "what")
  3. Focus & Organization (Criterion C): Clear thesis, structured paragraphs, answers the question?
  4. Language (Criterion D): Clear, precise, academic writing?
