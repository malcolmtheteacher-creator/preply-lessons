The Fourfold Cure

In which we go deeper into the Garden and discover that Epicurus's cure for suffering is brilliant, humane, almost complete — and yet something is missing

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We dive deep into each of the four remedies Epicurus offered: the cure for fear of gods, the cure for fear of death, the management of pain, and the redefinition of the good. We also examine what ataraxia really means — is it freedom or emptiness? Is the absence of suffering the same as joy? And most importantly: what happens when you have done something that haunts you?

II. The Story — Each Cure Examined

The First Cure: Fear of the Gods

In Epicurus's time, many people lived in terror. They feared that the gods were watching, judging, punishing. If you committed a crime, even in secret, the gods would know. If you broke a taboo, the gods would send plague or war. This fear was paralyzing.

Epicurus's response was radical in its gentleness. He did not say: the gods do not exist. Rather:

A blessed and imperishable being has no trouble itself nor causes trouble to any other being. Hence it is not subject to anger or gratitude, for all such things imply weakness. — Epicurus, Principal Doctrines

The gods, if they exist, are perfect and blessed. Perfection means freedom from all need and all concern. A perfect being would have no desire to punish you. It would be too busy being perfectly happy in the inter-mundia (the spaces between worlds) to care about human affairs.

This was comforting: you are not being watched. No hidden crime will be punished by divine retribution. But it raises a darker question that Epicurus doesn't address: what if the problem is not fear of punishment, but the knowledge of your own wrongdoing? What if you don't need the gods to judge you — your own conscience does?

The Second Cure: Fear of Death

This is perhaps Epicurus's most famous argument. Lucretius, writing in the first century BC, preserved it beautifully:

Death is nothing to us. When we exist, death is not; and when death exists, then we are not. Thus it never is experienced by either the living or the dead, since for the living it is not, and the dead no longer are. — Epicurus via Lucretius, De Rerum Natura

Think about it logically: You fear death because you imagine suffering when you die. But you won't experience dying. The moment of death itself is not painful to you because you won't be there to experience it. You will have been alive, then you won't be. There is no gap where you are dying in agony.

The symmetry argument: Before you were born, you existed in non-existence. That state did not trouble you. Why should the same state, after death, trouble you now? You have already experienced it. And you survived it.

This argument has comforted countless people. It is rational and clear. And yet: it doesn't address the grief of others. It doesn't address the pain of imagining those you love living without you. It speaks to the fear of your own suffering in death, but what about the fear of leaving unfinished business? What about dying with words unsaid, with wrongs unfixed?

The Third Cure: Pain

Epicurus observed something true: pain is usually not chronic. Acute pain passes. Wounds heal. Even chronic pain (the pain of illness, of age) becomes bearable through habituation — the mind adjusts, finds coping strategies, learns to live with it.

And pain, he said, can be soothed. By what? Not by luxury — expensive medicines don't work better than simple ones. But by what only another person can provide: comfort. A hand to hold. A friend present. Conversation. This is why Epicurus lived in community, not in solitude like Diogenes.

But there is a pain Epicurus does not address: the pain that comes from moral failure. The ache that stays even after the wound heals, because you know you caused it. The pain of remorse. This pain does not get better with time. Sometimes it gets worse. And no friend can entirely remove it, because the friend themselves was harmed.

The Fourth Cure: Vain Desires

Here Epicurus is devastating in his clarity. He asks: what are you really seeking when you pursue wealth? Status? Fame? You are seeking freedom from fear. But these things don't provide that. They provide the opposite.

The wealthy man fears losing his wealth. The famous man fears fading into obscurity. The powerful man fears being overtaken. Every vain desire creates a new, infinite fear. But the simple goods — bread, water, friendship, conversation, a place to sleep — these create no such fear. If you lose them, you can find them again. They are renewable. They are abundant.

When we say pleasure is the goal of life we mean not the pleasures of the depraved and those that consist in debauchery ... but freedom from bodily pain and mental anguish. — Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus

This is the core insight: pleasure rightly understood is not indulgence. It is the elimination of unnecessary pain and fear. And the goods that achieve this are simple and universally available.

Ataraxia: The Goal

If you master these four cures, Epicurus promised, you will achieve ataraxia — a state of undisturbed peace and freedom from fear. Not excitement, not ecstasy, not achievement. Peace. The quiet mind. Freedom from the internal storms of anxiety.

This was revolutionary. The Stoics would later teach something similar. But Epicurus was there first: teaching that the highest good is not power, not beauty, not wisdom, but peace of mind.

And for many people, in many circumstances, this is enough. The Epicurean who masters these cures finds himself unexpectedly free. Free from the hunger for more. Free from the fear of what others think. Free to sit in a garden with friends and be content.

♦ ♦ ♦

But Is Ataraxia the Same as Happiness?

Here we must pause and ask the deepest question. Ataraxia is the absence of pain and fear. But is the absence of pain the same as the presence of joy? Is tranquility the same as being alive?

Consider: a person on morphine experiences no pain. Their mind is at peace. They have ataraxia. But we would not call this happiness or flourishing. Something is missing.

And consider the person who has wronged another deeply. They sit in the garden. They eat simple food. They have no pain, no fear (if they've mastered Epicurus's cures). But there is an absence — a wrongness. An ache that has nothing to do with fear or physical pain, but with conscience. With knowledge of having caused harm. With the gap between who they are and who they wish they were.

This is the fifth question, the one Epicurus leaves hanging: Can you achieve peace of mind if you carry guilt? And if not, what philosophy can address that? What can cure the conscience?

III. The Ideas — The Limits of Rational Cure

Idea 1: Pleasure as Absence, Not Presence

The highest pleasure is not the presence of something good. It is the absence of something bad.

This is a inversion of how most people think about happiness. Most of us imagine happiness as the presence of something — love, achievement, beauty, excitement. Epicurus says: no. The real happiness comes from the absence of pain, anxiety, and false desires.

This seems pessimistic until you realize: he is right. What brings more lasting relief — getting something you want, or being free from the fear that you won't get it? The peace of not being afraid is deeper and more stable than the excitement of achievement.

Idea 2: The Hierarchy of Goods, Not the Hierarchy of Virtues

Not all goods are equal. The simple goods that bring peace are higher than the complex goods that bring anxiety.

Epicurus disagrees with both Plato and Aristotle here. Plato said the highest good is Knowledge of the Forms. Aristotle said it is the exercise of virtue in accordance with reason. Epicurus says: no. The highest good is friendship and simple pleasure. Knowledge and virtue are valuable, but only insofar as they contribute to peace of mind.

This is radical. It deprioritizes intellectual and moral achievement. It says: if philosophy, if virtue, if achievement costs you your peace, you should abandon them. Better to be a simple person at peace than a tormented genius.

Idea 3: The Sufficiency of Community — Friendship as Necessary, Not Optional

You cannot achieve the highest good alone. Friendship is not luxury; it is necessary.

This is where Epicurus differs most crucially from Diogenes. Diogenes achieved freedom through radical isolation. Epicurus achieves happiness through radical connection. The Garden was a community. Philosophy was done together. Simple food was eaten together.

This matters: it means that the Epicurean life is not about escape or rejection of society. It is about building an alternative society — one based on genuine friendship, not status or power.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Crisis at the Heart: What Ataraxia Cannot Cure

Here is the deepest problem, and it is not something Epicurus explicitly addressed, but it is implied in his philosophy and unresolved:

Think deeply before clicking: What if a person has harmed someone they love? What if that person has genuinely wronged another? Can the Tetrapharmakos cure that?

The Fifth Fear: Guilt. The knowledge of having done wrong. The ache of conscience.

Epicurus addresses four fears. All four are about external things: gods, death, pain, vain desires. But guilt is internal. It is the pain of knowing that you have harmed someone, that you have violated your own moral sense, that you have failed to be the person you wished to be.

Can the Tetrapharmakos cure this? Let's check:

The cure for fear of gods: "The gods are indifferent." But this doesn't help. Your friend is not indifferent. They know what you did.

The cure for fear of death: "Death is nothing." But guilt is not about death. It is about living with the weight of having wronged someone.

The cure for pain: "Pain is brief or bearable." But the pain of conscience is neither brief nor easily bearable. It can last a lifetime. It can intensify over time.

The cure for vain desires: "Pursue simple goods instead." But this doesn't address guilt. You can sit in a garden with friends and still carry the weight of having harmed one of them.

The answer is: the Tetrapharmakos cannot cure guilt. And this is not a flaw in the system. This is a gap. A real thing that Epicurus's philosophy does not and cannot address through reason and argument and simple living.

What cures guilt? Not philosophy alone. Not reason. Not absence of pain or fear. Perhaps forgiveness. Perhaps reconciliation. Perhaps something that philosophy, in all its clarity and power, cannot provide.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2-3 of these questions to reflect on.

The symmetry argument

Does Epicurus's argument that death is nothing really eliminate fear of death? Or does it only work intellectually? Does the mind really believe it?

Ataraxia and life

Is ataraxia (peace of mind, absence of pain and fear) the same as happiness? Can you be at peace and feel truly alive at the same time?

The fifth fear

What is the fear that the Tetrapharmakos doesn't address? Epicurus cures anxiety, fear, and unnecessary desire. But can his philosophy cure guilt?

Simple goods vs. complex goods

Epicurus says simple goods (food, shelter, friendship) bring more lasting happiness than complex goods (luxury, achievement, fame). Do you believe this? Does your life confirm it?

The Epicurean community

Unlike Diogenes, Epicurus built a community. Why is friendship essential to his vision of happiness, not optional?

Absence vs. presence

Epicurus says the highest pleasure is the absence of pain. But is that the same as joy? Can you be happy just by having nothing bad happen?

VII. Final Retrieval

Don't look back. Retrieve from memory.

1. The Four Cures in Detail

For each cure, write what it addresses and how Epicurus solves it:

Fear of Gods: Gods are blessed and indifferent; they don't punish; they live apart from human affairs in perfect peace

Fear of Death: Death is not experienced; it is either not yet here or already past; the symmetry argument makes it nothing to fear

Pain: Acute pain is brief; chronic pain is bearable; pain is soothed by friendship, simple living, and philosophy

Vain Desires: Recognize them as endless and empty; pursue natural and necessary desires that bring lasting peace

2. Ataraxia

What is ataraxia, and is it the same as happiness?

Ataraxia is freedom from pain and fear; it is the absence of internal disturbance; it is a state of peace and tranquility.

It may not be the same as happiness, which might include joy, excitement, fulfillment, growth. Ataraxia is peaceful but passive. It is the absence of suffering, not the presence of flourishing.

3. The Fifth Fear

What is the unresolved question that Epicurus's philosophy does not address?

The Fifth Fear: Guilt. The knowledge of having wronged someone. The weight of conscience. The pain of knowing you have harmed someone you care about.

Philosophy cannot cure this through reason alone because it is not a fear or a false belief. It is a truth. You have done harm. And no argument, no simple food, no friendship (if it was the friend you wronged) can entirely remove that weight.
