Evaluating Online Sources

When researching online, you need to determine if a source is reliable and trustworthy. This skill is essential for academic work.

Key Criteria for Evaluating Websites

  • Authority: Who wrote this? What are their qualifications?
  • Purpose: Why was this written? Is it trying to sell you something or inform you?
  • Currency: When was it published? Is the information up-to-date?
  • Accuracy: Are claims backed by evidence? Are sources cited?
  • Bias: Does the author present a balanced view or a one-sided perspective?

💡 Think About This

Would you trust medical advice from a blog post as much as from a hospital website? Why or why not?

Wikipedia: Useful Starting Point, Not Final Source

Wikipedia can be valuable for getting basic information and finding references, but it shouldn't be your only source for academic work.

Advantages of Wikipedia

  • Gives quick overview of topic
  • Contains links to credible sources
  • Updated regularly by community

Limitations

  • Anyone can edit (quality varies)
  • May contain errors or outdated info
  • Not acceptable as final source in academic papers

Finding Reliable Sources

Where to Look

  • Google Scholar: Academic papers and peer-reviewed articles
  • University Library: Databases, journals, books
  • Government Websites: Official statistics and reports (.gov domains)
  • Peer-Reviewed Journals: Articles checked by experts
  • Books by Academic Publishers: Vetted content from established publishers

Evaluating Sources Exercise

Read each scenario and decide if the source is reliable for academic research.

Question 1: Blog Post

You find a blog post about climate change written by someone named "Tom." The blog has nice design and interesting information. There are no dates or citations.

Not reliable. No author credentials, no dates, no citations or sources given. Good design doesn't mean accurate information.

Question 2: University Research

A published study from Oxford University researchers in a peer-reviewed journal about psychology. Published in 2024. Includes methodology and data.

Highly reliable. From respected institution, peer-reviewed, recent, transparent methodology. Good source for academic work.

Question 3: Product Website

A skincare company's website describing the benefits of their product. Information is detailed with testimonials.

Not reliable for unbiased information. Source has clear bias—they want to sell the product. Not peer-reviewed or independent.

Question 4: Government Health Report

An official health report from WHO (World Health Organization) published in 2023 with statistics and research references.

Highly reliable. International authority, recent, includes data and citations. Trustworthy for academic research.
