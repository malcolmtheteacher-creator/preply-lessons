How to Tell a Story About an Event

Five Stages of a Story

Good stories have a clear structure. When telling someone about an event you attended, use these five stages:

1. Introduction Give the main idea. What is your story about?
Example: "Have you ever been to a beach festival?"
2. Background Give context. When was it? Where were you? Who was with you?
Example: "I was quite young... It was at the local beach last summer."
3. Problem What happened? What was the problem or interesting part?
Example: "I started playing volleyball and then suddenly it started raining."
4. Resolution How did the problem end? What was the result?
Example: "We all ran inside, but then everyone laughed about getting wet."
5. Comment What is your final thought? Why is this story important?
Example: "I'll never forget that day. We all had such a good time!"

Practice: Match the Phrases

Read these phrases from a story about a cousin's wedding. Can you identify which stage each one belongs to?

1. "Have I ever told you about the time I set fire to my hair at my cousin's wedding?"
This is the Introduction — it's the opening line that gets attention and tells you what the story is about.
2. "I was quite young... It was my cousin Claire's wedding and it was somewhere in the countryside..."
This is the Background — it gives you context about when, where, and the situation.
3. "So I started playing with it and then he said he was going to the toilet."
This is the Problem — it describes what was happening and what went wrong.
4. "The next thing I knew was everyone was rushing at me, throwing any liquid they could lay their hands on at my head."
This is the Resolution — it describes how people reacted and what happened as a result.
5. "I won't forget that, nor will Mark let me."
This is the Comment — it's the final thought or reflection on the story and its lasting impact.

Past Time Linking Words & Phrases

When telling stories about events, use these phrases to show when things happened and link your ideas together. They also show your interest in the story.

Start Your Story

Well, I was quite young... — Use this to set the scene
It was my cousin's wedding... — Introduce the event and location
It was somewhere in the countryside... — Give more detail about the place

Show When Things Happened

When I was...years old — Say your age at the time
It was summer/winter — Say the season
That evening/That day — Say the time of day
Several years ago — Say how long ago it was
It was a long time ago... — Show time has passed

Link Events Together (Show Interest & Enthusiasm)

So I started playing with it... — Use 'So' to show what happened next
The next thing I knew was... — Use this when something sudden happens
The next thing happened when... — Link events in sequence
...and then he said... — Use 'and then' to continue the sequence
Right — Use to acknowledge and understand

Practice: Complete the Story Timeline

Scenario: Your friend asked you about a concert you attended.

"Well, I was about 19 years old. It was last summer. The next thing I knew was my favourite band announced a concert. I bought tickets immediately. That evening, I went with my friends. So we all stood in the crowd and waited..."
Notice how the time phrases help the listener understand: (1) your age at the time, (2) when it happened, (3) the sequence of events. The listener can follow the story easily!

Listen for These Linking Phrases

When someone tells you a story about an event, listen for these words. They help you follow what happened:

"I went to..." → The person is introducing a past event
"At first..." → Something happened at the beginning
"After that..." / "Then..." → Things happened in order
"Suddenly..." → Something unexpected happened
"In the end..." / "Finally..." → The story is coming to an end

Intonation: Sound Interested & Engaged

When telling a story, how you say words is as important as what you say. Intonation (how your voice goes up and down) shows your feelings about the event.

Phrases That Show Interest

When someone tells you a story, respond with these phrases. Your intonation matters!

"No. Go on. Tell us."
When to use: You want them to continue. Your voice goes UP at the end to show interest and curiosity.
Intonation: Start low, rise at the end ↗
"Go on."
When to use: You want to hear more. Use a rising tone to show interest.
Intonation: Rise at the end ↗
"Tell us."
When to use: You want to hear the story. This should sound friendly and encouraging.
Intonation: Rise at the end ↗
"No way!"
When to use: You're surprised or shocked by something in the story. You don't believe it or find it amazing!
Intonation: Rise at the end, show surprise ↗
"Right."
When to use: You understand and are listening. This shows you're following the story.
Intonation: Fall at the end ↘ (your voice goes down slightly, showing understanding)
"Yeah, I know how you feel."
When to use: You understand their feelings because you've had a similar experience.
Intonation: Start high and fall at the end ↘ (shows sympathy and understanding)

Practice Listening for Intonation

Does your voice go UP or DOWN? This changes the meaning:

"You went to a wedding?" (Voice UP ↗) = I'm interested! Tell me more!
"You went to a wedding." (Voice DOWN ↘) = I understand. That's good.

✓ When you tell your story, let your voice go UP when you're excited and DOWN when you're calm or sad.
