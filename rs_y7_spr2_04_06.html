Unit 4 Comparison Review

How Do These Religions Compare?

Here's a summary of the key forms of worship and prayer we've studied. Study this carefully — you'll need it for the assessment tasks.

Islamic Salah

Who/what is addressed: Allah (God) alone. Five specific prayers per day.
Key practices: Specific movements (standing, bowing, prostration), Arabic recitations, facing Mecca.
Physical actions: Wudu (ritual washing), specific prayer positions, structured movements.
Where: Anywhere clean (home, mosque, work, street). Fridays: congregational prayer in the mosque.
How often: Five times daily, at set times.
Role of community: Friday prayers bring community together. Friday prayer is obligatory for men (encouraged for women and children).

Christian Worship

Who/what is addressed: God (in Jesus Christ, for most Christians).
Key practices (Liturgical): Set prayers, readings from the Bible, hymns, Eucharist/Communion.
Key practices (Charismatic): Spontaneous prayers, emotional expression, music, prophecy, speaking in tongues.
Physical actions: Singing, kneeling, standing, taking Communion (bread and wine), raising hands.
Where: Churches (most common), but also at home, in prayer groups.
How often: Sundays (weekly), Eucharist varies (weekly to monthly). Daily prayer is encouraged.
Role of community: Very important. Sunday worship is communal. Different denominations vary in emphasis on community vs. personal faith.

Hindu Puja

Who/what is addressed: A chosen deity (through the murti). Different families worship different gods.
Key practices: Ringing bell, lighting diya, offering flowers/fruit/food, waving incense, chanting mantras, applying tilak, arti ceremony (in temples).
Physical actions: Touching feet of murti, waving incense, ringing bell, eating prasad, applying tilak mark.
Where: Home shrine (most common), mandir (temple).
How often: Daily (usually 15-30 min at home). Temple visits vary.
Role of community: Home puja is private/family. Temple puja brings community together. Prasad is shared across caste lines.

Buddhist Meditation

Who/what is addressed: No deity — this is personal mind training.
Key practices: Samatha (breath focus), vipassana (insight), metta bhavana (loving-kindness), zazen (sitting).
Physical actions: Sitting in stillness, focusing on breath, sometimes chanting mantras.
Where: Anywhere quiet — home, monastery, temple, nature.
How often: Daily (even 10-20 minutes helps). Monks meditate much more.
Role of community: Can be solo, but sangha (Buddhist community) provides support and group practice.

Key Differences at a Glance

  • Islamic salah is structured, obligatory, and the same for all Muslims worldwide.
  • Christian worship varies greatly by denomination but usually includes community, prayer, and reading Scripture.
  • Hindu puja engages all five senses and is very personal (each family chooses their deity).
  • Buddhist meditation is mind training, not prayer to a god. It's about personal insight and peace.
  • Islam emphasizes structure and obligation. Christianity emphasizes community and Christ. Hinduism emphasizes personal devotion and the senses. Buddhism emphasizes mental discipline.

Assessment Task 1: Knowledge Test

Quick-Fire Questions

Answer these 10 questions. They mix all the traditions we've studied. Click "Show Answer" to reveal the correct answer.

1. What is a murti?

Click to reveal answer
A sacred statue or image of a deity used as a focus for Hindu worship. Hindus believe the divine presence can inhabit the murti, making it a connection point (not an idol to be worshipped itself).

2. What are the five daily Islamic prayers called, in order?

Click to reveal answer
Fajr (dawn), Dhuhr (midday), Asr (afternoon), Maghrib (sunset), Isha (night).

3. In Christianity, what is the Eucharist (Communion)?

Click to reveal answer
A sacred meal where Christians eat bread and drink wine (or grape juice) to remember Christ's last meal and his sacrifice. Different denominations understand it differently (some see it as symbolic, others as a miracle).

4. What is vipassana meditation?

Click to reveal answer
Insight meditation. You observe your thoughts and feelings without reacting to them. The goal is to see reality as it truly is and move toward enlightenment.

5. What is darshan in Hinduism?

Click to reveal answer
The experience of "seeing" the deity (through the murti) and being "seen" by the deity. It's a moment of spiritual connection and blessing.

6. What is prasad?

Click to reveal answer
Food blessed by being offered to a deity in Hindu puja, then distributed to worshippers. Eating it is seen as receiving the deity's grace.

7. What is the difference between du'a and salah?

Click to reveal answer
Salah is obligatory prayer (five times daily, with set movements and words). Du'a is personal prayer (anytime, any words, asking God for help or guidance).

8. What is a mandir?

Click to reveal answer
A Hindu temple. Most Hindu worship happens at home in a shrine, but mandirs serve as communal worship spaces.

9. What is metta bhavana?

Click to reveal answer
Loving-kindness meditation. You systematically send good wishes toward yourself, people you love, neutral people, difficult people, and all beings. The goal is to develop compassion.

10. How many ways did we study for Christians to worship? Name them.

Click to reveal answer
We studied several styles: Liturgical (formal, set prayers), Charismatic (spontaneous, emotional), Quaker (silent, no ritual). Other forms exist (Evangelical, Catholic) but these were our focus.

Assessment Task 3: Full Evaluation

The Big Statement

"All forms of worship are trying to achieve the same thing."

Your Task

Write a full evaluation of this statement. Consider the traditions you've studied. Show both sides, then give your own reasoned conclusion.

FOR (Agree)

Think about what worship might be "trying to achieve." Does every tradition aim for:

  • Connection with the divine or transcendent?
  • Community and belonging?
  • Peace and spiritual fulfillment?
  • Moral guidance or growth?

If you agree, explain how different forms might be achieving the same core goal in different ways.

AGAINST (Disagree)

Think about the actual goals and beliefs:

  • Muslims pray to please Allah. Buddhists meditate to achieve enlightenment (no god involved).
  • Hindus might seek blessing from a chosen deity. Quakers seek silence and inner light.
  • A Christian seeks salvation through Christ. A humanist seeks meaning and community.

If you disagree, explain how these different goals make each form of worship fundamentally different.

Your Conclusion
