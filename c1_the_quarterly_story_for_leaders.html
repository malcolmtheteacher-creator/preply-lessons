The Quarterly Story

C1 Level · How senior leaders narrate the quarter upward — honestly, structurally, without losing credibility

The quarter is settled. The story isn’t.

By the time a senior leader walks into a quarterly review, the numbers have already happened. What is still open is how they get told: the structure, the attribution, the honesty about what was understood in advance and what genuinely surprised the team. Leaders who land their numbers well but tell the story badly lose credibility quietly — one quarter at a time. Leaders who land mixed numbers but tell the story well keep it. The art is honest narration: neither defensive nor self-flagellating, neither hiding the misses nor performing remorse. The senior listener — a CFO, a board, a global head — is reading not only what you say but the shape of how you say it.

Speak · press on the position

The working claim of this lesson: a quarterly review is a story you tell, not a list you read. Defend, complicate, refine. Two or three full sentences per answer.

  1. Think of a recent quarter — yours or your team’s. In two sentences: what was the headline, and what was the story underneath the headline?
  2. Most quarterly reports get the numbers right and the story wrong. Why is that so common, even among experienced leaders?
  3. Defend or complicate the following: “The most expensive thing in a quarterly review is an excuse that sounds like an explanation.”
  4. When have you watched a colleague tell a mixed quarter brilliantly — and what specifically did they do that you would steal?
Speak · sharpen the claim

Pick the one you most want to defend, and defend it for two minutes. Then pick the one you find weakest, and shore it up — even if you’d rather discard it.

  • (a) “A good quarterly story is built before the quarter ends, not the night before the meeting.”
  • (b) “The biggest mistake in a review is to talk about what happened without saying what it tells you about what is going to happen.”
  • (c) “A senior listener can forgive a missed number. They cannot forgive a missed number wrapped in vague language.”
  • (d) “The leader who flags a risk early loses one quarter of comfort. The leader who hides it loses two quarters of credibility.”

Eight chunks to put to work

Read each definition; close it; move on. The point is not to learn them on the card — it is to use them, out loud, in the speaking drill below and in the tasks throughout.

to land the quarter (against plan / against guidance) To finish the period at a particular level relative to what was committed to in advance. “We landed broadly in line with guidance.”
to overdeliver / underdeliver on To beat or fall short of a stated target. Senior-register and neutral — cleaner than “we smashed it” or “we missed.”
a headwind / a tailwind An external force pushing against you (headwind) or in your favour (tailwind). Useful, but be specific — vague headwinds sound like excuses.
to take a one-off hit To absorb a single, non-recurring negative event — a settlement, a write-down, a customer loss that won’t repeat. The opposite of structural.
structural vs. transitory The most important distinction in a quarterly review. Structural = a real, lasting change in the business. Transitory = a passing effect that will reverse.
to flag (a concern / a risk) early To name a problem upward, in advance, while there is still time to do something about it. The opposite of letting it be discovered.
to course-correct To make a deliberate, mid-flight adjustment to a plan in response to what the data is showing. A leadership verb, not a confession.
the forward book / the pipeline The volume of opportunity sitting in front of the business at the close of the period. What the next quarter will draw on.
Speak · rapid-fire drill

Your teacher will read each prompt aloud. Respond in one full sentence using at least one of the chunks. Do not write. If you stall, move on.

  1. “Describe, in one sentence, how your team landed the most recent quarter you remember well.”
  2. “Tell me about a one-off hit your business has absorbed — and how you described it upward.”
  3. “Name a current pressure on your numbers that is structural rather than transitory — and how you know.”
  4. “Tell me about a risk you flagged early — and what would have happened had you not.”
  5. “Describe a moment when your team had to course-correct mid-quarter, and what triggered it.”
  6. “Finish this: the strongest indicator of a healthy forward book is…”

One paragraph. Then we talk.

Read this once, slowly, aloud. It is a model of a well-told quarter — honest, structured, attributed. Notice the shape, not only the content.

The quarter landed broadly in line with the guidance we gave at the start of the year, although the underlying picture was less even than the headline suggested. The first two months delivered comfortably against plan, on the back of strong renewal activity in the core book; the third month was harder, partly because of a one-off settlement that had not been anticipated when guidance was issued, and partly because two larger opportunities slipped into the following quarter. The slippage looks transitory rather than structural — both opportunities remain in the pipeline at the same value — but it was right to flag it to the board in advance rather than allow it to be discovered afterwards. The forward book is healthy and we are not, on current trajectory, asking for a revision to full-year guidance, although we are watching the third-month dynamics carefully.

Phrasing · read aloud, pausing at each comma and semicolon. The sentences are deliberately long and need to breathe at their joints.

1. The writer calls the slippage “transitory rather than structural.” What does the distinction mean here, and why does the writer make it?

Show Answer
Transitory means the effect is passing — the opportunities are still alive in the pipeline at the same value, so the miss does not reflect a lasting change in the business. Structural would mean a real, durable shift — opportunity lost, demand weakened, the model broken. The writer makes the distinction because a senior listener needs to know which kind of miss this is, since each implies a completely different response from the board.

2. Why does the writer emphasise having flagged the third-month dynamics to the board in advance, rather than letting them be discovered afterwards?

Show Answer
Because in senior reporting the worst position is not a missed number; it is a missed number that surprises your listener. Flagging early protects credibility — it shows the leader was awake to the dynamics, not caught by them. The senior listener can then concentrate on the response rather than on the fact of the discovery.
Speak · what does the shape teach you?

The paragraph above is short, but it is highly structured. Walk through it in your own words — out loud, two minutes — saying what each move is doing: the headline, the qualification, the breakdown by period, the cause-attribution, the structural-vs-transitory call, the forward view. Naming the moves out loud is the point.

  1. What would change in your own working life if every quarterly review you gave had this shape?
  2. Pick a recent quarter of your own and re-tell it in this shape — headline, qualification, breakdown, attribution, structural call, forward view. Three minutes, spoken.
  3. What is the most common shape your colleagues use instead, and what does it cost them?

Three grammar clusters that do the work

Quarterly storytelling in English lives in three grammar habits. Read the cards, close them, then go straight into the oral drill below.

Layering time: past simple vs. past perfect

The quarter happened. Other things had already happened that set it up.

By the time we entered the third month, two larger deals had already slipped.

The settlement had not been anticipated when guidance was issued.

We had built the plan on assumptions the first half largely validated.

Contrastive markers

The senior register’s favourite move: yes, but also.

The headline was in line; by contrast, the composition was different.

Although the market softened, the renewal book held up.

Despite the one-off hit, the forward view remains intact.

The first half delivered; the second, whereas stronger in volume, was weaker in mix.

Cause & attribution

What caused what. Specific, not vague.

The strength of Q2 was driven by the renewal book.

The miss is largely attributable to two slipped opportunities.

The growth came on the back of strong new-business activity.

The softening reflects a wider shift in customer behaviour.

Speak · oral transformation drill

Your teacher reads a flat sentence. You reformulate it on the spot, aloud, using the structure indicated. Each item has a model hidden below.

1. Flat: “We hit our number. The market was tough though.”   →   use although…

Show Answer
Although the market was tough, we hit our number.

2. Flat: “Two big opportunities didn’t close in time. That’s what caused the miss.”   →   use attributable to…

Show Answer
The miss is largely attributable to two opportunities that did not close in time.

3. Flat: “When we set the guidance in January, the regulatory change in March hadn’t happened yet.”   →   rewrite using past perfect cleanly…

Show Answer
When we set the guidance in January, the regulatory change in March had not yet occurred. (The earlier event — setting guidance — in past simple; the later non-event in past perfect, viewed back from January.)

4. Flat: “The first half went well. The second half was much harder.”   →   use by contrast…

Show Answer
The first half went well; the second, by contrast, was much harder.

5. Flat: “The strong third quarter came because we won the X account.”   →   use on the back of…

Show Answer
The strong third quarter came on the back of winning the X account.

6. Flat: “We hit the target. But the underlying numbers tell a more complicated story.”   →   use despite…

Show Answer
Despite hitting the target, the underlying numbers tell a more complicated story.

Pick a real (or imagined) quarter from your own working life. In sixty seconds, describe it using at least one example from each cluster: a past-perfect layering of time, a contrastive marker, and a specific cause-attribution. Speak; don’t write.

Three speaking tasks — do all three

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · tell the quarter (4 minutes)

Tell the story of a recent quarter — your own team’s, or one you invent — in the shape of the reading paragraph in Tab 3.

  1. The headline — against what was committed to in advance.
  2. The qualification — what is true beneath the headline.
  3. The breakdown by period — what happened when.
  4. The attribution — what was driven by what.
  5. The structural-vs-transitory call — and how you know.
  6. The forward view — pipeline, guidance, what you are watching.

Weave in at least three vocabulary chunks from Tab 2 and at least two grammar moves from Tab 4.

Task B · demolish the cliche (3 minutes)

A peer says to you over coffee:

“Look, the market was tough, the comparisons were against an exceptional prior year, and we did our best in the circumstances. There’s not really much more to say in the review than that.”

Demolish this, in full sentences, for three minutes. Argue specifically that this is the answer that loses a senior leader credibility — that the absence of structure, attribution, and a forward view is precisely what marks an executive who hasn’t done the work. Use at least one past-perfect, one contrastive marker, and one specific cause-attribution phrase.

Task C · the sceptical listener (4 minutes)

Your teacher plays a sceptical senior listener — a CFO, a global head — who has seen your numbers in advance and now wants the story behind them. They will press where the story is thin.

Speak as you would in the room: structured, specific, neither defensive nor self-flagellating. Concede what must be conceded; hold what should be held. Then swap — you play the sceptical listener, and notice in your own mouth where the questions land hardest.

Final open discussion

  1. The numbers are settled by the time you walk in; what isn’t settled is how they get told. What does that imply for how a senior person should spend the days before a quarterly review — and what is currently consuming those days instead?
  2. A good review distinguishes structural from transitory, and is honest about which is which. When have you watched a colleague get that distinction wrong — and what did it cost them?
  3. “An excuse that sounds like an explanation.” How do you tell, in your own mouth, when you have crossed that line — and what is the rescue?
  4. Imagine the version of you reporting next quarter’s numbers. What is the single discipline you would put in place this quarter to make next quarter’s telling easier?
