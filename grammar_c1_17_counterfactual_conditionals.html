The Road Not Taken

Human beings spend a great deal of their lives in a world that never happened. English has a whole grammar for it — and it goes far beyond the third conditional.

Today: you'll work with the full range of unreal conditionals — mixed, inverted, elliptical and hedged — through the psychology of what might have been. By the end you should be able to build a counterfactual in four different shapes and choose between them.
Before we start — talk these through:
  • Think of a moment when something nearly happened, or nearly didn't. How often do you still replay it?
  • Which hurts more, in your experience: something you did, or something you failed to do?
  • When someone says “at least it wasn't worse”, does that help you?

What might have been, in three studies

Read each part. Answer the quick check from memory, then discuss.

Part 1 — The medal that makes people unhappy

At the 1992 Olympic Games in Barcelona, three psychologists — Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich — recorded the faces of medal winners at the moment their events ended and again on the podium. They then showed the footage, with no sound and no context, to observers who knew nothing about who had won what, and asked them simply to rate how happy each athlete looked.

The result, published in 1995, was uncomfortable. The bronze medallists looked happier than the silver medallists who had beaten them. Objectively, silver is the better outcome; emotionally, it appeared to be the worse one.

The explanation the researchers offered is about comparison. The silver medallist's mind runs upwards: had I been a fraction faster, I would have won. The bronze medallist's runs downwards: had I been a fraction slower, I would have gone home with nothing. Each athlete is measured against a different imaginary result — and it is the imaginary result, not the real one, that seems to set the feeling. A conceptual replication published in 2020 found broadly the same pattern.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What exactly did the observers see, and what were they asked to do?

2. What was the uncomfortable finding?

3. Which imaginary outcome does each of the two medallists compare themselves with?

1. Silent, context-free footage of medallists' faces; they rated how happy each athlete looked.

2. Bronze medallists appeared happier than silver medallists, although silver is objectively better.

3. Silver compares upwards with gold (“if I'd been slightly faster”); bronze compares downwards with fourth place (“if I'd been slightly slower, nothing”).

Discuss:
  • Where else do you see people made unhappy by an outcome that is objectively good?

Part 2 — Two men at an airport

Ten years earlier, Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky had put a scenario to their readers. Mr Crane and Mr Tees leave for the airport in the same car, and are held up in the same traffic jam for the same thirty minutes. Both arrive thirty minutes after their scheduled departure times. Mr Crane's flight left on time. Mr Tees's flight was delayed, and left five minutes ago.

Almost everyone questioned said Mr Tees would be the more upset — although both men missed their flights by exactly the same margin of effort, and neither could have done anything differently.

Kahneman and Tversky's explanation was that our feelings follow how easily the mind can run the alternative film. Five minutes is easy to undo: had the lights been kinder, had he not stopped for coffee, and he would have been on that plane. Thirty minutes is not. They called this mental habit the simulation heuristic, and noticed that the mind prefers to undo the unusual: we rewrite the one day someone took a different route home, not the twenty years they took the usual one.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. In what way are the two men's situations identical?

2. Why do most people say Mr Tees is more upset?

3. What kind of detail does the mind prefer to undo?

1. Same car, same traffic jam, same thirty-minute delay — both miss their flights.

2. Because the alternative is so easy to imagine: he missed it by only five minutes.

3. The unusual one — the day something was done differently, rather than the long-standing routine.

Discuss:
  • Think of a near miss in your own life. Which detail does your mind keep rewriting — and is it the one that actually mattered?

Part 3 — What the alternative film is for

Later researchers, notably Neal Roese and Kai Epstude, argued that counterfactual thinking is not a fault in the machine but a function of it. Upward counterfactuals — how it could have gone better — are largely preparative: they extract a lesson for next time. Downward ones — how it could have gone worse — are largely affective: they comfort. The trouble begins when the upward ones will not switch off, a pattern associated with anxiety and low mood.

There is also a curious pattern in what people regret. Gilovich and Medvec reported that in the short term we regret our actions — the thing we said, the money we spent — while over a lifetime we regret our inactions more: the course not taken, the letter never sent, the person never asked. The sting of a bad decision fades; the wondering does not.

The same grammar can be turned round and pointed forwards. The psychologist Gary Klein proposed a technique he called the pre-mortem: before a project begins, the team is told to imagine that a year has passed and the project has failed completely — and then to explain why. Teams that would never have raised an objection out loud will happily describe a disaster that has already, in the imagination, occurred.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What are upward and downward counterfactuals for, according to Roese and Epstude?

2. How does regret change over time?

3. What does a pre-mortem ask a team to do?

1. Upward ones prepare us for next time; downward ones comfort us.

2. In the short term we regret what we did; over a lifetime we regret what we didn't do.

3. Imagine the project has already failed a year from now, and explain why.

Discuss:
  • Why might it be easier to criticise a plan that has “already failed” than one that is only being proposed?

Grammar — Counterfactuals in Depth

Every sentence in that story lives in an unreal world, and English marks that world by moving the tense one step back. Below is the full range — not a ladder of first, second and third, but a set of choices about distance, direction in time and register.

1 · Mixed conditionals — the two directions. The two halves of a conditional can sit in different times.
Past condition → present result: “If she had taken that job in Lisbon, she would be fluent by now.”
Present condition → past result: “If he weren't so proud, he would have asked for help.”
Choose by asking: when is the condition, and when is the result?
2 · Inversion — the formal, literary register. Drop if and put the auxiliary first.
“Had the flight not been delayed, he would never have reached the gate.”
“Were the decision mine, I would postpone it.”  “Were he to resign, the whole board would follow.”
“Should you change your mind, the offer stands.” (= if you happen to)
Negatives are not contracted: “Had it not been for the rain…” — never ~~Hadn't it been~~.
3 · But for, were it not for, otherwise. Compressed conditions.
“But for a single dropped catch, England would have won.” (= if it had not been for)
“Were it not for the traffic, we'd be there.”  “I had a map. Otherwise I'd still be walking.”
4 · The unreal past after other triggers. The same backshift appears without if:
suppose / supposing / what if / imagine + past (or past perfect): “Suppose you had said nothing — what then?”
if only / I wish: “If only he had asked.”  I'd rather / I'd sooner + past: “I'd rather you didn't.”
it's (high) time + past: “It's high time we left.”  as if / as though + past: “He talks about it as though it were yesterday.”
5 · Degrees of distance. Compare: “If the price rises…” (a live possibility) · “If the price were to rise…” (deliberately remote, hypothetical) · “If the price should rise…” (unlikely, but let's plan) · “If the price happens to rise…” (chance).
This is how careful speakers hold an idea at arm's length without denying it.
6 · The traps. ~~If I would have known~~ → “If I had known”.  ~~Had I have known~~ → “Had I known”.  ~~I wish I would have gone~~ → “I wish I had gone”.  ~~unless he doesn't come~~ → “unless he comes”.  Were, not was, in the formal unreal: “If I were you”, “Were she here…”.

1. Rewrite without if

4. If the government were to raise the tax, imports would fall. (inversion) → , imports would fall.

5. We took the earlier train. If we hadn't, we would have missed the opening. (otherwise) → We took the earlier train; .

2. Mixed conditionals: which direction?

Going deeper — the same grammar, different work

Counterfactuals do not only describe; they assign blame, offer comfort, soften a claim and test an idea. The structure barely changes. What changes is what the speaker is doing with it.

Where the blame lands

Every counterfactual chooses something to undo, and the thing you choose to undo is the thing you quietly hold responsible. Notice how much moves between these two sentences, though the grammar is identical.

“If you hadn't left so late, you'd have caught it.”The undone element is a person's choice — so the sentence allocates fault, whatever the speaker intended.
“If the train hadn't been cancelled, you'd have caught it.”The undone element is circumstance. The same event, the same structure, no blame.

Comfort, and how it fails

“At least…” is a downward counterfactual offered to someone else. It can land as kindness or as dismissal, and the difference is mostly about whether the other person has finished describing the loss.

Compare the effect of “At least it was quick” with “I keep thinking how it might have gone otherwise” — the second stays inside the other person's version of events instead of replacing it.

Distance as tact

The further back you shift the tense, the less committed you sound — which is why the unreal forms do so much diplomatic work. Rewrite each blunt line as something tentative, using the structure in brackets. Say it aloud before you reveal.

1. You should have told me earlier. (had + inversion, no blame)

2. Your plan won't work. (were … to)

3. Stop talking about it. (I'd rather + past)

4. Tell me what you'd do if you lost the contract. (suppose + past)

5. This proposal will fail. (pre-mortem: imagine + present perfect / past)

1. “Had I known earlier, I could have done something about it.” — the sentence now undoes my ignorance rather than your silence.

2. “Were we to go ahead as planned, I think we'd run into trouble with the timing.”

3. “I'd rather we left that for another day.”

4. “Suppose the contract went elsewhere — what would the next six months look like?”

5. “Imagine it's a year from now and this has failed completely. What went wrong?”

Talk it through:
  • Is there a way to say “if only you had…” to someone that genuinely helps? What would have to be true about the moment?
  • Some languages mark the unreal much more lightly than English. Does a heavier grammar make the thinking heavier too?
  • The pre-mortem asks people to describe a failure that hasn't happened. Why does that produce honesty that a normal meeting doesn't?
