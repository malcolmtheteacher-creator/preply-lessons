Three Letters on an Invoice

When the world's seventh-largest shipping line went bankrupt overnight, half a million containers were stranded at sea — and who paid depended on Incoterms almost nobody had read.

Today: you'll work through the logistics disaster that turned trade terms into front-page news — and you'll master the language contracts are written in: provided that, in the event of, unless otherwise agreed, shall.

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Every trade contract carries a three-letter code — FOB, CIF, DDP — defining exactly where costs and risk pass from seller to buyer. These standardised codes are the Incoterms.
Incoterms — International Commercial Terms, published by the ICC and revised roughly every decade. Three letters that decide, among much else, whose problem the ocean is.
2. Under FOB — free on board — the seller's job ends as the goods cross the ship's rail; from that moment, everything that happens at sea is the buyer's affair. That moment is the transfer of risk.
risk transfer (n) — the point where responsibility for loss or damage changes hands. The single most important line in any Incoterm — and, this case shows, the least read.
3. The company that arranges the journey — booking vessels, handling documents, chaining carriers together — without necessarily owning a single ship is the freight forwarder.
a freight forwarder (n) — the travel agent of cargo. In this story, the party thousands of shippers called first — to discover the forwarder was as stuck as they were.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: bill of lading · demurrage · receivership. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means the document that is at once receipt, contract of carriage, and title to the goods?
a bill of lading (B/L) — the shipping industry's master document; whoever holds it, in principle, controls the cargo. In 2016 it turned out to control rather less than everyone assumed.
2. Which term means the daily charges that accumulate when a container overstays its free time at a port or terminal?
demurrage (n) — with its sibling detention (late equipment return). The meter that keeps running during every dispute — often exceeding the value of the goods themselves.
3. Which term means court-supervised administration of a company that cannot pay its debts?
receivership (n) — court protection while a receiver manages the assets; the Korean equivalent is what Hanjin filed for on 31 August 2016. Related: insolvency, bankruptcy, to wind up.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Ports refused Hanjin ships entry, fearing unpaid fees; creditors waited to seize vessels the moment they docked. To seize an asset means…
  (a) to inspect it   (b) to take legal possession of it by force of law   (c) to insure it
(b) — to seize (v); in shipping law, to arrest a vessel. The reason ninety ships became floating no-man's-land: docking anywhere meant capture.
2. Cargo owners learned that standard marine insurance covers loss and damage — but not delay. Goods arriving three months late for Christmas were, in insurance terms…
  (a) fully covered   (b) not covered at all   (c) covered at half value
(b) — the gap this case made famous: the cargo was safe, insured and utterly useless — and lateness itself was nobody's covered peril. Consequential loss is the term for what nobody had bought protection against.

D · Collocation & word building

1. Complete the set every shipper learned that autumn: goods in ___ (between origin and destination) · a supply ___ (the whole connected system) · a ___ point of failure — from the Maersk case, one node that breaks everything.
In transit · supply chain · single point of failure. Hanjin proved a carrier itself could be the single point — roughly 3% of global container capacity, gone between one evening and the next morning.
2. One verb completes all three, from the small print: to ___ liability (accept it) · to ___ the costs (carry them) · the buyer shall ___ all risk from the port of loading.
to bear — bear liability, bear the costs, bear all risk. The contract verb of this entire case: the argument was never about where the boxes were, but about who had agreed to bear what.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — The evening the music stopped

On 31 August 2016, Hanjin Shipping — South Korea's biggest container line and the seventh-largest in the world, carrying roughly three per cent of global container traffic — filed for court receivership. The background was an industry-wide sickness: years of overcapacity, freight rates below cost, and a rate war in which the giants could out-bleed everyone else. Hanjin's banks, led by the state development bank, had finally refused another rescue; its larger domestic rival had declined a merger; and a company that had been loss-making for years simply ran out of road between one evening and the next morning.

What made this bankruptcy unlike any other was its geography. A collapsing airline's planes are on the ground by nightfall; a collapsing shipping line's assets are in the middle of the ocean. At the moment of filing, Hanjin had some ninety vessels at sea, loaded with around half a million containers — cargo whose value was commonly estimated at fourteen billion dollars. Samsung and LG electronics for the American holiday season, European fashion, machine parts, furniture, toys: the pre-Christmas peak season, afloat.

Within hours, the system turned its back. Ports from Shanghai to Valencia refused Hanjin ships entry — why berth a vessel whose owner could no longer pay port dues, pilots or cranes? Fuel suppliers stopped bunkering on credit. And in every jurisdiction without court protection, creditors waited with arrest warrants: the moment a ship docked, it could be seized against Hanjin's debts, with the cargo trapped aboard as a hostage of proceedings. The rational move for every captain was the strangest one: stay at sea. Dozens of ships began drifting offshore — visible on tracking sites, fully loaded, crews aboard, legally radioactive — while the owners of half a million boxes reached for their contracts.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What collapsed on 31 August 2016, and why had it become unrescuable?

2. Why did loaded ships deliberately stay at sea?

1. Hanjin Shipping — world number seven, ~3% of container traffic — after years of industry overcapacity and below-cost rates; its banks refused further support and no merger came. It filed for court receivership.

2. Ports refused entry fearing unpaid fees; fuel credit stopped; and outside protected jurisdictions, docking meant arrest — the ship seized for debts with the cargo trapped aboard. Staying offshore was the only safe move.

Discuss:
  • Every party — ports, suppliers, creditors — acted rationally, and the sum was ninety ghost ships. Where have you seen individually rational moves assemble a collective absurdity?

Part 2 — What the three letters meant

Then came the education. Across thousands of companies, someone pulled out the sales contract and looked — often for the first time with real attention — at three letters. A buyer who had purchased FOB Shanghai discovered that risk had passed to them the moment the container crossed the ship's rail in China: the goods drifting off Long Beach were, and had been for weeks, entirely their problem, though they had never touched them. A buyer on CIF terms found the seller had indeed arranged insurance — marine cargo insurance, which covers sinking, fire and theft, and says nothing whatsoever about a carrier's bankruptcy or a three-month delay. Sellers on DAP and DDP terms — delivered duty paid, risk staying with the seller to the buyer's door — learned the opposite lesson: their revenue floated offshore, unearned, while penalty clauses for late delivery ticked against them. And importers everywhere met the harshest small print of all: freight paid to Hanjin was gone, and getting a box out of a terminal would mean paying again.

Because that became the market: ransom, politely invoiced. Where Hanjin ships did eventually berth — after Korean courts and then American and other courts granted protective orders — terminals and creditors holding the boxes demanded payment before release: outstanding handling fees, new haulage, deposits against the return of Hanjin's containers, figures that ran from hundreds to thousands of dollars per box. Retailers facing empty Christmas shelves paid. Samsung told an American court it had thirty-eight million dollars of goods on just two ships and offered to charter aircraft — air-freighting cargo it had already paid to ship by sea. Meanwhile the humans in the story drifted with the assets: crews rationed food and fuel off foreign coasts for weeks, unpaid, unable to land, the invisible casualties of a paperwork war.

The unwinding took months. Protective rulings let ships dock in designated safe ports; cargo trickled out terminal by terminal, negotiation by negotiation; the fleet was sold, the routes carved up, and in February 2017 the courts declared Hanjin bankrupt — the largest failure in container shipping history. Total costs to cargo interests were never cleanly counted: the boxes mostly arrived, eventually, which meant classic insurance rarely paid, which meant the losses — missed seasons, cancelled orders, penalty clauses, double freight — landed wherever the contracts had quietly left them.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did FOB, CIF and DDP buyers/sellers each discover?

2. What did it cost to retrieve a container, and who ultimately absorbed the losses?

1. FOB buyers: the sea risk had been theirs since the ship's rail in the origin port. CIF buyers: the included insurance covered loss and damage, not carrier bankruptcy or delay. DAP/DDP sellers: risk was still theirs, revenue unearned, late-delivery penalties running.

2. Hundreds to thousands of dollars per box in re-paid fees, haulage and deposits — freight already paid to Hanjin was simply lost. Since goods mostly arrived (late), insurance rarely responded; losses fell wherever the contract's risk-transfer point had put them.

Discuss:
  • "The goods were safe, insured, and useless." Whose job in a company is it to imagine that exact combination before it happens?

Part 3 — The audit nobody had done

The post-mortems converged on an uncomfortable finding: almost nothing about the catastrophe was unforeseeable, and almost nobody had bought protection against it. Incoterms had done exactly what they say on the tin — they are a risk map, agreed in advance, and the map had been treated as boilerplate: chosen by habit, copied from last year's contract, delegated to whoever typed the purchase order. Companies that had negotiated prices to the second decimal had never once negotiated the three letters that decided who owned a disaster. The gaps had names, afterwards: no carrier-insolvency cover (available, cheap, almost never bought), no delay cover, sole-carrier dependence, and bills of lading whose fine print favoured a carrier that no longer existed.

The industry adjusted the way industries do — partially, and while the memory lasted. Cargo insurers reported a surge in demand for insolvency and delay extensions; big shippers began auditing which carriers actually moved their boxes (alliances meant your contracted line often loaded your cargo onto someone else's ship — including, that autumn, onto Hanjin's); logistics teams gained, briefly, the board's attention. The deeper lessons kept proving themselves on schedule: Ever Given wedged in Suez, pandemic port chaos, Red Sea rerouting — each crisis re-asking Hanjin's question, who bears what, between which two points?, and each finding a new cohort of firms that had signed the answer without reading it.

Which is why this case closes the series' logistics file with a clerical moral rather than a heroic one. Ørsted's fate hung on interest-rate assumptions nobody wrote down; Hanjin's victims hung on risk clauses everybody had written down and nobody had read. The three letters were never small print — they were the whole allocation of a fourteen-billion-dollar question, sitting in plain sight on every invoice. The question the case hands every company that buys or sells across a border is brutally checkable: pull ten of your own live contracts today — who chose the Incoterm on each, when did risk last actually transfer on your goods, and if your carrier filed for receivership tonight, which of those ten contracts would you be reading for the first time?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the post-mortems find about foreseeability and protection?

1. Nothing essential was unforeseeable: Incoterms mapped the risk exactly as agreed, but firms had treated them as boilerplate — while carrier-insolvency cover and delay cover existed cheaply and went unbought, and sole-carrier dependence went unaudited.

2. A surge in insolvency/delay insurance, carrier audits (alliances meant cargo often sailed on other lines' ships), brief board attention — and each later crisis (Suez, pandemic ports, Red Sea) re-asked "who bears what, between which points?" to a fresh set of unread contracts.

Grammar — the language of contracts

The Hanjin disaster was decided by sentences written years earlier. Contract English is its own dialect — conditionals with special connectors, obligations carried by shall — and a professional needs to read it precisely and draft it safely.

The contract conditionals — refinements of "if":
• provided (that) / on condition that — permission with a leash: "The seller shall arrange insurance, provided that the buyer requests it in writing."
• unless — the default plus its exception: "Risk passes at the ship's rail, unless otherwise agreed." (= if not)
• in the event of + noun / in the event that + clause — the disaster clause: "In the event of the carrier's insolvency, the buyer shall bear all retrieval costs."
• subject to — this promise ranks below that document: "Delivery dates are subject to the carrier's schedule."
• should + subject + verb (formal inversion) — a polished "if": "Should the vessel be delayed, no liability shall attach to the seller."
The two modal pillars:
• shall — in contracts, not future but obligation: "The buyer shall pay within 30 days" = must, enforceably. Negative: shall not = is forbidden to.
• may — permission or right: "The carrier may substitute another vessel." — a sentence that cost many Hanjin shippers dearly.

Reading defensively: every subject to, unless and may is a door someone else can open. The professional's habit: for each one, ask who benefits if this clause activates? If the answer is never you, that is the clause to negotiate.

Going deeper

A note on the record: vessel, container and value figures are the widely reported estimates and vary by source; retrieval-fee ranges are drawn from contemporary reporting and court filings; individual companies' losses were mostly settled privately and never fully disclosed.

What the case actually tests. First, the boilerplate trap. Incoterms failed nobody — they performed their allocation flawlessly; what failed was the treatment of allocation as formatting. Any clause that is copied rather than chosen is a decision someone else made for you, renewed annually by inattention. The audit is mechanical: for each recurring contract, name the human who last consciously chose its risk terms. Where no name exists, the clause owns you.

Second, insurance as a shape, not a blanket. The victims were insured — for the wrong perils. Marine cover answers sinking; nothing standard answers "the goods are perfect, the season is over." Mapping cover to consequence (delay, insolvency, consequential loss) is dull, cheap and almost never done until afterwards — the Ørsted lesson's cousin: the unpriced assumption, here literally purchasable for a small premium.

Third, the invisible chain. Alliances meant your contracted carrier was often not your physical carrier — firms discovered Hanjin exposure they could not have named the day before. Modern parallels multiply: cloud subcontractors, component suppliers' suppliers, payment processors' banks. The Hanjin question generalises to all of them: through whose hands does your business actually pass, and what happens at each hand in the event of its failure? Companies that can answer are rare; every crisis since 2016 has published the list of those that couldn't.

Talk it through:
  • Run the "name the human" audit on your own paperwork — employment terms, supplier contracts, even your phone contract. Which clauses did nobody at your end ever choose?
  • Delay cover and insolvency cover existed and went unbought. Why do organisations insure dramatic risks and ignore probable ones? Name the bias, and its fix.
  • The crews drifted for weeks, unpaid, as a side effect of a paperwork war. Who owed them what — carrier, flag state, cargo owners, ports? Assign the duty honestly.
  • Series thread: Ørsted was undone by an assumption nobody wrote down; Hanjin's victims by clauses everybody wrote and nobody read. Which failure is more excusable — and which is more fixable?
