Now: the invisible architecture

What makes the difference between someone who speaks for 5 minutes and holds your attention vs. someone who loses you after 30 seconds? It's not vocabulary or accent or confidence alone. It's cohesion — the invisible threads that connect one idea to the next. When a listener can follow the logical or narrative path, you keep them. When ideas feel scattered or disconnected, even brilliant thoughts fall flat.

Now: The Problem

You've learned to FRAME responses with composure, POSITION arguments with structure, and NAVIGATE disagreement diplomatically. But here's the challenge: many advanced speakers can do all three — and still lose their audience during extended turns.

The Core Skill

Cohesion isn't about longer sentences or fancier vocabulary. It's about visible connectors that let listeners track your thinking in real-time. The difference between "rambling" and "sustained discourse" is the threads you make audible.

Consider two speakers during a 5-minute response about their career journey:

Same experience. Different coherence.

Speaker A: "I started in consulting. Then I moved to tech. I worked on some challenging projects. I learned a lot about leadership. There's something about building teams that fascinates me. I've now worked in startups and corporates. Both taught me different lessons..."

Speaker B: "I started in consulting because I wanted to understand how organisations work. Building on that foundation, I moved to tech because I wanted to see those theories applied at speed. What ties this together is a passion for solving problems that matter. To circle back to where I opened: I didn't follow a plan. The throughline was always learning. This connects to a broader question about what expertise really means in fast-moving fields."

Same career. Radically different impact. The difference? Visible cohesion markers that connect one idea to the previous one, creating a narrative arc rather than a list.

Today you'll learn 5 CONNECT strategies — the fifth step in the Discourse Flow Sequence. These are the expressions that turn isolated points into linked ideas: Building → Retrieving → Synthesising → Naming Pattern → Elevating.

Language Focus A: Forward and Backward Links

Three strategies that make your extended turns feel like a journey, not a list. Click each one, study it, then try the speaking task before looking at examples.

🔗 "Building on that point..." Click to expand
Function
Extends a previous idea rather than introducing something new; creates forward momentum
Register
Professional / Flowing — signals continuation, not digression
When to Use
Deepening the last point; adding a layer; listener feels carried along
Developing a career narrative:

"The initial rollout was slower than anticipated. Building on that point, we learned that training needed to be simultaneous with implementation, not sequential."

Stress "building" — signals continuation not digression.

Now You Try

Make this point: "The hardest part of learning a language isn't grammar — it's confidence." Then use "Building on that..." to develop it further with examples or evidence. Speak for 1-2 minutes. Or pick your own opening claim.

↩️ "To circle back to what I mentioned earlier..." Click to expand
Function
Retrieves an earlier point and reconnects it; shows intentional structure
Register
Professional / Conversational — "circle back" is idiomatic; use conversationally, not formally
When to Use
After exploring new territory; bringing back an earlier thread; closing loops
Connecting past and present:

"To circle back to what I mentioned earlier about analytical skills, that training is exactly what makes me useful in a creative industry."

Pronunciation tip: "Circle back" is idiomatic — use it conversationally, not formally.

Now You Try

Talk about how technology has changed the way people make friends. Make a clear opening point. Then explore a second angle (e.g. loneliness, online communities, social skills). Then circle back: "To circle back to what I said at the start..." The listener should feel: "Ah! That's how it all fits together." Or choose your own topic.

🧵 "What ties this together is..." Click to expand
Function
Identifies the common thread across several ideas; prevents scattered feeling
Register
Professional / Reflective — shows pattern recognition and synthesis
When to Use
After listing or exploring 3+ related ideas; unifying them; showing the pattern
Connecting multiple experiences:

"I've worked in consulting, startups, and corporate. What ties this together is a passion for solving problems that matter to people's daily lives."

Stress "ties" — you're actively synthesising.

Now You Try

List three things that have shaped who you are — places you've lived, hobbies, challenges, people who influenced you. Then use: "What ties this together is..." to identify what connects them. Make it feel like genuine insight, not an afterthought.

Language Focus B: Naming the Arc & Elevating

Two more strategies — these help you show self-awareness about the journey you're on, and elevate specific examples to bigger principles.

📖 "The throughline here is..." Click to expand
Function
Names the larger narrative or principle running through your story; shows self-awareness
Register
Professional / Reflective — "throughline" borrowed from drama/theatre; signals sophistication
When to Use
After telling a story or journey; identifying the deeper principle; showing you understand the arc
Reflecting on a learning journey:

"We pivoted twice and eventually succeeded. The throughline, though, is learning to be comfortable with ambiguity."

Pronunciation tip: "Throughline" — borrowed from drama/theatre. It's the thread that runs start to finish.

Now You Try

Tell the story of learning something difficult — a language, a sport, driving, cooking, anything. Describe the stages (2-3 minutes). Then step back: "The throughline here is..." What was the deeper lesson underneath the surface story?

🌐 "This connects to the broader question of..." Click to expand
Function
Elevates a specific example to a general principle; zooms out from details to big picture
Register
Academic / Intellectual — shows you can connect specific to universal
When to Use
After a specific example; connecting to larger themes; showing the stakes
Connecting experience to principle:

"We struggled to hire the right talent. This connects to the broader question of whether traditional hiring criteria actually measure what matters."

Pronunciation tip: "Broader question" — academic register, powerful in professional settings.

Now You Try

Give a specific example: a time you changed your mind about something important (2 minutes). Then zoom out: "This connects to the broader question of..." — how do people actually change their beliefs? Make the listener feel: "Oh, this is bigger than just one story."

Choose Your Challenge

The Comprehensive Overview

Give a complete picture of something you're learning, building, or working towards right now — a skill, a project, a goal, a life change. Why does it matter to you? How did you get here? The listener should feel like they understand the arc. Use cohesion markers to show how pieces relate.

Target: clear opening, layered development, visible connections between ideas, satisfying conclusion

The Problem-Solution Narrative

Describe a significant challenge you've faced (situation → difficulty → approaches → results). Use CONNECT to show how each phase led to the next and what you learned about solving problems.

Target: coherent narrative, causal links visible, throughline clear, listener understands the journey

The "Teach Someone" Challenge

Teach someone about a topic you know well — how to cook a perfect meal, how a language works, how a sport or game is played, how a country's culture differs from outsiders' expectations. Start from basics and build to complexity. Use CONNECT expressions to show how foundational ideas lead to advanced understanding.

Target: scaffolded learning, each idea builds on the previous, visible expertise, thread is clear

Self-Monitoring

As you speak: Is the thread clear? Can a listener follow the journey without getting confused? Where do you feel yourself jumping rather than building? Those moments reveal where your cohesion needs work.
