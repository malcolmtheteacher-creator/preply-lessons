BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Braveheart

A farmer's son leads a rebellion against an empire. The real story wears no kilt, and there was no princess.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Braveheart — a Scottish farmer's son who becomes the leader of a rebellion against an empire — discover how much of the film is legend and how much is real history, and master the two forms English uses to talk about the way things used to be, and the things people would do again and again in the past.
Before we begin — talk it through: What makes an ordinary person willing to risk their life for a cause bigger than themselves? Can a personal loss — losing someone you love — turn into a political rebellion? And when a country tells the story of its own history, should it be allowed to bend the facts to make a better legend?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. Under the old king's tyranny, ordinary people could be thrown in prison or killed for the smallest complaint, with no court to appeal to and no law to protect them.
tyranny (n) — cruel and unjust rule by a person or group with total power, and no real accountability.
2. The town had lived under an armed garrison for years — a company of foreign soldiers stationed there specifically to watch the streets and crush any sign of trouble.
a garrison (n) — a group of troops stationed in a town or fort to hold and control it.
3. High on the cliff, the old fortress had long served as a stronghold — a heavily defended base that no enemy army had ever managed to capture.
a stronghold (n) — a place that is strongly defended and hard for an enemy to take; a safe base for a group.
4. After his execution, the young preacher became a martyr: people who had never met him told his story for generations, inspired by a man who died rather than betray his beliefs.
a martyr (n) — someone who suffers death rather than give up their beliefs, and who is honoured afterward for it.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. unyielding is closest in meaning to: (a) easily persuaded   (b) refusing to give in, whatever the pressure   (c) uncertain and nervous
(b) refusing to give in, whatever the pressure — unyielding (adj) means firm and unbending; not giving way under any pressure.
6. vengeance is closest in meaning to: (a) forgiveness   (b) a formal apology   (c) punishment taken in return for a wrong, driven by anger
(c) punishment taken in return for a wrong, driven by anger — vengeance (n) — harm done to someone in return for harm they did to you or someone you love.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. The general sent extra soldiers to quash the small uprising before it could spread to other towns.
To quash a rebellion is: (a) to fund it secretly; (b) to crush it and stop it by force; (c) to negotiate a peaceful end to it.
(b) — to quash (v) — to put an end to something, especially a rebellion or a rumour, by force or authority.
8. The invading army wanted to subjugate the whole region, forcing every town to obey its laws and pay it tribute.
To subjugate a people is: (a) to bring them completely under your control by force; (b) to trade fairly with them; (c) to leave them alone.
(a) — to subjugate (v) — to conquer a person or group and force them into complete obedience.

D. Infer from context — two bigger ideas

9. His own cousin secretly told the enemy where he was hiding — an act of treachery that the family never forgave, even decades later.
treachery (n) — a deliberate betrayal of trust, especially of someone who believed they were safe from you.
10. Hearing the news, farmers and shepherds from every valley began to rally to the cause, arming themselves with whatever tools they had, ready to fight together.
to rally (v) — to come together and unite in support of a shared cause, especially in a difficult moment.

The film, in three parts

Mel Gibson's Braveheart (1995). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it. There is violence and tragedy in this story; we handle it plainly, without dwelling on it.)

Part 1 — A farmer's son and a stolen peace

In the late 13th century, Scotland lives under the rule of England's King Edward I, a ruthless monarch determined to control it completely. As a boy, William Wallace loses his father and older brother to English soldiers, and is raised by his uncle, who teaches him languages, learning, and the sword. Years later, grown into a quiet man who wants nothing to do with politics, William returns to his home village, wins back his childhood friend Murron, and marries her — in secret, to avoid drawing English attention to the match.

The peace does not last. When an English soldier from the local garrison tries to assault Murron, she fights back, and is captured and killed in retaliation, in full view of the village. Wallace's private grief turns, almost instantly, into public rebellion: he leads the men of his village in a raid on the garrison and kills the English lord responsible with his own hands.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happens to Wallace's family when he is a boy, and who raises him afterward?

2. What event turns Wallace from a quiet farmer into a rebel leader?

1. English soldiers kill his father and older brother; his uncle raises him and teaches him languages and swordsmanship.

2. His wife Murron is killed by the local English garrison after she resists an assault; Wallace responds by raiding the garrison and killing the lord responsible.

Discuss:
  • Is a rebellion that begins as one man's personal grief and revenge less legitimate than one that begins as a political idea — or does it not matter how a just cause starts?

Part 2 — Freedom, at a price

Word of Wallace's raid spreads fast, and men from across Scotland rally to him — farmers and clansmen with no formal army, only spears, courage and a shared fury. At the Battle of Stirling, his makeshift force faces a much larger, professional English army — and wins a stunning, decisive victory. Wallace is knighted and named a Guardian of Scotland, tasked with leading the resistance in the name of a Scottish nobility too divided and self-interested to unite behind him.

That division proves fatal. At the Battle of Falkirk, nobles who had promised Wallace their support pull their men from the field before the fighting even starts, and his forces are cut down by the retreating English. In the chaos, Wallace comes face to face with a mounted, masked knight who nearly kills him — and, tearing off the helmet, discovers it is Robert the Bruce, a Scottish nobleman he had trusted, fighting on the English side.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happens at Stirling, and what title is Wallace given afterward?

2. What goes wrong for Wallace at Falkirk, and who does he unexpectedly find fighting against him?

1. Wallace's outnumbered force defeats the English army; he is knighted and made a Guardian of Scotland.

2. Scottish nobles withdraw their promised support and his army is broken; he discovers Robert the Bruce fighting for the English.

Discuss:
  • Robert the Bruce is torn throughout the story between his father's caution and his own conscience. Is it ever right to compromise with an enemy for the sake of long-term survival, even if it costs an ally's trust?

Part 3 — Betrayal, capture and a cry for freedom

Ashamed of Falkirk, Robert the Bruce quietly begins to admire Wallace from a distance, but for years afterward Wallace fights on alone and in the shadows — striking English supply lines and vanishing, refusing every offer of pardon or compromise. He travels as far as France seeking support, and comes to know Princess Isabella, who grows to respect him and, in the film's version of events, secretly tries to protect him.

Eventually a Scottish nobleman sells Wallace's location to the English, and he is captured near Edinburgh. Put on trial in London for treason — a charge he rejects, since he says he never swore loyalty to an English king — he is condemned to a public execution. Even as he is tortured before the jeering crowd, he refuses to submit, and cries out a single word with his last breath: "Freedom." Watching this, Robert the Bruce finally takes up Wallace's cause and, years later, leads the Scots to the decisive victory Wallace never lived to see.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How is Wallace finally captured, and on what charge is he tried?

2. What does Wallace do with his final breath, and what does Robert the Bruce do afterward?

1. A Scottish nobleman betrays his location for money; he is tried in London for treason, a charge he denies.

2. He shouts "Freedom!" as he is executed; Robert the Bruce is inspired to lead Scotland to the victory Wallace never saw.

Discuss:
  • The film suggests Wallace's death is what finally unites Scotland. Can a martyr sometimes achieve more for a cause by dying for it than by living for it?

Grammar — used to & would

This is a story about a world that changed — the way Scotland used to be, and the things its people would do, before everything was forced to become different. English has two structures for exactly this kind of memory, and they are not interchangeable.

used to + verb — for a past habit or a past state, always implying a contrast with a different present. "Before the English occupation, William used to spend his days working his family's land." "Scotland used to be an independent kingdom, ruled by its own king." Negative: didn't use to — "The Scottish clans didn't use to fight under a single leader — that only happened once Wallace forced them together."
would + verb — only for a repeated past action, never a state. "In the evenings, the clan elders would gather by the fire and tell stories of Scotland's old kings." Careful: would cannot describe a state — you can say "Scotland used to be free" but never "Scotland would be free" (that would mean a prediction, not a memory).
Contrast with the past simple: a single, completed event — not a habit, not a state — always takes the plain past simple. "In 1297, Wallace's men defeated the English at Stirling." That happened once, so it can never be used to defeat or would defeat.

1. Used to or would? Choose the form the meaning needs

Read the clue in brackets, then complete the sentence. Remember: would cannot describe a state.

1. Before Edward's invasion, Scotland (have) its own king and its own laws, answering to no one. (a state, not an action)

2. On quiet evenings at the farm, William's uncle (teach) him Latin and swordplay. (a repeated action)

3. Many Scottish nobles (own) land on both sides of the border, and had sworn an oath of loyalty to Edward. (a state)

1. used to have (a state — would is wrong here)

2. would teach / used to teach (a repeated action — would fits well)

3. used to own (a state — would is wrong here)

2. Fix the form

Each sentence uses would to describe something that is really a state, not a repeated action. Rewrite the verb correctly.

1. Before 1296, William an ordinary man, with no thought of leading an army.

2. Many common Scots that Robert the Bruce's family had a rightful claim to the throne.

3. The Wallace family a small piece of land near Elderslie — nothing grand.

1. used to be (a state)

2. used to believe (a state)

3. used to own (a state)

All three describe states, not repeated actions, so would was wrong in every case — used to is correct.

3. Complete the story — mix used to, would and the past simple

Fill each gap, then read the story aloud.

Before the English came, William (help) his father on the family's land, and in the evenings the two of them (walk) together through the fields, talking about Scotland's old kings. Scotland (be) a free country then — until English soldiers arrived and everything changed. In 1297, Wallace's men (defeat) the English army at Stirling, and for a while, freedom felt possible again.

would help / used to help · would walk · used to be · defeated

Notice the pattern: the first two describe repeated actions (would or used to both work); the third is a state (used to only); the last is a single historical event, so it takes the plain past simple.

The Real Story — myth vs history

Braveheart is loosely based on a real man and real events — but almost every detail the film adds to make the story more dramatic turns out to be invented, and one of its most famous scenes leaves out the single fact that actually won the battle.

🌉 The battle that leaves out its own bridge

The real Battle of Stirling Bridge (11 September 1297) was won because of a bridge — the one thing the film's battle scene doesn't have. The River Forth could only be crossed at Stirling by a narrow wooden bridge, wide enough for just two horsemen riding side by side. William Wallace, commanding jointly with a Scottish nobleman named Andrew Moray, waited until roughly half the English army had crossed, then attacked — trapping thousands of soldiers on the wrong side of a bottleneck and cutting them down before the rest could reach them. The film restages this as an open-field battle with no bridge at all, and Andrew Moray, Wallace's co-commander, is left out of the story entirely.

A defeat the film gets mostly right — with one invented detail

The Battle of Falkirk (22 July 1298) really did end in a devastating Scottish defeat: English longbowmen broke Wallace's tightly packed schiltrons — circles of spearmen — with volley after volley of arrows, and Wallace resigned as Guardian of Scotland soon after. The film's most dramatic addition is the scene of Robert the Bruce riding for the English against Wallace at Falkirk. There is no historical evidence this happened. The Bruce family's loyalties genuinely shifted between the Scottish and English sides more than once in these years, but no record places Robert the Bruce personally on that battlefield fighting Wallace.

The kilt, the face paint, the princess — and the man who wasn't poor

Several of the film's most memorable images belong to the wrong century entirely. The belted plaid kilt the Scots wear did not exist until roughly 300 years after Wallace's death; medieval Scots wore tunics and cloaks, like other Europeans of the period. The blue woad face paint belonged to the Picts, who painted for battle roughly a thousand years before Wallace was born — by his time, the custom was long gone.

The romance with Princess Isabella of France is chronologically impossible: she was around two years old at the time of Wallace's rebellion, still living in France, and did not arrive in England until 1308 — three years after Wallace's execution. They never met. The film's "right of the first night," which Wallace and Murron marry in secret to avoid, is very likely a myth too: no genuine medieval legal document has ever confirmed it existed, and historians increasingly view it as a much later invention. And Wallace himself was not a poor commoner: he was the son of a minor landowner and knight — part of the lesser nobility — almost certainly educated in languages and combat from a young age. Even Murron may not have existed at all; the story of a beloved wife killed by an English sheriff first appears in a poem written some 170 years after Wallace's death, with no earlier record of her.

The real execution

Wallace was betrayed by a Scottish knight, John Menteith, and captured near Glasgow in August 1305. He was taken almost 400 miles to London for a trial that was never meant to be fair — he was not permitted a lawyer or the chance to speak in his own defence. Found guilty of treason, he was executed on 23 August 1305 by hanging, drawing and quartering: a brutal, multi-stage punishment Edward I was among the very first English kings to use, with Wallace's remains afterward displayed in four different towns as a warning. There is no historical record of his final words — the famous cry of "Freedom!" is a Hollywood invention.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, which of these details did you assume were true, and which surprise you most now?
  • The film invents a romance, a villainous betrayal and a poorer hero to make the story more moving. Does that honestly change how you feel about the real history — or does it not matter?
  • Wallace's most famous line was never actually said by him. Does that change its power?

Task B · The impossible choice (4 minutes)

William Wallace's fight begins with a personal loss and becomes a national rebellion against an occupying power. Build an argument: is violent resistance against an unjust ruler ever justified — and does it matter whether the people fighting have any official authority to do so, or only their own conviction? Bring in other examples you know: independence movements, resistance fighters, revolutions.

Task C · Myth vs history (4 minutes)

Every country tells stories about its own history that are not entirely true — braver, simpler, more heroic than the real events. Is that harmless, or dangerous? Make your case.
