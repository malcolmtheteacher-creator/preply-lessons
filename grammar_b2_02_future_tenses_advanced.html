Listen in on this conversation

Jess: What time are you setting off tomorrow?

Tom: About eight. I'll be driving past your street around half eight, actually.

Jess: Perfect timing — by then I'll have packed everything.

Tom: How long has the kitchen been out of action now?

Jess and Tom use three different ways of talking about the future in just a few lines. Can you spot all three? Say them before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "I'll be driving past your street" — will be + -ing, a snapshot of something in progress at a specific future moment.
2. "I'll have packed everything" — will have + past participle, finished before that moment arrives.
3. "we'll have been living on takeaways" — will have been + -ing, the stretch of time behind that moment.

These three structures don't just say what will happen — they say exactly when, in relation to a point in time. That precision is what today is about.
Today: the grammar of looking ahead precisely — will be doing, will have done, and will have been doing — so you can talk about deadlines, moments caught in progress, and how long things will have lasted, all with confidence.

Right in the Middle of It

The tense for a snapshot of the future — something already happening at a specific moment.

Spot the pattern

  • "I'll be working at nine tomorrow — call after ten if it's urgent."
  • "This time next week, I'll be lying on a beach somewhere."
  • "Will you be needing the meeting room this afternoon? I could use it myself for an hour."

What's the shared machinery, and what's the third sentence doing that's different? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
will / won't + be + -ing. Use it for an action already in progress at a specific future point — imagine a clock stopping at that exact moment. The third sentence uses the same form as a polite question about someone's existing plans — it just checks the natural flow of things, without pushing anyone to decide right now.
will / won't + be + verb-ing  (+ at [time point] / this time [tomorrow / next year])
A "Will you be + -ing...?" question sounds softer than "Are you going to...?" — it simply checks your plans, with no pressure to decide anything on the spot.

Quick check

1. This time tomorrow, you'll be mid-flight. Describe it — use the continuous form of fly:

"This time tomorrow, I over the Atlantic."

2. You want to borrow a colleague's laptop later — ask politely, without pushing for a decision, using use:

"Sorry to ask — will you your laptop this afternoon? I could really use it for an hour."

By Then, It'll Be Done

The tense for a deadline — something finished before a specific future point, not during it.

Spot the pattern

  • "By June, we'll have paid off the loan."
  • "She'll have finished her exams by the end of May."
  • "Don't call before seven — I won't have left the office yet."

What's the shared machinery, and what time-word keeps appearing? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
will / won't + have + past participle. Use it for something completed before a specific future point — the deadline word is almost always by (by June, by seven, by the time...). Nothing is happening AT that moment; it's already behind you.
will / won't + have + past participle  (+ by [time point] / by the time...)
The key contrast: AT vs BY. "I'll be working at nine" places you in the middle of something happening right then (future continuous). "I'll have finished by nine" places the finish line before or at that point — the job is already done (future perfect). Swap the two time-words and the whole meaning breaks.

Quick check

1. Guests arrive at seven. Reassure your partner about dinner — use cook, with the deadline by seven:

"Don't panic — by seven, I dinner."

2. Reassure a nervous colleague about the budget — negative, use spend, deadline by Friday:

"Relax — by Friday, we the whole budget."

How Long Will That Be?

The tense for the stretch of time behind a future point — not just the fact, but the duration.

Spot the pattern

  • "By then, I'll have been living here for ten years."
  • "When she retires, she'll have been teaching for three decades."
  • "By the time the train arrives, we'll have been waiting two hours."

Compare: "By June I'll have finished the course" (done) vs "By June I'll have been training for a year" (duration). What's the difference? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
will / won't + have + been + -ing. Use it when the DURATION itself is the point — how long something will have been going on by a future moment — not just the single fact that it happened. "Will have finished" names one achievement; "will have been training" names the whole stretch of time behind it.
will / won't + have + been + verb-ing  (+ for [duration] by [time point])

Quick check

1. You're renting your flat, moving out next spring, exactly five years after moving in. Describe it — use rent:

"By the time we move out, we this flat for five years."

2. Mark a language-learning milestone this June, using study:

"This June, I English for two years."

Now say it

SPEAK
