BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Interstellar

A father leaves a dying Earth to save it — and loses years of his children's lives to the physics of a black hole. The science is real.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Interstellar — a journey to save humanity that costs one man decades with his children — discover how much of its mind-bending science is actually true, and master the two tenses English uses to stand in the present and look at a finished future: the future perfect and the future continuous.
Before we begin — talk it through: Would you leave everyone you love for years — even for ever — if it were the only way to save them? What would you need to believe to say yes? And do you think humanity will one day have to leave the Earth?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. The early settlers pushed west to the frontier, out where the maps ran blank and the known, settled world finally gave way to wild, unexplored land.
a frontier (n) — the outer edge of known or settled territory; the boundary of what has been explored.
2. Trapped and cut off, they watched their water supply dwindle day by day, until a full barrel had shrunk to a few warm mouthfuls.
to dwindle (v) — to grow steadily smaller or fewer until almost nothing is left.
3. A blight spread through the fields, turning healthy green wheat black and rotten and wiping out the whole harvest in a matter of weeks.
a blight (n) — a disease that destroys plants and crops; more broadly, something that ruins or spoils.
4. The situation was dire: no food left, no fuel, the last road blocked, and a storm already darkening the sky.
dire (adj) — extremely serious, urgent or hopeless; as bad as it can get.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. relentless is closest in meaning to: (a) gentle   (b) never-stopping   (c) occasional
(b) never-stopping — relentless (adj) means continuing without any pause, mercy or let-up.
6. finite is closest in meaning to: (a) limited   (b) endless   (c) invisible
(a) limited — finite (adj) means having an end or a fixed limit; the opposite of infinite.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. One careless mistake could jeopardise the whole mission, putting every life on board at risk.
To jeopardise something is: (a) to complete it early; (b) to put it in danger; (c) to pay for it.
(b) — to jeopardise (v) — to put something valuable at risk of being lost or harmed.
8. Everyone said she would never walk again, but she defied them all and finished a marathon a year later.
To defy someone is: (a) to obey them; (b) to thank them; (c) to boldly resist or prove them wrong.
(c) — to defy (v) — to refuse to obey or accept something; to successfully resist or challenge it.

D. Infer from context — two bigger ideas

9. The old teacher's legacy lived on in the thousands of students she had shaped, long after she herself was gone.
a legacy (n) — something handed down from the past or left behind by a person: an influence, an achievement, a consequence that outlives them.
10. In a dark room the pupils of your eyes dilate, widening to let in as much light as they can.
to dilate (v) — to become wider or to stretch open. The noun is dilation — and, as you'll see, time itself can be stretched.

The film, in three parts

Christopher Nolan's Interstellar (2014). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A dying world

The Earth of the near future is quietly running out of time. A crop blight has swept the planet; the great harvests have dwindled, dust storms roll across the plains, and humanity has shrunk into a tired farming society that no longer looks up at the stars. Cooper, a widowed former test pilot and engineer, now grows corn with his father-in-law and his two children — teenage Tom and ten-year-old Murph, a fiercely curious girl who insists there is a "ghost" knocking books from her shelf.

The "ghost" turns out to be a message. Following its coordinates, Cooper and Murph stumble on the remains of NASA, now working in secret. The truth is dire: the blight will soon make the air unbreathable, and this generation of children may be the last. But there is a thread of hope. A wormhole — a shortcut through space — has appeared near Saturn, and beyond it lie possible new worlds. Cooper is asked to pilot humanity's final, desperate mission through it.

To go, he must leave his children behind, not knowing if he will ever return. Murph, who has worked out where he is going, is furious and heartbroken, and begs him to stay. He promises he will come back. Neither of them can know what that promise will cost.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What has happened to the Earth, and what does NASA discover near Saturn?

2. Why is Cooper's goodbye to Murph so painful?

1. A crop blight and dust are slowly making Earth uninhabitable; NASA finds a wormhole near Saturn that could lead to new, survivable worlds.

2. He must leave his children to fly the mission, with no way of knowing if he'll ever see them again — and Murph feels betrayed.

Discuss:
  • Cooper says a parent is "the ghost of their children's future." Is it braver to leave, to try to save everyone, or to stay with the ones you love for whatever time is left?

Part 2 — The planet where an hour costs seven years

Through the wormhole, Cooper's small crew must choose which distant worlds to explore — and every choice is shadowed by a monstrous fact. One promising planet, a shallow ocean world, orbits perilously close to an enormous black hole named Gargantua. So close that the black hole's gravity bends time itself: down on that planet, one hour passes for every seven years back on Earth.

They gamble that they can land, grab what they need, and leave before too much time is lost. It goes wrong. A delay of what feels to them like a couple of hours turns into a catastrophe of decades. When they finally claw their way back to their ship, a crew-mate who waited in orbit has aged twenty-three years. And somewhere far away, Cooper's children have been growing up — and growing old — without him.

In the film's most devastating scene, Cooper watches years of saved video messages all at once: Murph and Tom ageing before his eyes, birthdays and weddings and griefs he was not there for. His little girl is now a grown woman, the same age he is. He weeps. The mission has not aged him, but it has quietly stolen most of his children's lives.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does time run so differently on the ocean planet?

2. What does Cooper come back to after the delay on that world?

1. It orbits extremely close to the black hole Gargantua, whose immense gravity slows the passage of time — one hour there equals about seven years on Earth.

2. Decades of messages: his children have aged years while he lost only hours, and his daughter is now as old as he is.

Discuss:
  • Cooper doesn't die and doesn't age — yet he loses his children's whole childhood. Is lost time a worse thing to lose than a life? Why does this scene hurt so much?

Part 3 — Love across a black hole

The mission grows more desperate, and Cooper makes a final sacrifice: to save his crew-mate and send back vital data, he lets himself fall into the black hole itself. He should be destroyed. Instead — in the film's boldest leap — he finds himself inside a strange structure built in five dimensions, where time is laid out like a physical space he can move through. It is a window, from behind, into Murph's childhood bedroom: the very bookshelf, at every moment of her life.

Cooper realises the "ghost" was him all along. Unable to speak across time, he does the only thing he can: he pushes the books, nudges the dust, and taps out the crucial scientific data in code — trusting that his daughter, now a brilliant scientist, will read it. She does. The knowledge lets humanity finally escape the dying Earth.

Decades later, Cooper wakes on a space station, still a relatively young man. Murph is now old and dying, surrounded by her own children and grandchildren. They meet for a few minutes across the gulf of all those lost years. She sends him away — a daughter should not watch her father grieve her death — and tells him to go and find the crew-mate still waiting, alone, on a distant world. Love, the film insists, is the one thing that reaches across time and space.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was Murph's childhood "ghost," and how does Cooper finally save humanity?

2. In what state does Cooper finally reunite with Murph?

1. The ghost was Cooper himself, reaching back through a five-dimensional space inside the black hole; he taps the vital data to Murph in code, and her work saves humanity.

2. He is still young; she is old and dying, surrounded by descendants. They share only a few minutes before she sends him on.

Discuss:
  • The film claims love is a force that "transcends time and space." Is that a beautiful truth, or a cheat that dodges the science? What do you think?

Grammar — the future perfect & continuous

No film needs these two tenses more than Interstellar. Both let you stand in the present and look forward to a future moment — one asks what will be finished by then, the other what will be in progress. When years vanish in an afternoon, that is exactly the language you need.

The two structures:
• future perfect: will have + past participle — an action completed before a point in the future: "By the time he gets home, his daughter will have grown old." "In an hour here, seven years will have passed on Earth."
• future continuous: will be + -ing — an action in progress at a point in the future: "This time next year, they will be travelling through the wormhole." "While he explores, his children will be growing up."
Feel the difference:
• will have done = looking back from a future point at something already finished. Often with by, by the time, in + period.
• will be doing = zooming in on a future moment and catching the action mid-flow. Often with this time tomorrow / next year, while, at 8 o'clock.

1. Perfect or continuous? Choose the form the meaning needs

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence.

1. By the time the crew returns, sixty years (pass) on Earth. (completed before that point)

2. This time next week, the astronauts (sleep) in deep space. (in progress at that moment)

3. Don't call at nine — she (study) for her exam then. (in progress)

1. will have passed (future perfect — finished before the return)

2. will be sleeping (future continuous — mid-action)

3. will be studying (future continuous — in progress at nine)

2. Fix the form

Each sentence has the right idea but the wrong form. Write the corrected verb phrase.

1. By 2050, scientists hope humanity (reach) another star. (completed by then)

2. Look at the schedule — at midnight we (fly) past Saturn. (in progress)

3. In another hour on this planet, a decade (go) by at home. (completed by then)

1. will have reached (future perfect)

2. will be flying (future continuous)

3. will have gone (future perfect)

3. Complete the story — mix both tenses

Fill each gap with a future perfect or future continuous verb. Read it aloud when you're done.

"When you read this," Cooper records, "you (already / grow) up. This time next year, I (travel) somewhere no one has ever been, and by the time I come home — if I come home — you (become) a grown woman I hardly recognise."

will have already grown up · will be travelling · will have become

Notice the pattern: finished-by-then uses will have + past participle; caught mid-action uses will be + -ing.

Carry both into the Speak tab — the whole tragedy of this film lives in the gap between what will be happening to Cooper and what will have happened to his children.

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

Here's what makes Interstellar unusual: most of its strangest ideas are not invented. The film had a Nobel Prize–winning physicist working out the science, and much of what looks impossible is real, tested, everyday physics — just pushed to an extreme.

🔭 The science was done by a real physicist

The theoretical physicist Kip Thorne — who later won the 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics for helping detect gravitational waves — was an executive producer and the film's science advisor. He insisted that nothing should violate established physics, and worked the equations behind the story. The black hole "Gargantua" was rendered directly from his relativity equations; the images were so accurate that the visual-effects work fed into real scientific papers.

Time really does stretch — we measure it every day

The heartbreak of the film — an hour costing seven years — comes from gravitational time dilation, a real prediction of Einstein's general relativity: the stronger the gravity, the slower time runs. Near a giant, fast-spinning black hole, that effect becomes enormous. Interstellar's factor of roughly sixty thousand is extreme, but it is not magic; it follows the equations, and it needs a black hole spinning at nearly the maximum possible speed.

And this isn't only true in cinemas. The satellites of the GPS in your phone sit higher up in weaker gravity, where time runs very slightly faster than on the ground. If engineers did not correct for that tiny difference every day, your maps would drift wrong by kilometres. Cooper's tragedy is the same physics as your phone finding the right street.

The wormhole, the dust — and where the film reaches beyond the science

The wormhole — a tunnel joining two distant points in space — is a genuine, if unproven, solution to Einstein's equations (an "Einstein–Rosen bridge"); we have never found one, but the maths allows it. The dying Earth was grounded in history too: Nolan drew the dusty, exhausted world from real accounts of America's 1930s Dust Bowl, and the film's opening "interviews" echo survivors of that disaster.

Where does it stop being science? At the black hole's heart, where Cooper enters a five-dimensional space and "love" carries information across time — there the film openly leaves physics for poetry. That's the honest line to draw, and a great thing to argue about: which parts felt true, and which felt like a beautiful cheat?

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, which parts did you assume were pure fantasy — and which turn out to be real physics?
  • Does knowing the science is real make the film more powerful, or does it spoil the magic?
  • If time really runs slower in strong gravity, would you visit a place where a week for you meant your friends aged years — even once, to see it?

Task B · The impossible choice (4 minutes)

Cooper leaves his children to try to save the whole human race. Was he right? Build an argument: does a duty to humanity ever outweigh a duty to your own family — or is abandoning your children never justified, whatever the cause? Bring in other examples you know — soldiers, explorers, doctors who go to disaster zones.

Task C · Should we leave the Earth? (4 minutes)

The film imagines humanity forced to find a new home among the stars. Is spending vast sums on space exploration wise, or should we fix the planet we have first? Make your case.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Whether you personally would volunteer for a one-way mission to another planet.
  • A real explorer or scientist you admire who gave up an ordinary life for a mission.
  • Whether it's worth spending on space while there's poverty and crisis on Earth.
  • A time you had to choose between a big dream and the people close to you.
  • What you think humanity will have achieved in space by the year 2100.
  • A film or book that made a hard scientific idea suddenly feel emotional and real.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The film's real subject isn't space — it's time, and the fact that it's finite and can't be bought back. What in your own life has felt like time you can never get back?
  2. Cooper survives but loses his children's childhoods; Murph gets her father back for minutes after a lifetime apart. Who paid the higher price?
  3. The science is real; the "love transcends time" ending is not. Should a film about real physics be allowed that leap — or should it have stayed honest to the science?
