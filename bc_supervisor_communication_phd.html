Supervisor Communication

The emails, updates, and conversations that keep the relationship working — without burning your time or theirs

The supervisor relationship is professional, not personal

Most early-stage PhDs treat their supervisor either as a teacher (asks too much, defers too easily) or as a friend (over-shares, takes critique personally). Neither works. The supervisor relationship is a professional collaboration with asymmetric expertise.

Your job: communicate so they can do their job efficiently. Their job: signpost without rewriting your thesis for you.

💬 Honest reflection

Right now, do you tend to under-communicate with your supervisor (avoid them, only contact when you need something) or over-communicate (long emails, frequent updates, every small worry)?

Both extremes are common. The right cadence is somewhere between: a regular, structured update + targeted asks.

Three principles that govern every supervisor email

  1. Be specific. "I'm stuck on Chapter 2" is not actionable. "I can't find a methodological precedent for combining ethnographic data with archival sources in case-comparative work" is.
  2. Show you've tried. Supervisors respond faster to "I've tried X and Y, here's why neither works" than to "what should I do?"
  3. Make their reply easy. One question at a time. Yes/no when possible. Clear deadlines for their response.
💬 Quick test

Of the three, which one do you find HARDEST to do? Most PhDs struggle with #2 — showing they've tried — because they're worried it'll look like they should have figured it out themselves.

Showing your work is not weakness — it's professional courtesy. It tells the supervisor: "I respect your time enough that I'm not going to ask you to start from zero." It also tells them where your thinking actually is, so they can intervene at the right level. The PhDs who hide their attempts get worse feedback because their supervisors are guessing.

✍ Now you try

Take an issue you're currently dealing with in your research. Write ONE sentence that describes it specifically (Principle 1). Then write ONE sentence saying what you've already tried (Principle 2).

⚡ The shift

Your supervisor wants to help. Your job is to make helping you efficient.

The structured update email

Send one of these every 2-4 weeks, even when there's nothing urgent. It builds trust, surfaces problems early, and gives the supervisor a record of your progress without long meetings.

The four-section template

📧 To: supervisor@university.edu  ·  Subject: PhD update — week 14
Dear Professor [Surname], Brief update on progress over the past two weeks. 1. Done • Drafted §2.3 on theoretical framing (≈3,200 words) • Completed coding for case-study A interviews (n=12) • Read and notebooked Mac Ginty (2010), Richmond (2014) 2. In progress • Refining the comparison frame between cases A and B — currently struggling to align the temporal scope. 3. Stuck • I can't find a methodological precedent for combining the ethnographic and archival material at the analytical level. I've checked Burawoy's extended case method and Yin's case-study framework — neither quite fits. Any pointers welcome. 4. Next two weeks • Complete first full draft of §2.4 • Begin coding case-study B interviews • Send §2.3 for your review by [date] if helpful Best, [Name]
💬 Read the email above

What does the supervisor know after reading this? What do they NOT need to ask you?

They know: you're on track, you're reading, you're aware of what you don't know, and there's one specific thing you want help with. They don't need to ask: how's it going, are you reading enough, is anything blocking you. One short email replaces a 30-minute meeting — and the supervisor will reply faster precisely because the ask is specific.

Why the four sections

Done shows you've used the time well — but resist listing every small thing. Three or four meaningful items.

In progress tells them where you currently are and lets them intervene early if the direction is wrong.

Stuck is the heart of the email. Frame the stuck specifically. Show what you've tried.

Next two weeks commits you publicly. It also gives the supervisor a chance to redirect before you spend two weeks on the wrong thing.

⚡ Cadence

Every 2-4 weeks. Same structure every time. Predictability is a feature, not a limitation — your supervisor scans these in 90 seconds.

Asking for feedback on a draft

The most common supervisor email — and the one PhDs get wrong most often. The error is asking for feedback on too much, with too little direction, and no deadline.

Weak vs strong feedback request

⚠ Weak
"Hi — please find attached the latest draft of Chapter 2. Any thoughts welcome. No rush."

Three problems: no scope (the whole chapter), no specific concerns flagged, no deadline. The supervisor will defer it indefinitely — and probably skim when they finally read it.

✓ Strong
"Attached: §2.3 on theoretical framing (≈3,200 words). Two specific things I'd value your view on: (1) does the framing of the legitimacy debate hold, given the literature you flagged in our last meeting? (2) is §2.3.2 doing too much — should I split it? Happy with structural feedback only at this stage; line-level edits aren't useful yet. If a 20-minute read by [date 2 weeks ahead] is feasible, that would help me lock the section. If that timing doesn't work, please suggest an alternative."

Scope is bounded (one section). Two specific questions. Type of feedback specified. Deadline proposed but flexibly.

💬 Why "type of feedback specified"?

What's the difference between asking for "structural feedback" and asking for "line-level edits" — and why does it matter?

Supervisors waste enormous time correcting commas in chapters that will be restructured. If you tell them what kind of feedback you want, they give you better feedback faster. Structural early, line-level late. Saying "structural only at this stage" tells them you know what you're doing.

Three feedback types — name the one you want

  • Structural: "Does the argument flow? Is the section doing what it claims to do? Is anything missing?"
  • Conceptual: "Is the theoretical framing defensible? Have I represented the debate accurately? Are my assumptions visible?"
  • Line-level: "Is the prose clear? Are claims qualified appropriately? Are the citations formatted correctly?"
✍ Draft a feedback request

Pick a piece of writing you have or are about to send. Draft the email. Specify scope, two questions, the type of feedback, and a flexible deadline.

⚡ The unwritten rule

The more bounded your ask, the better the feedback. A four-page chapter with one specific question gets better feedback than a forty-page chapter with no question at all.

When you disagree with your supervisor

It will happen. They will recommend something you think is wrong, or push back on a position you're confident in. The instinct is either to capitulate (accept everything to keep the peace) or to argue defensively. Neither serves the thesis.

The professional move is to disagree in writing, with reasons, and offer them an easy way out.

💬 The capitulation trap

If you accept every supervisor suggestion uncritically, what happens to your thesis by viva day?

You can't defend it. The viva panel will ask you why you made certain choices — and the answer "my supervisor told me to" is fatal. The thesis has to be defensibly YOURS. Disagreement, handled properly, is part of the apprenticeship.

The diplomatic-disagreement template

📧 Reply to supervisor on a methodological recommendation
Dear Professor [Surname], Thank you for the suggestion of [their recommendation] — I've been thinking through it carefully. I've taken it seriously and worked through the implications. My current view is that [your alternative position], for three reasons: (1) [evidence-based reason — cite literature or data] (2) [methodological reason — what their suggestion would compromise] (3) [thesis-coherence reason — how their suggestion would unsettle other parts of the thesis] That said, I'm aware I may be missing something — particularly around [the area where their expertise outweighs yours]. Could we test the disagreement in our next meeting? I've drafted a one-page note setting out both positions; I'll bring it. Best, [Name]
💬 Notice three moves

What three moves does this email make that turn disagreement from confrontation into collaboration?

  1. Acknowledges the suggestion seriously ("thinking through it carefully" — not dismissive)
  2. Gives reasons, not feelings (three structured reasons grounded in evidence, method, and thesis coherence)
  3. Names the limit of your own expertise ("I may be missing something around X") — this is the diplomatic move that lets the supervisor enter the conversation without having to overturn your position to be useful

Useful phrases for written disagreement

  • "I've taken it seriously and worked through the implications" — signals respect
  • "My current view is..." — softens the position; leaves room to update
  • "On reflection, I'm not yet persuaded that..." — disagrees without confrontation
  • "That said, I'm aware I may be missing..." — opens the door to their expertise
  • "Could we test the disagreement in our next meeting?" — turns disagreement into agenda
✍ Draft a disagreement email

Imagine your supervisor recommended a methodological change you don't agree with. Write the reply — using the structure above.

⚡ The principle

You can disagree and stay collaborative. The signal is reason + humility + invitation to talk. Avoid sarcasm, avoid victimhood, avoid the passive voice. Be direct and warm at the same time.

Difficult situations

Some supervisor situations need careful handling. Here are the four you're most likely to face — and the language for each.

1. They've gone silent

💬 Quick check

You sent a chapter draft three weeks ago. No reply. Email three? Or wait?

Send a polite, low-pressure follow-up. The trick is to NOT make them feel guilty (which makes them avoid replying further) and to give them an easy way out.

📧 Following up on silence
Dear Professor [Surname], Just a brief follow-up on the §2.3 draft I sent on [date] — no urgency, just flagging in case it has slipped down the inbox. If a 20-minute read isn't feasible in the next two weeks, please let me know — I can move the section to a later review cycle and prioritise [other work] in the meantime. Best, [Name]

2. The feedback was harsh

💬 Honest moment

You receive feedback that feels brutal. Your instinct is to write back defending the work. Should you?

No. Wait 24 hours. Then read the feedback again. Then write a calm, professional response that thanks them, asks for clarification on one or two specific points, and proposes next steps. Defending in heat damages the relationship and obscures whether the feedback was right.

Useful phrases after harsh feedback (after 24 hours)

  • "Thank you for the detailed feedback — it's clearly identified some structural problems I hadn't seen."
  • "Could I check my understanding of one point? On [issue X], are you suggesting [interpretation A] or [interpretation B]?"
  • "I'd like to revise the section along the lines you've suggested. May I send the revised version by [date] for a quicker second look?"

3. Two supervisors disagree

If you have co-supervisors and they give you contradictory advice, do not pick a side privately. The professional move is to name the contradiction in writing, copy both supervisors, and ask them to agree a position.

📧 To both supervisors
Dear Professor X and Professor Y, I want to flag a difference in the feedback I've received that I'd value your help resolving. Professor X (in our meeting on [date]) suggested [position A], specifically because [reason]. Professor Y (in feedback on the draft, [date]) suggested [position B], specifically because [reason]. These are not necessarily incompatible, but I'd benefit from a shared steer before I commit further effort. Could we agree a brief joint conversation — or, if that's hard to schedule, could one of you take the lead on the methodological direction so I have a single point of reference for this section? Best, [Name]
💬 Why this works

What is the email NOT doing? What is it carefully avoiding?

It's not taking sides. It's not implying either supervisor was wrong. It's framing the contradiction as a coordination problem (their problem) rather than a judgment problem (yours). It also offers them an out — "one of you take the lead" — which they may accept.

4. You're behind schedule

💬 The instinct

You're three weeks behind on a milestone. Do you (a) work harder and try to catch up silently, or (b) email your supervisor to flag it?

(b), every time. Supervisors hate being surprised by missed milestones at month 18. They are fine with delays they've been informed about — and often have practical solutions you don't have access to (extension funding, rescheduled meetings, etc.). Silence is the worst option.

📧 Flagging a delay
Dear Professor [Surname], Brief update: I'm currently around three weeks behind on [milestone], primarily because [specific reason — illness, methodological pivot, data access delay, etc.]. I've revised the timeline as follows: [revised milestone date]. I'm confident I can hit the revised date because [specific plan]. If this delay creates downstream problems (with funding, with the upgrade panel, with your other commitments), please let me know — I'd rather adjust now than later. Best, [Name]

⚡ The hidden lesson

The supervisor relationship works best when you treat your supervisor as a busy, intelligent collaborator — not as a teacher, friend, or judge. Brief, structured, specific, professional. Same as any working relationship that needs to last three or four years.
