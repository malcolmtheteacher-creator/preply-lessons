Why Systematic Reviews Exist

Single studies tell you what one group of researchers found under specific conditions. Conflicting studies leave you paralysed. A systematic review answers: What does all the evidence together tell us?

It's about understanding:

  • Why studies conflict (different populations, methods, quality)
  • Where evidence is strong and weak
  • What we can confidently conclude and what remains uncertain
  • What next research should focus on

The Protocol: Six Core Concepts

Click each to see what it is, why it matters, and how you'll speak about it.

1. PICO Framework▼

What it is: PICO is your research question in four components: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome.

Real example

Population: Adults 18-65 with major depressive disorder
Intervention: Sertraline at standard clinical doses
Comparison: Cognitive behavioural therapy
Outcome: Reduction in depressive symptom severity (PHQ-9)

How you speak about it

"We defined our population as adults aged 18-65 with major depressive disorder. Our intervention was sertraline at therapeutic dosages, compared to cognitive behavioural therapy. Our primary outcome was symptom reduction as measured by the Patient Health Questionnaire-9."

2. Search Strategy▼

What it is: Your search strategy specifies which databases, search terms, combinations, and filters you'll use. It must be reproducible — another researcher should get the same results.

Real example

Databases: PubMed, PsycINFO, CENTRAL, Web of Science
Search terms: (sertraline OR SSRI) AND (depression) AND (cognitive behavioural therapy OR CBT)
Filters: 2015-2023, English, human studies
Hand-searching: Reference lists, grey literature via OpenGrey

How you speak about it

"We systematically searched four major databases with a pre-specified search strategy combining key terms with Boolean operators. We applied date and language restrictions to ensure feasibility whilst maintaining comprehensiveness. We documented our strategy using PRESS guidelines and conducted supplementary hand-searching."

3. Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria▼

What it is: Explicit criteria defining which studies are in and which are out. Must be objective and pre-specified before screening.

Real example

Inclusion: Randomised controlled trials, adults 18-65 with major depressive disorder, sertraline vs CBT, outcome measured within 12 weeks

Exclusion: Studies combining sertraline with other medications, participants with comorbid diagnoses (unless clearly separated), conference abstracts only

How you speak about it

"We restricted our review to randomised controlled trials involving adults with major depressive disorder receiving either sertraline monotherapy or CBT. We excluded studies where these interventions were combined or where participants had secondary psychiatric diagnoses, as this would confound our comparison. This scope allowed us to generate a focused answer whilst maintaining methodological homogeneity."

4. Quality Appraisal▼

What it is: Systematic assessment of bias and quality in included studies using validated tools to evaluate trustworthiness.

Real example

RCTs assessed using Cochrane Risk of Bias tool:
- Selection bias (randomisation sequence, allocation concealment)
- Performance bias (blinding of participants and staff)
- Detection bias (blinding of outcome assessors)
- Attrition bias (incomplete outcome data)

Two reviewers independently rate as Low, Unclear, or High risk.

How you speak about it

"We appraised bias risk using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool, with two independent raters assessing each included study across key domains. Three studies showed low overall bias risk; four showed unclear allocation concealment, limiting confidence in their findings. We conducted sensitivity analyses excluding high-risk studies."

5. Synthesis of Evidence▼

What it is: How you combine findings: meta-analysis (statistical pooling) or narrative synthesis (thematic summary). Heterogeneity (variation) determines which is appropriate.

Real example

Scenario A (low heterogeneity): All 8 studies used the same outcome measure, similar populations, similar timeframes. Meta-analysis is appropriate with pooled effect size and 95% confidence intervals.

Scenario B (high heterogeneity): Different outcome measures, participants from mild to severe depression, interventions varied in intensity. Narrative synthesis better captures these differences.

How you speak about it

"Due to substantial heterogeneity in outcome measurement and intervention duration, we conducted narrative synthesis rather than meta-analysis. We synthesised findings thematically, identifying convergence and divergence. While six studies showed advantage for sertraline, three showed no significant difference, attributed to differences in sample severity and treatment fidelity."

6. PRISMA Reporting▼

What it is: PRISMA is a checklist of 27 items specifying what your systematic review report must contain. Ensures transparency and rigorous methodology assessment.

Real example — must-include items

- Registered protocol with registration number
- PRISMA checklist submitted
- Flow diagram: numbers screened, assessed, included, excluded (with reasons)
- Characteristics table of included studies
- Risk of bias summary for all included studies
- Summary of findings table (for key comparisons)

How you speak about it

"This review was conducted and reported according to PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Our protocol was registered with PROSPERO prior to study selection. We report a comprehensive flow diagram detailing screening and selection, characteristics of all included studies, and detailed risk of bias assessment. These allow readers to evaluate methodological rigour."

The Methodology Challenge

Six scenarios a reviewer actually faces. Each reveals as you progress. 45 seconds per question.

1
How did you decide which databases to search? What might you have missed?

You chose PubMed, PsycINFO, and CENTRAL, but your colleague suggests EMBASE. Your search strategy is locked. What do you say?

0:45
2
Your inclusion criteria excluded studies from low-income countries. How do you justify that?

You limited to English-language, high-income country studies for feasibility. A stakeholder challenges this as bias. Defend or revise?

0:45
3
Two of your included studies have high risk of bias. Does this change conclusions or confidence?

They have unclear allocation concealment and blinding, but report the largest effects in your favour. Explain the distinction between true effect and confidence.

0:45
4
You found 12 studies but only 3 point the same direction. How do you synthesise this?

Some show intervention works, others show no effect, one shows harm. Can you pool these? Should you?

5
What's the difference between "evidence suggests X" and "X is established"?

Your meta-analysis shows moderate effect (p=0.04) with wide confidence intervals and heterogeneity. The media calls it "proof". Explain the language distinction.

Your review missed 4 studies published in the last 3 months. What do you do?

Your review took 18 months. The field moves fast. Do you update? Publish an addendum? Acknowledge limitations? Explain your approach.

After the Review: Reflection & Language Reference

Reflection Questions

What was the hardest decision in designing your review?▼

Common hard decisions: deciding inclusion criteria (comprehensiveness vs feasibility), choosing PICO (shapes everything downstream), deciding between meta-analysis and narrative synthesis, handling heterogeneity. Reflect on which aspect felt most consequential and why.

How would you explain systematic review methodology to a newcomer?▼

This is translation. Try: "A systematic review is structured detective work on evidence. You ask a precise question, search systematically (not cherry-picking), appraise quality (so you know what you can trust), and synthesise — combining findings to answer your original question." Can you do this in your own words?

Where do you still feel uncertainty about methodology?▼

Productive areas of uncertainty: handling heterogeneity, deciding quality thresholds, deciding whether to pool conflicting studies, knowing when narrative synthesis is rigorous enough. These are judgment calls, not failures. Knowing where you're uncertain is the first step to addressing it.

Systematic Review Language Reference

Presenting Your Protocol

"We registered our protocol with PROSPERO prior to study selection, defining our population, intervention, comparison, and outcomes a priori to prevent post-hoc bias."

"Our review question was deliberately structured using the PICO framework to ensure clinical relevance and methodological clarity."

Justifying Search Strategy

"We searched multiple databases (PubMed, PsycINFO, CENTRAL) to reduce publication bias. We used a pre-specified search strategy combining subject headings and keywords, documented according to PRESS guidelines."

"We employed a sensitive search strategy prioritising recall over precision, accepting higher initial false positives to ensure comprehensiveness."

Discussing Inclusion/Exclusion

"Our inclusion criteria were intentionally narrow to reduce clinical heterogeneity and allow meaningful synthesis."

"We excluded studies where [reason], as including them would confound our intervention-control comparison."

Describing Quality Appraisal

"Two independent reviewers assessed bias risk using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool, with disagreements resolved through discussion."

"We found substantial variation in methodological quality, ranging from low-risk RCTs with adequate blinding to observational studies with significant confounding."

Synthesising Conflicting Evidence

"Due to substantial heterogeneity (I² = 78%), we present both meta-analytic estimates with uncertainty intervals and narrative synthesis highlighting sources of divergence."

"Studies showed conflicting findings which we attributed to variations in population severity, intervention intensity, and outcome measurement."

Hedging Language (Appropriate Caution)

"The evidence suggests..." — use when effect is plausible but not certain
"The evidence is consistent with..." — weaker, compatible with but not proving
"Emerging evidence indicates..." — limited studies, newer area
"This finding is robust to..." — resists sensitivity analysis
"We cannot confidently conclude..." — evidence is insufficient

Addressing Limitations

"This review is limited by heterogeneity in outcome measurement, which prevented meta-analysis and required narrative synthesis."

"Our exclusion of non-English-language studies may have introduced bias, though we acknowledge this trade-off against feasibility."

Implications & Next Steps

"This review highlights a critical gap: few studies compare our interventions in real-world settings."

"Our findings suggest that evidence is insufficient to definitively recommend one approach. Until higher-quality evidence emerges, decisions should be individualised to patient context and preference."
