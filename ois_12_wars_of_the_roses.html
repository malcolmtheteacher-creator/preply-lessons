The Bridge: A Dynasty Tearing Itself Apart

The Plantagenet dynasty that Henry II founded has ruled for nearly 400 years. But now, in 1455, something terrible happens. The family itself turns to war. A weak king, a powerful family divided into rival branches—and the question of who truly wears the crown becomes a question written in blood.

This is the Wars of the Roses. And H.E. Marshall tells it as a tragedy—a kingdom eating itself alive.

II. Lancaster and York: The Roses Divide

The Wars of the Roses are not really a war between two nations. They are a family war, a succession crisis, and a question that will tear the kingdom for 30 years: Who has the rightful claim to the throne?

The Origins: Two Branches of One Tree

Both sides descend from Edward III. His sons had many sons. John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster, fathered Henry Bolingbroke—who became Henry IV and founded the House of Lancaster. Edward, Duke of York, had descendants who claimed they had a better right to rule.

Then one after another all the nobles who were there plucked red or white roses. Those who were for Lancaster, that is the King, because he was descended from John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster, wore red roses in their caps; those who were for the Duke of York wore white roses in theirs. And ever after, during all the years that the wars lasted, red and white roses were the sign or badge of the two parties, and the wars were called the Wars of the Roses. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story
The historian's note: The plucking of roses in the Temple Garden is a dramatized scene, possibly invented by Shakespeare's plays. But the symbolism stuck. Red for Lancaster (the Tudor rose, later), white for York. Even today, these symbols carry the weight of those 30 years.

Henry VI: The Gentle King in a Violent Time

At the heart of the conflict stands King Henry VI—one of Marshall's most compassionate portraits in the book. He was born to be king, but nature gave him a kingdom that needed a warrior.

It seemed indeed as if the King needed a protector, for he was not only weak and foolish, but at times he was quite mad and unable even to speak for days. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

Henry VI was gentle. Pious. A man of prayer in an age of swords. And that was his tragedy.

Margaret of Anjou: The Warrior Queen

If Henry was too gentle, his wife was not. Marshall tells us of Margaret of Anjou, who fought with a mother's ferocity for her son's inheritance.

The historian's note: Margaret of Anjou became a fierce political and military leader. While Henry grew more withdrawn and mentally ill, she took up command of armies. She was called the "She-Wolf of Anjou" by her enemies. Marshall portrays her as a warrior queen fighting for her son's rights—a complex portrait of a woman who did what her husband could not.

The First Battle: St Albans, 1455

The war begins not with great armies but with a clash of nobility. The Battle of St Albans, on 22 May 1455, is the first of many. It lasts only about three hours. But it marks the moment when words turned to steel.

1455 — Battle of St Albans

The Duke of Somerset is killed. The King is wounded. The first blood is spilled between red rose and white.

1461 — Edward IV becomes King

The Yorkists triumph. But Henry does not disappear. A kingdom cannot have two kings.

1470 – 1471 — Henry Briefly Restored

In a remarkable turn, Henry is freed from captivity and restored to the throne for a few months. Then Edward returns and defeats the Lancastrians at Tewkesbury.

III. Richard III and the Princes in the Tower

By 1483, Edward IV is dead. His two young sons—Edward V and Richard, Duke of York, boys aged 12 and 9—are heirs to the throne. Their uncle Richard, Duke of Gloucester, is made their protector. But a protector has access to power. And power, once touched, is rarely released.

The Mysterious Disappearance

Richard declares that his nephews are illegitimate—that Edward IV had made a secret marriage before marrying their mother. A Parliament agrees. Richard is crowned king. And the two boys? They vanish into the Tower of London.

Marshall tells the story with great poignancy:

That night the little Princes went to sleep with their arms round each other's necks, each trying to comfort the other. They lay together in a great big bed, happy in their dreams, with tears still wet upon their cheeks. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

Marshall suggests—though she does not state it as certain—that Richard had them murdered. She writes:

Then these wicked men took the bodies of the two little Princes, threw them into a hole which they had made under the staircase, covered them over and fled away. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

The truth? We may never know. The bodies were never found. But the suspicion clung to Richard like a shroud.

The historian's note: Richard III has had his defenders in recent decades. Some argue he did not kill the princes. Some point to Henry VII as a possible culprit. But in 2012, the remains of a boy matching the elder prince's description were found buried beneath a car park in Leicester—buried with a bent spine, the kind Richard was said to have had. A mystery remains a mystery, but the shadow has grown longer still.

Richard III: Villain or Victim?

Marshall paints Richard as a villain. Shakespeare painted him worse. But history has become more complex. Was Richard a usurper and murderer? Or a competent administrator trying to save a kingdom from being ruled by children during chaos?

We see a glimpse of his doubt in Marshall's account:

Now that Richard had murdered the rightful King and his brother, he was no happier. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

Perhaps that is the deepest tragedy. Richard could not unsee what he had done.

The Battle of Bosworth Field, 1485

Richard's reign lasts only two years. An army assembles against him, led by Henry Tudor, a distant Lancastrian claimant. On 22 August 1485, the two forces meet at Bosworth Field near Leicester.

Richard fights bravely. But his nobles betray him at the crucial moment. He dies on the field, the last king of the Plantagenet line.

As Richard fell in battle, the crown which he had worn over his helmet rolled away under a hawthorn tree, where it was found by Lord Stanley who set it upon Henry Tudor's head. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

A crown lost in thorn bushes. A kingdom waiting for a new dynasty. And Henry Tudor, now Henry VII, marries Elizabeth of York, Edward IV's daughter, uniting the roses at last.

The historian's note: The Battle of Bosworth marks the traditional end of the Middle Ages in England and the beginning of the Tudor age. It is one of history's dramatic turning points—though the turning was gradual, not sudden. The Wars of the Roses had already exhausted themselves. The nobility had killed so many of their own that a new kind of power was needed: the power of a single, strong king who could rebuild.

V. Room 12 of Your British History Palace

You have now filled 11 rooms. Room 12 holds the Wars of the Roses.

BRITISH HISTORY PALACE

Room 12: The Wars of the Roses (1455–1485)

This room is darker than the others. You can feel the weight of civil war, of families turning against families, of a crown that passes from hand to hand like a cursed thing.

The 5 Images for Room 12

1. A red rose and a white rose crossed like swords — they are not flowers but weapons, symbols of a kingdom divided against itself.

2. Henry VI kneeling in prayer — while the rose gardens burn, while armies clash, the gentle king prays, helpless and heartbroken.

3. Margaret of Anjou in armour — fierce, commanding, doing what her husband cannot. A queen who becomes a general. Steel where there should have been silk.

4. Two young boys locked in a dark tower — Edward and Richard, the Princes, holding each other in the night, waiting for a tomorrow that will never come.

5. A crown caught on a hawthorn bush — thorns tearing at gold, a king dead, a new dynasty rising. The end of one age, the beginning of another.

VI. Your Turn: Discussion & Reflection

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

The weakness of a good man

Henry VI was gentle, pious, and kind—qualities we might admire in a priest. But as a king, they were a disaster. Can goodness be a weakness? Is there a leader good enough to be both kind and strong?

Margaret of Anjou's choice

A queen became a general because her husband could not fight. She fought for her son's inheritance. Was she a hero or did ambition turn her into a she-wolf? Can you sympathise with both sides?

The Princes in the Tower mystery

For 500+ years, we have not known whether Richard III killed them or if they escaped or if someone else murdered them. What does it mean to live with a historical mystery? Can we ever judge Richard fairly?

The power of symbols

Red roses and white roses turned a succession dispute into a symbol that lasted centuries. Why are symbols so powerful? How do symbols shape how we remember history?

Prophecy and doom

Medieval writers saw the Wars of the Roses as a punishment for regicide. Henry IV had killed Richard II decades earlier. Does history repeat itself? Are there cycles of crime and punishment?

When empires turn on themselves

A strong dynasty, a kingdom at peace, suddenly tears itself apart. What makes a civilization turn to civil war? Is it bad leadership, or are there deeper structural reasons?
