Legal English through real cases · C1

Beyond Reasonable Doubt

Six real cases that made the rules we live under — a snail in a bottle, an advert, a lifeboat, an interrogation room, an accounting system and a courtroom built from nothing.

Every case here actually happened, and each one created a rule that still governs ordinary life. A woman in Paisley who had not paid for her drink, and therefore had no claim against anyone — until five judges decided that responsibility does not run through receipts. A company that promised £100 to anyone who caught influenza, and discovered that an advertisement can be a contract. Three starving men in an open boat, and a court that refused to write down how you choose who dies. A labourer in Phoenix who signed a confession stating that he understood rights nobody had told him about. Hundreds of sub-postmasters prosecuted because the law assumed the computer was working and only the prosecutor could prove that it wasn't. And four governments who could have shot the men in their custody, and built a court instead.

Each case is a 50-minute C1 lesson: the real vocabulary of the case, the story in three parts — the facts, the argument, the judgment — one legal-register grammar focus drawn from the case itself, and timed speaking tasks. Every lesson makes you argue the losing side properly, because that is where the language actually lives. The Going Deeper tab always names the dissent and argues at least two readings; the lesson never announces a moral. Nothing here is legal advice, and jurisdictions differ — that is part of the discussion.

The six cases

CASE 01
The Snail in the Bottle
Donoghue v Stevenson · 1932 · negligence
A woman drinks ginger beer she did not buy, from a bottle she could not see into. Having no contract with anybody, she had no claim — so the House of Lords invented one, and with it the duty of care.
The question: who, then, in law, is my neighbour — and who is not?

CASE 02
The Advert That Became a Promise
Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball · 1893 · contract
£100 to anyone who caught influenza after using the product as directed — and £1,000 deposited at the bank to prove they meant it. One woman used it for two months, fell ill, and sued.
The question: when does confident selling become a promise you must keep?

CASE 03
Four Men in a Boat
R v Dudley and Stephens · 1884 · criminal defences
Twenty days adrift, no food, no water, and a practice sailors had quietly followed for centuries. The court accepted that they would probably have died — and convicted them anyway.
The question: by what measure is the comparative value of lives to be measured?

CASE 04
The Right to Say Nothing
Miranda v Arizona · 1966 · rights & procedure
He signed a form declaring that his confession was voluntary and made with full knowledge of his rights. Nobody had told him what they were. Five judges to four, the Supreme Court wrote the script.
The question: is a right nobody has told you about a right at all?

CASE 05
The Computer Was Never Wrong
The Horizon scandal · 1999–today · evidence
The law presumed the accounting system was working properly. To rebut that, a sub-postmaster needed the error logs — which were held by the organisation prosecuting them. Around 700 were prosecuted.
The question: where should the burden sit when only one side can see inside the machine?

CASE 06
A Court for a Crime With No Name
Nuremberg · 1945–46 · international law
Churchill wanted them shot; Stalin wanted a verdict agreed in advance. The Americans argued for a real trial — with defence counsel, testable evidence, and the possibility of acquittal. Three men were acquitted.
