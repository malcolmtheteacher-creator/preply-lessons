SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B2/C1 · 50–60 MIN

The Machine Stops

Humanity lives underground, communicates through screens and trusts one global system for everything. Then the system begins to fail.

Adapted for B2/C1 from E. M. Forster's 1909 story

Memory first

Have a go without notes; uncertainty is useful here.

  1. At 8 p.m. tomorrow, what will you be doing? Answer with future continuous.

    One possible answer: “At 8 p.m. tomorrow, I will be having dinner.” Use will be + -ing for an action in progress then.

  2. By the end of this week, what will you have completed? Answer with future perfect.

    One possible answer: “By the end of this week, I will have completed this book.” Use will have + past participle for completion before a deadline.

  3. Explain the difference between at a future time and by a future time.

    At places us inside an action at that future moment; by sets a deadline before which the action will be complete.

The story in three movements

After each movement, answer from memory before revealing the response. The turn remains hidden until you commit to a prediction.

1 · A room containing the world

Vashti lived in a room beneath the earth. It had no window and no door that she needed to open herself. Buttons brought food, music, medicine, light and a bed. Another button connected her to thousands of people, each alone in an identical room.

Her son Kuno called while she was preparing a lecture. His face appeared on the blue screen.

“I want you to come and see me.”

“But I can see you now.”

“Not through the Machine. I want to see you without it.”

The request embarrassed her. Travel was tiring and direct contact had become almost indecent. Ideas mattered; bodies did not. Still, Kuno insisted, and eventually she entered an airship that carried her across the world.

When they met, he did something shocking: he touched her hand.

Vashti pulled away. She had spoken to Kuno hundreds of times, had watched illness and anger pass across his face, yet the warmth of his hand felt more private than any confession. The Machine allowed minds to meet while keeping bodies safely separate.

“You are forgetting the Machine,” Vashti whispered.

“That is why I asked you here.”

Kuno said he had walked through forbidden tunnels until he reached the surface. He had seen grass, stars and open sky—not pictures of them, but the things themselves. People were living above ground, he believed.

Then the Machine's officials had found him. If he tried again, he would be made homeless: put outside, where everyone was taught that human life was impossible.

Answer from memory

Which human abilities have become unnecessary, and what might happen when unused abilities disappear?

Walking, repairing, travelling, direct conversation and practical survival have weakened. A population that cannot perform them becomes completely dependent on the system.

2 · Small failures, perfect excuses

Vashti returned to her room and to the warm, steady voice of the Machine. Years passed. Kuno's story became an uncomfortable memory.

Then small things began to go wrong.

The music developed a rough sound. The bath water smelled. Her bed did not rise smoothly from the floor. Vashti reported each fault, and each time a calm voice thanked her for her patience.

Others advised her not to be selfish. The Machine was larger than any individual inconvenience. Complaints became unfashionable; then repairs became impossible. The organisation responsible for maintenance had been abolished long ago because people believed the Machine could repair itself.

Lecturers produced theories explaining why the new faults were actually improvements. Rough music was said to be more intellectually demanding. Weak air encouraged stillness and reflection. The less the Machine provided, the more carefully its users defended it.

Human beings adapted. If the air was less fresh, they decided fresh air had never been necessary. If the lectures became difficult to hear, they learned to speak less.

Kuno called again. His face broke into squares on the screen.

“The Machine is stopping.”

Vashti smiled with the patience one uses for a child.

“The Machine cannot stop.”

“You say that because you no longer know how anything works.”

The screen went black before she could answer. A moment later it returned, and Vashti felt such relief that she knelt before the Book of the Machine and pressed it to her lips.

Answer from memory

Why do the small malfunctions fail to produce immediate action?

Each failure is tolerable alone, and the culture has taught people to interpret the Machine as more competent than human judgement.

3 · When the voices stop

The end did not arrive as one great explosion. It came as a series of failures that no one knew how to reverse.

The lights weakened. Food stopped appearing. The artificial air grew hot and thin. Millions of frightened voices filled the communication system, all asking for instructions from a power that had never truly been alive.

Then the voices stopped.

Vashti forced open the door of her room and entered the tunnel. For the first time she saw her neighbours as bodies rather than faces on screens. They pushed towards the transport platforms, but the trains no longer moved. Some prayed to the Machine. Others struck its metal walls.

People who had delivered lectures on every subject could not open a ventilation panel. Hands trained only to press buttons tore uselessly at smooth metal. Knowledge still filled their minds, but none of it could produce air, water or a way out.

A hand found hers in the darkness. It was Kuno.

“The surface people may survive,” he said. “They remember what we chose to forget.”

The tunnel roof began to fall. Vashti thought of real grass, real stars and the sky she had refused to see. Around her, the most advanced civilisation in history waited helplessly for a button that would not answer.

The Machine had once been created to serve humanity. Then humanity had reorganised itself to serve the Machine. At the end, there was no person who understood enough to save either one.

In the last darkness, mother and son held each other without a screen between them.

Answer from memory

Is the Machine the villain, or is dependency the real antagonist?

The Machine follows the role humans gave it. The deeper antagonist is a culture that surrendered knowledge, responsibility and resilience.

Grammar — future continuous & future perfect

will be + -ing

Tomorrow, millions will be speaking through screens.

Action in progress at a future time.

will have + past participle

By then, they will have forgotten how to repair it.

Action completed before a future point.

time marker choice

At midnight it will be failing; by midnight it will have failed.

At = viewpoint inside; by = deadline/completion.

Mixed practice

Complete all six before revealing. Explain the choice, not only the missing words.

At this time tomorrow, Vashti ___ another lecture. (give)

By next year, the repair organisation ___. (disappear)

At midnight, engineers ___ for a solution. (search)

By the time the public understands, they ___ key skills. (lose)

In ten years, people ___ even more services through one system. (access)

By 2050, which technologies ___ obsolete? (become)

  1. will be giving — The action will be in progress at a specified future time, so use will be + -ing.
  2. will have disappeared — By next year is a future deadline, so use future perfect for a completed change.
  3. will be searching — At midnight places us inside an activity in progress.
  4. will have lost — By the time looks back from a future point at a loss already completed.
  5. will be accessing — The sentence describes an ongoing future pattern rather than a completed total.
  6. will have become — By 2050 asks which changes will already be complete by that deadline.

Going deeper

Predictive estrangement

Familiar habits—screens, remote contact and automated services—are made strange by being taken to an extreme.

Normalised absurdity

Characters treat fresh air and direct touch as dangerous while accepting total dependency as sensible.

Failure by increments

The end begins with tolerable inconveniences, not one dramatic explosion.

Reversal of progress

The advanced underground world becomes helpless; the supposedly primitive surface may hold the future.

A short analysis

Give your answer first. Then reveal a model and identify one idea you handled differently.

The loss of repair knowledge is the story's most durable warning. Screen communication and automation are visible technologies, but dependence becomes dangerous only when people can no longer understand alternatives. Forster therefore targets not invention itself but the quiet disappearance of human capability.
