Can you ever be completely certain of anything?

You wake up from a dream so real that for a few seconds you don't know where you are. A photo on your phone might be real, or it might be made by a computer. How do you know what is real? Almost four hundred years ago, a French mathematician decided to find out by doubting everything.

Today: You'll meet René Descartes and his method of doubt, so you can say how certain you are about things and explain why he said "I think, therefore I am."

Meet Descartes

A soldier with three dreams

René Descartes was born in La Haye, France, in 1596. At school he loved mathematics, because its answers were clear and certain, unlike much of what he was taught. As a young man he travelled and joined an army. In November 1619, alone in a warm room in Germany, he had three powerful dreams. He took them as a sign that he should rebuild all knowledge on a solid foundation.

What happened to Descartes in November 1619?

Alone in a warm room in Germany, he had three powerful dreams. He took them as a sign that he should rebuild knowledge on a solid foundation.
🌫️

Doubt everything

In 1628 Descartes moved to the Netherlands, where he could write more freely. He decided to doubt everything that could possibly be doubted. Our senses sometimes trick us, so he doubted them. Dreams can feel real, so perhaps he was dreaming now. He even imagined an evil demon who was deceiving him about everything, even mathematics. His aim was not to give up on knowledge. He wanted to find one thing that no doubt could touch.

Why did Descartes imagine an evil demon?

To push his doubt as far as it could go. If even a powerful demon could not trick him about something, that thing must be certain.
💡

I think, therefore I am

Then he found it. Even if a demon was tricking him, he was still thinking, and something that thinks must exist. In his Discourse on the Method (1637) he wrote: "I think, therefore I am." From this one certainty he tried to rebuild the rest, including God and the world outside. In 1649 he went to Stockholm to teach Queen Christina of Sweden. According to the usual story, the lessons began at five in the morning. He died there in 1650.

Why can't you doubt that you are thinking?

Because doubting is itself a kind of thinking. The moment you doubt, you prove that you think, and so that you exist.
Talk: Descartes doubted his senses because they sometimes trick us. Is it reasonable to doubt everything because of a few mistakes, or is that going too far?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. After checking the results three times, she could say with certainty that the answer was correct.
certainty (n) — the state of being completely sure that something is true. Example: "There's no certainty that the train will be on time tomorrow."
2. The magician didn't really cut the woman in half; he deceived the audience with mirrors.
deceive (v) — to make someone believe something that is not true. Example: "The advert deceived customers into thinking the phone was free."
3. In the desert, the lake he saw was an illusion: when he got closer, there was only sand.
illusion (n) — something that seems real but is not, or a false idea about something. Example: "The two lines look different lengths, but that's just a trick of the eye."
4. If the foundation of a house is weak, the whole building can fall down.
foundation (n) — the basic idea or fact that something else is built on. Example: "Trust is the foundation of any good friendship."
5. When he said he'd won the lottery, his friends were sceptical and asked to see the ticket.
sceptical (adj) — not easily convinced; tending to doubt what you hear or read. Example: "I'm sceptical about diets that promise quick results."
6. My old car is slow, but it's very reliable: it has never broken down.
reliable (adj) — able to be trusted to be correct or to work well. Example: "Is this website a good source of information? Is it reliable?"
7. There's no evidence that eating carrots helps you see in the dark; it's just an old story.
evidence (n) — facts or signs that show something is true. Example: "The police found no evidence that he was at the scene."

See it in action

Here is the method in action. You look at your hand and think, "I'm absolutely certain this is my hand." Descartes asks: "Are you? Last night you dreamed you were holding a cup, and you were sure then too." So you can't be sure the hand is real. But wait: you are wondering about it right now. Dream or no dream, someone is doing the wondering. That much cannot be doubted.

Connect

Compare

Pyrrho and Descartes: Pyrrho also doubted almost everything, but he used doubt to stop judging and find peace. Descartes used doubt to find something certain. What is the difference between doubting to relax and doubting to build?
For Pyrrho, doubt was the goal: if you stop insisting you are right, you find calm. For Descartes, doubt was only a tool: he pushed it as far as possible so that whatever survived could be a solid foundation for knowledge.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From Niccolò Machiavelli

What did Machiavelli argue about how rulers should act?

That a ruler must deal with power as it really is: sometimes he has to lie or be cruel to keep the state safe, and he will be judged by results.
From Thomas Aquinas

How did Aquinas build every argument in the Summa?

He asked a question, listed the strongest objections against his own view, gave his answer with reasons, and then replied to each objection.
From Augustine of Hippo

What did Augustine mean by a divided will?

That part of us wants to do what is good while another part says 'not yet', so the will fights against itself. Looking back honestly helps us see this and change.
From Plato

What does Plato's story of the cave show?

That we may be like prisoners who take shadows for reality: our senses show us appearances, and only careful thinking leads us to the truth.
