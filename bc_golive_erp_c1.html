Go-Live

Enterprise systems run most of the world's companies — and replacing one is the biggest bet a business ever makes on software. This is the story of the days the switch was flipped.

Today: you'll work through the strange world of giant business systems — projects measured in years and hundreds of millions — and you'll master the verb patterns where meaning turns on one small choice: they stopped testing versus they stopped to test.

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. One system holding the whole company — finance, stock, orders, payroll — so that every department finally works from the same numbers. That is ERP: enterprise resource planning.
ERP (n) — the company's central nervous system in software. When it works, one version of the truth; when it fails, nothing ships, nobody is paid, and the company is blind.
2. The date when the old system is switched off and the new one takes over live operations — rehearsed for months, feared by everyone — is the go-live.
a go-live (n) — to go live; a go-live date; a big-bang go-live (everything at once) versus a phased rollout (site by site). The single most dramatic word in enterprise IT — and this case's stage.
3. Rather than adopt the software's standard way of working, the company paid to rebuild the software around its own habits — thousands of modifications. Heavy customisation is where these projects go to die.
customisation (n) — to customise; heavily customised; the opposite: out of the box / standard. Every modification must be rebuilt at every upgrade, forever — a debt the project meeting rarely prices.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: rollback · migration · legacy system. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means moving data and operations from one system to another?
a migration (n) — to migrate data; a cloud migration. The unglamorous heart of every project: decades of messy records moved house — and cleaned, or not, on the way.
2. Which term means the old system still running the business — aging, patched, and understood by three people near retirement?
a legacy system (n) — from the Kodak case's "legacy business", here in IT form: old, critical, and expensive to leave. To decommission / retire a legacy system.
3. Which term means reversing a failed launch — switching back to the old system?
a rollback (n) — to roll back. The safety net every plan promises; the catch, as this case shows, is that after enough months live, there is no longer an "old system" to roll back to.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. The project was six months late, so leadership compressed the testing phase to protect the go-live date. To compress a phase means…
  (a) to cancel it   (b) to squeeze the same work into less time — or quietly less work into the time   (c) to outsource it
(b) — to compress (v) — the politest verb in project management, and the one that appears, renamed, in every post-mortem in this story.
2. Orders existed in the system; the warehouse simply couldn't see them. Products sat in the building while customers' shelves stayed empty. The company could not fulfil its orders. To fulfil an order means…
  (a) to receive it   (b) to pick, pack and deliver it   (c) to invoice it
(b) — to fulfil (v); order fulfilment. The failure mode that turns an IT story into a business-page story: the goods exist, the customers exist, and the system between them has gone dark.

D · Collocation & word building

1. Complete the project set: to run over ___ (cost more than planned) · to slip behind ___ · scope ___ — the quiet growth of what the project is supposed to deliver.
Over budget · behind schedule · scope creep. The three horsemen of every mega-project — IT, construction, anywhere — and they usually ride together.
2. One verb completes all three, and the whole case turns on when companies do it too late: to ___ the plug on a failing project · to ___ back from a go-live date · to ___ the emergency brake.
to pull — pull the plug, pull back, pull the brake. From the Blockbuster case — but inverted: there the plug was pulled too early; in this story, the tragedy is almost always that nobody pulled it at all.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss. Customer companies are named; the software vendors — several different ones across these stories — are deliberately not: the pattern belongs to the projects, not to any product.

Part 1 — The Halloween with no chocolate

First, understand the bet. An enterprise system replacement is not an IT purchase; it is open-heart surgery on a running company. Every order, invoice, pallet and payslip must keep flowing while the organs that process them are swapped. The projects run for years, cost from tens of millions into the billions, and consume armies of consultants — an entire global industry exists just to implement them. Boards approve them for a genuine prize: one system, one set of numbers, one version of the truth, replacing the tangle of aging legacy systems held together by three specialists near retirement. Roughly half of these projects, decade after decade of surveys agree, end up seriously over budget, behind schedule, or worse. The stories that follow are the "worse".

The genre's founding legend is Hershey, 1999. America's biggest chocolate maker was replacing its core systems and, running late, made two decisions that now appear in textbooks with red circles around them: it compressed the testing schedule to protect the date, and it chose a big-bang go-live — everything at once — in July, precisely when orders for its two biggest seasons, Halloween and Christmas, come flooding in. The new system came up; the flow of orders through it did not. Warehouses held over a hundred million dollars of Kisses and Jolly Ranchers that the system could not steer onto trucks. Shelves went empty in the most chocolate-critical quarter of the year, sales fell, the share price dropped sharply, and business schools acquired their favourite opening slide: the company that had the chocolate, had the customers, and lost Halloween in the software between them.

Hershey survived, fixed it, and later completed upgrades smoothly — a detail the legend drops but this series keeps: the same company, chastened, became a case study in doing it right. The lesson was never "systems don't work". It was that a go-live date had been treated as a promise to the calendar instead of a conclusion from the testing — and the calendar, unlike the testing, cannot tell you anything.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is an ERP replacement "open-heart surgery", and what is the prize that justifies it?

2. Name Hershey's two red-circled decisions and their consequence.

1. The business must keep running while its core processes are replaced — years, nine-figure budgets, consultant armies. The prize: one system and one version of the truth instead of a fragile tangle of legacy systems.

2. Compressing testing to protect the date, and a big-bang go-live in July — straight into Halloween and Christmas ordering. Over $100m of candy sat unshippable, sales and the share price fell, and the case entered every textbook.

Discuss:
  • "A promise to the calendar instead of a conclusion from the testing." Where in your world do dates outrank evidence — and who has the power to say so out loud?

Part 2 — The catalogue

The pattern repeats with the reliability of physics, and the catalogue is worth reading like a coroner. FoxMeyer, in the mid-1990s, is the extreme case: a five-billion-dollar drug distributor bet on a new ERP and an automated warehouse simultaneously, while also signing aggressive new contracts priced on savings the unbuilt system was supposed to deliver. Orders misfired, stock vanished into the automation, costs exploded — and in 1996 the company collapsed into bankruptcy and was sold off for a fraction of its size. The trustees sued the software maker and the consultants for hundreds of millions; the settlements are confidential; the corpse is not. It remains the reference answer to "can a software project actually kill a company?"

The gentler entries rhyme. A US utility went live on schedule in 2012 — in the same week a hurricane devastated its service area — because contract penalties and project fatigue made the date immovable; payroll promptly broke, workers were overpaid, underpaid or unpaid through the storm recovery, and the total repair bill ran toward a billion dollars. A famous cosmetics company's 2018 go-live jammed its main distribution centre so badly it could not fulfil orders from its biggest customers — and it learned the modern coda: shareholders now sue over botched go-lives, arguing the risk was known and understated. A major British city council's finance-system project ran so far over — from tens of millions towards ten times that — that it became a contributing factor in the council's effective bankruptcy. And in the industry's most discussed anti-disaster, a giant European discount retailer simply stopped: seven years and roughly half a billion euros into its programme, it cancelled the whole thing and returned to its old system — reportedly because the software assumed one way of valuing inventory and the company refused to abandon its own. Ridiculed at the time as waste, the decision has aged into grudging respect: it is the catalogue's only entry where somebody pulled the plug before the go-live did it for them.

Read as one text, the catalogue keeps confessing the same three sins. Testing compressed to protect a date. Complexity multiplied by customisation — every "but we've always done it this way" hard-coded into the machine at premium prices. And the sunk-cost engine from this series' other cases: with two hundred million spent, no executive can afford to be the one who stops — so the only acceptable direction is forward, which is how a late project becomes a disaster instead of just a late project.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What makes FoxMeyer the extreme case?

2. Why has the abandoned half-billion-euro project aged into respect?

1. A $5bn distributor attempted ERP plus warehouse automation at once, priced new contracts on savings the system hadn't yet delivered, lost control of orders and stock — and went bankrupt in 1996. The project didn't damage the company; it ended it.

2. Because it is the only case where leadership stopped before go-live: seven years and ~€500m in, the retailer refused to let the software rewrite how it ran its stock, cancelled, and kept its working old system. Everyone else's disasters made its "waste" look like discipline.

Discuss:
  • Two hundred million in, stopping means admitting it's gone. Design the governance that makes "stop" sayable — who says it, with what protection?

Part 3 — Change the software, or change the company

Strip away the technology and every entry in the catalogue is the same collision: standard software encodes one way of running a business, and a company is a thousand people's habits pretending to be processes. Go-live is the moment those two meet without an interpreter. That is why the projects are hard in a way that building a bridge is not: the bridge does not argue back. The industry's own veterans put it as a fork — change the software to fit the company, and inherit complexity forever; or change the company to fit the software, and fight every department whose "always" is being deleted. The failed projects almost all tried to do the first while claiming to do the second; the famous cancellation happened because one company understood the fork and refused both prongs honestly.

Two quieter truths complete the picture. The successes are invisible by definition — thousands of companies cross these go-lives every year without headlines, usually the ones that tested until the testing said yes, went phased instead of big-bang, cleaned their data before moving it, and treated the project as business change with software attached rather than the reverse. And the game never ends: entire generations of these systems are now reaching end-of-support, pushing a worldwide wave of migrations to newer cloud versions on vendor deadlines — thousands of companies queued for their own go-live day this decade, consultants scarcer and dearer than ever, every board reading the old catalogue nervously. The people who carry these projects — the analysts, consultants and administrators who translate between the company's habits and the system's rules — have quietly become some of the most consequential people in any business: the interpreters at the collision.

So the case ends where the practitioner lives, with the question every project in the catalogue answered too late: when the company's way and the system's way disagree, who decides which one bends — and is that person in the room? Companies that can answer before the go-live get the boring, invisible success. Companies that can't find out on the day the switch is flipped — with the trucks waiting, the payroll due, and Halloween coming.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the fork, and how did the failures typically handle it?

2. Why are the successes invisible, and what wave is happening now?

1. Change the software to fit the company (permanent complexity) or change the company to fit the software (organisational war). The failures tried the first while claiming the second — customising heavily under a banner of standardisation.

2. Because a smooth go-live makes no news: tested-until-yes, phased, clean data, business change first. Meanwhile end-of-support deadlines are pushing a global wave of migrations to newer cloud versions — thousands of go-lives queued this decade, with the catalogue as bedtime reading.

Discuss:
  • "The interpreters at the collision." Is that a fair description of the people who work with these systems — and what does it imply they should be paid and asked?

Grammar — verb patterns that change meaning

English has a small set of verbs whose meaning flips depending on whether -ing or to follows — and project language leans on exactly these verbs: stop, try, remember, forget, regret, go on, mean. Get the pattern wrong and your status report says the opposite of the truth.

The five that matter most:
• stop + -ing = the activity ends: "They stopped testing." · stop + to = pause in order to do something: "They stopped to test one last scenario." One project survived; guess which.
• try + -ing = experiment with a method: "They tried phasing the rollout." · try + to = attempt something difficult: "They tried to hit the July date."
• remember/forget + to = the duty (before): "Remember to back up the legacy data." · remember/forget + -ing = the memory (after): "Nobody remembers signing off that change."
• regret + -ing = past act you're sorry about: "They regretted compressing the tests." · regret + to = formal bad news now: "We regret to inform you the go-live is postponed."
• go on + -ing = continue the same: "They went on customising." · go on + to = move to the next stage: "The company went on to complete the upgrade smoothly."
And the sleeper: mean + -ing = involve ("Standardising means abandoning old habits") vs mean + to = intend ("Nobody meant to skip the checks").

1. Choose the form — and know what you just said

Complete with the correct form of the verb in brackets, then say aloud what the sentence means.

1. Under deadline pressure, the team stopped (run) the full test suite entirely.

2. Hershey went on (complete) later upgrades without incident — the chastened sequel.

3. We regret (inform) you that the launch has been moved to the first quarter.

1. running (the activity ended — the fatal reading)

2. to complete (the next stage in the story, not more of the same)

3. to inform (formal bad news now)

2. Two sentences, two worlds

Write both versions and state the difference. Full sentences.

1. (stop / test) The night before go-live, they stopped — versus — they stopped

2. (try / phase) The steering committee tried the rollout — versus — tried the rollout on schedule.

3. (remember / migrate) The team remembered the archive — versus — remembered the archive last spring.

1. stopped testing (gave up on tests) / stopped to test (paused for one more test)

2. tried phasing (experimented with a phased method) / tried to phase (attempted it, probably against resistance)

3. remembered to migrate (did the duty) / remembered migrating (has the memory of doing it)

3. The post-mortem — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with -ing or to + verb, then read the findings aloud.

The programme began well: the team remembered (clean) the master data and tried (pilot) the system in two small markets first. But when the schedule slipped, leadership stopped (listen) to the test reports and went on (customise) against advice — hitting the date now meant (cut) the final rehearsal. Nobody meant (endanger) the launch; nobody remembers (approve) the shortcut either. Going deeper

A note on the record: figures are rounded from public reporting and court filings; several settlements are confidential; failure-rate statistics vary by definition ("challenged" is doing heavy work in most of them); and every named company here has also done many things well — the catalogue collects their worst days, not their character.

What the case actually tests. First, the mirror problem. An ERP project is the only moment a company is forced to write down how it actually works — and most discover they don't know. The processes live in habits, exceptions and one veteran's head; the software demands they be stated. Much of what gets billed as "software failure" is the company meeting itself in the mirror and refusing the reflection. Corollary: the cheapest phase of any such project is the honest process inventory before it — and it is the phase most often skipped as too obvious.

Second, dates as social objects. A go-live date starts as an estimate, hardens into a commitment, and ends as an identity — executives bonused on it, contracts penalised around it, careers attached to it. By the end nobody in the room can afford to hear the testing. The fix is structural, not moral: authority to move the date must live with someone who is rewarded for the system working, not for the date holding — and the catalogue is a list of companies where no such person existed.

Third, the practitioner's reframe. This series keeps finding the human who carries the real product — the interpreter's accountability, the reviewer's sign-off — and here it is the person who can translate between how the business behaves and what the system requires. That skill is portable across every vendor, every version, every migration wave; the configuration screens change, the collision never does. For anyone working in this world, the career advice hiding in the catalogue is one sentence: be the person who can say, in both languages, what will break — early enough for it to matter.

Talk it through:
  • The mirror problem: pick a process you know well and try to state it as rules. Where do the exceptions live — and who is the "veteran's head"?
  • Design the date authority: who should hold the power to move a go-live, how are they paid, and how do you protect them the morning after they use it?
  • The abandoned project has aged into respect. Will it still look wise in twenty years — or will the company's old system become its own catalogue entry? Argue both.
  • Series thread: Hanjin's disaster was written in unread clauses; go-live disasters are written in unheard test reports. What single organisational habit addresses both?

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

You are the programme director, four weeks from go-live. Testing has found serious defects; moving the date triggers contract penalties and a furious board. The CEO asks, in front of everyone: "Every project has bugs. Ours has a deadline. Are you telling me you can't deliver — or that you won't?"

Answer properly: refuse the false binary, translate the defects into business consequences (which orders, whose payroll), state what going live now would mean — with precise verb patterns — and give the decision you are formally recommending. Then compress it to ninety seconds.

Task C · The interpreter's meeting (4 minutes)

The collision in one room: a department head insists "we've always done it this way"; the system standard says otherwise. As the analyst in the middle, run the meeting — make the department's real requirement visible, price the customisation honestly (forever, not once), and land a recommendation both sides can repeat upstairs.

Prefer a ready-made collision? Take one of these:

  • Sales wants its hand-crafted discount rules kept; the standard offers four discount types.
  • The warehouse counts stock its own way every Friday; the system counts continuously.
  • Finance closes the month with a spreadsheet ritual nobody fully understands anymore.
  • A country office insists its market is "completely different" — for the ninth time.
