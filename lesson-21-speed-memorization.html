Thirteen seconds

A large hall. Rows of tables. At each table there is a competitor with a pack of cards face down in front of them, a stopwatch, and a pair of ear defenders. The room is silent. When the signal comes, every competitor turns over the pack and begins.

This is the speed cards event, one of the most watched at memory championships. The task: look at a shuffled pack of fifty-two cards and learn the order as fast as you can. When you are ready you stop the clock, hand over the pack, and later you must put a second, new pack into the same order. Both the time and the accuracy count.

What must a speed cards competitor do?
Learn the order of a shuffled pack as fast as possible, stop the clock, and later arrange a fresh pack in the same order.
Talk about it. Would you rather be fast and sometimes wrong, or slow and always right? In which situations?

In recent years the best competitors have got the time for a whole pack below thirteen seconds. That is about a quarter of a second for each card. It sounds impossible, and it would be, if each card needed a new idea. But it does not.

The answer is that nothing new happens at all. Every card is already a fixed picture, and every place is already a fixed place. The competitor is not inventing. They are looking up pictures and putting them in places they have walked a thousand times. The work was done in training, long before the day.

How can a competitor learn a pack in about a quarter of a second per card?
Nothing is invented on the day: each card is already a fixed picture and each place is a fixed place, so it is look-up and placing.
Talk about it. What skill have you practised so much that you now do it without thinking? How long did it take?

The most important lesson of speed is the least obvious. You do not get faster by trying to go faster. You get faster by finding the one step that is slow, and making it automatic, and then finding the next.

In this lesson you will do exactly that, using ordinary words and pictures instead of cards. You will time yourself, find where the seconds go, and train in short rounds that raise your speed in steps.

How do you get faster?
Find the slowest step, make it automatic, then find the next one.
Talk about it. What would you like to be able to do ten times faster?

Finding where the seconds go

Memorising an item has four small steps. Speed comes from making each one smaller.

  1. Look-up. Turn the item into its fixed picture.
  2. Build. Make the picture act in a vivid way.
  3. Place. Put it at the next place on the route.
  4. Move on. Step to the next item without checking or going back.

How to train

  • Accuracy first. Do not time yourself until you can do the task correctly at a slow pace.
  • Short rounds. Thirty to sixty seconds, then stop and rest. Many short rounds beat one long one.
  • Raise the speed by about a tenth each time. If you make errors, go back.
  • Keep a log. Write the time and the number of mistakes. Progress is visible.
  • Rest. Sleep is part of training.
