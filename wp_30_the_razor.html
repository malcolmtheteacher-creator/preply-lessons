The Razor

In which a Franciscan friar cuts away everything unnecessary—and accidentally sets the stage for the end of medieval philosophy

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet William of Ockham, who wielded a principle of such power that it reshaped Western thought: do not multiply entities beyond necessity. His razor cuts away Aquinas' grand synthesis, denies the existence of universals, and opens the door to nominalism, empiricism, and the modern world. Medieval philosophy ends not with a bang but with a razor’s cut.

I. Before We Begin

What do you know about Ockham's Razor? Nominalism? The simplification that followed Aquinas?

♦ ♦ ♦

The Principle

“Do not multiply entities beyond necessity.” This is Ockham's Razor, and it is the simplest, most powerful principle in philosophy. When two explanations explain the same thing equally well, choose the simpler one. Do not assume what you don't need to assume.

This principle, applied ruthlessly, will demolish Aquinas' cathedral.

II. The Story—The Razor Wielded

William of Ockham was born around 1287 in a small village in Surrey, England. He was a Franciscan friar, and as a Franciscan, he belonged to an order that valued poverty and simplicity. This is key to understanding his philosophy: he was not trying to be clever or iconoclastic. He was trying to be simple.

The Crisis

By Ockham's time, medieval philosophy had become enormously complex. Aquinas' synthesis was magnificent but intricate. He posited universals (the form of “humanity” that all humans share), essences distinct from existence, multiple layers of being. Each layer required explanation. The system was complete and elaborate.

Ockham asked: do we really need all of this? Can we explain what we observe using fewer assumptions?

Nominalism

Ockham's radical answer was nominalism: universals do not exist. There is no general form of “humanity.” There are only individual humans. The word “humanity” is a name (nomen in Latin—hence “nominalism”) we apply to the collection of individual humans. But the universal itself has no real existence.

Universals are not things. They are names. A universal is a concept (intentio) in the mind. Real things are only individuals. — William of Ockham, Ordinatio

This seems like a small point, but it was revolutionary. If universals don't exist, then the entire Platonic and Aristotelian framework that Aquinas built on collapses. Plato's Forms? Gone. Aristotle's essences? Gone. Ockham replaced them with a simpler picture: the world consists of individual things and our names for groups of individual things.

The Triple Cut

Ockham's razor cut in three directions simultaneously:

1. Against Universals

Do not assume that universals exist. Explain everything through individual things and our concepts.

2. Against Elaborate Metaphysics

Do not multiply entities (beings, essences, qualities) beyond what observation requires. A simpler ontology is preferable.

3. Against Authority

Do not accept a doctrine just because Aristotle or Aquinas said it. Use your own reason to examine what can be known from experience.

Politics and Separation

Ockham also challenged the authority of the Church in temporal matters. He argued that the Church and state have distinct spheres. This was radical in his time. His political writings on the separation of church and state were as revolutionary as his metaphysics.

Because of his views, Ockham was summoned to Avignon in 1324 to answer charges of heresy. He was never formally condemned, but he spent the rest of his life in exile, at the court of King Louis of Bavaria. He died in 1347, probably from the plague.

The Irony: Ockham intended to defend Christian theology from over-elaboration. Instead, he ended medieval philosophy. His razor didn’t defend faith; it cut the ground out from under the grand syntheses that had tried to defend it.

III. The Ideas—Simplicity, Plurality, and Autonomy

Ockham's influence extends far beyond medieval philosophy. His three key ideas shaped everything that followed.

Idea 1: Simplicity Is Powerful

The simplest explanation that accounts for the facts is preferable to a complex one.

This principle has become foundational in science. When Newton and Einstein developed their theories, they applied Ockham's razor—simpler equations explained more. Modern physics is Ockhamic: find the simplest laws that account for observation.

But notice: this is not the same as truth being simple. Reality might be complex. But we should prefer simpler explanations unless we have evidence for complexity.

Idea 2: The World Is Made of Individuals

Only particular things exist. Universals are names, not entities.

This leads directly to empiricism. If only individuals exist, then knowledge must come from experience of individuals. General principles and universal laws must be derived from observation of particulars. This is the foundation of the scientific method.

Idea 3: Authority Is Not Enough

A claim is not true just because an authority says it. Use reason to examine it.

This is the most dangerous idea, from the perspective of the medieval establishment. Ockham is not saying reason is infallible. He is saying reason is necessary. You cannot hide behind Aquinas or Aristotle. You must think for yourself.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Unintended Consequence

Ockham intended to defend faith by clearing away unnecessary metaphysical complications. But his razor cut so deeply that it undermined the entire framework that medieval theology depended on. When you remove universals, you remove the basis for natural theology. When you remove essences, you remove the foundation for virtue ethics. When you assert the autonomy of reason from authority, you open the door to challenges to authority itself.

Medieval philosophy was built on the synthesis of faith and reason, particularly through universals and essences. Ockham's nominalism and empiricism made that synthesis impossible. Philosophy had to find a new path.
