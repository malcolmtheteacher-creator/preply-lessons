A Retrieved Reformation

O. Henry's tale of a safe-cracker who buries his past — until one locked door forces him to dig it up again.

Today: you'll tell the story of a criminal who tries to become an honest man — and master the grammar of the buried past, the past perfect, the tense that lets a writer reach back behind the moment and show us what had already happened.

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Behind a locked steel door, the bank kept its money in a heavy iron safe that only two people knew the combination to.
a safe (n) — a strong metal box or cabinet with a lock, used to keep money and valuables secure.
2. He was a burglar of rare skill, slipping into locked houses at night and leaving with the jewels before anyone woke.
a burglar (n) — a criminal who breaks into buildings in order to steal.
3. After serving time behind bars, he was granted a pardon and walked out of the prison gates a free man.
a pardon (n) — an official decision to forgive a crime and release someone from punishment.
4. The patient detective had trailed the thief for months, following him quietly from town to town, waiting for a single mistake.
to trail (v) — to follow someone secretly over time, watching their movements.
5. Wanting a clean start, he took a new name and a new identity, so that no one in the town would know who he had once been.
an identity (n) — who a person is; the name, history and character by which they are known.
6. He spread out his fine set of tools — the drills, the picks and the polished instruments he had made himself for opening any lock.
tools (n, plural) — instruments used to do a particular job; here, a burglar's specialised kit for breaking into safes.
7. Meeting her, he felt something shift inside him, and quietly resolved on a complete reformation — to leave crime behind and live an honest life.
a reformation (n) — a deep change for the better in how a person lives and behaves.
8. The new bank had installed a modern vault with a heavy time-lock — a room-sized safe strong enough, they boasted, to hold a fortune securely.
a vault (n) — a large, reinforced room with a thick locking door, used by a bank to store money safely.
9. He longed to be respectable at last — a decent, trusted man of business whom the whole town would greet with a smile.
respectable (adj) — considered good, honest and worthy of respect by society.
10. The little girl was trapped inside the airless vault, unable to open the door from within, while the family panicked outside.
trapped (adj) — caught in a place or situation from which one cannot escape.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A prisoner set free

Jimmy Valentine sat in the prison shoe-shop, patiently stitching, when a guard came to fetch him to the warden's office. There Jimmy was handed his pardon, signed that morning by the governor. He took it without much joy. He had served nearly ten months of a four-year sentence — though he had expected to stay no more than three, given how many powerful friends a man like Jimmy Valentine had on the outside.

"Now, Valentine," said the warden, "you'll go out in the morning. Brace up, and make a man of yourself. You're not a bad fellow at heart. Stop cracking safes, and live straight." But Jimmy only looked mildly surprised. He had never, he said, cracked a safe in his life. The warden laughed. Everyone knew Jimmy Valentine was the finest safe-burglar in the country — they had simply never been able to prove a single job against him.

The next morning Jimmy walked free. He went straight to a good restaurant and enjoyed the first honest pleasures of liberty: a fine meal, a cigar, a walk in the open air. Then he took a train to a small town, went up to his old room, and pulled from a hidden panel a beautiful, dust-free set of burglar's tools — the finest ever made, which he had designed himself. Within a week, a fresh string of daring safe robberies had puzzled the police in three different states.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Jimmy come to leave prison, and how did he react to it?

2. What did Jimmy do in the days immediately after his release?

1. He received a governor's pardon after serving under a year of a four-year sentence; he took it calmly, without joy, and denied ever having cracked a safe.

2. He enjoyed a good meal and his freedom, then collected his hidden set of burglar's tools and quickly returned to robbing safes across several states.

Discuss:
  • The warden begs Jimmy to "make a man of himself." At this point, does Jimmy show any real wish to change? What has to happen first?

Part 2 — A new name, a new life

A detective named Ben Price took an interest in the robberies. Price knew Jimmy's methods well — the neat drilling, the cool nerve, the clean escape — and he had studied them for years. Quietly, he began to gather what he needed to catch Jimmy at last. Somewhere out there, Price was patiently on the trail.

But Jimmy, stepping off a train in the little town of Elmore, Arkansas, had walked straight into something he had never planned for. A young woman had crossed the street towards the bank, and their eyes had met. In that instant, the Jimmy Valentine who cracked safes seemed to vanish. He became, on the spot, another man. He learned that she was Annabel Adams, and that her father owned the town bank. Jimmy took a new name — Ralph D. Spencer — invented a story about coming to town to start a shoe business, and settled down.

The reformation was complete, and it was genuine. Mr. Ralph Spencer's shoe shop prospered. He won the respect of the town, made many friends, and — best of all — grew close to Annabel and won her love. Within a year he was engaged to be married, trusted by her father, and folded warmly into the Adams family. The old life had died; he had become the respectable man he had once only pretended to be. He wrote one last letter to an old friend, saying he was giving away his precious tools for good: he would never need them again.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was Ben Price, and what was he doing?

2. What changed Jimmy in Elmore, and what new life did he build there?

1. A detective who knew Jimmy's safe-cracking methods and had been patiently building a case to catch him.

2. Falling in love with Annabel Adams, the banker's daughter; he took the name Ralph Spencer, opened a successful shoe shop, became respectable, and got engaged — and meant it.

Discuss:
  • Jimmy's change is real, yet it rests on a lie about who he is. Can a new life built on a hidden past ever be fully safe?

Part 3 — The locked door

One day the whole Adams family, with Jimmy, went to the bank, where Mr. Adams was proudly showing off a fine new vault — a small room with a heavy time-lock door. Jimmy had brought his old tool-case along, meaning to take it away and give it up that very day. As the grown-ups admired the shining new door, two little girls were playing nearby. In a careless moment, one child, Agatha, shut the other, May, inside the vault — and the heavy door swung to and locked. The time-lock had not yet been set, but the bolts had shot home. The child was sealed in, in the dark, with only a few minutes of air. She could not be reached, and there was no one who could open that door.

Annabel turned to the man she loved. "Ralph — can't you do something? Try, won't you?" He looked at her with a strange, soft smile. In that moment Ralph Spencer knew that saving the child would mean destroying Ralph Spencer forever. Ben Price, it happened, had just walked quietly into the bank and was leaning against the rail, watching. Jimmy said, "Get away from the door, all of you." He set his tool-case on the table, and — for the first time in the man's honest new life — Jimmy Valentine returned. Faster than anyone had ever seen it done, he drilled into the door of the vault. In ten minutes he had beaten his own record; the bolts drew back, and he pulled the door open. May, faint but safe, was gathered into her mother's arms.

Jimmy Valentine put on his coat and walked towards the front of the bank. He thought he heard a far-off voice call "Ralph!" but he did not pause. At the door stood Ben Price. "Hello, Ben," said Jimmy, still with that quiet smile. "Got around at last, have you? Well, let's go. It doesn't make much difference now." And then the strange thing happened. Ben Price behaved rather oddly. "Guess you're mistaken, Mr. Spencer," he said. "Don't believe I recognise you. Your buggy's waiting for you, isn't it?" And Ben Price turned and walked away down the street.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What emergency forced Jimmy to make his choice, and what did that choice cost him?

2. What did Ben Price do at the end, and why is it surprising?

1. A little girl was accidentally locked in the airless new vault; to save her, Jimmy had to use his safe-cracking skill in front of everyone — revealing that Ralph Spencer was really Jimmy Valentine.

2. Price, who had come to arrest him and had watched the whole thing, pretended not to recognise him and walked away — quietly letting the reformed man go free.

Discuss:
  • Jimmy chooses to save the child even though he is sure it will cost him his freedom and his love. What does that choice tell us about who he has really become?

Grammar — Past Perfect & Flashback

This story is built out of hidden pasts. The whole plot turns on what Jimmy had done before the present of the story — and on the moment that buried past comes back. English marks that deeper, earlier past with the past perfect, and it is the writer's key tool for the flashback: stepping back behind the main action to show what had already happened.

How it works:
• Past simple tells the main story, event after event: "Annabel crossed the street and their eyes met."
• Past perfect (had + past participle) reaches further back, to something completed before that point: "By the time Price arrived, Jimmy had already drilled through the door."
• Use the two together to show order: "He gave away his tools because he had decided never to steal again." (First he decided; then he gave them away.)
The flashback move: a writer narrates in past simple, then drops into past perfect to open a window on an earlier time — "Jimmy set the case on the table. He had built those tools himself, years before, and he had never once been caught with them." The past perfect signals: we have stepped back in time. When we return to the main line, we go back to past simple.

1. Past simple or past perfect?

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence with the correct form of the verb.

1. When the warden handed him the pardon, Jimmy (serve) less than ten months. (completed before that moment)

2. Their eyes met, and in that instant Jimmy (become) a different man. (the main event)

3. He gave away his tools because he (decide) never to steal again. (the earlier reason)

1. had served (past perfect — completed before the pardon)

2. became (past simple — a main-line event)

3. had decided (past perfect — the decision came first)

2. Fix the form

Each sentence needs the past perfect, but the verb is in the wrong form. Write the corrected verb.

1. Nobody in Elmore knew that Ralph Spencer (be) a famous burglar.

2. By the time Price reached the bank, Jimmy (already / open) the vault.

3. In his letter he explained that he (give) away his tools for good.

1. had been (had + past participle)

2. had already opened (adverb between "had" and the participle)

3. had given ("give" → past participle "given")

3. Tell the flashback — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the past simple or the past perfect, so the order of events is clear. Read it aloud once you're done.

Jimmy (set) the tool-case on the table. He (build) those instruments himself, years before, and until that day he (not / touch) them since coming to Elmore. Now, as the child cried inside the vault, the past he (try) so hard to bury came quietly back.

set (past simple, main line) · had built (past perfect, earlier) · had not touched (past perfect, earlier) · had tried (past perfect, earlier)

Notice the pattern: the main action stays in past simple, and every step back into the buried past takes the past perfect.

Going deeper

O. Henry made his name with endings that turn on a single, surprising twist, and "A Retrieved Reformation" is one of the most quietly moving. The story never lets us relax into a simple moral. Jimmy has to use his sin to prove his virtue: the very skill that made him a criminal is the only thing that can save the child. And the law, in the shape of Ben Price, chooses mercy over duty — an ending that asks whether justice is really about the rules, or about the person in front of you.

Worth noticing: the title itself is a puzzle. A "reformation" is a change for the better; but it is "retrieved" — recovered, pulled back — as if his old self had to be dug up one last time to make the new self real. Watch how the whole tale is a study in double identities: Jimmy / Ralph, criminal / respectable man, the detective who catches him / the man who lets him go.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Jimmy has to reveal his true, criminal self in order to do the most good thing in the story. What is O. Henry saying about the relationship between our worst skills and our best actions?
  • Ben Price breaks the law he is paid to uphold by letting Jimmy go. Was he right? Is mercy ever a betrayal of justice?
  • The title calls this a "retrieved" reformation. Why "retrieved" and not simply "a reformation"? What does that one word add?
  • Put yourself in Jimmy's place at the vault: Annabel is watching, your freedom is at stake, and a child is suffocating. What would you have done — and how quickly?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Jimmy only becomes truly good at the exact moment he throws away everything good in his new life. Is real virtue only proved when it costs us something?
  2. The story suggests that love, not fear of the law, is what finally reforms Jimmy. Do people change more through hope than through punishment?
  3. Ben Price never explains himself; he just walks away. Was that a failure of duty, or the finest thing he ever did?
