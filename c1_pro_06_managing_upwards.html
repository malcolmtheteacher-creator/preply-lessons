Managing Upwards

C1 Level · Career Strategy

What Your Boss Actually Wants

The paradox: Your boss isn't thinking about you. They're thinking about their boss. They're stressed about delivering their targets. They're managing up while managing down. Understanding this is the key to managing upwards effectively.

The Manager's Dilemma

Your manager is caught between:

  • Executive pressure: They have targets, timelines, and expectations from above.
  • Team expectations: They need to support, develop, and retain their team.
  • Resource constraints: They never have as much budget, time, or headcount as they need.
  • Political reality: They need to maintain relationships across the organization.

Your job isn't to solve their problems. But your job is to make their life easier — or harder. Managing upwards means being the kind of report who makes your boss's job easier.

Different Boss Archetypes

The Micromanager

Motivations: Control, fear of failure, perfectionism. They want visibility into everything.

How to work with them: Overcommunicate. Give frequent updates (even if you don't think you need to). Be transparent about problems early, not late. Ask for guidance on approach before diving in. This paradoxically gives you more autonomy — they'll trust you because they know what you're doing.

The Absent Boss

Motivations: Busy with higher priorities, checked out, distracted. They're not paying attention.

How to work with them: Be proactive. Don't wait for guidance. When you do get time, make it count — come with a clear agenda and specific asks. Build relationships with their peers so you're not completely dependent on them. This is an opportunity to own your work, but it can also feel lonely.

The Visionary

Motivations: Big picture, innovation, changing the game. They focus on strategy, not execution.

How to work with them: Show how your work connects to the vision. Focus on impact and opportunity, not process. They'll delegate implementation details to you — be clear about what you need to deliver and bring them progress on strategy.

The Analytical

Motivations: Data, rigor, evidence. They want to understand the logic before deciding.

How to work with them: Come prepared with data. Don't ask for gut feeling — bring research. Anticipate questions. They respect thorough thinking. Walk them through your logic. They're not difficult; they just want to understand.

Question: What archetype is your boss? How do you typically interact with them? What would happen if you adjusted your approach?

Strategies for Managing Upwards

1. Create Alignment Constantly

Your boss has targets. Make sure your work clearly supports them. Before you start major work, ask: "Here's what I'm planning to do. Does that align with your priorities?"

2. Bring Solutions, Not Just Problems

❌ "This project is off track." ✓ "This project is off track. Here's what I think caused it [analysis], and here are three options [solutions]. I recommend [the best one]. What's your instinct?"

3. Build Trust Through Transparency

Share problems early, not late. "I want to flag something that might impact our timeline..." This builds trust because your boss won't be blindsided. They know they can trust you.

4. Manage Expectations Relentlessly

Underpromise and overdeliver. "I can have this by Friday" and deliver Wednesday. "This will cost $100k" and it costs $80k. Your boss will learn to trust your estimates.

5. Get Credit Right

Your boss should know what you're accomplishing. Not arrogant — just factual. "We shipped the API integration, which unblocked the mobile team." Make sure your boss can articulate your wins to their boss.

6. Find a Sponsor

Your manager is one person. Find someone more senior who believes in you and will advocate for you. This might be your manager's boss, a mentor, or a peer in another department. Sponsors are how you advance.

Three Real Scenarios

Practice managing upwards in these situations:

10:00

Scenario 1: Proposing an Idea When Your Boss Is Risk-Averse

Context: You've got an innovative idea for a new market approach. Your boss is cautious and risk-averse. They tend to say no to new initiatives.

Your task: How do you frame this pitch?

Approach: De-risk it

"I have an idea for testing a new market segment. I know we usually move cautiously, and I want to show this is low-risk. Here's what I'm proposing:

Pilot scope: Spend $50k on testing in Q2 (under existing budget). Limited to one region.

Clear success metrics: If we get [specific measurable result], we move forward. If we don't, we've learned something valuable for only $50k.

Decision gate: We review at the end of Q2. You decide whether to expand.

This lets us test innovation without big commitment. What's your thinking?"

Why this works: You're not asking for a big yes. You're asking for a small yes with an exit ramp. You've addressed the risk proactively. You show you respect their cautious style.

Scenario 2: Asking for Resources Without Sounding Demanding

Context: You need to hire another person to deliver on your goals. But your boss is resource-constrained. You need to make a business case.

Your task: How do you ask?

Approach: Lead with impact, not need

"I want to talk about resourcing for Q3. Right now, we're tracking to deliver [X outcome]. With [Y resource], I believe we can deliver [X + Z outcome]. Here's the business case:

Extra hire would enable: [Specific higher outcome — revenue, efficiency, speed]

ROI: [Hire costs $150k, extra revenue/savings is $500k]

If we can't hire: I'll need to deprioritize [specific project/outcome]. Which would you rather we deprioritize?

What's your thinking on this tradeoff?"

Why this works: You're not asking them to give you something nice to have. You're presenting a business decision. You've done the analysis. You acknowledge constraints (which shows you respect their reality). You force them to choose, which often leads to them finding resources.

Scenario 3: Giving Your Boss Feedback They Need to Hear

Context: Your boss has a blind spot that's affecting team morale. They interrupt people in meetings, sometimes dismissing ideas without hearing them fully. You're trusted, so you could offer feedback.

Your task: How do you approach this?

Approach: Private, specific, positioned as partnership

"Can I share some feedback? I'm telling you because I value our relationship and think you'd want to know.

In the last few team meetings, I've noticed you sometimes interrupt when someone is mid-thought — especially [specific example]. I don't think you mean to, but the team has mentioned feeling like ideas aren't being heard. I mention it because I know you care about engagement, and this might be an unintended impact."

Then give them an out: "Does that land? I could be reading it wrong."

Why this works: You're not accusing. You're sharing observation + impact. You're assuming good intent ("I know you care"). You're specific (not vague). You let them respond. This takes guts, but it builds immense respect and trust.

Building Your Career Strategy

The Long Game

Managing upwards isn't about manipulating your boss. It's about understanding what they need and making sure they know you're delivering it. It's about being professional, reliable, and strategic about your career.

When Managing Upwards Becomes Manipulation

Line crossed: You're hiding information. You're taking credit that isn't yours. You're being inauthentic. You're playing politics to get ahead at others' expense.

Healthy boundary: You're strategic about how you communicate. You present information in the light that's most likely to be heard. You make your contributions visible. You align yourself with organizational priorities. There's nothing wrong with this.

Political Savvy vs. Office Politics

Political savvy: Understanding how organizations work. Knowing who influences whom. Building relationships. Timing your messages. This is a valuable skill.

Office politics: Playing games. Creating drama. Leveraging gossip. Undermining colleagues. This is destructive.

You can be politically savvy without playing politics.

Your Boss Changes

You might have a manager for 2-3 years. Then they move on, or you change roles. Your management upwards strategy needs to adapt. Don't get too invested in any one boss. Keep building visibility with other leaders. Keep developing skills that matter.

Reflection questions:

  • Does your boss know about your major wins? How do you keep them informed?
  • What's your boss's biggest challenge right now? How could you help solve it?
  • Who is a sponsor for you — someone more senior who advocates for you?
  • What would it look like to be more proactive in your role?
