Whose Is This? Asking who owns it

For lost property, mixed-up bags, and anything left lying around.

Spot the pattern

Three questions you might hear at a lost-and-found desk:

  • "Whose umbrella is this? Someone left it by the door."
  • "Whose jacket is on my chair?"
  • "Whose are these gloves — anyone?"

Before you check: what job does "whose" do in all three?

Check the pattern CLICK
Whose asks "who owns this?" — it can go straight before a noun ("whose umbrella") or stand alone at the front of a question ("whose are these gloves?"). Careful: whose sounds exactly like who's ("who is"), but they're completely different words. "Whose bag?" = who owns the bag. "Who's coming?" = who is coming.
Whose + noun + is/are + this/these/that/those?
Whose + is/are + this/these/that/those?

From "I don't know who" to "whose is it"

A scarf is on the sofa. Nobody knows whose.
→
"Whose scarf is this?"

You use "whose" the moment you don't know the owner and want to find out.

My Bag or Mine? Two ways to say it's yours

One word goes before a noun; the other stands completely alone.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday answers:

  • "That's my bag." / "That bag is mine."
  • "Is this your jacket?" / "Is this jacket yours?"
  • "That's her umbrella." / "That umbrella is hers."

Look at where the noun "bag/jacket/umbrella" is in each pair. What's different?

Check the pattern CLICK
my / your / her always stand right before a noun (my bag, your jacket, her umbrella) — they never stand alone.
mine / yours / hers replace "the noun + belongs to me/you/her" — the noun disappears completely. You can't say "this is mine bag" — once you use "mine", the noun is gone.
my / your / his / her / our / their + noun
mine / yours / his / hers / ours / theirs (no noun after)

From "my keys" to "the keys are mine"

"Are these your keys?"
→
"Yes, they're mine."

Notice "his" and "hers" both come from "he/she" but "his" stays the same in both jobs, while "her" changes to "hers".

Anna's Keys: adding 's to a name

For talking about family, friends, and whose things they are.

Spot the pattern

  • "That's Anna's jacket on the chair."
  • "We're going to my parents' house this weekend."
  • "Have you met James's new dog?"

Look closely at where the apostrophe goes in each one. What's the difference between "Anna's" and "parents'"?

Check the pattern CLICK
One person or singular noun → 's: Anna's jacket, James's dog. A plural noun already ending in s → just add the apostrophe: my parents' house, my friends' car. If the plural noun doesn't end in s (children, people), add 's as normal: the children's toys. Say it out loud: "Anna's" and "parents'" often sound almost the same — the spelling is what shows the difference.
singular name/noun + 's → Anna's, James's, the dog's
plural noun ending in s + ' → my parents', the neighbours'
plural noun NOT ending in s + 's → the children's

From "the house of my parents" to "my parents' house"

"the car of my sister" (unnatural)
→
"my sister's car"

For people, English almost always uses 's, not "of" — "of" sounds formal and odd for everyday things and people.
