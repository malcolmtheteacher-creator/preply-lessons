Gravity

A medical engineer fights alone through a field of hypersonic debris to reach home — and the disaster that strands her has a real name and a real, growing danger behind it.

Today: you'll tell the story of Gravity — a medical engineer's fight to survive alone in a field of hypersonic debris, hundreds of kilometres above the Earth — discover that the disaster which strands her has a real name and a real, worsening danger behind it, and master the clauses English uses to lock two future events together in sequence: as soon as, once, the moment (that), until, by the time.

The words for today's story

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. A single falling domino can start a cascade: each piece knocks down the next until the whole row collapses in one unstoppable chain reaction.

2. Rock climbers clip themselves to a tether — a strong cord that keeps them attached to a fixed point so they can't drift loose or fall away.

3. As the hot-air balloon lost height too fast, the pilot began to jettison the sandbags, throwing them over the side to lose weight quickly.
to jettison (v) — to throw away or abandon something on purpose, usually to reduce weight or danger.
4. Once the loaded truck started rolling downhill, its momentum was so great that slamming the brakes couldn't stop it for another fifty metres.
momentum (n) — the force that keeps a moving object in motion; roughly, how hard it is to stop something once it's moving.

(b) empty and lifeless — desolate (adj) describes a place that is bleak, abandoned and utterly without life or comfort.
6. composure is closest in meaning to: (a) panic   (b) calmness under pressure   (c) exhaustion
(b) calmness under pressure — composure (n) is the ability to stay calm and in control, especially in a stressful situation.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. Engineers recalculated the rocket's trajectory so it wouldn't collide with anything on its path to orbit.
A trajectory is: (a) the fuel a vehicle carries; (b) the curved path an object follows through space; (c) the name of a launch site.
(b) — a trajectory (n) — the curved path a moving object follows, shaped by its speed and by gravity.

The film, in three parts

Alfonso Cuarón's Gravity (2013). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — Servicing the Hubble

Dr Ryan Stone, a medical engineer, is on her first shuttle mission, floating outside the craft to repair equipment she designed for the Hubble Space Telescope. Beside her works Matt Kowalski, a veteran astronaut on his last flight before retirement, drifting easily around the shuttle on a jet-powered backpack, trading calm jokes with Houston as if this were any other day at the office.

Then Houston's voice turns urgent: a Russian missile has just destroyed one of their own defunct satellites, and the wreckage has set off something far worse than anyone anticipated. Within minutes, a storm of debris travelling many times faster than a bullet tears through the shuttle. The rest of the crew are killed instantly. Stone, thrown loose from her safety line, tumbles away from the wreck in silence, spinning end over end into the emptiness, her oxygen supply already ticking down.

Kowalski finds her by following her panicked, half-coherent radio calls, matches her spin, and clips her to his own line. They are now alone, hundreds of kilometres above the Earth, with no shuttle, a dead crew, and a debris cloud that Kowalski calculates will circle back and hit them again in ninety minutes.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What sets off the disaster, and what does it do to the shuttle?

2. What is Stone's situation when Kowalski finds her?

1. A missile strike destroys an old satellite, and the resulting debris cloud tears through the shuttle at enormous speed, killing the rest of the crew.

2. She has been thrown loose, is tumbling and disorientated, alone in space, with limited oxygen and no way back to a craft.

Discuss:
  • Stone is a scientist, not a career astronaut — this is her first mission. Why do you think the film chose an ordinary, unprepared person as its hero rather than a seasoned astronaut?

Part 2 — Ninety minutes

Kowalski and Stone's only hope is the International Space Station, and they use his remaining thruster fuel to drift towards it as the clock runs down. As they travel, Kowalski keeps Stone talking — about her young daughter, who died years earlier in a freak accident, and about how Stone has since buried herself in work rather than face the grief. It is the first time in the film she says the girl's name.

They reach the station just as the debris field returns, but it has already torn through the ISS: the crew has evacuated, one escape capsule's parachute has been triggered by accident and is useless, and the station itself is venting air and slowly beginning to spin. As Stone finally reaches the airlock, her tether catches on the station's structure and will not free itself in time.

With seconds left before the debris arrives again, Kowalski unclips his own line from Stone's, choosing to drift away rather than risk pulling her free of the airlock with him. He tells her, calmly, to let him go. She does. He disappears into the dark, and for the first time, Stone is entirely alone.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Kowalski learn about Stone during their journey to the ISS?

2. Why does Kowalski unclip himself from Stone's tether?

1. That her young daughter died in an accident, and that Stone has been avoiding her grief by burying herself in work ever since.

2. Their tether is snagged and the debris field is seconds from returning; he sacrifices himself so his weight doesn't pull Stone away from the safety of the airlock.

Discuss:
  • Kowalski chooses to let go rather than risk both their lives. Was there really no other option — or could Stone have tried harder to save him too?

Part 3 — Across to Tiangong

Alone on the crippled ISS, Stone puts out a fire, finds a working Soyuz capsule, and discovers — too late — that its parachute has already deployed and tangled around the outside, making it useless for landing. Exhausted and out of options, she briefly gives up, lowering the oxygen and preparing to let herself drift into sleep, until a hallucinated visit from Kowalski reminds her that Soyuz capsules use the same docking system as the nearby Chinese station, Tiangong — and that its escape capsule could still get her home.

She fires the Soyuz towards Tiangong, but runs out of fuel just short of it, as the debris field tears past yet again and damages the station further. With nothing left to propel her, she improvises: bracing a fire extinguisher and firing it like a makeshift thruster, using the force of the escaping gas to push herself, hand over hand, across the last stretch of empty space.

She reaches Tiangong, forces her way into its Shenzhou escape capsule, and undocks just as the station itself begins to break apart around her. The capsule falls, burns, and finally splashes down in a lake. Water floods in as it sinks; Stone forces the hatch open, swims up through the wreckage, and drags herself onto the shore — standing, for the first time in the film, on solid ground.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Stone finally cross the gap to Tiangong when the Soyuz runs out of fuel?

2. How does the film end?

1. She uses a fire extinguisher as an improvised thruster, letting the escaping gas push her across the remaining distance.

2. Her escape capsule crash-lands in a lake; she nearly drowns escaping it, then swims to the shore and stands up on land.

Discuss:
  • The film's final image is Stone learning to stand and walk again, as if newly born. Why do you think Cuarón chose to end a space disaster film with an image about gravity and the ground, rather than about space itself?

Grammar — time & sequence clauses

Almost every crisis in Gravity is a race against a deadline — ninety minutes until the debris returns, seconds until the tether snaps, minutes of oxygen left. English has a set of clauses built exactly for locking two events together in time: as soon as, once, the moment (that), until, by the time.

The connectors and the rule:
• as soon as / the moment (that) / once — the second event follows the first immediately: "As soon as she reaches the airlock, she'll be safe." "The moment the debris passes, they'll have ninety minutes."
• until — up to the point when something happens, and then it stops or changes: "She won't relax until she's back on Earth."
• by the time — sets a deadline: one thing will already be true when the other happens: "By the time the debris returns, they'll have reached the station."
The key rule: in these time clauses, English does not use will for the future — it uses the present simple or present perfect, even though the meaning is future: "As soon as she reaches the station, she'll radio Houston" (NOT "as soon as she will reach").
Feel the difference:
• once / the moment / as soon as = an immediate trigger — event two starts right after event one.
• until = a limit — event two doesn't happen, or a state continues, up to that point.
• by the time = a deadline — looks at what will already be finished when the second event arrives. Often paired with the future perfect: "By the time help arrives, the oxygen will have run out."

The Real Story — Kessler Syndrome

The disaster that strands Ryan Stone isn't science fiction. It has a name, a real scientist behind it, and it describes a danger that is measurably getting worse, right now, above your head.

🛰️ A real warning from 1978

In 1978, NASA scientists Donald Kessler and Burton Cour-Palais published a paper called "Collision Frequency of Artificial Satellites: The Creation of a Debris Belt." Its argument is exactly the mechanism the film dramatises: once there are enough objects in low Earth orbit, a single collision creates fragments; those fragments go on to strike other objects, creating still more fragments; and the process can become self-sustaining — a cascade that could eventually make some orbits too dangerous to use. This scenario is now known as the Kessler syndrome.

The numbers are real, and they are rising

According to the European Space Agency, roughly 40,000 objects large enough to be individually tracked are currently in orbit, alongside an estimated 1.2 million fragments between 1 and 10 centimetres, and around 130 million smaller fragments still large enough to disable a satellite or pierce a spacesuit on impact. None of these numbers are shrinking.

The film's inciting event — a satellite deliberately destroyed, instantly seeding a dangerous debris cloud — has already happened for real. In 2007, a Chinese anti-satellite missile test destroyed the defunct weather satellite Fengyun-1C, creating over 3,000 trackable fragments in a single strike, still the worst debris-generating event on record. In 2021, a Russian anti-satellite test destroyed the satellite Kosmos 1408; the resulting debris cloud forced the seven astronauts then aboard the International Space Station to shelter inside their docked capsules for two hours, and threatened to pass near the station roughly every ninety minutes — the very rhythm the film invents for its own debris cloud.

Where the film bends the truth — and where it doesn't

Here's the honest part: the geography of Gravity is fiction. The Hubble Space Telescope, the ISS and the Chinese station Tiangong orbit at very different altitudes and angles — roughly 555 km, 420 km and 350–400 km respectively, on orbital paths tilted at different angles to the equator. In reality none of them is ever visible to, let alone reachable from, either of the others; Cuarón compressed three unrelated locations into one journey purely for the story. He never claimed otherwise — it's a deliberate artistic liberty, not an error he missed.

What the film gets right is the physics of moving your own body in zero gravity: Newton's third law, played out when Stone propels herself with a fire extinguisher — the escaping gas pushes her one way exactly as hard as it pushes the gas the other. The astrophysicist Neil deGrasse Tyson publicly listed a long string of the film's small errors (debris orbiting the wrong direction, Sandra Bullock's hair not floating with the rest of her) — and then, just as publicly, said he loved the film and praised its accurate depiction of floating water, the correct positions of the stars, and exactly that fire-extinguisher scene.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, did you know that a real event very like the film's opening disaster has already happened, more than once?
  • Does it change how you see the film, knowing the three space stations could never actually reach each other?
  • Kessler syndrome describes a slow-moving danger with no single dramatic moment. Do you think humanity takes slow, cumulative risks like this seriously enough?
