Today's aim: Today we're working on explaining how simple rules can produce complex collective behaviour, so you can discuss emergence and the limits of explaining things by their parts, with care.

Words you'll need

emerge (v) — to appear or develop from something simpler. Example: "A flock emerges from simple rules followed by each bird."
component (n) — one of the parts that something is made of. Example: "Each component is simple; the whole is not."
collective (adj) — done or shared by a group rather than one individual. Example: "A traffic jam is a collective phenomenon."
reductionism (n) — the view that a whole is fully explained by its smallest parts. Example: "Reductionism says chemistry follows from physics."
property (n) — a quality or characteristic that something has. Example: "Temperature is a property of many particles together."
complexity (n) — the state of having many connected parts that interact in complicated ways. Example: "The complexity of a city cannot be read from a single house."
organise (v) — to arrange or form into an ordered pattern. Example: "Ants organise themselves without a leader."
local (adj) — limited to a small area or to the nearest neighbours. Example: "Each bird responds only to local information."
phenomenon (n) — an observable event or pattern. Example: "A traffic jam is a striking phenomenon."
consciousness (n) — the experience of being aware of yourself and the world. Example: "Consciousness is a famous puzzle for emergence."

The hook: a crowd with no conductor

In 1972 the physicist Philip Anderson wrote an essay called "More Is Different". His point: knowing the laws that govern individual particles does not let you simply read off how crowds of them behave. A single water molecule has no temperature and is not wet; a billion of them do.

Starlings, too, move in swirling clouds of thousands, turning together in an instant, and no bird is in charge.

Say it aloud: Before you read on, say what you think "more is different" could mean.

Roughly: new properties and patterns can appear at larger scales that are not obvious from the parts alone, even though nothing but the parts is there. The next tabs ask how seriously to take that, and where the idea might be overstated.

Talk it through:

Sort the six properties into "a single part can have it" and "only the collection has it". What marks out the second group? Hint: ask whether the property describes what one part is, or how many parts relate to each other. Then test your idea on the question: "Is a flock anything more than the birds?"

Properties such as temperature, wetness, a flock's shape or a traffic jam describe patterns in the relationships among many parts, and they have no meaning for a single part. They are still made of nothing but the parts, yet the pattern is real and can be studied in its own right.

Three ideas, in small steps

Idea 1 · Simple local rules can build large patterns

In 1986 Craig Reynolds simulated flocking with three rules that each "bird" follows using only its nearest neighbours: stay close, match direction, avoid collisions. There is no leader and no overall plan, yet convincing flocks form.

Idea 2 · New levels need new descriptions

Temperature, pressure, life and markets are useful descriptions at their own level. In principle everything is made of particles, but in practice we explain a traffic jam with cars, not with atoms.

Idea 3 · Weak and strong emergence

Weak emergence: the pattern is surprising but could in principle be derived from the parts and the rules (flocks, traffic jams). Strong emergence: a property that could not be derived even in principle. Whether anything is strongly emergent is disputed; consciousness is the most often cited candidate.

Is the whole more than the sum of its parts?

Everyone accepts that wholes are made of parts. The dispute is over how much the parts alone explain. Three views are on the table:

PositionSupporters argue…Critics counter…
A · Strong reductionismEverything is in principle explained by fundamental physics; higher-level sciences are shorthand.Explaining something "in principle" tells us little in practice, and higher-level concepts do real explanatory work that particle talk cannot replace.
B · Weak emergenceLarger-scale patterns are derivable from the parts but often unpredictable without simulation, so they need their own descriptions.It may be a statement about our limits rather than about the world.
C · Strong emergenceSome properties, perhaps consciousness, are genuinely new and cannot be derived from the parts even in principle.It is hard to see how new properties could arise without being derivable, and no clear case has been demonstrated.

Precision toolkit: distinguishing levels and pressing a claim

Distinguishing levels
· At the level of …
· In principle …; in practice …
· At the scale of individual …
· Taken together, …
Pressing a claim
· That depends on what is meant by …
· What exactly is meant by "emergent"?
· It doesn't follow that …
· That begs the question of …
Rewrite it: rewrite each overstated claim so that it is accurate and carefully hedged. Attempt first, then compare.

1. "A flock is just a lot of birds, so there is nothing more to explain."

2. "Consciousness emerges from neurons."

3. "Complex systems cannot be understood because they are too complicated."

1. A flock is made of nothing but birds, but its shape and movement are patterns that are best explained at the level of the flock, not of a single bird.
2. Many researchers hold that consciousness depends on the activity of neurons, but whether it is explained by them in the way a flock is explained by birds remains disputed.
3. Complex systems can often be understood by finding the simple rules and the right level of description, even when predicting every detail is impractical.
