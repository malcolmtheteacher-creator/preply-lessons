At the Vet

He's off his food, off-colour, and looking at you like you'll know what to do. You don't — but the vet might, if you can explain it right.

The Scene 🎬

Biscuit is a soft-eared, permanently hopeful spaniel who has never once refused a meal in his life. So when his breakfast sat untouched this morning — and again at lunch — you knew something was wrong. He's flat, a little shivery, and he flinched when you touched his side.

You rang the surgery and, to your relief, they said they could fit you in. Now you're in the waiting room with a nervous dog on your lap and a head full of worst-case scenarios you found on the internet at two in the morning. The trouble is, Biscuit can't say what hurts. That job falls to you — and how clearly you describe it may decide how quickly he gets better.

Across the room, the vet calls your name. Calm, kind, quietly thorough — the sort of person who has reassured a thousand frightened owners, and knows the fear is often worse than the diagnosis.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Have you ever had to explain a problem you didn't fully understand — a pet, a child, a car, your own body — to an expert? How did you find the words?
  2. When something you love is unwell and you can't fix it yourself, what does that feeling do to you? Do you go calm and practical, or straight to the worst case?

Who's who

You
The Worried Owner 🐾

Anxious but trying to keep it together. You need to describe what's wrong, answer the vet's questions honestly, understand the advice and the cost — and then decide.

The other voice
The Vet 🩺

Unhurried, gentle, precise. Asks the questions that matter, explains without jargon, and is honest about what things cost — but the final call is yours.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how the owner describes symptoms precisely, hedges when they're not sure, and raises the subject of cost without a flicker of awkwardness.

VetCome on through. So this is Biscuit — hello, trouble. What's brought you both in today?
YouThanks for squeezing us in. I'm a bit worried about him, to be honest — he's not himself at all. He's gone right off his food, which for Biscuit is unheard of.
VetThat's always the first thing we notice, isn't it. When did it start?
YouIt came on quite suddenly — he was completely fine yesterday morning, and by the evening he'd gone all quiet and flat. He was sick once overnight, too.
VetRight. And has he had anything he shouldn't — food off the side, something on a walk, a chewed-up toy?
YouNot that I've noticed, no — but I couldn't swear to it. He's a scavenger on walks, and now you mention it, he did have his nose in a hedge yesterday for a good while.
VetGood to know. Let's have a feel. … He's a touch tender through the tummy here — did you see that? — and he's a little dehydrated, but nothing that's ringing alarm bells yet.
YouThat's a relief to hear. So just to make sure I've got this right — you don't think it's anything serious?
VetMost likely it's an upset stomach — something he ate that disagreed with him. But I can't rule out a blockage without a proper look, and given the tenderness, I'd rather be safe. I'd suggest a blood test today, and possibly an X-ray.
YouWhatever he needs. Although — and I hope this isn't a rude question — could you give me a rough idea of what I'm looking at, cost-wise? I'd just rather know than get a shock at the desk.
VetNot rude at all — always ask. The bloods are around ninety pounds; if we go on to the X-ray, you're looking at another hundred and forty or so on top. I'd start with the bloods and only do the X-ray if they point us that way.
YouThat makes sense. Is the X-ray something we could hold off on for now, then — see what the bloods say first?
VetAbsolutely. Nine times out of ten the bloods tell us all we need, and he's spared the sedation. If anything looks off, we'll talk before we do a thing.
YouThen let's go ahead with the blood test and take it from there. And be honest with me — is there anything I should be watching for tonight?
VetSmall meals of something bland, plenty of water, and keep an eye on him. If he's sick more than twice, or goes really listless, ring the out-of-hours line straight away — don't sit on it. Otherwise, I suspect he'll be raiding the bin again by the weekend.
YouThat's put my mind at rest — thank you. Come on, Biscuit, let's get you sorted.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The owner first says the dog hasn't eaten anything he shouldn't — then changes course. What does the vet's question make them remember, and why is that little correction so useful to the vet?

2. The owner leaves having agreed to ONE test but not another, even though the vet offered both. Which did they choose, which did they postpone, and what was the reasoning that made everyone comfortable?

3. Twice the owner apologises for something before saying it. What are the two moments — and what does that little habit reveal about how they're feeling in the room?

1. Pressed on whether Biscuit ate anything he shouldn't, the owner first says "not that I've noticed" — then recalls him with his nose deep in a hedge on yesterday's walk. That detail matters enormously: it hands the vet a plausible cause (he may have eaten something out there) and steers what they check for. Being honest about what you're not sure of is often more useful than a confident wrong answer.
2. They agreed to the blood test now and postponed the X-ray, on the vet's own advice: start with the bloods, which usually give the answer, and only X-ray if the results point that way — sparing Biscuit the sedation and the owner the extra cost unless it's genuinely needed.
3. They soften the money question ("I hope this isn't a rude question") and the request for the full picture ("be honest with me"). Both little run-ups show an anxious owner who doesn't want to seem difficult — cushioning the questions that feel awkward before actually asking them.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the day you have to explain a problem you don't fully understand — at the vet, the doctor's, the garage, anywhere. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚪 Getting in and getting started

Phrase 1

You rang first thing, genuinely worried, and want an appointment today — but you know you're asking them to bend a full diary. Which way of asking makes it easiest for them to say yes?

A. "I need an appointment today."
B. "Have you got anything today or not?"
C. "I don't suppose you could squeeze him in this morning?"
C — "I don't suppose you could squeeze him in this morning?"
"I don't suppose…" politely expects a no, so it's painless to give one — and "squeeze him in" acknowledges you're asking for a favour on a busy day. Far more likely to get a yes than a flat demand.
Phrase 2

The whole visit starts with one idea: your normally lively pet is behaving unlike himself. There's a four-word idiom that says exactly that, without naming a single symptom: "He's ___ ___ ___ ___."

"He's not himself at all."
The perfect opener when you can't yet point to one thing — it tells the expert "something is off, trust me". Human cousins: "I've not felt myself lately", "She's not been herself since the move".

🔎 Describing what's wrong

Phrase 3

The single most useful symptom you can report is that he's stopped eating. Don't say "he doesn't eat" — use the idiom a vet hears every day. Put the pieces in order:

right offhe'shis foodgone
"He's gone right off his food."
"To go off something" = to lose your appetite or liking for it; "right off" makes it total. Works for people too: "I've gone right off coffee lately." Precise, idiomatic, instantly understood.
Phrase 4

The vet's first question is always when and how fast. Your pet was fine yesterday and unwell by the evening. What natural phrase captures that quick, out-of-nowhere onset — "It ___ ___ quite suddenly"?

"It came on quite suddenly."
"To come on" is the verb for a symptom or illness starting: "the pain came on after lunch", "a cold coming on". Pair it with a timeline — "fine yesterday, flat by the evening" — and you've given the expert gold.

❓ Answering when you're not sure

Phrase 5

The vet asks whether he ate anything he shouldn't. You honestly don't know — you weren't watching every second. Which answer is the most useful to an expert?

A. "No."
B. "Definitely not, I'd have seen it."
C. "Not that I've noticed — but I couldn't swear to it."
C — "Not that I've noticed — but I couldn't swear to it."
A flat "no" you can't back up may send the vet down the wrong path. "Not that I've noticed" answers honestly; "I couldn't swear to it" flags the doubt. Honest uncertainty beats confident guessing every time.
Phrase 6

A question jogs a memory you'd forgotten — the hedge he sniffed for ages yesterday. There's a three-word phrase that flags "your question just reminded me of something". Reorder it:

mention ityounow
"Now you mention it…"
The phrase for a detail that surfaces only because they asked — "now you mention it, he did seem a bit off yesterday." It signals a genuine, useful recollection rather than a rehearsed answer.

💷 Understanding advice & cost

Phrase 7

The vet has said a lot and you want to check one crucial thing before you relax: that it isn't serious. Which phrase confirms your understanding without putting words in their mouth?

A. "So it's nothing serious then."
B. "So just to make sure I've got this right — you don't think it's anything serious?"
C. "It's fine, right?"
B — "So just to make sure I've got this right — you don't think it's anything serious?"
A and C tell the vet the answer; B invites them to confirm or correct you. "Just to make sure I've got this right…" is the polite, everywhere-useful way to check you've understood an expert.
Phrase 8

You need to ask about money — never comfortable — but you'd rather know the figure now than be startled at the desk. Complete the tactful version: "Could you give me a ___ ___ of what I'm looking at, cost-wise?"

"Could you give me a rough idea of what I'm looking at, cost-wise?"
"A rough idea" asks for an estimate, not a binding quote — easy to give. "What I'm looking at" and "cost-wise" are relaxed, grown-up ways to raise money without embarrassment. Prefix "I hope this isn't a rude question" and it's effortless.

✅ Deciding & leaving

Phrase 9

You want the cheaper test now and to delay the expensive one until you see the results — but you want to check that's sensible, not reckless. There's a phrasal verb meaning "delay / not do just yet": "Is the X-ray something we could ___ ___ on for now?"

"Is the X-ray something we could hold off on for now?"
"To hold off on something" = to postpone it, wait before committing. Framing it as a question ("could we…?") lets the expert say "yes, sensibly" or "no, and here's why" — you decide together.
Phrase 10

You've made your decision: do the blood test, then react to whatever it shows. Which line commits to a first step while leaving the next one open — the sort a calm, sensible owner says?

A. "Just do everything."
B. "Let's go ahead with the blood test and take it from there."
C. "I don't know, you decide."
B — "Let's go ahead with the blood test and take it from there."
"Go ahead with" commits cleanly to one action; "take it from there" means decide the next move once you know more. Together they're the sound of someone staying calm and in control of a worrying situation.
Quick test: close your eyes and walk the whole visit out loud using all ten, in order — get seen → "he's not himself" → describe the symptoms → answer honestly, even the "I'm not sure" → check you've understood → ask the cost → decide one step at a time. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

🌩️ The number you weren't ready for

One more round — no script, no safety net. This time the news is harder. The vet suspects Biscuit really has swallowed something, and to be sure they need the X-ray, the sedation, and possibly a small operation — and the estimate is far more than you can comfortably afford this month. Start the timer and talk it through: ask exactly what's essential versus what can wait, whether there's a payment plan, a cheaper first step, or a way to hold off safely for a day. Be honest that money is tight without giving up on your dog. And on the other side of the desk — how does a good vet deliver a frightening price with kindness, and help an owner find a way through? One rule: nobody in the room is allowed to pretend the money doesn't matter.

The Echo 🕰️

In Kipling's tale, a flood washes a half-drowned young mongoose into an English family's garden in India. They dry him by the fire, feed him, and half-adopt him — and Rikki-Tikki-Tavi repays them by fighting two cobras to the death to keep the household safe.

It's a strange, old bargain, and you're both ends of it today. We take a small wild-ish animal into our homes, feed it, name it, love it — and somewhere along the way it stops being "an animal" and becomes family. Which is exactly why, when Biscuit won't eat, you drop everything and carry him to the vet.

Rikki guarded his family with his life. The least we can do is guard our animals with ninety pounds and an afternoon off.
