At the Vet

He's off his food, off-colour, and looking at you like you'll know what to do. You don't — but the vet might, if you can explain it right.

The Scene 🎬

Biscuit is a soft-eared, permanently hopeful spaniel who has never once refused a meal in his life. So when his breakfast sat untouched this morning — and again at lunch — you knew something was wrong. He's flat, a little shivery, and he flinched when you touched his side.

You rang the surgery and, to your relief, they said they could fit you in. Now you're in the waiting room with a nervous dog on your lap and a head full of worst-case scenarios you found on the internet at two in the morning. The trouble is, Biscuit can't say what hurts. That job falls to you — and how clearly you describe it may decide how quickly he gets better.

Across the room, the vet calls your name. Calm, kind, quietly thorough — the sort of person who has reassured a thousand frightened owners, and knows the fear is often worse than the diagnosis.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Have you ever had to explain a problem you didn't fully understand — a pet, a child, a car, your own body — to an expert? How did you find the words?
  2. When something you love is unwell and you can't fix it yourself, what does that feeling do to you? Do you go calm and practical, or straight to the worst case?

Who's who

You
The Worried Owner 🐾

Anxious but trying to keep it together. You need to describe what's wrong, answer the vet's questions honestly, understand the advice and the cost — and then decide.

The other voice
The Vet 🩺

Unhurried, gentle, precise. Asks the questions that matter, explains without jargon, and is honest about what things cost — but the final call is yours.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Listen for how the owner describes symptoms precisely, hedges when they're not sure, and raises the subject of cost without a flicker of awkwardness.

VetCome on through. So this is Biscuit — hello, trouble. What's brought you both in today?
YouThanks for squeezing us in. I'm a bit worried about him, to be honest — he's not himself at all. He's gone right off his food, which for Biscuit is unheard of.
VetThat's always the first thing we notice, isn't it. When did it start?
YouIt came on quite suddenly — he was completely fine yesterday morning, and by the evening he'd gone all quiet and flat. He was sick once overnight, too.
VetRight. And has he had anything he shouldn't — food off the side, something on a walk, a chewed-up toy?
YouNot that I've noticed, no — but I couldn't swear to it. He's a scavenger on walks, and now you mention it, he did have his nose in a hedge yesterday for a good while.
VetGood to know. Let's have a feel. … He's a touch tender through the tummy here — did you see that? — and he's a little dehydrated, but nothing that's ringing alarm bells yet.
YouThat's a relief to hear. So just to make sure I've got this right — you don't think it's anything serious?
VetMost likely it's an upset stomach — something he ate that disagreed with him. But I can't rule out a blockage without a proper look, and given the tenderness, I'd rather be safe. I'd suggest a blood test today, and possibly an X-ray.
YouWhatever he needs. Although — and I hope this isn't a rude question — could you give me a rough idea of what I'm looking at, cost-wise? I'd just rather know than get a shock at the desk.
VetNot rude at all — always ask. The bloods are around ninety pounds; if we go on to the X-ray, you're looking at another hundred and forty or so on top. I'd start with the bloods and only do the X-ray if they point us that way.
YouThat makes sense. Is the X-ray something we could hold off on for now, then — see what the bloods say first?
VetAbsolutely. Nine times out of ten the bloods tell us all we need, and he's spared the sedation. If anything looks off, we'll talk before we do a thing.
YouThen let's go ahead with the blood test and take it from there. And be honest with me — is there anything I should be watching for tonight?
VetSmall meals of something bland, plenty of water, and keep an eye on him. If he's sick more than twice, or goes really listless, ring the out-of-hours line straight away — don't sit on it. Otherwise, I suspect he'll be raiding the bin again by the weekend.
YouThat's put my mind at rest — thank you. Come on, Biscuit, let's get you sorted.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The owner first says the dog hasn't eaten anything he shouldn't — then changes course. What does the vet's question make them remember, and why is that little correction so useful to the vet?

2. The owner leaves having agreed to ONE test but not another, even though the vet offered both. Which did they choose, which did they postpone, and what was the reasoning that made everyone comfortable?

3. Twice the owner apologises for something before saying it. What are the two moments — and what does that little habit reveal about how they're feeling in the room?

1. Pressed on whether Biscuit ate anything he shouldn't, the owner first says "not that I've noticed" — then recalls him with his nose deep in a hedge on yesterday's walk. That detail matters enormously: it hands the vet a plausible cause (he may have eaten something out there) and steers what they check for. Being honest about what you're not sure of is often more useful than a confident wrong answer.
2. They agreed to the blood test now and postponed the X-ray, on the vet's own advice: start with the bloods, which usually give the answer, and only X-ray if the results point that way — sparing Biscuit the sedation and the owner the extra cost unless it's genuinely needed.
3. They soften the money question ("I hope this isn't a rude question") and the request for the full picture ("be honest with me"). Both little run-ups show an anxious owner who doesn't want to seem difficult — cushioning the questions that feel awkward before actually asking them.

The Echo 🕰️

In Kipling's tale, a flood washes a half-drowned young mongoose into an English family's garden in India. They dry him by the fire, feed him, and half-adopt him — and Rikki-Tikki-Tavi repays them by fighting two cobras to the death to keep the household safe.

It's a strange, old bargain, and you're both ends of it today. We take a small wild-ish animal into our homes, feed it, name it, love it — and somewhere along the way it stops being "an animal" and becomes family. Which is exactly why, when Biscuit won't eat, you drop everything and carry him to the vet.

Rikki guarded his family with his life. The least we can do is guard our animals with ninety pounds and an afternoon off.

Two bigger questions

  1. Rikki-Tikki-Tavi risks everything for a family that took him in only weeks before. What do we actually owe the animals we bring into our lives — and do they owe us anything back?
  2. People will spend a fortune, and sit up all night, for a pet that can't say a word of thanks. What is it about caring for a creature that depends on us so completely? What does it give us in return?
