The Big Short

A handful of outsiders read the paperwork nobody else bothered to read, worked out that the world's biggest housing market was built on loans that could never be repaid — and bet everything on the collapse. It happened.

Today: you'll tell the story of The Big Short — a few strange, awkward outsiders who saw a catastrophe coming and could not make anyone listen — discover how much of it really happened and how much the film admits it invented, and master the language English uses for quantity and approximation: roughly, in the region of, upwards of, just under, a fraction of, the best part of, give or take — and when each one belongs in speech and when it belongs in writing.
Before we begin — talk it through: Have you ever been sure that something everyone else believed was simply wrong? What happened when you said so? And when a whole industry, or a whole country, makes the same mistake at the same time — whose fault is that?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. After eight months without wages he stopped paying the monthly instalments on his car, and once he had defaulted, the finance company sent two men to take the car away.
to default (v) — to fail to make the payments you have legally promised to make on a debt. The noun is a default.
2. The plan held together beautifully for a year. Then one supplier missed one delivery, a thread was pulled, and within six weeks the whole arrangement had unravelled in front of them.
to unravel (v) — to come apart thread by thread; to fall to pieces gradually once one part gives way.
3. The contract ran to ninety pages of dense legal language, and afterwards even the lawyers who had signed it admitted that they had found it entirely opaque.
opaque (adj) — impossible to see into or understand; deliberately or accidentally unclear. The opposite is transparent.
4. Ten straight years without a single accident bred a dangerous complacency in the crew: they stopped checking the equipment, and nobody could imagine anything going wrong.
complacency (n) — a comfortable, unearned satisfaction with how things are, which stops you noticing danger. The adjective is complacent.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. reckless is closest in meaning to: (a) cautious   (b) dangerously careless   (c) generous
(b) dangerously careless — reckless (adj) means acting without any thought for the risk you are creating, for yourself or for other people.
6. a contrarian is closest in meaning to: (a) someone who follows the crowd   (b) someone who deliberately bets against the crowd   (c) someone who refuses to have an opinion
(b) — a contrarian (n) is a person who takes the opposite position to the majority on purpose, usually because they think the majority is wrong. Also used as an adjective: a contrarian view.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. For six years her colleagues laughed at her theory; when the results finally came in, she was completely vindicated.
To vindicate someone is: (a) to prove them right after a long period of doubt; (b) to pay them what they are owed; (c) to quietly replace them.
(a) — to vindicate (v) — to show that someone was right, or free them from blame, after they have been doubted or accused. The noun is vindication.
8. The guards helped themselves to whatever they wanted from the storeroom with total impunity: nobody was ever fined, charged or even questioned.
To act with impunity is: (a) to act very quickly; (b) to act without suffering any punishment or consequence; (c) to act politely.
(b) — impunity (n) — freedom from punishment or from the normal consequences of what you have done. Almost always in the phrase with impunity.

D. Odd one out & collocation

9. Three of these four describe a person who doubts a claim and wants to see the proof. Which is the odd one out?
(a) sceptical   (b) doubtful   (c) gullible   (d) unconvinced
(c) gullible is the odd one out — a gullible (adj) person believes anything they are told. Sceptical (adj) means unwilling to believe a claim until you have seen the evidence; the noun is scepticism.
10. Which word completes this naturally? The bank had taken on far too much ______: for every pound it actually owned, it had borrowed roughly thirty more, so a small fall in prices was enough to wipe it out.
(a) leverage   (b) advantage   (c) coverage
(a) leverage (n) — the use of borrowed money to make a much larger bet than your own money would allow. It multiplies your gains and your losses equally. The verb is to leverage.

The film, in three parts

Adam McKay's The Big Short (2015). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — The man who actually read the documents

In 2005, a fund manager in California called Michael Burry does something nobody else in finance appears to have bothered doing: he sits down with the loan documents behind America's mortgage bonds and reads them, line by line, thousands of pages of them. A former doctor, barefoot in his office with heavy-metal music playing, he is not a natural salesman and does not pretend to be. What he finds is simple. Enormous numbers of the home loans packed inside these supposedly safe bonds have been made to people who cannot afford them, on cheap introductory rates that are about to jump. When the rates jump, the borrowers will default in waves.

So he asks the great banks of Wall Street for something that does not yet exist: a way to bet against the American housing market. They are delighted. They design the contracts for him and take his money, and behind his back they laugh at him — the housing market has never fallen nationally, so as far as they are concerned he has volunteered to pay them a fee every month for nothing.

The trouble is that he must keep paying. Month after month, the loans go bad exactly as he predicted and the bonds do not move, and his own investors watch their money drain away on a bet against the impossible. They are furious. They want out. Burry, certain and completely alone, refuses to let them go.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Burry discover in the loan documents, and what does he ask the banks to sell him?

2. Why do the banks think he is a fool, and why do his own investors turn on him?

1. That huge numbers of the loans inside "safe" mortgage bonds were made to people who cannot repay them once the cheap introductory rate ends. He asks the banks to create a contract that pays out if those bonds fail.

2. Nobody believes American house prices can fall nationally, so the banks think they are collecting a fee for nothing. Meanwhile Burry has to pay that fee every month while nothing visibly happens, and his investors see only losses.

Discuss:
  • Burry is right and everyone else is wrong — and it very nearly destroys him. Is being right too early any different from being wrong? How long would you hold a position that everybody you respect thinks is madness?

Part 2 — Following the smell

Word of the strange trade spreads. A slick bond salesman, Jared Vennett, realises there is a fortune to be made not in making the bet but in selling it to other people, and a wrong number puts his pitch in front of Mark Baum, a permanently furious fund manager who runs a small team inside a large bank and who despises the industry he works in.

Baum's team go and look. They drive through half-empty new housing estates in Florida where the gardens have gone to weeds; they find a tenant who has no idea her landlord has stopped paying; they sit in a bar with two mortgage brokers who cheerfully boast about lending to people with no income, no job and no documents, because nobody ever asks. At an industry conference in Las Vegas, Baum has dinner with a man who manages the products built out of the very worst of these bonds, and who explains — pleasantly, without embarrassment — that when the good bonds run out he simply repackages the bad ones, and then creates fresh side-bets on top of them, so that the same weak loans can be sold again and again to people who will never see them.

Baum works out, sitting at that table, that the fraud is not hidden in the machine. The fraud is the machine. Elsewhere, two young investors running a tiny fund out of a garage stumble on the same idea, and with help from a retired banker who long ago walked away in disgust, they buy the same protection on the bonds everyone considers safest — the cheapest bet of all, and the one that pays most if the whole thing goes.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What do Baum's team find when they go and inspect the housing market in person?

2. What does the man in Las Vegas explain that he does with the worst bonds?

1. Empty and abandoned new estates, tenants whose landlords have already stopped paying, and mortgage brokers openly boasting that they lend to people with no income, no job and no documents.

2. He repackages them into new products that are then rated as safe, and builds further side-bets on top of them, so the same weak loans are effectively sold over and over again.

Discuss:
  • Everyone in this part is being paid to look away. If your salary depends on not noticing something, can you honestly be blamed for not noticing it? Where does carelessness end and dishonesty begin?

Part 3 — Being right, and hating it

Through 2007 the loans fail on schedule — and still the bonds hold their value. This is the part that breaks the characters. The market is not simply mistaken; it is being held up. Ratings are not cut, prices are not marked down, banks quietly sell what they can before admitting what they own. Burry, under siege from his own investors, locks the fund and waits. Baum, watching the machine refuse to die, becomes convinced the whole system will be allowed to collapse before anyone tells the truth about it.

Then it goes. Ratings tumble, the price of the protection explodes, and a new fear arrives with the profits: the banks that sold these bets are the same banks that now have to pay them, and some of them may not survive long enough to do it. In September 2008 one of the oldest firms on Wall Street files for bankruptcy, and the young investors walk through its emptying offices watching people carry boxes to the lifts.

The film refuses to let anyone enjoy the win. When the two youngest men celebrate, the retired banker turns on them: what they are cheering, he says, is millions of ordinary people losing homes, savings and jobs. Baum, sickened, holds his position until the last possible moment and then sells without a word of triumph. The closing captions are colder still. The banks blamed immigrants and poor people. Almost nobody went to prison. The taxpayer paid. And within a few years the same instruments were quietly being sold again under a friendlier name.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is 2007 so agonising for the characters even though they are right?

2. How does the film treat the moment when they finally win?

1. The loans fail exactly as predicted, but the bonds keep their value because ratings are not cut and banks sell quietly before admitting what they hold — so the characters are right and still losing money.

2. As a defeat. The retired banker reminds the young investors that their profit is measured in other people's lost homes and jobs, and Baum sells in silence rather than celebrating.

Discuss:
  • These men made fortunes out of a disaster they warned about and could not stop. Are they whistleblowers, or vultures, or simply the only people in the room who did their homework?

Grammar — quantity & approximation

This is a film built out of numbers, and almost none of them are exact. English has a whole toolkit for standing next to a figure without committing to it — and, crucially, the tools are not interchangeable. Some belong in a bank's annual report; some belong in a bar. Getting that wrong is one of the clearest markers of a non-native speaker.

Where they sit in the sentence:
• before the number — roughly, approximately, around, about, nearly, just under, just over, well over, upwards of: "The fund put roughly two thirds of its money into the bet." "The recession lasted just over eighteen months."
• before a noun phrase — in the region of, somewhere in the order of, in excess of: "The bank went down with debts in the region of six hundred billion dollars." "The agencies were approving somewhere in the order of a thousand new bonds a week."
• after the number — give or take: "He waited two years, give or take." "Thirty brokers in that room, give or take a few."
What each one actually means — they are not synonyms:
• roughly / approximately / around / about = could be a little more or a little less.
• just under = slightly less. just over = slightly more. Both mean very close.
• well over / upwards of = more, and possibly a great deal more. Never less.
• in the region of / somewhere in the order of = a deliberately loose ballpark, normally for large or hard-to-pin-down figures.
• a fraction of = a very small share of something else: "They bought it for a fraction of what it later cost." (Not a number on its own.)
• the best part of = almost the whole of a period or an amount: "It took him the best part of two years."
Register — and this is the real lesson:
• Spoken and relaxed: around, roughly, about, give or take, the best part of, or so. "It cost around ten grand, give or take."
• Written and formal — reports, journalism, finance, academic work: approximately, in the region of, in the order of, in excess of, upwards of, just under, well over. "Losses were in the region of ten billion dollars."
• Mixing them the wrong way sounds odd in both directions: "Approximately ten quid, give or take" is a strange sentence, and so is "The company lost about loads of money" in an annual report.

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

Nearly all of it. The Big Short is adapted from Michael Lewis's 2010 book The Big Short: Inside the Doomsday Machine, and it won the Academy Award for Best Adapted Screenplay. The people are real, the instruments are real, and the numbers underneath are, if anything, worse than the film has time to show.

🏦 What a mortgage bond actually is — in plain language

A bank lends you money to buy a house: a mortgage. The bank does not want to wait thirty years, so it sells your loan on. Thousands of loans are tipped into one pool, and slices of that pool are sold to investors as a mortgage-backed security — a bond that pays out from your monthly payments. The top slices get paid first and are called safe; the bottom slices are paid last and carry the risk. When nobody wanted the bottom slices any more, banks poured them into a second pool and sold slices of that: a CDO. A synthetic CDO went one step further — it contained no loans at all, only side-bets on loans, so the same weak mortgages could be gambled on many times over. And a credit default swap was insurance on a bond you did not have to own. That last one is what Michael Burry went shopping for in 2005.

The loans got worse because the machine needed feeding

The pool needs mortgages. Once every reliable borrower already had one, the only way to keep the machine running was to lend to people who could not repay. Subprime lending grew from around $65 billion in 1995 to something in the region of $173 billion by 2001, and kept climbing. Between 2004 and 2006 the share of the American mortgage market taken by exotic products — interest-only loans, option adjustable-rate mortgages, forty-year balloon loans — rose from roughly 7 per cent to about 29 per cent.

These loans were designed to look affordable for two years and then reset upwards. That reset date is the entire thesis of the film. Burry did not predict a mood or a panic; he read the paperwork, found the dates, and worked out when the payments would jump. The film's version of him reading thousands of pages of prospectuses is not a flourish — it is the point. Almost nobody else did it.

The seal of approval: how weak loans became AAA

None of this could have been sold without a top credit rating, and the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission — the official US investigation — was blunt about who supplied it. The three big rating agencies, it concluded, were "key enablers of the financial meltdown" and "essential cogs in the wheel of financial destruction"; the securities "could not have been marketed and sold without their seal of approval."

The scale is hard to take in. Moody's alone rated nearly 45,000 mortgage-related securities as triple-A between 2000 and 2007. In 2006 it was stamping that grade on roughly 1,500 securities every working day — and 88 per cent of the mortgage securities it rated triple-A that year were later downgraded. The conflict of interest was structural: the agencies were paid by the banks whose products they were grading, and a bank that disliked its rating could walk down the street to a competitor. Years later, S&P paid about $1.375 billion in a February 2015 settlement without being required to admit criminal wrongdoing, and in January 2017 Moody's paid nearly $864 million to the US Justice Department and a group of states.

The people: Burry, Eisman, and the cost of being early

Michael Burry is the only major character whose real name the film keeps. A doctor who left medicine for investing, he founded Scion Capital, turned his attention to the subprime market in 2005, and bought credit default swaps against mortgage securities from firms including Goldman Sachs. The investor pressure in the film is real: he faced an investor revolt from clients who were convinced he was wrong and demanded their capital back. He was eventually vindicated — a personal profit of about $100 million, and more than $700 million for the investors who stayed. He shut Scion Capital down in 2008 anyway. In April 2010 he wrote a piece in The New York Times under the title "I Saw the Crisis Coming. Why Didn't the Fed?"

Steve Carell's Mark Baum is Steve Eisman, who ran a fund at FrontPoint Partners, a Morgan Stanley subsidiary, and bet heavily against subprime mortgage products. Ryan Gosling's Jared Vennett is based on the Deutsche Bank salesman Greg Lippmann; Brad Pitt's Ben Rickert on the retired trader Ben Hockett; and the two young men on Charlie Ledley and Jamie Mai of Cornwall Capital, who really did start from almost nothing.

September 2008, and the bill

On 15 September 2008, Lehman Brothers filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy with over $600 billion in assets — still the largest bankruptcy filing in United States history. The Dow Jones closed down just over 500 points that day, a fall of 4.4 per cent.

Then came the part the winners were cheering. The recession ran from December 2007 to June 2009 — eighteen months, the deepest downturn since the Second World War. US GDP fell 4.3 per cent. Unemployment more than doubled, from under 5 per cent to 10 per cent. Between the start of the recession and the employment low point in February 2010, the American labour market lost 8.7 million jobs. House prices fell by more than a fifth nationally between early 2007 and mid-2011. CoreLogic counted approximately 7.8 million completed foreclosures between the beginning of 2007 and the end of 2016, and around 8.6 million homes lost to foreclosure since home ownership peaked in 2004. The Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas put the total cost at somewhere between $6 trillion and $14 trillion — the equivalent of 40 to 90 per cent of a full year's national output, or $50,000 to $120,000 for every American household.

And the accountability? The banks paid: Bank of America alone agreed to $16.65 billion with the Justice Department in August 2014. The individuals did not. Kareem Serageldin, a former Credit Suisse managing director sentenced in November 2013 to thirty months for inflating the value of mortgage-backed bonds, is the only banker in the United States who went to prison as a result of the crisis. The Commission's own verdict on all of it was unambiguous: "We conclude this financial crisis was avoidable… the result of human action and inaction, not of Mother Nature or computer models gone haywire."

Where the film admits it is lying

This is the unusual, honest thing about The Big Short: it tells you. It stops the story and hands the explanation to a celebrity — Margot Robbie in a bath explaining mortgage-backed securities, Anthony Bourdain using three-day-old fish in a stew to explain a CDO, Selena Gomez at a blackjack table with the economist Richard Thaler explaining synthetic CDOs — precisely because it knows you would otherwise stop listening. Then characters turn to the camera and grade their own scenes. One admits that the young investors did not really find the pitch in a bank lobby: they say so, and add that the invented version is more fun. Another confirms that a particular wrong-number phone call genuinely happened.

Some changes it does not flag, and they are worth knowing. Almost every name was changed; only Burry's is real. The suicide of Mark Baum's brother stands in for a real bereavement in Steve Eisman's life — the death of his infant son — and the substitution was made at the family's request. Timelines are compressed, and the danger to Morgan Stanley is dramatised harder than the record supports. The closing captions, though, are accurate in spirit and in substance: within a few years banks were selling a product called a "bespoke tranche opportunity", which is a CDO with a new name on the box.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, which part sounded most invented to you — and which of those turns out to be documented fact?
  • The rating agencies were paid by the people whose products they graded. Is that a scandal, or simply an obviously broken design that everyone accepted because it worked for them?
  • One banker went to prison; the banks paid billions in fines. Is a fine a punishment if the company pays it and the people who decided keep their money?
  • Does a film have to break its own story to explain a CDO — or is that a confession that the system had become deliberately opaque?
  • Now compare the two great crashes. In 1929 the market broke on Black Thursday, 24 October, when sixteen million shares were dumped in a day; by 1933 unemployment in America had reached 24.9 per cent, the banking system had collapsed, and there was no deposit insurance to catch anyone — which is why the 1933 Banking Act created the FDIC and split ordinary banks from investment banks. That split was repealed in 1999. In 2008 unemployment peaked at 10 per cent, deposits were insured, and governments bailed the banks out. What was the same in 1929 and 2008 — and what had genuinely changed? Did the lessons of the first crash prevent the second, soften it, or quietly get repealed before it arrived?
