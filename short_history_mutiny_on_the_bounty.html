Mutiny on the Bounty

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A ship, a captain, and a paradise

In 1787, a small British naval ship, HMS Bounty, sailed from England on an unusual mission. Its task was botanical, not military: to collect breadfruit plants from Tahiti and carry them to the Caribbean, where the government hoped this cheap, filling fruit would feed enslaved people on the sugar plantations. In command was Lieutenant William Bligh — an outstanding navigator who had once sailed with the great Captain Cook. His second-in-command, and his friend, was a young officer named Fletcher Christian.

The voyage out was long and gruelling. Bligh tried and failed to round Cape Horn in furious storms, and had to turn the whole ship around and cross the Atlantic the other way, adding months at sea. He was a demanding commander with a sharp tongue, and by the time the Bounty finally reached Tahiti, tension had been building on board for a long time.

Then came Tahiti itself. The crew spent almost five months on the island, gathering and potting the young plants — but also living, for the first time in their hard lives, in warmth, ease and plenty. Many of the men formed close relationships with Tahitians. When Bligh at last gave the order to sail for the Caribbean, he was asking them to trade paradise for the iron discipline of a cramped ship. Few of them wanted to go.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the Bounty's mission, and who commanded the ship?

2. Why were many of the crew reluctant to leave Tahiti?

1. To collect breadfruit plants from Tahiti and carry them to the Caribbean to feed enslaved workers; it was commanded by Lieutenant William Bligh, with Fletcher Christian as his second-in-command.

2. They had spent nearly five months there in warmth and comfort, many forming close relationships with Tahitians, and leaving meant returning to hard discipline aboard a cramped ship.

Discuss:

Part 2 — The morning of the mutiny

Three weeks after leaving Tahiti, in April 1789, the mood on board had curdled. Bligh had a habit of humiliating his officers in front of the men, and he singled out Fletcher Christian in particular, accusing him of theft over a trivial matter of missing coconuts. Christian, proud and increasingly desperate, is said to have considered simply throwing himself overboard rather than endure more.

Instead, in the darkness before dawn on 28 April, he acted. With a small group of supporters, Christian seized the ship's weapons and burst into Bligh's cabin. They dragged the captain up onto the deck in his nightshirt, his hands bound, and told him the ship was theirs. It was mutiny — and under the law, every man who took part could hang for it.

What followed was strange and almost restrained. Rather than killing Bligh, the mutineers lowered the ship's small open launch and forced him into it, along with eighteen men who stayed loyal to him. They handed down a little food, some water, a compass and a few tools — then cut the boat loose. As the Bounty sailed away toward the horizon, Bligh and his crowded, sinking-low boat were left to drift in the vast emptiness of the Pacific, thousands of miles from any European settlement.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the immediate trigger for the conflict between Bligh and Christian?

2. What did the mutineers do with Bligh instead of killing him?

1. Bligh publicly humiliated his officers and accused Christian of stealing coconuts; the proud, exhausted Christian finally broke and seized the ship.

2. They forced him, with eighteen loyal men, into the ship's small open launch, gave them minimal supplies and a compass, and set them adrift in the Pacific.

Discuss:
  • The mutineers chose to set Bligh adrift rather than kill him. Was that mercy, cowardice, or something more calculated?

Part 3 — Two impossible journeys

What happened next split into two remarkable stories. Bligh, whatever his failings as a leader, was a navigator of genius. In a boat built for coastal work, dangerously overloaded and with almost no charts, he kept his men alive across roughly 6,700 kilometres of open ocean — one of the greatest feats of small-boat navigation ever recorded. After forty-seven days he reached the Dutch settlement of Timor, having lost only one man, killed in a clash with islanders along the way. Eventually he sailed home to England to tell his story and see justice done.

The mutineers, meanwhile, knew the Royal Navy would never stop hunting them. After returning to Tahiti, the group divided. Some stayed on the island, where a navy ship later found and arrested them; several were court-martialled, and three were hanged. But Fletcher Christian led a smaller band of mutineers and their Tahitian companions in search of somewhere the navy would never look. They found Pitcairn — a tiny, remote, wrongly-charted island — landed, and burned the Bounty in the bay so that no passing ship would spot it.

Hidden there, the little community descended into violence and did not last as its founders imagined; Christian himself was dead within a few years. Yet the settlement survived. Astonishingly, the world only learned of it eighteen years later, when a passing ship discovered English-speaking descendants of the mutineers still living on Pitcairn. Their families are there to this day — the strange, living echo of one dawn's decision aboard a small ship in 1789.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Bligh achieve after being set adrift, and why is it remarkable?

2. What happened to the two groups of mutineers?

1. He navigated the tiny open boat some 6,700 km to Timor in forty-seven days, losing only one man — one of the greatest feats of small-boat navigation ever recorded.

2. Those who stayed on Tahiti were caught by the navy and some were hanged; Christian's group hid on remote Pitcairn, burned the Bounty, and their descendants live there to this day.

Discuss:
  • Bligh is remembered as a tyrant, yet his open-boat voyage was heroic. Can someone be both a bad leader and a brilliant one at the same time?

Grammar — reported speech

This whole story is a chain of things people said: Bligh ordered the crew to sail, Christian told him the ship was theirs, the men refused to leave Tahiti. When we retell what someone said without quoting their exact words, we use reported speech — and the tenses, pronouns and time words usually shift back.

The basic shift (backshift):
• present → past: "The ship is mine" → he said that the ship was his.
• past → past perfect: "I stole nothing" → he said that he had stolen nothing.
• will → would: "We will hang" → they knew they would hang.
• pronouns and time/place words shift too: I → he, my → his, now → then, here → there, today → that day.
Reporting verbs carry the meaning. Choose the verb that shows how something was said, and follow its pattern:
• tell + person + that…: Christian told Bligh that the ship was theirs.
• say + (that)…: Bligh said that he would see them hanged. (no person after say)
• order / tell + person + to + verb: he ordered them to sail.
• refuse + to + verb: the men refused to leave.
• ask + person + (not) to + verb: he asked them not to cut the boat loose.
