Her Ex

She had coffee with Tom on Thursday and didn't mention it. Now you have to say something — knowing that the feeling in your chest is not one you'd choose to own up to.

The Scene 🎬

Nadia's phone was face up on the kitchen table when it buzzed, and you didn't go looking — you just read what was there, because it was there. Good to see you Thursday. Same again soon? And under it, a name you know without ever having met the man: Tom. Four years of her life, before you.

Thursday was three days ago. She told you about her sister, and the boiler man, and a woman at work who's leaving. She didn't tell you about the coffee. It probably means nothing. It has, nevertheless, sat in your chest all weekend like a swallowed stone.

So here you are on a Sunday evening, with the washing-up done and the telly off, about to say the least flattering sentence you own: I'm jealous. Not "you've done something wrong" — she hasn't, as far as you know. Just: this got to me, and I'd rather tell you than sit on it and go strange.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Should your partner be friends with people they used to be with? Where's the line for you — texting, coffee, holidays, nothing at all?
  2. Some feelings are embarrassing to admit to. Are you someone who says them out loud anyway, or someone who waits for them to pass? Which has worked out better for you?

Who's who

You
The One Who's Bothered 🪨

You know your case is thin. Nothing has happened, you've no evidence and no right to make demands. You'd still rather say it badly than swallow it and spend a month being quietly odd about Thursdays.

The other voice
Nadia, Your Partner 📱

Straight with you about everything else, which is why the silence stings. She didn't mention the coffee for reasons that are boring rather than sinister — and she's braced, because she's had this conversation before, with someone else, and it went badly.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how the speaker keeps admitting to the feeling rather than building a case out of it — no evidence, no cross-examination, no demands. Notice how often they say something unflattering about themselves before Nadia can say it for them. That's what stops it becoming a row.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for what the speaker never does: accuse, demand, or pretend to be calmer than they are.

NadiaYou've gone a bit quiet. You've had that face on since about six. Is it work, or —
YouIt's not work. Can I say something, and can you not make me feel mad for saying it?
Nadia…that is an alarming way to start a sentence. Go on.
YouYou had coffee with Tom on Thursday. And you didn't — you didn't say. And honestly, it's the not-mentioning-it that got me, more than the coffee. I don't mind the coffee. I don't think I mind the coffee.
NadiaDid I not say? I thought I'd — no. No, I didn't, did I. It wasn't a thing, though, it was forty minutes, he was in town, he's just — mm. You know what he's like. Well. You don't, actually.
YouNo, and that's — that's sort of the whole of it, isn't it. It's not that I don't trust you. It's that I don't know him. He's a name and a photo and four years I wasn't there for.
NadiaSo you want to meet him? Because that feels a bit —
YouNo. I don't know. Maybe, eventually, whatever, but no. I'm not asking you to stop seeing him. I want to be really clear about that bit, because I know how this sounds.
NadiaHow does it sound?
YouLike a bloke who's about two weeks off going through your phone. Which I haven't and I won't — it was face up when it buzzed, that's all. But I'd rather hear it from you than see it on your phone. And, look. I'm a bit jealous, and I'm not proud of it. There. That's it, that's the whole thing, and it sounds worse out loud than it did in my head.
NadiaOh. God. Right. No, that's — okay. That one's fair. You've got nothing to be jealous of, though.
YouProbably not. Can I ask you one thing straight, though? What is he to you now, honestly?
NadiaHe's someone I was with for four years who now sends me photographs of his dog. That's genuinely it. I didn't say anything because I knew it'd turn into a conversation, and I was tired, and then it'd been three days, and saying it late looked worse than — sorry, what was I — yeah. Worse than not saying it at all. Which I realise is exactly how you end up here.
YouIf it were the other way round, would you be fine with it?
Nadia…no. God, no. I'd have been unbearable about it. I'd have looked her up on my lunch break. Right, so — what do you want me to do?

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Nadia says something about Tom early on, half-abandons it, and then admits she's wrong. The speaker picks that scrap up and builds their whole point out of it. What did she say, and what did they turn it into?

2. Nadia eventually explains why she didn't mention the coffee. Her reason has almost nothing to do with Tom — so what is it, and why does the speaker let it go?

3. The speaker's final request comes in two halves: something they want from her, and something they promise about themselves. Why would the request not work without that second half?

1. Defending the coffee, she says "you know what he's like" — then stops, and corrects herself: "well, you don't, actually." The speaker takes that and makes it the point. The problem was never her behaviour; it's that Tom is a stranger to them and four years of her life happened somewhere they weren't. That framing lets them describe the feeling without putting her on trial for it.
2. She knew it would turn into a conversation, she was tired, and once three days had gone by, mentioning it late looked worse than staying quiet — so she stayed quiet, which is how the small thing grew. It's ordinary avoidance rather than concealment, and the speaker recognises it as such, so there's nothing left to argue about.
3. They ask her to tell them when she's seeing him, and promise not to pull a face when she does. Without the promise, honesty costs her something every time she's honest, so she'd quietly go back to saying nothing — which was the original problem. The promise is what makes the ask cheap enough to actually keep.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for admitting to an unflattering feeling without turning it into a charge sheet. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Jealousy said fast and flat sounds like an accusation; jealousy said slowly, with a shrug in your voice, sounds like a confession — and people argue with accusations while they forgive confessions. So take the speed out of it. Let yourself sound slightly embarrassed, because you are. The moment you sound like a prosecutor, they stop listening and start defending.

🚪 Opening it up

Phrase 1

You're about to say something you can't fully defend, and what you dread is the look that says you're being irrational. So you ask for two things at once: permission to speak, and mercy afterwards. Put the pieces in order:

make me feel madCan I say something,for saying it?and can you not
"Can I say something, and can you not make me feel mad for saying it?"
"Mad" here doesn't mean angry — in British English it means slightly unhinged, out of your right mind, and that's precisely the fear: not that she'll shout, but that she'll make you feel like you've lost the plot for minding. Asking for that in advance is disarming, because you've admitted your own weak position before she can point at it.
Phrase 2

You can hear yourself. What you're saying could very easily be heard as controlling, and you'd rather name that reading yourself than wait for her to name it. Which line does that job?

A. "Sorry, ignore me."
B. "I know how this sounds."
C. "You're going to think I'm being ridiculous now."
B — "I know how this sounds."
A takes the whole thing back, so nothing gets said and the feeling stays in your chest; C hands her the word "ridiculous" and more or less invites her to agree with it. B is short and it works because it proves you're still listening to yourself, which is exactly what the controlling partner never does — and once you've said the ugly reading out loud, it stops being the only one available.

🔍 Saying what it actually is

Phrase 3

She is about to hear "I'm suspicious of you", and she'll defend herself against that for an hour. Separate the two things she's confusing — what you think of her, and what you know about him. Put the pieces in order:

know him.It's not thatIt's that I don'tI don't trust you.
"It's not that I don't trust you. It's that I don't know him."
The order matters more than the words: you clear away the accusation she's braced for, and only then say what's actually true. It moves the problem off her character and onto a plain fact — there's a man in her past you've never met — which is a fact rather than a fault, so there's nothing for her to defend.
Phrase 4

The coffee itself you can live with. What stung was finding out three days late, from a screen. Which line puts the weight on the right thing?

A. "Why didn't you tell me you were meeting him?"
B. "So you're hiding things from me now."
C. "It's the not-mentioning-it that got me, more than the coffee."
C — "It's the not-mentioning-it that got me, more than the coffee."
A is a question you already know the answer to, which makes it an accusation wearing a question mark; B calls her a liar, a far bigger charge than the one you actually want to bring. C is precise about the size of the complaint — the meeting is fine, the silence isn't — and "got me" is the mild, ordinary word for something that landed harder than you expected it to.

🚧 Taking the threat out of it

Phrase 5

She's braced for a demand, because that's what this conversation usually turns into. Take the demand off the table before she starts defending her freedom. Which line does that?

A. "Do what you like, obviously."
B. "I'm not asking you to stop seeing him."
C. "I'd just prefer it if you didn't, that's all."
B — "I'm not asking you to stop seeing him."
A is permission granted through gritted teeth and she'll hear the teeth; C is the demand with a coat on, and "that's all" doesn't make it smaller. B says plainly that the thing she's about to defend is not under attack, and once she isn't defending it she has some attention left over for what you actually came to say.
Phrase 6

Now own the feeling itself, without dressing it up as concern, principle or protection. Which admission is the one that actually helps you?

A. "I'm just looking out for us."
B. "I'm not the jealous type, but this is different."
C. "I'm a bit jealous, and I'm not proud of it."
C — "I'm a bit jealous, and I'm not proud of it."
A dresses jealousy up as protection, and she'll notice the costume; B denies the feeling in the same breath as displaying it, which is the least convincing sentence in the language. C names it and then, crucially, adds that you're not proud of it — so she can hear that you already know it isn't a reasonable demand, and a feeling you're not defending is a feeling nobody has to fight.

📱 Asking for the truth

Phrase 7

You didn't snoop — the screen lit up in front of you — but you'd rather not live like that, and you want her to know why the silence is the expensive bit. Put the pieces in order:

than see ithear it from youon your phone.I'd rather
"I'd rather hear it from you than see it on your phone."
It's an offer more than a complaint: you're telling her which version of the same information you can take, and the answer is the one that comes from her mouth. It also quietly explains why you're upset without ever accusing her of hiding anything, because you're talking about your preference rather than her crime.
Phrase 8

You want one honest sentence about where he stands now — not four years of history, not reassurance. Complete the chunk: "What ___ ___ ___ you now, honestly?"

"What is he to you now, honestly?"
"Now" does the heavy lifting — you're not asking what he was, which would be pointless and cruel, only what's left of it today. And "honestly" tacked on the end isn't suspicion, it's an invitation: it tells her you'd rather have an awkward true answer than a smooth kind one.

🤝 Landing the ask

Phrase 9

She thinks you're overreacting. Rather than argue, ask her to stand where you're standing and check the view for herself. Complete the chunk: "If it were the ___ ___ ___, would you be fine with it?"

"If it were the other way round, would you be fine with it?"
"The other way round" means with the two positions swapped — her in your chair, you having the coffee. It's the safest kind of challenge, because you're not telling her she's wrong, you're handing her the question and letting her answer it, and people believe conclusions they reached themselves.
Phrase 10

You've said the difficult part and she asks what you actually want. Make it one small, concrete thing that costs her almost nothing — and make clear there's no second thing hiding behind it. In your own words, what do you ask her for?

e.g. "Just tell me when you're seeing him. That's genuinely all."
Notice how small it is. You could have asked her to stop, or to let you read the messages, or to explain herself — instead you ask for a sentence. "That's genuinely all" is the important half, because without it she'll spend the next month waiting for the real demand to arrive, and an ask she believes is finished is an ask she can actually agree to.

🔑 Make them yours

Naming an unflattering feeling out loud, plainly, without building a case around it, is a skill that reaches far past one ex and one coffee. Pin three of these to something real in your own life, so they're there when you need them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"Can I say something, and can you not make me feel mad for saying it?" — something you've been sitting on, with someone who might make you feel silly for minding. What is it?

"I'm a bit jealous, and I'm not proud of it." — a feeling you'd normally dress up as something more respectable. Say the plain version instead.

"Just tell me when you're seeing him. That's genuinely all." — turn a big vague grievance of yours into one small thing you could actually ask for.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your kitchen, your Sunday evening. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer — in a real conversation you don't get thirty seconds to polish a sentence.

Swap sides. You had the coffee, you didn't mention it, and your partner has just said the jealous thing out loud. This is the harder half: you're being told about a feeling you think is unfair, and you have to take it well anyway — without laughing at them, without promising things you won't do, and without going on the attack because you're embarrassed.

The Twist

😬 She says yes — and then keeps talking

One more round, no script. You make your small ask and Nadia agrees to it straight away, no argument, almost relieved. And then, because you've made it safe to talk, she carries on: the reason it would have turned into a conversation is that Tom's marriage has fallen apart, he's been ringing her at night for about a month, and she's been the person who picks up. Thursday wasn't the first coffee. It was the fourth. She's telling you this because you asked her to — and she's watching your face. Start the timer. Your tidy little ask has just become far too small for what's actually happening, and you have to decide, out loud, in the next thirty seconds: do you keep your word and be fine with it, or do you say the bigger thing you promised you weren't going to say? Either is defensible. Pick one and mean it. The only way to lose this round is to say nothing and let your face do the talking.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said the embarrassing word out loud — I didn't hide the feeling inside a complaint about her behaviour.
  • I never accused her of anything I couldn't prove, and I never asked a question I already knew the answer to.
  • I made clear I wasn't demanding she stop seeing him, before she had to defend it.
  • What I finally asked for was small, specific, and something she could say yes to on the spot.
  • When she told me more than I wanted to hear, I answered with words rather than a face.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Jealousy has almost no good arguments. That's what makes it hard to say — not that it's shameful, exactly, but that it arrives with no evidence and asks for things it hasn't earned, and you can hear how it sounds while you're still saying it.

So most people do one of two things with it. They sit on it, and it comes out later as something else — a flat mood, a snide remark about someone's dog. Or they build a case around it, because a case sounds better than a feeling, and then they have to defend the case instead of admitting the feeling.

There's a third option, and it's the one this whole conversation runs on: say the feeling, admit it's not much of an argument, and ask for something small. It doesn't make you look good. It does, on the whole, tend to work.

Two bigger questions

  1. Is jealousy a warning worth listening to, or just noise your mind makes? Have you ever been jealous and right — and how would you have known the difference at the time?
  2. We tell people to be honest about their feelings, and then we judge them for the feelings they're honest about. Is there a feeling you'd never admit to out loud, even to someone who loves you? What would happen if you did?
