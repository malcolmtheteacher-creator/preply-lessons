Does looking honestly at your past mistakes make you a better person, or just an unhappier one?

A famous footballer writes his life story. Instead of only the goals and the trophies, he writes about his bad decisions, the people he hurt and the years he wasted. The book becomes a best-seller. Why do we trust people more when they admit their mistakes? About 1,600 years ago, a man in North Africa wrote a book like this, and it changed how people write about themselves.

Meet Augustine

Read one part at a time. After each part, close your eyes and answer the question before you check.

I

A brilliant, restless young man

Augustine was born in 354 in Thagaste, a town in North Africa, in what is now Algeria. His mother, Monica, was a Christian; his father was not. Augustine was brilliant with words, and he became a teacher of public speaking in Carthage, then in Rome and Milan. As a young man he lived a wild life and tried one philosophy after another, but nothing gave him peace. He felt restless inside.

What was Augustine's job as a young man?

He taught public speaking, in Carthage, Rome and Milan.
II

Stolen pears and 'not yet'

Later, Augustine wrote about his mistakes with surprising honesty. As a teenager, he and his friends stole pears from a neighbour's tree. They weren't hungry, and they threw most of the pears to pigs: they did it just for the thrill of doing something wrong. He also admitted that as a young man he prayed, 'Give me chastity and self-control, but not yet.' Part of him wanted to change; another part did not.

Why did Augustine and his friends steal the pears?

Not because they were hungry, but just for the thrill of doing something wrong.
III

Take up and read

In 386, in a garden in Milan, Augustine was deeply unhappy and at war with himself. According to his own account, he heard a child's voice singing, 'Take up and read.' He opened a book of the Bible, read a few lines and felt his doubts disappear. He became a Christian, and later bishop of Hippo, in North Africa. His book about his life, the Confessions, is often called the first real autobiography. He died in 430.

Why is the Confessions so important?

It is often called the first real autobiography: an honest story of his own life, mistakes included.
Talk: Augustine wrote about stealing pears many years after it happened. Why do you think such a small mistake stayed in his mind for so long? Do small mistakes sometimes teach us more than big ones?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. After a week of silence, she confessed to her sister that she had lost the borrowed necklace.
confess (v) — to admit that you have done something wrong or embarrassing. Example: "He finally confessed that he had broken the window."
2. With a box of chocolates on the table, the temptation was too strong, and he ate the whole thing.
temptation (n) — a strong wish to do or have something, especially something you know you shouldn't. Example: "There's always a temptation to check your phone during a long meeting."
3. He couldn't sleep after lying to his friend: his conscience kept him awake all night.
conscience (n) — the inner sense that tells you whether what you are doing is right or wrong. Example: "My conscience wouldn't let me keep the money I found in the street."
4. He moved to a new city every two years and was never happy for long: he was a restless person.
restless (adj) — unable to stay calm or satisfied, and always wanting something different. Example: "After ten years in the same job, she felt restless and wanted a change."
5. Years later, she still felt remorse for the cruel things she had said to her best friend.
remorse (n) — a strong feeling of being sorry for something wrong that you have done. Example: "The driver showed real remorse in court and apologised to the family."
6. She's kind and clever, but impatience is her greatest flaw.
flaw (n) — a weakness or fault in someone's character, or in something. Example: "His biggest flaw is that he never really listens."
7. Instead of writing his essay, he procrastinated all weekend: he cleaned his room, watched films and tidied his desk twice.
procrastinate (v) — to keep delaying something you should do, often because it is boring or unpleasant. Example: "I always procrastinate when I have to fill in official forms."

The idea: The divided will

1
KnowYou know what would be good for you.
2
WantPart of you really wants to do it.
3
ResistAnother part says 'not yet', and the two fight.
4
Look backHonest memory shows the pattern and helps you change.

See it in action

Here is the idea in action. Sam knows he should stop scrolling on his phone at night. He really wants to sleep better. But every evening he tells himself, "Just ten more minutes." Augustine would say that Sam doesn't have one will, but two, at war with each other: one wants rest, the other wants the screen. Looking back honestly, Sam says: "I shouldn't have stayed up so late. It happens every time I take my phone to bed." Seeing the pattern is the first step to changing it.

Connect

Compare

Marcus Aurelius and Augustine: Marcus Aurelius and Augustine both wrote honestly about themselves. Marcus wrote advice 'To Himself' about how to act; Augustine wrote his Confessions to God, looking back at his past. What is the difference between giving yourself advice and confessing?
Marcus looks forward: 'Remember to be patient today.' Augustine looks back: 'This is what I did, and this is why it was wrong.' Advice tries to prevent mistakes; confession tries to understand them. Both need honesty, but confession also needs the courage to admit that you failed.
Plato and Augustine: Before he became a Christian, Augustine read books by followers of Plato. They helped him think about a perfect, unchanging reality beyond the world we see. What might Augustine have found useful in Plato's ideas?
Plato taught that beyond our changing world there is a perfect, unchanging world of Forms, such as Goodness itself. This helped Augustine to think of God as perfect and unchanging. But he felt that ideas alone could not heal his divided will.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From Pyrrho

What did Pyrrho think we should do, since we can't be sure of anything?

Suspend judgement: don't insist that anything is true or false. He believed this brings peace of mind.
From Zeno of Citium

What was Zeno's main lesson about things we can't change?

Divide things into what is up to you and what is not. Accept calmly what is not up to you, and put your energy into your own choices.
From Plato

What does Plato's story of the cave show?

That we may be like prisoners who take shadows for reality: our senses show us appearances, and only careful thinking leads us to the truth.

Final review

From memory, out loud. Then check.

1. What did Augustine mean by a divided will?

That part of us wants to do what is good while another part says 'not yet', so the will fights against itself. Looking back honestly helps us see this and change.

2. What is Augustine's most famous book, and why is it special?

The Confessions, his honest life story. It is often called the first real autobiography.

3. Why did the stolen pears matter so much to him?

Because he didn't steal them out of hunger, but just for the thrill of doing wrong. It made him think hard about why people choose what is wrong.

4. What does 'procrastinate' mean? Give an example.

To keep delaying something you should do. For example, cleaning your room instead of writing an essay.
