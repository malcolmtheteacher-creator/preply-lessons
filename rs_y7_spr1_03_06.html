Humanism: A Different Approach

Humanism

Key difference: Humanists don't believe in revealed sacred texts. Instead, they draw on human reason, science, and ethics. They value books by philosophers (like Richard Dawkins, Carl Sagan) but these are not "sacred." Humanists use evidence and logic to decide what's true, not religious authority.

How this relates: Humanists challenge the idea that any text is unchangeable or perfect. They believe people should question, debate, and update their understanding—similar to Jewish chavruta learning, but without the assumption of divine authority.

Quick-Fire Questions

Try to answer each question before clicking "Reveal Answer." Keep track of how many you get right.

Q1: What are the first five books of the Hebrew Bible called?

The Torah (or Pentateuch). Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy.

Q2: Define: What is a hafiz?

A Muslim who has memorized the entire Qur'an.

Q3: What is the Talmud, and what is it NOT?

The Talmud is a record of rabbinic debate and commentary on the Torah. It's NOT a single book or a final authority—it's a conversation between rabbis who sometimes disagreed.

Q4: What language is the Qur'an always written in, and why does this matter?

Arabic. Muslims believe that Arabic is the language God used to reveal the Qur'an, and the beauty and meaning of the words is lost in translation.

Q5: Who declared the Guru Granth Sahib to be the eternal Guru?

Guru Gobind Singh, the 10th and final human Guru of Sikhism.

Q6: Name three things that show how Sikhs treat the Guru Granth Sahib as a living person.

Any three of: it's awakened each morning (prakash); it sits on a throne (manji sahib); it's fanned with a chaur sahib; it's given rest at night (sukhasan); it has its own room (Darbar Sahib); it's carried on the head; no one places it on the floor.

Q7: What is chavruta learning?

The Jewish method of studying Torah and Talmud in pairs, through debate and questioning. Learning happens through arguing about what the text means.

Q8: What is a sofer, and what are they responsible for?

A trained Jewish scribe who hand-writes Torah scrolls. They must write every letter perfectly. One mistake means starting the entire scroll over.

Q9: What is the Mool Mantar?

The opening lines of the Guru Granth Sahib that express Sikhism's foundational belief: "There is one God, Truth by name, Creator, Without fear, Without hatred..."

Q10: How many pages does every copy of the Guru Granth Sahib have?

1,430 pages. Every copy is identical—no variations.

Deeper Reflection

Beyond the assessment tasks, let's think about the bigger patterns and questions that connect all of Unit 3.

The Pattern Across Traditions

Christianity

Authority: The Bible is authoritative, but interpretation varies widely. Some Christians read it literally; others read it metaphorically or historically. There's no single "correct" interpretation.

Islam

Authority: The Qur'an is the perfect, literal word of God. It's unchanging and complete. Islamic scholars interpret it, but they begin from the assumption that it's absolute truth.

Judaism

Authority: The Torah is sacred, but debate about meaning is encouraged. The Talmud isn't a final answer—it's a record of disagreement. Wisdom comes through questioning.

Sikhism

Authority: The Guru Granth Sahib IS the eternal guru. It's treated as a living teacher, but it also includes voices from other religions, suggesting truth is broader than one book alone.

Humanism

Authority: No sacred text has ultimate authority. Humanists use reason, evidence, and ethics to decide what's true. All ideas—including religious ones—can be questioned.

The Big Idea

What Gives Authority?

Different traditions answer this differently:

  • Christianity: Divine inspiration of human authors
  • Islam: Divine revelation, word-for-word perfect
  • Judaism: Divine origin + human interpretation through debate
  • Sikhism: The book ITSELF is divine guru; includes non-Sikh voices
  • Humanism: Human reason and evidence, not divine authority

Quick-Fire from the Whole Year

Q1: What does Tawhid mean in Islam?

The oneness of God; the belief that Allah is absolutely unique and has no partners or equals.

Q2: Name the five pillars of Islam.

Shahada (faith), Salah (prayer), Zakat (charity), Sawm (fasting), Hajj (pilgrimage).

Q3: What is the Tanakh?

The Jewish scriptures: Torah (Teaching), Nevi'im (Prophets), Ketuvim (Writings).

Q4: What is karma?

The law of cause and effect—your actions have consequences in this life and beyond, affecting your rebirth and spiritual progress.
