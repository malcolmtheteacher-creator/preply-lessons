Check your idea CLICK
Each form does a different job: was walking sets the scene — it was already happening. realised / had to run tells the main events, in order. had left / had already gone points back to something that happened even earlier. Real stories mix all three — that's what makes them sound natural.
Today: past continuous for the background, past simple for what happened, and past perfect for what had already happened earlier — plus used to for old habits — so you can tell a natural, everyday story.

Setting the Scene: "I was walking when..."

For the background of a story — what was already happening when something else happened.

Spot the pattern

Three moments from everyday stories:

  • "I was making dinner when the phone rang."
  • "We were queuing for tickets when it started to snow."
  • "She was driving to work when she saw the accident."

Before you check: what's happening in each bold verb, and what's happening in the plain one (rang, started, saw)?

Check the pattern CLICK
was / were + -ing = the longer background action, already in progress. The plain past form (rang, started, saw) is the shorter action that interrupts it. English uses this pair together to paint a scene.
was / were + verb-ing (the background) ... simple past (the shorter action)

From two facts to one story

"I read a book." + "My sister came home."
→
"I was reading a book when my sister came home."

The longer action (reading) becomes the background; the shorter one (came home) stays simple past and does the interrupting.

What Had Already Happened: "The train had already left"

For the earlier of two past events — the thing that happened before the main story moment.

Spot the pattern

  • "When I got to the station, the train had already left."
  • "By the time we arrived at the restaurant, our friends had ordered the food."
  • "I couldn't get into the flat because I'd forgotten my keys."

Two past events in each sentence — which one happened first?

Check the pattern CLICK
had + past participle marks the EARLIER of the two events (left, ordered, forgotten). The later event — the one closer to "now" in the story — stays simple past (got, arrived, couldn't get). "I'd forgotten" = I had forgotten; in speech we usually shorten it.
had + past participle (the earlier event) ... simple past (the later event)
Careful: past perfect is ONLY for the earlier of two out-of-order events. If things happened in the order you say them — "I got up, had breakfast and left" — just use simple past for all of them. No "had" needed.

How Things Used to Be: "I used to..."

For habits and situations in the past that are different now.

Spot the pattern

  • "I used to walk to school every day."
  • "We used to live in a small flat near the station."
  • "She didn't use to like coffee, but now she loves it."

Look at the spelling of "use(d)" in each one. What changes between the positive sentence and the negative one?

Check the pattern CLICK
Positive: used to + base verb — only one "d". Negative and questions: the "d" disappears — didn't use to / did you use to...? — because "did" already carries the past.
used to + base verb (positive)
didn't / did not use to + base verb (negative)
did + subject + use to + base verb? (question)
