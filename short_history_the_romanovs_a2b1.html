The Romanovs

One family ruled Russia for 300 years — and then lost everything in one year.

Today: tell the story of the Romanov family, and talk about kings, power and "what if". Grammar: the second conditional (If I were…, I would…).

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — The last king of Russia

For almost 300 years, one family ruled Russia. They were called the Romanovs. The last king was Nicholas II. He became king in 1894. Russia was huge — a very big empire. But Nicholas was not a strong leader. He was a kind man and a good father, but he did not rule well.

Nicholas married a German princess called Alexandra. They had four daughters and one son, called Alexei. Alexei was the heir — one day, he would be king. But the boy was very ill. He had a blood illness, and a small cut was very dangerous for him.

Alexandra was very afraid for her son. A strange holy man called Rasputin said he could help the boy, and sometimes Alexei did get better. So Alexandra trusted Rasputin completely. Soon, this strange man had a lot of power over the royal family. Many people in Russia did not like this at all.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What kind of king was Nicholas II?

2. Why did Alexandra trust Rasputin?

1. He was a kind man and a good father, but he was a weak leader and did not rule well.

2. Her son Alexei was very ill, and Rasputin seemed to help him get better — so she trusted him completely.

Talk about it:
  • Nicholas was a good father but a bad king. Is that important for a leader — to be a good person, or to make good decisions?

Part 2 — The year it all ended

In 1914, the First World War started. It was terrible for Russia. Many soldiers died. People in the cities had no food. Everyone was angry and tired.

In 1917, the people of Russia had enough. There was a revolution. The people came onto the streets. Even the soldiers joined them. Nicholas had no friends left. He had to give up his power — he stopped being king. After almost 300 years, the Romanov family did not rule Russia any more.

Later that year, a new group called the Bolsheviks took control. They did not like the royal family at all. They took Nicholas, Alexandra and the five children far away to a cold town in the east, called Yekaterinburg. The family were now prisoners. Guards watched them all the time.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the First World War change things in Russia?

2. What happened to Nicholas in 1917?

1. Many soldiers died, people had no food, and everyone was angry and tired. This helped start the revolution.

2. There was a revolution; Nicholas had no support left, so he gave up his power and stopped being king. Later the family became prisoners.

Talk about it:
  • The family ruled for 300 years, but it ended very fast. Why do you think it ended so quickly?

Part 3 — The mystery

In July 1918, something terrible happened. One night, the guards killed the whole family — Nicholas, Alexandra and all five children. Then they hid the bodies in a forest. For a long time, nobody knew exactly what had happened.

Because of this secret, a strange story began. People said that maybe one daughter, Anastasia, did not die. Maybe she escaped! A woman in Germany said, "I am Anastasia." Her name was Anna Anderson. For many years, some people believed her. There were even films about her.

But it was not true. Many years later, scientists found the bodies in the forest. They used DNA tests (tests that compare family blood). The tests showed that the bodies really were the Romanov family. And the tests showed that Anna Anderson was not Anastasia. Finally, after many years, the mystery was over.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was there a mystery for so many years?

2. How did scientists finally solve it?

1. The family were killed in secret and the bodies were hidden, so nobody was sure what had happened. Some people thought a daughter, Anastasia, had escaped.

2. Scientists found the bodies and used DNA tests. The tests showed the bodies were the Romanovs, and that Anna Anderson was not Anastasia.

Talk about it:
  • For many years, people wanted to believe that Anastasia was alive. Why do people like a story with hope in it?

Grammar: the second conditional

We use the second conditional to imagine things that are not real — to say "what if?".

How we make it: If + past simple, … would + verb.
Example: "If Nicholas were a stronger king, the family would be safer."
Example: "If I were the king, I would listen to the people."
We often use were for all people here: "If I were…", "If he were…".

1. Choose the right verb

1. If Nicholas (be) a stronger leader, the people (would / be) happier.

2. If the family (leave) Russia, they (would / be) safe.

3. If I (be) a king, I (would / help) the poor people.

1. were / would be

2. left / would be

3. were / would help

2. Make your own sentences

Finish each sentence with your own idea. Then read it out loud.

1. If I were the leader of my country, I .

2. If I had a lot of money, I .

3. If I could meet a king or queen, I .

Your answers will be different! Just check the shape: would + verb. For example: "…I would build more schools." / "…I would travel the world." / "…I would ask many questions."
