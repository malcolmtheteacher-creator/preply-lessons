Marco Polo

A young merchant who came home with the most famous travel story ever told — and a question that has never gone away: did he really go?

Today: you'll tell the story of the world's most famous traveller, and weigh up whether to believe him — reporting what people said and claimed, and using the language of travel, trade and doubt.

2. They travelled east along the great caravan routes, joining long lines of traders, camels and pack animals crossing the deserts together for safety.

an envoy (n) — an official messenger or representative, especially one sent by a ruler on a mission.

a marvel (n) — a wonderful or astonishing thing; something that fills you with amazement.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The road east

Marco Polo was born in Venice around 1254, into a family of merchants who traded across the known world. His father, Niccolò, and his uncle Maffeo had already journeyed deep into Asia and come back with tales of the court of the Mongols. So when they set out east again in 1271, they took the teenage Marco with them. He was perhaps seventeen years old, and the journey ahead would swallow the whole of his youth.

The road was the network of caravan routes later generations would call the Silk Road — months of mountains, deserts and trading cities, of danger, sickness and slow, grinding distance. After three or four years of travel, the Polos finally reached the realm of Kublai Khan, grandson of Genghis Khan and Mongol emperor of China. They had crossed half the world to stand in his court.

Kublai Khan, it is said, was charmed by the sharp young Venetian. Marco was clever, observant, and good with languages, and the emperor reportedly took him into his service. For something like seventeen years Marco stayed in the East, sent out as an envoy across the empire and bringing back reports of the provinces, the cities and the customs he found there.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who did Marco travel with in 1271, and why was the family suited to such a journey?

2. Whose court did the Polos eventually reach, and what role did Marco come to play there?

1. He travelled with his father Niccolò and his uncle Maffeo, who had already been deep into Asia. They were Venetian merchants, used to long-distance trade — so a journey east along the caravan routes was an extension of the family business.

2. They reached the court of Kublai Khan, the Mongol emperor of China. Marco reportedly entered the Khan's service and was sent out as an envoy to report on different parts of the empire.

Discuss:
  • A teenager leaves home and does not return for about twenty-four years. What would that do to a person — and what would they become?

Part 2 — The book that conquered Europe

After roughly twenty-four years away, Marco came home to a Venice that barely remembered him. He had returned a wealthy man, but his greatest fortune was the one he could not yet see: the story locked inside his head. That story might have stayed there, half-told at dinner tables, had fate not intervened in a strange way.

Venice was at war with the rival city of Genoa, and Marco was captured and thrown into a Genoese prison. There he met a fellow prisoner named Rustichello da Pisa, a writer of romances. To pass the long, idle months, Marco dictated his travels and Rustichello wrote them down. The result was a book Europe came to know as The Travels of Marco Polo — also called Il Milione, "The Million."

It was a sensation. In an age before printing, the manuscript was copied by hand and spread across Europe, describing marvels its readers could scarcely believe: a city of impossible size and wealth, stones that burned (coal), and money made of paper. For generations it was the West's richest window onto the East. Two centuries later, a Genoese sailor named Christopher Columbus owned a copy, scribbled notes in its margins, and sailed west hoping to reach the very lands Marco had described. The book had become a map of the imagination.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Marco's story come to be written down at all?

2. Why was the book so influential, and what later explorer did it help inspire?

1. Captured in Venice's war with Genoa, Marco was imprisoned and met the writer Rustichello da Pisa. Marco dictated his travels and Rustichello wrote them down, producing The Travels of Marco Polo.

2. Copied by hand and spread widely, it gave Europe its richest picture of Asia — its wealth, cities and wonders. Among many it inspired was Christopher Columbus, who owned and annotated a copy and sailed west hoping to reach those lands.

Discuss:
  • If Marco had never been captured, the book might never have existed. How much of history do you think turns on accidents like that?

Part 3 — But did he really go?

Here the story turns into an argument that has lasted seven hundred years. Almost from the start, people were sceptical. The book was so full of riches and round numbers that the nickname Il Milione may have been half a joke — Marco of the Million Lies. On his deathbed in 1324, friends are said to have begged him to take back his tall tales. He reportedly refused, insisting he had not told the half of what he saw.

Modern doubters have pressed the case further. If Marco truly spent years criss-crossing China, why does his book never mention the Great Wall? Why no tea, no chopsticks, no Chinese writing, no foot-binding — the very things a European could hardly have missed? Some scholars argue he never reached China at all, and instead pieced his account together from Persian and Arabic guidebooks picked up at trading posts nearer home.

Yet the case for Marco is strong too. He describes paper money, the burning of coal, and the workings of Mongol salt taxes and currency with a precision that fits the records remarkably well — details a stay-at-home faker would have struggled to invent. So the debate runs on. He must have seen something extraordinary to describe it so exactly; yet he can't have got everything right, or remembered it all perfectly, decades later in a prison cell. Perhaps the truth is the most human answer of all: a real journey, a real wonder, and a storyteller who could never quite resist making a good tale better.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What things in (and missing from) the book make some people doubt he reached China?

2. What evidence pulls the other way, suggesting he really did see what he described?

1. The doubts: the book omits things a traveller in China could hardly miss — the Great Wall, tea, chopsticks, Chinese writing, foot-binding — and is full of huge, suspicious numbers. Some argue he gathered the account second-hand from Persian and Arabic sources.

2. The defence: his accurate descriptions of paper money, burning coal, and the detail of Mongol salt taxes and currency match the historical records very closely — hard to fake from guidebooks alone.

Discuss:
  • A story can be partly true and partly exaggerated at the same time. When someone tells you about an amazing trip, how do you decide what to believe?

The grammar this story runs on: reported speech

Marco Polo's whole legacy is a report — a book of what he claimed he had seen — and seven centuries of people arguing over what he really said. So this is the perfect story for reporting other people's words: what they said, asked, and claimed.

The tools:
• say / tell — you say something, but you tell someone something: "Marco said that he had served the Khan"; "He told his friends that he had not lied."
• backshift — when the reporting verb is past, the tenses usually step back one: present → past, past → past perfect, will → would. ("I serve the Khan" → he said he served the Khan.)
• reported questions — no question mark, normal word order, and if/whether for yes–no questions: "People asked whether he had really been to China."
• modals of deduction (past) — for the debate: "He must have seen it" (I'm sure he did); "He can't have invented all of it" (I'm sure he didn't); "He might have exaggerated" (maybe).

1. say or tell?

Remember: tell needs a person straight after it; say does not.

1. Marco that the city was vast.

2. He Rustichello about the court of the Khan.

3. The doubters that the numbers could not be true.

4. On his deathbed he his friends that he had not told the half of it.

1. said (no person after the verb)

2. told (told + person)

3. said

4. told

2. Direct → reported (backshift)

Rewrite each line as reported speech, stepping the tenses back. The reporting verb is given.

1. "I serve the emperor." → Marco said that he the emperor.

2. "I have seen marvels." → He claimed that he marvels.

3. "We will sail west." → Columbus said that they west.

4. "Did he really go to China?" → People asked he had really gone to China.

1. served (present → past)

2. had seen (present perfect → past perfect)

3. would sail (will → would)

4. whether / if (reported yes–no question — no question mark, normal word order)

3. The debate — modals of deduction

Fill each gap with must have, can't have or might have + the past participle, to argue about Marco the way historians do.

1. His details are so exact — he (see) the paper money with his own eyes.

2. No one could invent all of that; he (make) the whole thing up.

3. He never mentions the Great Wall, so he (miss) it — or it simply wasn't as we picture it then.

1. must have seen (near-certainty that he did)

2. can't have made (near-certainty that he didn't)

3. might have missed (a real possibility)
