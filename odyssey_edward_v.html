The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. His uncle Richard became Lord Protector.
a protector (n) — a regent.
2. The boys were lodged in the Tower.
to lodge (v) — to stay somewhere.
3. They were declared illegitimate.
illegitimate (adj) — (here) not legally born, so without rights.
4. Richard claimed a precontract invalidated Edward IV's marriage.
a precontract (n) — an earlier marriage promise.
5. The boys seemed to vanish.
to vanish (v) — to disappear.
6. Bones found in 1674 may be their remains.
remains (n) — parts of a dead body.
7. Richard is the main suspect.
a suspect (n) — someone thought guilty.
8. The bones were placed in an urn in Westminster Abbey.
an urn (n) — a decorative container.
9. The evidence is inconclusive.
inconclusive (adj) — not leading to a firm result.
10. There are many theories.
a theory (n) — an idea that explains something.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The boy king

Edward V became king at twelve in April 1483. His uncle Richard, Duke of Gloucester, met him on his way to London, arrested his mother's relatives, and was named Lord Protector. Edward was lodged in the Tower — then a royal residence — and his younger brother Richard joined him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who became Protector?

2. Where were the boys lodged?

1. His uncle Richard, Duke of Gloucester.

2. In the Tower of London.

Discuss:
  • Why did Richard arrest the boy's maternal relatives?

Part 2 — Disinherited

In June 1483 a sermon and a meeting of nobles declared that Edward IV's marriage had been invalid because of an earlier precontract, so his children were illegitimate; Parliament confirmed this in 1484. Richard was crowned king on 6 July. The boys were seen less and less, and by the autumn they had disappeared from view.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why were the boys declared illegitimate?

2. When was Richard crowned?

1. Edward IV's marriage was said to be invalid because of a precontract.

2. 6 July 1483.

Discuss:
  • Was the precontract a real reason or an excuse?

Part 3 — The mystery

What happened is unknown. Many contemporaries and later writers believed they were murdered on Richard's orders; others suspect the Duke of Buckingham or even Henry VII. In 1674 workmen found bones of two children in the Tower; they were placed in Westminster Abbey, but have never been tested with modern methods.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who are the suspects?

2. What was found in 1674?

1. Richard III (main suspect), the Duke of Buckingham, Henry VII.

2. The bones of two children.

Discuss:
  • Should the bones be tested?

Grammar — Past Modals of Deduction

When the evidence is thin, historians must deduce. Past modals show how certain we are about what happened.

How it works:
• must have + past participle — nearly certain: "He must have known."
• might / may / could have — possible: "The princes may have been killed."
• can't have — nearly impossible: "He can't have been in London that night."

1. How sure are we?

Complete each sentence about Edward V. Use the word in brackets.

1. Richard (must / know) what happened to the boys.

2. Buckingham (might / act) alone.

3. Henry VII (can't / kill) them in 1483, because he was in exile.

1. must have known

2. might have acted

3. can't have killed

2. Complete with a past modal

1. The boys (may / die) of illness.

2. Someone (could / smuggle) them abroad.

3. Richard (should / show) them in public to stop rumours.

1. may have died

2. could have smuggled

3. should have shown

Going deeper

The Princes in the Tower remain a historical cold case. The lack of evidence lets every generation tell its own version.

Worth noticing: the Richard III Society argues for his innocence; most historians still see him as the most likely culprit.
Learning from the past — for today: Silence feeds suspicion. When people can't see what's happening, they assume the worst. Transparency protects reputations.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • What do you think happened?
  • Why is the mystery so popular?
  • When has silence created suspicion around you?
