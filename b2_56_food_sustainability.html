Seasonal Eating & Food Miles

Seasonal eating means consuming food that's naturally grown or produced during that time of year. When you eat out-of-season produce, it often travels thousands of miles to reach your supermarket. This transportation generates significant carbon emissions.

Consider these questions:

  • Can you identify three seasonal foods in your region right now?
  • How does eating local and seasonal affect environmental impact?
  • What challenges exist for consumers wanting to buy seasonal produce?

Think & Share: Have you ever noticed the difference between food that's in season and food that's not? What about the price difference?

The Reality of Food Waste

Globally, approximately one-third of all food produced is wasted. This happens at every stage: farms, transport, supermarkets, and homes. When food decomposes in landfills, it releases methane, a potent greenhouse gas. The resources used to produce wasted food—water, energy, land—are also wasted.

Reflection

How much food does your household waste in a typical week? What are the main reasons food goes uneaten in your home?

Sustainability Language

Let's explore key vocabulary for discussing food sustainability:

Carbon Footprint The total greenhouse gases produced by human activities, measured in units of carbon dioxide
Food Miles The distance food travels from farm to consumer, reflecting environmental cost of transport
Biodegradable Able to decompose naturally without harming the environment
Food Waste Food discarded or lost at any stage from production to consumption
Sustainable Agriculture Farming methods that maintain productivity while minimising environmental damage
Plant-based Food derived from plants rather than animal products
Food Chain The sequence of organisms that feed on each other, transferring energy from one level to the next
Fair Trade A system ensuring producers receive equitable payment and work in good conditions

Reading: The Environmental Impact of Food Choices

The global food system accounts for approximately 25-30% of greenhouse gas emissions. This includes farming, processing, packaging, and transportation. Livestock production, particularly beef and dairy, is one of the most resource-intensive sectors, requiring vast amounts of water, feed, and land. A single kilogram of beef requires roughly 15,000 litres of water to produce, whilst the same amount of plant-based protein requires a fraction of that.

However, sustainability isn't simply about eating less meat. It's about understanding the complex systems behind food production. Local, small-scale farms may have lower transportation emissions but higher labour inputs. Industrial agriculture maximises efficiency but often depletes soil and requires heavy pesticide use. The most sustainable choice depends on context, season, and geography.

Consumer choices matter, but systemic change requires policy support, investment in sustainable agriculture, reduction of food waste in supply chains, and innovation in food technology. Individual actions—buying seasonal, reducing waste, supporting fair trade producers—contribute meaningfully when part of larger cultural and political shifts.

Key Phrases for Discussion

  • "To minimise impact" — to reduce harm to the environment
  • "Supply chain" — the entire process from production to consumer
  • "Trade-off" — a situation where you must sacrifice one thing to gain another
  • "Resource-intensive" — requiring large amounts of energy, water, or land
  • "To deplete" — to reduce the amount of something available

The Food Debate

Motion: "Everyone should eat a plant-based diet to save the planet."

In Favour

Argument 1: Environmental Impact

Plant-based diets require significantly fewer resources. Livestock farming is responsible for 14-18% of global greenhouse gas emissions, plus deforestation for grazing and feed crops. Shifting to plant-based proteins would dramatically reduce our collective carbon footprint.

Counter: Not all plant-based foods are equally sustainable. Avocados require intensive water use in arid regions; almond farming depletes water supplies. Some regions rely on livestock for nutrition and economy. Blanket statements ignore local context.
Argument 2: Health Benefits

Plant-based diets reduce risks of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and certain cancers. Lower cholesterol and saturated fat intake improve health outcomes. This reduces healthcare burden and improves quality of life.

Counter: Well-planned plant-based diets are healthy, but so are omnivorous diets that include moderate meat consumption. The issue is ultra-processed food, not animal products per se. Cultural food traditions matter too.

Against

Argument 1: Practical Reality

Global food insecurity already affects millions. Asking everyone to adopt plant-based diets ignores economic, cultural, and geographical realities. Many regions depend on pastoral farming. Forcing dietary change is unrealistic and unethical.

Counter: Sustainability advocates aren't forcing anything. They're pointing out that current consumption patterns, especially in wealthy nations, are unsustainable. Education and gradual transition are different from force.
Argument 2: Nutritional Completeness

Animal products provide complete proteins and B12, which aren't naturally available in plant sources. While supplementation exists, this requires infrastructure and knowledge not available to all populations globally.

Counter: B12 is also supplemented in livestock feed. Diverse plant-based diets provide complete nutrition. The issue isn't plant-based diets per se, but access to diverse, affordable foods globally.

Your Choices

Lab-Grown Meat & Innovation

Cultivated meat is grown from animal cells without raising and slaughtering animals. It requires less land, water, and feed than conventional livestock. Yet it's expensive, faces regulatory uncertainty, and is culturally unfamiliar to many. Some question whether it's truly "natural," whilst others see it as revolutionary.

Consider: Would you eat lab-grown meat? What would need to change for you to try it? What concerns do you have?

Vertical Farming & Urban Agriculture

Growing food in stacked layers within buildings uses 90% less water and produces crops year-round, independent of weather. Crops grow without pesticides in controlled environments. However, energy consumption for lighting and climate control can be substantial unless powered by renewables.

Your Shopping & Eating Habits

Real change starts with individual choices, but remember: individual responsibility alone can't solve systemic problems. Shopping consciously matters, but so does advocating for policy change, supporting sustainable agriculture, and normalising conversations about food systems.

Discussion: What's one change you could realistically make to your eating habits? What would make sustainable eating easier for you? What barriers do you face?

Practical Steps

  • Buy seasonal and local when possible
  • Plan meals to reduce food waste
  • Choose products with minimal packaging
  • Support fair trade and sustainable brands when budget allows
  • Experiment with plant-based meals without eliminating all animal products
  • Learn about your local food system and agricultural practices
  • Have conversations—change cultural norms through dialogue

Final Thought: Sustainability isn't about perfection or guilt. It's about understanding systems, making informed choices, and working collectively toward a food system that nourishes both people and planet.
