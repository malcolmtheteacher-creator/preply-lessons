Recall: Imamate and the Sunni-Shi'a Split

Time to recall what you learned last lesson

Last lesson you explored the concept of Imamate and the origins of the Sunni-Shi'a division. Let's recap before we bring all the beliefs together.

What caused the Sunni-Shi'a split after Muhammad's death?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: The community disagreed over who should lead. Sunnis believed Abu Bakr was rightfully elected by community consultation (shura). Shi'a believed Ali ibn Abi Talib was divinely appointed as Muhammad's successor.

What happened at the Battle of Karbala in 680 CE?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Husayn ibn Ali (Muhammad's grandson, 3rd Shi'a Imam) refused to submit to the unjust Umayyad caliph Yazid. Husayn and 72 companions were killed. This martyrdom is commemorated annually during Muharram and Ashura.

Who is the Twelfth Imam?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Muhammad al-Mahdi — Shi'a believe he went into occultation (hiding) in 874 CE and is still alive. He will return as the Mahdi (guided one) before the Day of Judgement to restore justice on earth.

What are the five Usul ad-Din in Shi'a Islam?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Tawhid (oneness of God), Adalah (justice of God), Nubuwwah (prophethood), Imamate (divinely appointed leadership through the Twelve Imams), and Mi'ad (Day of Judgement and resurrection).

From Belief to Practice: How Tawhid, Angels, and Prophets Shape Daily Life

Islamic beliefs are not abstract ideas — they directly shape how Muslims live every single day. This lesson brings together everything you've learned in Units 23 and 24 to show how belief becomes action.

Tawhid (Oneness of God) in daily life:

  • The Shahadah — "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is His messenger" — is recited daily in prayer, whispered into a newborn baby's ear, and spoken at the moment of death
  • Rejection of shirk: Muslims avoid anything that could be seen as worship of something other than Allah — idols, excessive attachment to money or status, even superstition
  • Humility: Because only Allah is great, Muslims cultivate humility. The physical act of prostration (sujud) in prayer — placing your forehead on the ground — is a bodily expression of submission to Tawhid
  • All actions as worship (ibadah): When done with the right intention (niyyah), even eating, working, and caring for family become acts of worship

Belief in angels:

  • Kiraman Katibin (the noble scribes) record every good and bad deed — this creates constant awareness that actions matter
  • The angel of death (Azrael) reminds Muslims that life is temporary and they must prepare for akhirah
  • Jibril's role in revealing the Qur'an gives Muslims confidence in scripture's divine origin

Following the prophets:

  • Muslims follow the Sunnah of Muhammad in everything — from how to pray, eat, and greet others, to how to resolve disputes and treat neighbours
  • Saying "peace be upon him" (PBUH) after mentioning any prophet shows ongoing respect
  • Prophetic stories teach specific virtues: Ibrahim's courage, Musa's perseverance, Muhammad's mercy

Akhirah, Al-Qadr, and the Holy Books in Practice

Akhirah (afterlife) in daily life:

  • Ethical motivation: Knowing every action is recorded and will be judged motivates honesty, generosity, and integrity even when no one is watching
  • Patience in suffering: This world's hardships are temporary tests. A just Allah will reward patience. This gives comfort during illness, poverty, and grief
  • Charity: Zakat (obligatory charity) and Sadaqah (voluntary charity) are investments in the afterlife. The Qur'an repeatedly links generosity to rewards in Jannah
  • Avoiding haram: Fear of Jahannam discourages alcohol, gambling, dishonesty, and exploitation

Al-Qadr (predestination) in daily life:

  • Tawakkul (trust in Allah's plan): When things go wrong, Muslims find comfort in believing that Allah has a purpose for everything. "Perhaps you hate a thing and it is good for you" (Qur'an 2:216)
  • Effort is still required: Al-Qadr does not mean Muslims sit back and wait. They must strive, work hard, and make good choices — but ultimately accept the outcome as Allah's will
  • Gratitude in good times: When things go well, Muslims thank Allah (saying "Alhamdulillah" — praise be to God) because they recognise all good comes from Him

The Qur'an in daily life:

  • Recited in every salah (prayer) — Surah al-Fatiha is repeated at least 17 times daily
  • Memorised — a hafiz who memorises the entire Qur'an is deeply respected
  • Consulted for guidance on ethical dilemmas, family disputes, business decisions, and personal struggles
  • Treated with reverence — wudu before handling, stored on high shelves, never placed on the floor

The Five Pillars: Where Belief Meets Action

The Five Pillars of Islam are the practical expression of Muslim beliefs. Each pillar directly connects to core theological ideas:

1. Shahadah (Declaration of Faith): "There is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is His messenger." This affirms Tawhid and prophethood in a single sentence. It is the foundation of everything else.

2. Salah (Prayer, 5 times daily): Physical submission to Allah. Facing Makkah unites the global ummah. The movements — standing, bowing, prostrating — express humility before the Creator. Reciting Qur'anic verses keeps Allah's word central to daily life.

3. Zakat (Obligatory Charity, 2.5% of savings): Expresses belief that wealth belongs to Allah and must be shared. Motivated by akhirah — charity is an investment in the afterlife. Builds justice and equality in the community.

4. Sawm (Fasting during Ramadan): Develops self-discipline and empathy for the poor. Commemorates the month when the Qur'an was first revealed. Breaking the fast (iftar) each evening strengthens community bonds. Encourages spiritual reflection and gratitude.

5. Hajj (Pilgrimage to Makkah): Every Muslim who is physically and financially able must perform Hajj at least once. Walking around the Ka'bah connects to Ibrahim's legacy. Wearing white ihram (pilgrimage garment) symbolises equality before Allah — rich and poor, old and young, all are the same.

Sunni Six Articles of Faith vs Shi'a Usul ad-Din and Furu ad-Din

Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share the same core beliefs but organise them differently:

Sunni: Six Articles of Faith (Iman)

  • Belief in Allah (Tawhid)
  • Belief in Angels (Malaikah)
  • Belief in Holy Books (Kutub)
  • Belief in Prophets (Risalah)
  • Belief in the Day of Judgement (Akhirah)
  • Belief in Predestination (Al-Qadr)

Shi'a: Five Usul ad-Din (Roots of Religion)

  • Tawhid — Oneness of Allah
  • Adalah — Justice of Allah
  • Nubuwwah — Prophethood
  • Imamate — Divinely appointed leadership
  • Mi'ad — Day of Judgement

Shi'a: Ten Furu ad-Din (Branches of Religion) — the practical duties that flow from the roots, including Salah, Sawm, Zakat, Khums (20% tax), Hajj, Jihad (striving), commanding good, forbidding evil, loving the Ahl al-Bayt, and disassociation from enemies of the Ahl al-Bayt.

Key difference: Sunnis include al-Qadr (predestination) as a core belief; Shi'a emphasise Adalah (divine justice) and Imamate instead. Both systems lead to similar ethical outcomes but with different theological reasoning.

Why Understanding Beliefs Matters for GCSE

For AQA GCSE Religious Studies, you need to be able to explain not just what Muslims believe, but how those beliefs affect daily life. Here's a framework for connecting any belief to practice:

The belief-practice chain:

  • Identify the belief (e.g., "Allah is omniscient — He knows everything")
  • Explain its logical consequence (e.g., "Therefore, every action matters because Allah sees it all")
  • Give a specific practice (e.g., "Muslims are honest in business even when no one is watching, because they know Allah is watching")
  • Support with evidence (e.g., "The Qur'an says 'Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it' — Surah 99:7")

This four-step approach works for any belief: Tawhid → no idolatry → rejecting materialism → "Do not set up equals with Allah" (Surah 2:22). Or: Akhirah → judgement is coming → charitable giving → "Those who spend their wealth in the way of Allah... will have their reward with their Lord" (Surah 2:262).

The strongest GCSE answers always show this chain from abstract belief to concrete daily action, supported by Qur'anic evidence.

GCSE Practice: 12-Mark Evaluation

'The most important effect of Muslim beliefs on daily life is ethical behaviour.' Evaluate this statement.

Consider multiple beliefs and their practical effects. Refer to specific practices, Qur'anic teachings, and different perspectives. Write approximately 200–300 words.

Words: 0/300

Guidance

AQA 12-mark structure:

  • For the statement: Akhirah motivates honesty and charity. Tawhid demands humility and rejection of materialism. The Qur'an and Sunnah provide detailed ethical guidance. "Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it" (Surah 99:7).
  • Against the statement: Worship (salah, sawm, hajj) is equally important — it shapes daily routine and community identity. Community belonging and spiritual experience may matter as much as ethics. Some argue the Five Pillars (ritual practice) are the most visible effect.
  • Conclusion: Ethics and worship are inseparable in Islam — ethical behaviour IS an act of worship (ibadah). The most complete answer recognises that beliefs create a whole way of life, not just one effect.

Review: How Beliefs Shape Muslim Life

Give two ways Tawhid shapes daily Muslim life.

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: 1. The Shahadah is recited in every prayer, affirming 'no god but Allah.' 2. Muslims reject shirk — avoiding worship of anything besides Allah, including excessive attachment to wealth or status. Prostration in prayer physically expresses submission to the one God.

How does belief in akhirah motivate ethical behaviour?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Muslims know their actions are recorded by the Kiraman Katibin (recording angels) and will be judged on the Day of Judgement. Fear of Jahannam discourages sin. Hope for Jannah motivates charity, honesty, and patience. 'Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it' (Surah 99:7).

What is al-Qadr and how does it affect daily life?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Al-Qadr is the belief that Allah knows and has determined everything that will happen. It affects daily life through tawakkul (trust in Allah's plan) — Muslims accept hardships as tests, say 'Alhamdulillah' for blessings, but still strive and make effort because that is what Allah requires.

Name the Five Pillars and connect each to a core belief.

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Shahadah — Tawhid and prophethood. Salah — submission to Allah. Zakat — wealth belongs to Allah, motivated by akhirah. Sawm — self-discipline, commemorates Qur'an's revelation. Hajj — unity of ummah, connects to Ibrahim's legacy.

How do Sunni and Shi'a Muslims organise their core beliefs differently?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Sunnis have Six Articles of Faith (Allah, angels, books, prophets, Day of Judgement, predestination). Shi'a have five Usul ad-Din (Tawhid, Adalah, Nubuwwah, Imamate, Mi'ad) plus ten Furu ad-Din (practical duties). The key differences: Shi'a include Imamate and Adalah; Sunnis include al-Qadr.
