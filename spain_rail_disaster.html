January 18-20, 2026 | Adamuz, Spain

Spain's Deadliest Rail Disaster in 13 Years

When warnings fall on deaf ears

What Happened?

On Sunday evening, January 18, 2026, two high-speed trains collided near the small town of Adamuz, about 360km south of Madrid. The rear carriages of one train derailed onto the opposite track - directly into the path of an oncoming train travelling in the other direction.

41+ Confirmed dead
292 Injured
489 Passengers on both trains
250 km/h Speed limit (both trains below)

Sources: Spanish Civil Guard, Transport Minister Oscar Puente (January 20, 2026)

Salvador Jimenez - Journalist on Board

"There was a moment when it felt like an earthquake and the train had indeed derailed." Salvador, a reporter for Spanish broadcaster RTVE, was travelling on one of the trains. He described passengers using emergency hammers to smash windows to escape, with some walking away with minor injuries while others remained trapped in twisted metal.

Francisco Carmona - Regional Fire Chief

"The problem is that the carriages are twisted, so the metal is twisted with the people inside." Rescue crews worked through the night in pitch-black conditions. The front carriages of one train had plunged down a 4-metre embankment, making access extremely difficult. Some bodies were found hundreds of metres from the crash site.

The Warning That Was Ignored

In August 2025 - five months before the disaster - the train drivers' union SEMAF warned railway operator Adif about serious wear and tear on the tracks, including the exact section where the derailment occurred. The warnings mentioned potholes, bumps, and overhead power line problems. According to reports, no action was taken.

Source: SEMAF union letter, August 2025

The Uncomfortable Questions

Transport Minister Oscar Puente called the crash "truly strange" - the track had been renovated in May 2025 with a 700 million euro investment. The train was manufactured in 2022 and passed its last safety inspection just three days before the crash. Both trains were travelling under the speed limit.

Investigators have now found a broken rail joint at the crash site. But the question remains: if workers had warned about track problems months earlier, why wasn't anything done?

"When infrastructure fails, who is really responsible - the workers who sound the alarm, the managers who ignore it, or the system that allows warnings to be dismissed?"

Language Focus A

Click each expression to reveal meaning, form, and practice.

1. "to fall on deaf ears"

▼
Meaning

When warnings, advice, or complaints are ignored or not taken seriously by the people who should listen.

Form

[Warning/complaint/advice] + fell on deaf ears

[Warning/complaint/advice] + seems to have fallen on deaf ears

Register

Neutral to formal. Common in news reporting and professional contexts. Often carries a tone of frustration or criticism.

"The union's warnings about track maintenance fell on deaf ears - five months later, 41 people are dead."

"Employees had raised safety concerns for years, but their complaints fell on deaf ears."

NOW YOU TRY

Think of a time when someone's advice or warning was ignored. What happened? Use "fell on deaf ears" to describe it.

2. "a tragedy waiting to happen"

▼
Meaning

A dangerous situation where a disaster is predictable because warning signs have been ignored.

Form

[Situation] + was/is a tragedy waiting to happen

It was only a matter of time before... (often paired)

Register

Journalistic, dramatic. Used in hindsight to criticise inaction. Implies blame - that people should have prevented it.

"With 18 track incidents in three years, this was a tragedy waiting to happen."

"The building had failed multiple safety inspections. It was a tragedy waiting to happen."

NOW YOU TRY

Can you think of any situations in your country or workplace that feel like "a tragedy waiting to happen"? What warning signs exist?

3. "to point the finger at"

▼
Meaning

To blame someone or hold them responsible for something that went wrong.

Form

point the finger at + [person/organisation]

fingers are being pointed at... (passive, common in news)

Register

Neutral. Can sound accusatory. The passive form ("fingers are being pointed") is more diplomatic and common in formal reporting.

"The Transport Minister is careful not to point the finger at anyone until the investigation is complete."

"After the data breach, fingers were pointed at the IT department for not updating security."

NOW YOU TRY

In this rail disaster, who do you think fingers will be pointed at? The train company? The maintenance workers? Government regulators? Why?

Language Focus B

Two more essential expressions for discussing accountability and revelation.

4. "to come to light"

▼
Meaning

When hidden or unknown information is revealed or discovered, especially damaging information.

Form

[Information] + has come to light

It has come to light that...

New evidence/details came to light

Register

Formal, journalistic. Very common in news reporting about scandals, investigations, and cover-ups.

"It has come to light that the union warned about track problems five months before the crash."

"New evidence has come to light suggesting the company knew about the defect for years."

NOW YOU TRY

Think of a recent scandal or news story. What information "came to light" during the investigation? Use the expression to describe it.

5. "to be held accountable"

▼
Meaning

To be required to explain and accept responsibility for one's actions, especially when something goes wrong.

Form

[Person/organisation] + should/must be held accountable

Who will be held accountable for...?

to hold [someone] accountable (active form)

Register

Formal, often used in political, legal, and corporate contexts. Carries weight - implies consequences should follow.

"Families of the victims are demanding that those responsible be held accountable."

"The CEO resigned, but critics say the entire board should be held accountable."

NOW YOU TRY

In your opinion, is it enough for companies to apologise after disasters, or must individuals be held accountable personally? Give an example to support your view.

Your Turn - Discussion

Choose 2-3 cards that interest you most. Your teacher will facilitate discussion.

1

The Blame Game

In this case, fingers will likely be pointed at multiple parties - the rail operator, the infrastructure managers, government regulators. In your view, when a disaster involves multiple organisations, how do you decide who should be held accountable? Should blame be shared equally?

2

Whistleblowers

The union's warnings fell on deaf ears for five months. Why do you think organisations often ignore internal warnings? Have you ever raised a concern at work that wasn't taken seriously? What happened?

3

Safety vs. Profit

Spain invested 700 million euros in these tracks, but 18 incidents occurred in 3 years. Some argue this was a tragedy waiting to happen despite the spending. In your country, do you think enough money is invested in infrastructure maintenance, or is it often cut to save costs?

4

Media Responsibility

When disasters happen, details come to light quickly through media investigation. But is there a risk that pointing fingers too early - before investigations are complete - creates unfair blame? How should journalists balance speed with accuracy?

5

Trust in Systems

Spain has Europe's largest high-speed rail network and prides itself on safety. Will this disaster change how Spanish people feel about train travel? Think about your own country - have there been events that changed public trust in systems people relied on?

6

Learning from Tragedy

The 2013 Santiago disaster led to new safety systems being installed. What changes do you predict will come from this crash? In general, do you think major disasters are the only way to force meaningful safety improvements, or should we be able to prevent them proactively?
