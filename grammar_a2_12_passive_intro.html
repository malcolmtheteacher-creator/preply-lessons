Today: two ways to talk about things without saying who did them — is/are made (facts about now) and was/were stolen, built, found (things that happened) — so you can talk about where things come from and what happened to your things.

Where Things Come From: "Made in Italy"

For facts that are still true today — where things come from, what's normal, what's true in general.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday facts:

  • "These shoes are made in Italy."
  • "Coffee is grown in Brazil."
  • "English is spoken in many countries."

Before you check: what two ingredients appear in every bold phrase?

Check the pattern CLICK
am / is / are + past participle (made, grown, spoken). We choose "am" for I, "is" for he/she/it, "are" for we/you/they — for example, "I'm invited to my cousin's wedding."
am (I) / is (he, she, it) / are (we, you, they) + past participle
Careful: not every present-tense sentence needs the passive. Times and routines usually stay active: "The shop opens at 9" (NOT "is opened"), "The film starts at 8." Those are just facts about time — nobody is "doing" anything to the shop or the film.

What Happened: "My Bag Was Stolen"

For things that happened and finished — lost property, news, history.

Spot the pattern

Three things that happened:

  • "My bag was stolen last night."
  • "This bridge was built in 1900."
  • "The window was broken yesterday."

What's the structure this time? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
was / were + past participle. Same idea as before, but this time the action is finished and in the past.
was (I, he, she, it) / were (we, you, they) + past participle

From "someone did it" to "it was done"

"Someone took my seat."
→
"My seat was taken."

The thing you care about ("my seat") moves to the front. "Someone" disappears — you don't know or don't need to say who it was.

Your turn

2. Someone found my keys under the sofa.

Tell your flatmate: "My keys under the sofa."

3. Alexander Graham Bell invented the telephone in 1876.

Say it about the telephone: "The telephone in 1876."

Active or Passive?

Both forms exist for a reason. Here's how to choose.

Spot the pattern

Compare each pair:

  • "A local baker makes this bread." → "This bread is made fresh every morning."
  • "Somebody left an umbrella in the classroom." → "An umbrella was left in the classroom."
  • "Workers built the new bridge last year." → "The new bridge was built last year."

Why does English switch to the passive version each time? Say your idea before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
Because who did it is unknown or unimportant — the speaker wants to talk about the bread, the umbrella, the bridge, not about the person. When the doer really matters, English stays active: "My grandmother made this bread" (we care WHO).
Active: doer + verb + thing
Passive: thing + am/is/are/was/were + past participle

What Do You Remember?

First, a quick check on something more basic

1. Simple past tense — complete it: "Yesterday, my sister (clean) the kitchen."

2. Say it out loud: in an active sentence like "The chef cooked the pizza," which word usually comes first — the person doing the action, or the thing?

Compare with the answer CLICK
In an active sentence, the person (the subject) usually comes first. In a passive sentence, the thing comes first instead — that's the big difference from today: "The pizza was cooked."
