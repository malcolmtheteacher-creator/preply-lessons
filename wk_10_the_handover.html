The Handover

You leave on Friday. Four years of work, forty minutes, and one colleague who is already carrying too much. Being thorough is the one thing that won't help.

The Scene 🎬

It's Wednesday afternoon. A small meeting room with a table that's slightly too big for two people, and a laptop between you showing a folder you spent all of Tuesday tidying. On Friday you go — new job, or three months' leave, it doesn't really matter which. Priya is taking your work, and Priya is already full.

You have forty minutes, and you want to be thorough, because being thorough feels like doing it properly. It isn't. Nobody absorbs four years in forty minutes, and the enormous document you're planning to write is mostly a way of making yourself feel better about leaving, rather than something she will ever open. What she actually needs is much smaller and much harder to say: the three things that will hurt her, the one person the whole thing quietly depends on, and the honest admission of the job you never got round to.

Across the table, Priya is competent and perfectly pleasant about it, and somewhere under that she is annoyed — not with you exactly, but with the fact that your leaving has become her problem. She's far too professional to say so, which doesn't mean she isn't thinking it.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of a job, a project or even a flat you handed over to someone else. What did you tell them, and what did they actually need to know? Were they the same thing?
  2. If you disappeared tomorrow, what part of your work exists only in your head — no document, no system, just you? Be honest about how much of it there is.

Who's who

You
The One Leaving 🚪

Four years in this role, and the temptation is to prove it — to explain everything, hand over a beautiful folder, and leave feeling useful. Your real job today is to throw away nearly all of it and say the few things that matter.

The other voice
Priya 📋

Good at her job, and already doing one and a half of them. She'll take this on because that's who she is. She'd like you to be quick, useful and honest, roughly in that order — and she will not tell you that she's annoyed.

First: read the conversation in the next tab — and read it for what isn't there. Watch how often the speaker starts explaining something and drops it halfway, because it doesn't matter. Almost everything they know goes unsaid, and that's the reason the handover works.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's untidy on purpose: people cut themselves off, talk over each other, and leave sentences lying on the floor. That's what a real forty minutes sounds like.

PriyaRight. Sorry — I've got till, um, till about half two, because I've got the thing with — anyway. Will that do?
YouThat's plenty. Honestly. Look — I could talk for three hours, so stop me — what do you actually need?

YouNo, that's — yeah. Right. So. There are three things that'll bite you. Everything else is in the folder, and to be honest you'll probably never open the folder, and that's fine.

You—no, it's real. Sorry, go on.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Twice, Priya starts saying something about her own situation and stops herself. What was she about to say, do you think — and why doesn't she finish it?

2. The speaker names a person the whole job depends on. What is the practical piece of advice Priya walks away with — and why could no handover document ever have told her that?

3. Near the end, the speaker gives Priya two instructions about the future — one about how she should work, one about him. What are they, and what do they have in common?

1. She's about to say that she's already got too much on, and that this handover is landing on top of it. She stops because saying it out loud would sound like a complaint about a colleague who's leaving, and she'd rather be professional than fair to herself. The unfinished sentence is the whole feeling — she doesn't say it, but you can hear it.
2. Go and have a coffee with Ferran before she needs anything from him. It's the sort of thing that can't sit in a document because it isn't a process — it's a relationship, and writing "be friendly with Ferran" in a file would be both odd and useless. It only travels when one person tells another.
3. Don't copy his way of working, and don't keep ringing him after the first month. Both of them are the same move: he's removing himself, so that the job can actually become hers rather than a copy of his that she's babysitting.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the forty minutes. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Tone: flat, quick and a bit unsentimental. A handover goes wrong when it becomes a performance of how much you knew, so these phrases are all short, and several of them are slightly rude about your own work, which is the point. You're not proving anything — you're getting a few important things into someone else's head before she has to go to another meeting.

📐 Setting the shape

Phrase 1

You sit down. You know an enormous amount and she has forty minutes. Which opening hands the steering wheel to her instead of to your own memory?

A. "Right, let's start at the beginning and work forwards."
B. "I could talk for three hours, so stop me — what do you actually need?"
C. "I've put everything in a document, so just shout if anything's unclear."
B — "I could talk for three hours, so stop me — what do you actually need?"
It admits the problem out loud (there's far too much) and then makes her the one who decides what gets said, which is sensible because she knows her gaps and you don't. "What do you actually need" is doing real work there — "actually" quietly cancels the polite answer and asks for the honest one. A starts a lecture; C hands over a document instead of a conversation.
Phrase 2

You need to shrink four years to a number she can hold in her head, and give everything else a home so it doesn't feel abandoned. Put it in order:

Everything elsethat'll bite you.There are three thingsis in the folder.
"There are three things that'll bite you. Everything else is in the folder."
Three is small enough to remember, so the number itself is the gift. The second sentence matters just as much, because it lets you stop talking without seeming careless — everything you're not saying has somewhere to be.
If something bites you, it hurts you suddenly and without warning, usually because it was sitting there quietly all along. We say a problem will "come back and bite you" when it's been ignored. It's ordinary spoken English, not aggressive — a slightly grim, useful little word.

🧨 Naming the landmines

Phrase 3

One system reports itself as healthy every single month, and has done for years — and it's the most dangerous thing you're handing her. You want to say that in eight words. Complete it: "This is the one that looks fine and ___."

"This is the one that looks fine and isn't."
The flat contradiction at the end is what makes it land — you set up the reassurance and then take it away in one word, so she remembers it. It's also honest about the specific danger, which is not that the thing is broken but that it looks well.
Phrase 4

You can't tell her whether the warehouse handoff will fail, but you can tell her exactly where and roughly when. Complete it: "If it goes wrong, ___ ___ ___ ___, and it'll be a Thursday."

"If it goes wrong, it'll go wrong here, and it'll be a Thursday."
Repeating "go wrong" instead of finding a smarter second verb is what gives the line its weight, because the repetition sounds like someone who has watched it happen. The oddly specific ending is a real handover move: a vague warning ("watch out for the warehouse system") gets forgotten, whereas a strange precise one gets remembered on a Thursday.

🤝 The bit nobody writes down

Phrase 5

Every process here officially depends on systems. In practice it depends on one man in procurement, and she needs to know that today. Which line tells her the truth that the documentation can't?

A. "Ferran's a really good contact, you should introduce yourself."
B. "There's a note about Ferran somewhere in the shared drive."
C. "The thing that isn't in any document is that it all runs on Ferran."
C — "The thing that isn't in any document is that it all runs on Ferran."
It names the gap between the official version and the real one, which is the single most valuable thing a leaver can hand over, and it does it without any drama. A is friendly but tells her nothing she'd act on; B pretends the knowledge is written down somewhere, which is exactly the comfortable lie.
If something runs on a person, that person is what keeps it working — like an engine running on fuel. It's a slightly wry way of admitting that an organisation which looks like a machine is actually a few human beings doing favours for each other.
Phrase 6

The approval takes a fortnight according to the rulebook. In real life it takes two days, or a month, depending entirely on who's asking. Complete it — one word in each gap: "___ it's a two-week process. It ___."

"Officially it's a two-week process. It isn't."
"Officially" is the flag: it tells her that what follows is the version on paper, and the two-word sentence afterwards demolishes it without you having to explain the politics. It's a very English construction — you state the rule, pause, and simply decline to believe it.

🧹 Being honest about your own mess

Phrase 7

The supplier list has been a mess for three years. You knew. You kept pushing it down the list. Now she's inheriting it, and you have to say so — out loud, no excuse attached. Say it your way first, then reveal.

"I never got to this, and I should have."
Eight words, and crucially there's no "because" on the end. The moment you explain why you didn't do it, the sentence turns into a defence and she has to be nice to you about it, which wastes both of you. Admitting it plainly is also practical: she now knows the state of the thing before she trips over it.
Phrase 8

Much of how you work is habit rather than method, and she'll copy it out of politeness unless you stop her. Put it in order:

just becausemy way of doing itDon't inheritit's mine.
"Don't inherit my way of doing it just because it's mine."
It gives her permission she'd otherwise wait months for, and it's honest about why people copy a predecessor — not because the method is good, but because it's there.
You inherit things from someone who has gone: normally money or a house from a relative who's died, but at work it's used lightly for whatever the last person left behind — their spreadsheets, their systems, their mess. The joke buried in the word is that you don't get a choice about it.

🚪 Handing it over properly

Phrase 9

She's going to be cautious with your work, and cautious is no use to her. Which line actually lets go?

A. "I'd leave it as it is for the first few months, then see."
B. "It's yours now — you don't need my permission to change it."
C. "Just let me know before you change anything major, so I'm in the loop."
B — "It's yours now — you don't need my permission to change it."
It says the transfer out loud, which is worth doing, because otherwise a decent person will keep imagining you looking over her shoulder. A and C both sound generous and are quietly the opposite — they keep you in the chair after you've left it.
Phrase 10

You want to be available while she finds her feet, and then you want to be gone, because a leaver who's still on the phone in November is a problem. Complete it: "Ring me for a month. After that, honestly, ___."

"Ring me for a month. After that, honestly, don't."
The offer has an end date on it, which is the difference between a real offer and the vague "any time!" that everybody says and nobody means. "Honestly" softens the refusal into something friendly rather than cold, and it's also true — after a month you'd be guessing, and she'd know more than you.

🔑 Make them yours — before you hand anything over

Do this for your own work, not an imaginary job. Write in full sentences and then say them out loud, as if the other person were sitting opposite you.

The three things that'll bite whoever takes your work. Not the ten. Three.

Your Ferran — the person or relationship the work quietly runs on, that no document mentions.

The thing you never got to. Say it plainly, with no "because" on the end.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your forty minutes. Speak your answers out loud FIRST — and let them be untidy. Use the timer, because the whole difficulty of a handover is that the clock is the thing making your decisions for you.

The Twist

🎯 The question you hoped she wouldn't ask

Back in your own chair, thirty-five minutes gone, and Priya closes her notebook and says: "Can I ask you something? The two people who did this job before you both left as well. Why does everyone leave this role?" And you know the answer, because it's your answer — it's the reason you're going too, and it's about the job itself, not about you finding something shinier. Start the timer and handle it live. You can be honest, or you can be loyal to the company, or you can find some third thing — but you can't take thirty seconds of silence, and you can't lie to someone who's about to sit in your chair. Whatever you decide to say, say it in under a minute.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I asked her what she needed instead of deciding for her.
  • I named three things and let the rest go — including things I was proud of.
  • I told her about a person, not just a system.
  • I admitted something I hadn't done, without explaining it away.
  • I actually let go — no "check with me first", no open-ended offer.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that's hardest for you — run the twist again and see whether it comes out this time.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Every organisation keeps a surprising amount of itself in people's heads and nowhere else — which supplier to ring first, whose signature actually moves things, the report that's wrong in a way everyone has silently agreed to live with. None of it is written down, because it isn't the sort of thing that survives being written down, and so it moves from person to person in conversations like this one, or it doesn't move at all.

A good handover is you proving you were replaceable, which is what you should want and not, if you're honest, what you feel. Most of us half-hope things fall over slightly after we've gone — not badly, just enough that someone mentions it.

And there's a real sadness in it, too, that nobody quite names: you built something, it worked, and now you're explaining it quickly to someone who is already thinking about her next meeting. That's not a failure of the handover. That's just what handing something over is.

Two bigger questions

  1. If a job can be handed over in forty minutes, what were the four years for? Or is the opposite true — that only someone with four years behind them could know which forty minutes to choose?
  2. We say we want to be replaceable, and we don't entirely mean it. Where does that come from — pride, fear, or something more reasonable than either — and what would it actually cost you to make yourself genuinely easy to replace?
