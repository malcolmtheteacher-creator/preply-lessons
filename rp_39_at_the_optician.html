At the Optician

You've been holding menus at arm's length for a year and pretending it's the lighting. Today you face the machine that puffs air at your eye, the questions you're scared of answering wrong — and afterwards, a salesperson with a wall of designer frames and opinions about your face.

The Scene 🎬

The eye test is the only exam where trying to do well ruins the result. Your job today is to be honestly, relaxedly unsure.

It's been four years since your last test, which you know because the receptionist just told you, with an eyebrow. In the dark little room waits Mr Farooq the optometrist, the letter chart, and the strange intimate quiz of "better with one… or two?" — which panics almost everyone, because it feels like a test you can fail.

Here's the secret that changes the whole appointment: the machine doesn't need you to be right, it needs you to be honest. "They look the same" is a perfectly good answer — often the most useful one. Guessing confidently is the only way to get it wrong, because a lens ground to your guesses gives you headaches for two years.

Then, the second act: the frames. The prescription is science; the shop floor is retail, and the two share a doorway on purpose. Lens coatings, thinning, blue-light filters, the designer wall — some of it is genuinely worth it, some of it is the car-hire desk with better lighting. Your questions from that desk work here too: what's included, what's extra, and what happens if it doesn't feel right in a week.

  1. Why do people delay eye tests for years — cost, vanity, or the strange fear of being told the truth about ourselves?
  2. "Better with one, or two?" — why does a question with no wrong answer make everyone nervous?

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the optician — and for every appointment where honest uncertainty beats confident guessing, and every shop attached to a service. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Two rooms, two registers. In the dark room: slow, honest, unafraid of "the same". In the bright room: the friendly auditor from the car-hire desk — what's included, what's extra, what do I actually need. Neither room needs you to perform.

👁️ The dark room — honest data

Phrase 1

The job: opening with the symptom, plainly. Complete it:

"It's been a while — I think my eyes have ___."

"It's been a while — I think my eyes have changed."
No apology for the four years, no minimising — the fact and the suspicion, then let the professional work. And notice the detail that helps most: the concrete symptom ("menus at arm's length", "the phone font has grown"). Specific dailiness beats medical vocabulary every time; he can translate a travelling menu into dioptres, but only you can report it.

Take it anywhere: "It's been a while — I think ___ has changed." Doctors, dentists, mechanics: the honest re-entry line.
Phrase 2

The job: the answer everyone's afraid to give. Which one?

A. "Two! Definitely two. …Or one. Two. I'll say two."
B. "Honestly? They look the same."
C. "Whichever you think is best — you're the expert."
B — "Honestly? They look the same."
A is the panic-guess, and it grinds your panic into the lenses. C hands back a question only your eye can answer — he's the expert in optics; you're the world's only expert in what you're seeing. B is the answer people are scared to give and opticians love to hear: "the same" means the two options bracket your truth, and he's closing in. The test isn't a test. It's a measurement, and you're the instrument — instruments don't bluff.

Take it anywhere: "Honestly, they seem the same to me." Wine lists, paint samples, salary bands — licensed indifference is real information.
Phrase 3

The job: unwinding a rushed answer. Complete it:

"Could we do that one ___? I answered too ___."

"Could we do that one again? I answered too fast."
The correction nobody dares make, in the one room where corrections are free and always granted. A rushed "three!" left standing becomes a lens; asked again, it became "four — the edges sharpened", which is the real answer. The deeper skill transfers everywhere: noticing IN THE MOMENT that you answered to end the question rather than to answer it — and rewinding.

Take it anywhere: "Can I take that again? I answered too fast." Interviews, surveys, doctors, arguments.
Phrase 4

The job: translating the diagnosis into your actual days. Complete it:

"What does that mean for everyday life — ___, ___, small print?"

"What does it mean for everyday life — screens, driving, small print?"
"Mild myopia with presbyopia" is a fact about your eyes; "screens are where you'll feel it, driving's legal without" is a fact about your LIFE — and only the second one tells you what to do. Naming your big three (screens, driving, print) hands the expert the translation targets. Every diagnosis in every field deserves this question before you leave the room.

Take it anywhere: "What does that mean for everyday life?" Medical results, legal advice, survey reports — demand the lived translation.
Phrase 5

The job: the usage question that shapes everything downstream. Complete it:

"Do I need them ___ the ___, or just for reading?"

"Do I need them all the time, or just for reading?"
The either/or that determines what you should buy before you've seen a single frame — glasses-for-sometimes and glasses-for-always are different purchases at different prices with different trade-offs (the varifocal fortnight of "your brain complaining"). Settle the usage in the dark room, where the answer is medical, before entering the bright room, where every answer costs money.

Take it anywhere: "Do I need it all the time, or just for ___?" Medication, equipment, software licences — usage first, shopping second.

🕶️ The bright room — the friendly audit

Phrase 6

The job: the boundary between service and shop, drawn in one question. Put the pieces in order:

and what costsWhat's included in the standard price —extra?
"What's included in the standard price — and what costs extra?"
The car-hire question, wearing glasses. Asked FIRST — before the tour of the wall — it forces the whole price architecture into the open: the sixty-nine baseline, then each extra as a named, priced, decidable thing. Without it, extras arrive pre-bundled in a total you can't unpick. Every service with a shop attached gets this question at the doorway between them.

Take it anywhere: "What's included — and what's extra?" Dentists, vets, garages, weddings, funerals. Especially funerals.
Phrase 7

The job: testing an extra against YOUR case, not the general pitch. Complete it:

"My prescription's mild — do I actually ___ the thinner lenses, or is that for stronger eyes?"

"Do I actually need the thinner lenses, or is that for stronger eyes than mine?"
Every extra is worth it for SOMEONE — thin lenses genuinely rescue strong prescriptions from bottle-bottom glass. The question is whether that someone is you, and asking it with your specifics attached ("mine's mild") invites the honest answer Chloe actually gave: yours will be thin anyway. Watch the flip side too — the anti-glare survived the same test, because "screens all day" IS your case. The audit isn't about refusing everything; it's about matching extras to the life.

Take it anywhere: "Do I actually need ___, or is that for ___?" Insurance tiers, software plans, gym memberships.
Phrase 8

The job: the graceful exit from the designer wall. Complete it:

"I'd rather start with a ___ pair and see how I get ___."

"I'd rather start with a basic pair and see how I get on."
The walk-before-we-run of retail: a first pair of reading glasses is an experiment, and you don't run experiments in designer equipment. "See how I get on" makes it unarguable — it's not poverty, it's method — and the warm coda ("if we become inseparable, I'll come back and let you loose on the wall") leaves the salesperson a future instead of a defeat. She'll be twice as honest next visit. She was already more honest this one.

Take it anywhere: "I'd rather start basic and see how I get on." First bike, first kit, first anything — upgrade after the habit proves itself.
Phrase 9

The job: the safety net, checked before paying. Complete it:

"If they don't feel ___ after a week, can I come ___?"

"If they don't feel right after a week, can I come back?"
New glasses sometimes need a second visit — a nose-pad tweak, occasionally a remake — and the time to learn the policy is now, cheerfully, at the till, not in week two with a headache and a receipt you can't find. The answer here was generous (free recheck, three months; adjustments forever) — but you only own the policy once you've heard it said. The fallback question, one more time, in its natural habitat.

Take it anywhere: "If it doesn't feel right, can I come back?" Shoes, mattresses, haircuts-adjacent purchases, hearing aids.
Phrase 10

The job: closing both rooms well. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "You've made it all very clear — thank you. Five days, then me and my new arm's-length freedom."
"You've made it very clear" is precise praise for the thing that actually happened: a potentially murky transaction conducted in the open — and it rewards Chloe for the honesty about the thin lenses, which cost her commission. The little joke closes the human account. You'll be back in two years; leave the shop glad of it.

Take it anywhere: "You've made that very clear — thank you." For every professional who chose transparency when the murk would have paid better.

The Echo 🕰️

Two swindlers came to the city with an invisible cloth. Only the clever could see it, they said — and so the emperor saw it, and his ministers saw it, and the whole court admired the weight and fall of nothing at all. Everyone answered the same question — "better, isn't it? finer?" — and everyone gave the answer that made them look good instead of the answer that was true.

It took a child on a street corner — someone with nothing to prove — to say the measurement out loud: he isn't wearing anything. Andersen's tale is two centuries old and it plays out daily in every dark room with a letter chart: the fear that "I can't see the difference" makes us look stupid, when it's actually the only honest data anyone can offer. The emperor's court would have failed their eye tests magnificently — confident, unanimous, and wrong.

Be the child, not the courtier: "they look the same, honestly" is the sentence that gets you glasses that work — and it's free, which is more than anyone can say for the cloth.
