At the Optician

You've been holding menus at arm's length for a year and pretending it's the lighting. Today you face the machine that puffs air at your eye, the questions you're scared of answering wrong — and afterwards, a salesperson with a wall of designer frames and opinions about your face.

The Scene 🎬

The eye test is the only exam where trying to do well ruins the result. Your job today is to be honestly, relaxedly unsure.

It's been four years since your last test, which you know because the receptionist just told you, with an eyebrow. In the dark little room waits Mr Farooq the optometrist, the letter chart, and the strange intimate quiz of "better with one… or two?" — which panics almost everyone, because it feels like a test you can fail.

Here's the secret that changes the whole appointment: the machine doesn't need you to be right, it needs you to be honest. "They look the same" is a perfectly good answer — often the most useful one. Guessing confidently is the only way to get it wrong, because a lens ground to your guesses gives you headaches for two years.

Then, the second act: the frames. The prescription is science; the shop floor is retail, and the two share a doorway on purpose. Lens coatings, thinning, blue-light filters, the designer wall — some of it is genuinely worth it, some of it is the car-hire desk with better lighting. Your questions from that desk work here too: what's included, what's extra, and what happens if it doesn't feel right in a week.

  1. Why do people delay eye tests for years — cost, vanity, or the strange fear of being told the truth about ourselves?
  2. "Better with one, or two?" — why does a question with no wrong answer make everyone nervous?
YouFour years overdue, arm's-length menu reader, mildly afraid of answering the lens quiz wrong — and on a budget the designer wall doesn't respect.
Mr FarooqThe optometrist: patient, precise, has heard every panicked guess. Then Chloe on the shop floor: friendly, commission-adjacent, full of opinions about your cheekbones.

The Conversation 💬

The dark room first, then the bright one. Mr Farooq dims the lights and swings the strange goggles down.

Mr FarooqSo — what brings you in? Any changes you've noticed?
YouIt's been a while — I think my eyes have changed. Menus have started living at arm's length, and my phone's font has mysteriously grown twice this year.
Mr FarooqThe travelling menu — most honest symptom in the business. Right, chin on the rest. Read me the lowest line you can manage.
YouE… F… P… then it's either O or D, and honestly I'm guessing from there down.
Mr Farooq"Guessing from there down" is exactly what I need to know — never guess to please me, it only ruins your own glasses. Now: better with one… or two?
YouHm. Honestly? They look the same. Is that an allowed answer?
Mr FarooqIt's the BEST answer when it's true — "the same" means we're close. One more: better with three… or four?
YouThree — no, wait. Could we do that one again? I answered too fast.
Mr FarooqAlways allowed. Take your time… three… or four?
YouFour. Definitely four — the edges sharpened. So what's the verdict — and what does that mean for me, in practice? Screens, driving, reading?
Mr FarooqMild short-sight, plus your arms have officially become too short for your age — reading glasses territory. Driving's legal without, screens are where you'll feel the difference. Nothing dramatic; four years of drift.
YouThat's a relief, mostly. Do I need them all the time, or just for reading?
Mr FarooqReading and screens — take them off for the world. If you'd rather not juggle two behaviours, varifocals do both, but they cost more and take a fortnight of your brain complaining. Chloe outside will talk you through the options. *meaningfully* All of the options.
You*the bright room; Chloe approaches, radiating helpfulness* I heard that. Before we tour the designer wall: what's included in the standard price — frames, lenses, the lot? And what costs extra?
ChloeStandard pair from sixty-nine, basic lenses included. Extras: anti-glare coating, scratch-resist, thinner lenses if your prescription's strong — and these frames here are the new season, gorgeous on your face shape—
YouMy face shape thanks you, my budget doesn't. My prescription's mild, so — do I actually need the thinner lenses, or is that for stronger eyes than mine?
Chloe…Honestly? Yours are mild, standard lenses will be thin anyway. The anti-glare's worth it if you're on screens all day though — that one I'd genuinely have.
YouScreens all day is my whole life, so — anti-glare yes, season's-gorgeous no. I'd rather start with a basic pair and see how I get on. If reading glasses and I become inseparable, I'll come back and let you loose on the wall.
ChloeDeal. These three frames are all in the sixty-nine range and none of them will embarrass your face. Ready in five working days.
YouThe middle ones. Last question: if they don't feel right after a week — headaches, weirdness — can I come back?
ChloeFree recheck within three months, adjustments any time, forever — that part's not even a policy, it's just Tuesday. You've been easy, you know. Most people fight the wall and lose.
YouThe wall is very persuasive. You've made it all very clear — thank you. Five days, then. I'll see you when I can see you.

  2. In the shop, which extra does You refuse and which does You take — and on what logic?

The Echo 🕰️

Two swindlers came to the city with an invisible cloth. Only the clever could see it, they said — and so the emperor saw it, and his ministers saw it, and the whole court admired the weight and fall of nothing at all. Everyone answered the same question — "better, isn't it? finer?" — and everyone gave the answer that made them look good instead of the answer that was true.

It took a child on a street corner — someone with nothing to prove — to say the measurement out loud: he isn't wearing anything. Andersen's tale is two centuries old and it plays out daily in every dark room with a letter chart: the fear that "I can't see the difference" makes us look stupid, when it's actually the only honest data anyone can offer. The emperor's court would have failed their eye tests magnificently — confident, unanimous, and wrong.

Be the child, not the courtier: "they look the same, honestly" is the sentence that gets you glasses that work — and it's free, which is more than anyone can say for the cloth.
