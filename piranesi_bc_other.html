The Other

Only one other living person comes to the House. He is polite, helpful and always in a hurry. How do you decide whether to trust someone who is the only person you know?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — The only visitor

For the narrator, the Other is not only a person. He is the connection to everything outside the narrator's own routine. He brings small supplies and news of the search he is engaged in, and he tells Piranesi what is useful, what is not, and what he should or should not do.

The Other is courteous, but there is something slightly cold under the courtesy. He gives instructions as if they were requests. He seems pleased when Piranesi is cheerful and puzzled when he is not.

The narrator describes him fairly and with obvious respect. It takes the reader some time to ask whether that respect has been earned.

2. How does he behave?

1. Small supplies, news of his search, and advice on what Piranesi should do.

2. Courteous, but slightly cold; he gives instructions as if they were requests.

3. Whether the narrator's respect for him has been earned.

Discuss:
  • Think of a relationship in which one person decides what the other needs to know. How can you tell when it is helpful and when it is control?

Part 2 — Small signs

Little things begin to accumulate. The Other's explanations are tidy, perhaps too tidy. He answers some questions at length and avoids others. He worries aloud about Piranesi's health and sometimes says that he is not quite himself, which has the effect of making Piranesi doubt his own judgement.

The narrator notices this in his own way. He does not accuse anyone. He records, compares and waits. What changes is the kind of question he writes in his notes: not only what happened but who told him so.

This is the point at which the book becomes a quiet kind of thriller. Nothing explodes. The tension comes from tone and from the gap between what is said and what is done.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

2. What does he say about Piranesi's health?

3. Where does the tension come from?

1. At length on some, and he avoids others.

2. That Piranesi is not quite himself, which makes him doubt his own judgement.

3. From tone, and from the gap between what is said and what is done.

Discuss:
  • Have you ever been told you were not quite yourself by someone who gained from your doubt? How did you decide what to believe?

Part 3 — Choosing whom to believe

Eventually signs appear that the Other is not the only person in the House, and the narrator has to choose between competing accounts of who is trustworthy. He does not simply swap one belief for another. He tests things in small ways, watches what people do and compares it with what they said.

That, in the end, is the book's idea of trust: not a feeling but a habit of paying attention. It also makes the novel a good one to discuss in a group, because readers disagree about how much the narrator should have noticed and when.

Whichever side you take, it is worth asking what exactly made you trust or distrust each person, and how soon.

How the tool works

You can use the same habit the narrator develops. The Trust Check is a loop of four moves for deciding how far to believe someone: listen to what they say, watch what they do, ask what it costs them, and test a small thing.

Using it, step by step

  1. Listen: note exactly what was said, not what you assumed. He said it would take a week.
  2. Watch: compare it with what happens. It has now taken a month, and he has not mentioned it.
  3. Ask what it costs them: who gains if you believe this, and who loses? If I believe him, he keeps control of the schedule.
  4. Test: check one small thing that you can verify. If it holds, trust a little more. If not, trust a little less. Never decide on one test.

Try it yourself

Each sentence describes something a person might do in a relationship of trust. Which move of the Trust Check does it belong to?

1. I wrote down exactly what she promised, word for word.
2. I noticed that he agreed to everything but never did any of it.
3. I asked myself who would benefit if I believed the story.
4. I checked one small fact he had given me, to see if it was true.
5. She says she is busy, but her calendar was empty.
6. What was the exact wording he used about the date?
7. If I agree, will he gain anything from it, and what will I lose?
8. Let me ask a question whose answer I already know.

1. Listen — it records the statement itself.

2. Watch — it compares what is said with what is done.

3. Ask what it costs — it looks for the interest behind the words.

4. Test — a small, checkable step.

5. Watch — behaviour versus statement.

6. Listen — it focuses on what was actually said.

7. Ask what it costs — it weighs gain and loss.

8. Test — a simple way to check reliability.

Where the tool breaks

Do not become a detective in every relationship. Most people most of the time deserve a little trust. Use the check when something does not add up.

One test is not enough. A single lie can be a mistake; a pattern is information.

Your own motives matter too. Sometimes we want to believe, or to doubt. Ask what you want to be true.

Talk it through:
  • Which move do you tend to skip, and what has it cost you?
  • Is it possible to trust someone and also check what they say?

Language — Reading between the lines

To talk about what is implied and not stated, English has a handful of structures that make careful, polite and precise judgements.

Reporting what people imply: He implied that she was not well. She suggested that the plan had changed. It sounded as if he was hiding something. Use imply, suggest, hint for meanings that are not stated.
Hedging your own judgement: It may be that he was trying to help. Perhaps he simply did not know. I get the impression that he was not telling the whole story. The hedge keeps your claim fair.
Contrast for tone: He was polite, but there was something cold in it. Although he sounded kind, he never answered the question. Use but, although, yet to hold two readings together.
