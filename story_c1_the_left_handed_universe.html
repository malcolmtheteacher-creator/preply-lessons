The story, in three parts

A true story, told here in three parts. Everything in it happened — the dates, the temperatures, the bet. Read each part; after it, try the quick check from memory, then talk about the question.

Part 1 — The question nobody wanted to ask

In 1956 physics had a small, ugly problem called the theta–tau puzzle. Two particles had been found that were identical in every measurable way — same mass, same lifetime — except that one decayed into two pions and the other into three. Under the rules, that should have been impossible: the two decays had opposite 'parity', and parity, everyone knew, was conserved. Parity is mirror symmetry. It says that the laws of nature cannot tell left from right: film any experiment, project the film in a mirror, and what you see is also a possible experiment. It had been assumed for so long that nobody thought of it as an assumption. It was just how the universe was.

Two young theorists at Columbia University and the Institute for Advanced Study, Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang, did something unusual that summer. Instead of inventing a clever explanation to save the rule, they asked whether the rule had ever been tested. They went through the literature. For gravity, for electromagnetism, for the strong force that holds nuclei together, the evidence for mirror symmetry was overwhelming. For the weak force — the force responsible for radioactive beta decay — there was none. Nobody had ever checked. In October they published a paper suggesting that parity might simply not be conserved in weak interactions, and proposing experiments that could decide it.

The reaction was not excitement. Wolfgang Pauli, one of the founders of quantum mechanics, wrote to a colleague that he could not believe God was a weak left-hander, and offered to bet a large sum that the experiments would find nothing. Richard Feynman took a bet at fifty to one against. Most physicists thought the question was barely worth asking: the idea that the universe might secretly prefer one hand over the other seemed not so much wrong as absurd. The experiments Lee and Yang had proposed were also fiendishly difficult, which was a good excuse for not doing them.

One person did not think it absurd. Chien-Shiung Wu, a professor at Columbia, was the most exacting experimenter in beta decay alive. Born near Shanghai in 1912, she had come to America in 1936 to study, had worked on the Manhattan Project, and had spent a decade cleaning up other people's careless measurements of exactly the kind of decay Lee and Yang were talking about. Lee had come to her for advice before the paper was written. When she read it, she understood at once that there was a way to do the test — and that if she did not do it immediately, somebody else would.

Part 2 — A hundredth of a degree

The experiment Wu designed sounds simple and was anything but. Take cobalt-60, a radioactive nucleus that decays by emitting an electron. Each nucleus has a spin, a built-in direction like a tiny arrow. Line up all the arrows so they point the same way, then count the electrons: how many fly out along the direction of the spin, and how many against it? In a mirror-symmetric universe, the two numbers must be equal, because a mirror turns one direction into the other and the laws cannot know the difference. If more electrons came out one way than the other, the universe was, in a precise sense, left-handed.

The difficulty was the lining up. Nuclei are jostled by heat; at any ordinary temperature their spins point every which way. To align them you need a powerful magnetic field and a temperature so low that thermal motion almost stops — about a hundredth of a degree above absolute zero. Only a handful of laboratories in the world could reach it. One of them was the National Bureau of Standards in Washington, where Ernest Ambler and a small team had mastered a technique called adiabatic demagnetisation, which cools a crystal by switching off the field that had been holding its atoms in order. Wu needed their refrigerator, their crystal and their patience; they needed her cobalt, her detectors and her certainty about what a clean result would look like.

There was a cost. Wu and her husband, the physicist Luke Yuan, had booked passage on the Queen Elizabeth for a long-planned journey to Asia — the first time either had been back in twenty years. Wu cancelled. Her husband went alone. Through the autumn she shuttled between New York and Washington, teaching at Columbia during the week and living in the Washington laboratory at weekends, and the team fought a series of exhausting technical battles: the cobalt had to sit on the surface of the cooling crystal in a layer thin enough not to warm it, the detectors had to work inside the cryostat, and every time the apparatus warmed by a fraction of a degree the alignment was lost and the count had to begin again.

In the days before Christmas 1956 the first counts came in. The electrons were not symmetric. Far more of them were flying out against the direction of the nuclear spin than along it. Wu telephoned Lee and Yang, but she refused to announce anything. Being meticulous, she assumed the effect was an error in her own apparatus until it had been proven otherwise, and spent two more weeks trying to destroy her own result — reversing the field, checking the thermometry, ruling out every alternative she could imagine. It survived everything. By early January the team was certain. The asymmetry was enormous, not a subtle statistical whisper but a shout: the mirror image of their experiment was one that could not happen in this universe.

Part 3 — Who gets the credit

Word travelled fast. At Columbia, Leon Lederman heard of Wu's preliminary result over lunch in early January and, with two colleagues, redesigned an experiment on decaying muons that was already running; within days they had an independent confirmation. On 15 January 1957 Columbia held a press conference — a rare thing in physics then — and the New York Times put the story on its front page: a basic concept of physics had been overthrown. Pauli, who had been so sure, wrote that he was glad he had not actually placed the bet, because he could have afforded to lose some reputation but not the money. Feynman paid up.

The consequences went far beyond a puzzle about two particles. If the weak force distinguishes left from right, then the universe is not indifferent to handedness after all; the neutrino, it turned out, comes in only one form, always spinning one way relative to its motion, like a screw that only turns anticlockwise. Within a year physicists had found that other cherished symmetries were also broken by the weak force, and the search for what is and is not conserved became one of the central themes of the century, leading eventually to the questions of why the universe contains matter and not antimatter. All of it began with the assumption that nobody had thought to test, and the woman who tested it.

In the autumn of 1957 the Nobel Prize in Physics was awarded to Lee and Yang, for the question. It was not awarded to Wu, for the answer. The reasons have been argued about ever since: the prize's reluctance to honour experimenters who confirm a theory; the number of people on her team; the fact that Lederman's group had published almost simultaneously; and, many have concluded, the fact that she was a woman and an immigrant in a profession that gave its prizes to men. Wu never campaigned. She said in later years that she was hurt by it, and went on working. She became the first woman to be president of the American Physical Society; she received the inaugural Wolf Prize in 1978, an award created partly to recognise those the Nobel had passed over; students called her the First Lady of Physics, and a generation of experimenters learned from her that a result is not a result until you have failed to break it.

She died in 1997. The cobalt experiment is still taught in every university physics course in the world, usually under a name that gives the credit correctly at last: the Wu experiment.

The grammar this story runs on: cause and effect

To explain physics — or anything — you have to connect a cause to its effect precisely: what makes what happen, and how surely. Vague connectors ('and then', 'so') make an explanation sound like a story. These make it sound like an argument.

1. Giving the cause.
• because of / due to / owing to + noun: "The spins point every way due to thermal motion."
• as / since + clause (the reason is already known): "Since nobody had tested it, the assumption survived."
• result from / stem from / arise from: "The asymmetry results from the weak force."
2. Giving the effect.
• lead to / result in / give rise to / bring about + noun: "Cooling the crystal leads to alignment."
• thereby + -ing (formal — the effect of the action just described): "They switched off the field, thereby cooling the crystal."
• which meant (that) / which is why: "The count fell to zero, which meant that the alignment had been lost."
3. Purpose and condition.
• so that / in order that + clause; so as to / in order to + verb: "She cooled the sample so that the nuclei would align."
• If… then for logical consequence: "If parity is conserved, then the two counts must be equal."
The stronger your verb — results in rather than leads to — the more certain you sound. Choose deliberately.

Why this story stays with you

Physics likes to present itself as a machine for finding truth, and this is one of the cases where the machine worked: a puzzle, a brave question, a decisive experiment, a front page, a prize, a new picture of the world, all inside eighteen months. But the story also shows the machine's parts. Pauli and Feynman — two of the greatest minds of the century — were sure enough to bet, and were wrong, not because they were foolish but because an assumption that has never been tested feels exactly like a fact. Lee and Yang's genius was to notice a feeling and call it an assumption.

And then there is the question of credit. The Nobel committee has rules, and the rules favoured the theorists; the omission of Wu was, on paper, explicable. It was also part of a pattern — Lise Meitner, Rosalind Franklin, Jocelyn Bell Burnell — of women whose experiments and observations made other people's prizes possible. Wu's own answer was not a campaign but a standard: the two weeks she spent trying to destroy her own result are the reason the result stood. Whatever the committee decided, the universe was left-handed, and she was the one who had found out.

Look again at the mirror. Before 1957, if you had been shown a film of any physical process and its mirror image, you could not have said which was the real world. After Wu, you can: watch a cobalt nucleus decay and see which way the electrons go. The mirror world is not merely different — it is impossible. What does it mean that the universe has a handedness at all?
