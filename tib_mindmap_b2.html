The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — Notes covered in mud

Drawing ideas as branches is very old. In the third century, the philosopher Porphyry of Tyre showed Aristotle's categories as a branching diagram, and in the Middle Ages the thinker Ramon Llull used similar techniques.

Tony Buzan was born in 1942 in Palmers Green, north London, and moved with his family to Vancouver, Canada, when he was 12. Studying subjects including psychology, English and mathematics at the University of British Columbia, he noticed something odd. The students who did well did not necessarily have the neatest notes. Theirs were often messy — full of doodles, arrows and key words.

He began taking notes the same way. “It was as if I'd been driving all my life with my windscreen caked in mud,” he said later, “and suddenly I could see clearly.”

2. They were often messy, with doodles, arrows and key words — not neat.

Part 2 — Use Your Head

In the 1970s Buzan presented a BBC television series called Use Your Head, and in 1974 the BBC published a book of the same name. (Sources differ on whether the series began in 1973 or 1974.) It introduced viewers to his mind maps: one central idea in the middle of the page, main themes branching out from it, and details branching off those.

The rules were simple: start in the centre, use one keyword per line, use colour and pictures, and let the branches show how the ideas connect. His BBC books sold more than three million copies. He wrote around 80 books, and in 1991 he founded the World Memory Championships.

Buzan's organisation registered “Mind Maps” as a trademark, and in 2006 Buzan launched his own mind-mapping software, iMindMap. The doodles had become a brand.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the name of Buzan's BBC series?

2. What are the basic rules of a mind map?

3. What did Buzan's organisation do with the name?

1. Use Your Head.

2. Start in the centre, one keyword per line, use colour and pictures, show the connections with branches.

3. It registered “Mind Maps” as a trademark.

Discuss:
  • Should anyone be able to own the name of a way of thinking?

Part 3 — Genius or salesman?

Buzan was a showman, and not everyone was convinced. His obituary in The Times noted that some people thought him “little more than a good salesman”. Critics also pointed out that branching diagrams were centuries older than Buzan.

So what does the research say? In a 2002 study of 50 medical students in London, the group taught to use mind maps remembered about 10% more of a text a week later than a group using their own study methods — a modest gain, and not a certain one. The mind-map group was also less motivated to use the technique.

Buzan died in April 2019. Mind maps did not turn ordinary people into geniuses, as some of the advertising suggested. But for planning a project, an essay or a wedding, a single page that shows the whole picture can still be surprisingly useful.

1. That he was “little more than a good salesman”.

2. The mind-map group remembered about 10% more after a week — a modest, uncertain gain.

3. The students using mind maps were less motivated to use the technique.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think some people love mind maps and others can't stand them?

How the tool works

The topic goes in the middle. Main ideas branch out from it; details branch off the main ideas. The further from the centre, the more specific the idea.

Using it, step by step

  1. Turn the page sideways and write the topic in the centre — with a picture if you can.
  2. Draw thick lines for the main ideas, one keyword on each.
  3. Add thinner branches for details. Jump around freely; there's no right order.
  4. Look for connections between branches and draw arrows — this is often where the best ideas appear.

Try it yourself

A couple are mind-mapping their wedding. Where does each item go: the centre, a main branch, or a sub-branch?

1. Venue
2. Garden or hall?
3. Our wedding
4. Vegetarian menu
5. Food
6. Invite the cousins from Spain

1. Main branch — one of the big themes of the plan.

2. Sub-branch — a detail of the Venue branch.

3. Centre — the topic of the whole map.

4. Sub-branch — a detail of the Food branch.

5. Main branch — another big theme.

6. Sub-branch — a detail of a Guests branch.

Where the tool breaks

Pretty isn't the same as clear. It's easy to spend more time on colours than on thinking.

One centre only. Problems with several equally important parts don't fit a single middle point.

Hard to share. A map that makes perfect sense to you can be a mystery to everyone else.

Grammar — phrasal verbs for ideas and notes

When people talk about ideas and notes, they use a lot of phrasal verbs: come up with an idea, jot down a word, sum up a meeting. The tricky part is where the object goes.

Phrasal verbs = verb + particle (up, down, out, over…). The meaning often isn't obvious from the parts.
“jot down” = write quickly  “sum up” = summarise  “go over” = review  “leave out” = omit  “branch out” = move into new areas
Separable verbs: a noun object can go before or after the particle.
“Write down the word.” = “Write the word down.”
Inseparable verbs: the object always comes after the particle.
“She came across an old notebook.” (= found it by chance)  “Look at the centre.”
Three-part verbs keep all their parts together.
“come up with a plan”  “run out of space”  “keep up with the lecture”
Watch out: with separable verbs, a pronoun must go in the middle: ~~Write down it~~ → “Write it down.” ~~Sum up them~~ → “Sum them up.”
