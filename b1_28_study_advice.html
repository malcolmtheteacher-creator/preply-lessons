Second Conditional: Giving Advice

We use the second conditional to give advice about hypothetical situations. It sounds polite and thoughtful.

Example Sentences

"If I were you, I'd find someone to practise with."
"If you had a private teacher, you'd save some time."
"How would you feel if you spent an hour each day?"
"You'd feel a lot better if you made a detailed study plan."

True or False?

Read each statement and decide if it's true or false:

1. After "would" we use the infinitive.

TRUE — "you'd find", "you'd save", "you'd feel"

2. We use the present simple after "if".

FALSE — We use the past simple after "if": "If you had...", "If you spent..."

3. The subject and "would" don't change position for questions.

FALSE — In questions, the subject and "would" switch places: "How would you feel?" and "You'd feel..."

4. These are all hypothetical suggestions.

TRUE — They describe situations that aren't real, and we're giving friendly suggestions.

5. In speech, "you" + "would" = "you'd".

TRUE — "you'd" is the contraction of "you would"

Practice: Complete the Advice

Read each problem and complete the second conditional sentence:

Problem: "I don't have the energy to study."

Sentence: If you ________ (rest/sleep more), you'd have more energy.

If you rested more or got more sleep, you'd have more energy.

Problem: "I don't know what I should focus on."

Sentence: If you ________ (plan), you'd have a better idea.

If you made a study plan or planned your studies, you'd have a better idea.

Problem: "I know the grammar rules, but I can't use them in real conversations."

Sentence: If you ________ (practise), you'd be able to use grammar in conversations.

If you practised speaking or talked to native speakers, you'd be able to use grammar in conversations.

Modals for Advice

We can use different modals to give advice with different levels of strength. Pay attention to which ones sound strong and which sound more relaxed.

Advice Sentences

Listen or read carefully. Notice which modals are stressed and which are unstressed in natural speech.

"You ought to invest in an expensive coursebook."
(unstressed — soft advice)
"You could enrol on an English course but you don't have to."
(could = stressed; don't have to = unstressed — optional choice)
"You might need to make a study plan."
(unstressed — possibility, not strong advice)
"You don't have to study every day but you really ought to."
(don't have to = unstressed; ought to = stressed — gentle but clear advice)
"You ought to read lots of different types of text."
(unstressed — good idea)
"You need to focus on memorising all the grammar rules."
(unstressed — necessary but not urgent)
"You don't have to communicate in real situations."
(unstressed — optional, not required)

Discussion Questions

Which sentence gives the strongest advice?

Probably "You need to focus on memorising all the grammar rules" — it sounds most necessary.

Do you agree with these pieces of advice?

Which ones are most helpful for your English learning? Which ones do you disagree with?

For example: "I agree that I ought to read different types of text, but I don't think I need to memorise all the grammar rules — I think I just need to use them!"

Quick Reference: Strength of Advice

STRONGEST: You need to, You have to, You must
MEDIUM: You ought to, You should
GENTLE: You could, You might, You don't have to

Talking About Study Problems

Think of 1–2 problems with your English learning. Prepare to talk about them using this framework.

Framework: Four Steps

1. What is the problem?

Example: "I don't have energy to study after work."

2. How does it affect your studies?

Example: "I can only study for short periods."

3. What have you already tried?

Example: "I have tried getting more sleep, but it doesn't always help."

4. What solutions have people recommended?

Example: "People say I should listen to podcasts — that way I can study while I'm tired."

Practice Questions

Use the framework above to answer these questions:

What are your biggest challenges when learning English?

Use the four-step framework to explain.

In your experience, what works best for you?

What study methods have you found helpful?

When you speak:
  • Use second conditional to suggest solutions: "If you tried listening to podcasts, you'd..."
  • Use modals to give advice: "You could...", "You might...", "You ought to..."
  • Be a good listener when your partner shares their problems

Discussion

These are conversation questions. Try to give longer answers and explain your ideas.

Explore These Questions

What are some of the biggest challenges you've faced when learning English?

Think beyond just grammar or vocabulary. What about motivation, time, confidence, or context?

Do you think having a teacher is absolutely necessary?

What are the advantages and disadvantages of learning with a teacher vs. on your own?

How important is having a study partner?

Have you studied with a partner before? What was your experience?

Have you tried language learning apps? Have they been effective for you?

Which apps or platforms have you used? What did you like or dislike about them?

What strategies have been key in your language learning journey?

What do you do regularly that really helps you improve?

Tip for deeper conversation:

Try to connect your answers to personal experience. Don't just say "yes" or "no" — give examples, explain why something works for you, and ask follow-up questions about your partner's experiences.
