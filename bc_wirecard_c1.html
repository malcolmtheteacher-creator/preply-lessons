The Company That Wasn't There

Wirecard was Germany's fintech champion, worth more than Deutsche Bank — until an auditor asked to see the money, and 1.9 billion euros wasn't anywhere.

Today: you'll follow the collapse of Germany's biggest accounting fraud — a story told entirely in competing claims — and you'll master the verbs that keep claims at the right distance: the company claimed, the journalists alleged, the auditors conceded, the regulator denied.
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • When a respected institution and a journalist tell opposite stories, how do you decide whom to believe — and how long do you suspend judgement?
  • What does a country want so badly from a "national champion" that it stops checking?
  • Auditors sign accounts every year for companies that later collapse. What do you think an audit actually verifies?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Nearly two billion euros was said to sit in accounts held by a lawyer on the company's behalf — money parked with a third party under agreed conditions, in escrow.
escrow (n) — funds held by a neutral third party until conditions are met. In escrow; an escrow account; an escrow agent. The case's fatal address: the money was always somewhere else, held by someone else.
2. The profitable business, the company said, was payment processing done by partner firms in Asia on its behalf — activity the auditors could see only in the partners' paperwork. Everything hung on this third-party business.
third-party (adj) — done by an outside firm rather than in-house. The structural genius of the fraud: the real-looking business was, by design, always in somebody else's books.
3. Investors who believed the journalists sold the shares they did not own, hoping to buy back cheaper after the fall. The company called these short sellers criminals; they turned out to be the market's only working alarm.
a short seller (n) — an investor positioned to profit from a fall. To short a stock; a short position; a short-selling ban. Reviled in booms, vindicated in frauds — this case is their monument.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: insolvency · an audit · a whistleblower. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means the independent examination of a company's accounts?
an audit (n/v) — to sign off the accounts; an unqualified opinion (= a clean one); a special audit — the one KPMG performed here, with consequences.
2. Which term means an insider who reports wrongdoing, usually at personal risk?
a whistleblower (n) — to blow the whistle on. The Singapore legal staff who reported internal book-cooking in 2018 were among several this case ignored.
3. Which term means the legal state of being unable to pay your debts?
insolvency (n) — to file for insolvency (German: Insolvenz) — which Wirecard did within a week of the money not existing, the first DAX company ever to do so.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Instead of investigating the company, the regulator investigated the journalists — and banned betting against the shares. BaFin had, critics said, shot the messenger. This means…
  (a) protected the source   (b) punished the bringer of unwelcome news instead of acting on it   (c) leaked the story
(b) — to shoot the messenger. The 2019 sequence — short-selling ban plus criminal complaints against Financial Times reporters — is the textbook institutional example.
2. The chief operating officer, the man closest to the missing business, vanished days before the collapse and remains a fugitive, reportedly at large abroad. At large means…
  (a) under arrest   (b) not yet caught   (c) cooperating with police
(b) — at large — free despite being sought. Jan Marsalek's flight — via private jet, reportedly to Belarus and on to Russia — moved the case from business pages to espionage coverage, where parts of it remain.

D · Collocation & word building

1. You know to cook. Complete the fraud collocations: to cook the ___ · to ___ revenue (invent it) · to ___ profits (state them too high).
To cook the books · to fabricate revenue · to overstate / inflate profits. The opposite pair: to understate, and the neutral to restate — what accounts do after the truth arrives.
2. One verb completes all three, from the auditor's world: to ___ a balance (check it exists) · to ___ a signature · unable to ___ that the accounts were accurate.
to verify — and its professional sibling to confirm. The entire case compresses into one auditing sentence: for years, balances were confirmed by documents instead of verified at the bank.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — The champion Germany wanted

Wirecard began in the late 1990s on the unglamorous edge of the internet, processing payments for businesses banks preferred not to discuss, and spent two decades ascending towards respectability: a banking licence, blue-chip clients, and a story perfectly shaped for its moment. Germany — an industrial superpower embarrassingly short of technology champions — was offered a home-grown fintech conquering the world's payments from a suburb of Munich. Its chief executive, Markus Braun, an Austrian who dressed and spoke in deliberate imitation of Silicon Valley, promised compounding double-digit growth, and delivered it, on paper, every single year.

The engine of the miracle, according to the accounts, was third-party acquiring: in markets where Wirecard lacked its own licences — notably across Asia — partner companies processed payments on its behalf, and the resulting profits, ultimately said to be parked in escrow accounts in the Philippines, made up the majority of the group's reported earnings. The structure had one remarkable property: the group's most profitable activity was permanently located in other people's companies, other people's paperwork and other people's banks.

In 2018 the ascent reached its summit. Wirecard entered the DAX, the index of Germany's thirty most valuable listed companies, displacing Commerzbank — the fintech evicting the traditional bank, a symbolism nobody missed. At its peak the company was valued around twenty-four billion euros, more than Deutsche Bank. Politicians promoted it abroad; index funds were obliged to buy it; and the sheer institutional weight now attached to its success — national pride, regulatory reputation, a decade of clean audits — had quietly become part of its defences.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What story made Wirecard so valuable to Germany specifically?

2. Describe the third-party structure and its "remarkable property".

1. A home-grown technology champion for a country that had none: a Munich fintech beating Silicon Valley at payments, delivering double-digit growth every year, crowned by DAX membership in place of Commerzbank at a valuation above Deutsche Bank's.

2. Partner firms in Asia processed payments where Wirecard had no licences; the profits — most of the group's earnings — sat in escrow in the Philippines. The core business was thus permanently inside other people's companies and books, unverifiable by construction.

Part 2 — Five years of warnings, shot down

The extraordinary feature of this fraud is not that it was hidden but that it was reported, publicly, for years. From 2015 the Financial Times journalist Dan McCrum published a series — under the running title House of Wirecard — alleging inflated balances and suspicious accounting; short sellers circulated detailed dossiers; and in 2018 whistleblowers in the company's own Singapore office reported internal evidence of forged and backdated contracts, triggering a police raid there in early 2019. Each time, the company responded with the same repertoire: it denied everything, attributed the stories to a criminal conspiracy between journalists and short sellers, sued, and announced growth.

What happened next remains the most painful part for Germany's institutions. In February 2019, as the FT's Singapore reporting hit the shares, the financial regulator BaFin did something without precedent: it banned short-selling of a single stock — protecting Wirecard — and filed criminal complaints against the FT journalists. The message a national regulator sent, in effect, was that the most plausible explanation for detailed allegations of fraud was market manipulation by the reporters of a major newspaper. The auditors, EY, had signed unqualified opinions for a decade — relying, for the escrow billions, on documents and confirmations rather than independently verifying the cash at the banks said to hold it. Under pressure, Wirecard commissioned a special audit from KPMG; its report, published in April 2020, was written in careful auditor's prose whose substance was devastating: for the third-party business generating most of the profits, KPMG could not confirm that it existed.

The end then came with astonishing speed. In June 2020, EY — finally demanding direct confirmation — was presented with documents from two Philippine banks attesting to the 1.9 billion euros; the banks themselves declared the documents forgeries and the accounts nonexistent. The company's trustee had, in the banks' words, no such deposits. On 18 June Wirecard announced that 1.9 billion euros "probably do not exist"; Braun resigned and was arrested within days; Jan Marsalek, the chief operating officer responsible for the Asian business, flew out of Austria on a chartered jet and disappeared — reportedly via Belarus into Russia, where reporting has since tied him to intelligence circles. A week after the announcement, Wirecard filed for insolvency: the first DAX company in history to collapse into it, roughly two years after joining the index.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. List the warning systems that fired between 2015 and 2019, and the company's standard response.

2. What did BaFin do in 2019, and what finally broke the fraud in June 2020?

1. FT investigations from 2015, short sellers' dossiers, Singapore whistleblowers in 2018, a police raid in 2019, and finally KPMG's special audit. The company's repertoire each time: deny, blame a journalist-short-seller conspiracy, sue, announce growth.

2. BaFin banned short-selling of the stock and filed criminal complaints against the journalists — protecting the fraud. The break came when EY demanded direct bank confirmation: the Philippine banks declared the documents forged and the €1.9bn nonexistent.

Part 3 — The reckoning, and the verbs

The aftermath rewired German financial supervision. A parliamentary inquiry ran for months; BaFin's leadership departed and the agency was restructured with new powers; the audit reform law that followed tightened rotation and liability; EY faced investor litigation, professional sanctions and a two-year ban on taking new listed-company audit mandates in Germany; and the collapse vaporised index-fund savings across the country — Wirecard having been, until days before the end, a mandatory holding for anyone tracking the DAX. Braun, who has denied wrongdoing and portrayed himself as deceived alongside the shareholders, went on trial in Munich in 2022 in proceedings that ran for years. Marsalek remains at large, on Europe's most-wanted lists, the subject of espionage reporting that grows stranger by the year. McCrum and the FT, once under criminal complaint, collected the profession's major awards.

Look back and the case is a five-year experiment in how claims are weighed. On one side: a company that claimed, asserted, insisted and denied; an auditor that confirmed; a regulator that endorsed the company's framing to the point of prosecuting its critics. On the other: journalists who alleged, whistleblowers who reported, short sellers who bet. Every institution with formal authority pointed one way; every party with skin in the game and no reputational stake in the champion pointed the other. The deciding evidence, when it finally came, took one letter from two banks. It could have been requested in any of the preceding six years.

Which is why this case belongs to the grammar of reporting verbs. For five years, the difference between the FT alleges and Wirecard confirms carried billions of euros of consequence; retail investors who read "allege" as "probably false" and "audited" as "true" paid for the distinction with their savings. The discipline the case teaches is brutal and simple: a claim inherits the verification behind it, not the authority in front of it — and the verbs we choose when repeating claims are either transmitting that difference or erasing it.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Name three institutional consequences of the collapse.

1. Any three: BaFin's leadership change and restructuring with new powers; the audit reform law; EY's sanctions, litigation and new-mandate ban; the parliamentary inquiry; DAX index-rule changes; the criminal trials.

2. A claim inherits the verification behind it, not the authority in front of it — an audit that confirmed documents was worth less than a journalist who verified facts, whatever the letterheads said.

Grammar — reporting verbs, and the distance they keep

Every sentence that repeats someone's claim makes a second, hidden claim: how much the speaker vouches for it. English encodes that in the reporting verb — and this case ran for five years on people reading those verbs carelessly.

The commitment scale, low to high:
• allege / claim / assert / maintain / insist — the speaker takes no responsibility for truth: "The FT alleged that the balances were inflated." (claim hints at doubt; insist adds resistance to pushback)
• state / report / announce / note — neutral transmission: "The company announced record growth."
• acknowledge / admit / concede — the reported speaker accepts something unwelcome: "EY conceded that it had not verified the balances directly."
• confirm / verify / establish / demonstrate — the truth is vouched for: "The banks confirmed that no such accounts existed." Use these only when verification actually happened.
• deny / dispute / dismiss / refute — rejection; and note the trap: refute means to disprove, not merely to reject. Wirecard denied for five years; it refuted nothing.
The grammar patterns that follow them:
• + that-clause: claim / admit / deny that…
• + -ing: deny doing, admit having done — "Braun denies orchestrating the fraud."
• + object + of + -ing: accuse someone of, suspect someone of
• + to-infinitive: claim to be / to have done — "The partners claimed to process billions."

Distance markers for extra caution: reportedly, allegedly, according to, is said to have, supposedly — "Marsalek reportedly fled to Russia." One word, full deniability.

Going deeper

A note on the record: criminal proceedings ran for years after the collapse and some findings remain contested; statements about individuals reflect charges, court proceedings and their own public positions — Braun maintains his innocence; Marsalek has never answered any charge.

What the case actually tests. First, the unfalsifiable core. The genius of the structure was not concealment but placement: the profitable business lived, by design, where no auditor's request could reach without extraordinary effort — other companies, other jurisdictions, other banks. Any claim structured so that checking it is someone else's job should be priced accordingly; the case's one-letter ending proves the check was always possible, merely never demanded.

Second, the champion's shield. Germany did not fail to see; it declined to look, because too much was invested in the thing being real — national technology pride, regulatory prestige, index credibility, a decade of professional sign-offs. The instructive horror of BaFin's 2019 move is that it was sincere: the institutions genuinely found "criminal journalists" more plausible than "fraudulent champion." The bias to audit in your own organisation is exactly that one: which of our beliefs would cost the most people the most face if false — and who is allowed to test it?

Third, the vindication of the unrespectable. The parties who were right — short sellers, a tabloid-adjacent-sounding investigation, fleeing whistleblowers — were precisely those the system codes as suspect, and the parties who were wrong wore its best letterheads. That is not an accident of this case but its mechanism: skin in the game buys accuracy, authority buys the benefit of the doubt, and frauds live in the spread between the two. The Nokia and VW cases showed truth failing to travel upward inside a company; Wirecard shows it failing to travel inward from outside — with the added lesson that when it finally arrives, it arrives all at once.
