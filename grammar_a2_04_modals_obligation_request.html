Rules & Obligations: Must & Have To

For rules and things you need to do — at work, at home, or anywhere.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday rules:

  • "You must wear a helmet on this building site."
  • "I have to finish this report by five o'clock."
  • "She has to wake up early for work."

Before you check: does "must" ever change its form? What about "have to"?

Check the pattern CLICK
must never changes — I must, you must, he must, they must. have to changes like any other verb — I/you/we/they have to, but he/she/it has to (same "-s" rule as any present simple verb).
must + base verb (no "to")
have to / has to + base verb
Watch out: "must" never takes "to". "You must to work" is wrong — it's just "You must work."
Watch out: with he/she/it, "have" becomes "has". "He have to go" is wrong — it's "He has to go."

Not Allowed or Not Necessary? Mustn't vs Don't Have To

Two negative forms that look similar but mean completely different things.

Spot the pattern

  • "You mustn't smoke in this building." (forbidden)
  • "You don't have to bring your own towel — the gym has some." (optional)
  • "We don't have to leave yet, our train is at nine." (optional)

What's the difference in meaning between the first sentence and the other two?

Check the difference CLICK
mustn't = it is forbidden — do NOT do this. don't have to / doesn't have to = it's not necessary — you can if you want, but you don't need to. Same negative shape, opposite meanings!
mustn't (must not) + base verb → not allowed
don't / doesn't have to + base verb → not necessary
Watch out: "You don't must" doesn't exist — "must" never takes "don't" in front of it. For "forbidden", use mustn't. For "not necessary", use don't have to / doesn't have to.

Advice & Polite Requests

Softer language — for giving advice and for asking people for things nicely.

Spot the pattern

  • "You should see a doctor about that cough." (advice)
  • "You shouldn't drink so much coffee before bed." (advice)
  • "Could you pass the salt, please?" (polite request)
  • "I'd like a coffee, please." (polite way to say what you want)

What comes straight after "should" and "shouldn't"? And what comes straight after "could you"?

Check the pattern CLICK
should / shouldn't + base verb, for advice about what's good or bad for someone. Could you + base verb ...? is a polite question asking someone to do something. I'd like (= I would like) + a noun or "to" + verb is a soft, polite way to say what you want — softer than "I want".
should / shouldn't + base verb
Could you + base verb ...?
I'd like + noun / to + verb
Watch out: "should" never takes "to". "You should to rest" is wrong — it's just "You should rest."

2. Your friend eats fast food every night.

Give gentle advice: "You fast food every night — try cooking sometimes."

3. You're in a restaurant and want the bill. Make this polite.

Direct: "Bring the bill." Polite: " the bill, please?"
