Communicating Supply Chain Disruptions

The language managers use when things do not go to plan

Before we start — a few questions

Supply chain disruption has become a permanent feature of operations management: port closures, strikes, shortages, geopolitical pressure, energy price shocks, weather events. The hard part is rarely the problem itself — it is communicating it.

  1. When something goes wrong in the supply chain, who are the first three people you need to communicate with, and in what order?
  2. What is harder — telling a client that a delivery will be late, or telling your own leadership that a target will be missed? Why?
  3. Is it better to send a disruption notice early (when details are incomplete) or later (when you have a clear plan)? What is the trade-off?
  4. What is the biggest communication mistake you have seen a manager make during a disruption?
  5. In your experience, do clients care more about what went wrong or what you are doing about it?

We are going to build the language you need to handle all of this — from the first internal escalation to the final client update. Every phrase today is one you could use in an email tomorrow.

Core vocabulary — click each card to flip

These are C1-level terms you will hear in any operational conversation. They appear again in the scenario and role-play.

Bottleneck(noun)
A point in a process where flow slows or stops, limiting overall throughput. "Customs clearance has become our main bottleneck."
Lead time(noun phrase)
The total time from placing an order to receiving the goods. "Lead times have doubled since the strike began."
To escalate(verb)
To raise an issue to a more senior level for decision or awareness. "I need to escalate this before the end of the day."
Contingency(noun)
A plan designed to cope with an event that may or may not happen. "We need a contingency for a second delay."
To mitigate(verb)
To reduce the severity or impact of something negative. "We are doing everything we can to mitigate the delay."
Downstream(adjective / adverb)
Referring to the later stages of a supply chain (closer to the end customer). "The impact will be felt downstream within 48 hours."
Knock-on effect(noun phrase)
A secondary consequence triggered by an earlier event. "The port closure had a knock-on effect on our entire schedule."
Root cause(noun phrase)
The fundamental reason behind a problem — not its visible symptoms. "The root cause turned out to be a systems integration error."
Workaround(noun)
A temporary solution that bypasses a problem without fixing it fully. "We have a workaround in place until Friday."
To revert(verb)
To get back to someone with more information (common in Asian and European business English). "I will revert by close of play tomorrow."
Buffer stock(noun phrase)
Extra inventory held to absorb unexpected demand or supply shocks. "We are running down our buffer stock faster than expected."
Force majeure(noun phrase)
A contract clause excusing non-performance due to extraordinary events beyond anyone's control. "The carrier has invoked force majeure."

Quick check: Without looking at the cards, can you explain the difference between a root cause and a bottleneck? Between a workaround and a contingency?

Root cause vs bottleneck: a root cause is why a problem happens; a bottleneck is where flow slows. A bottleneck can be a symptom of a deeper root cause.

Workaround vs contingency: a workaround is a temporary fix used during a current problem; a contingency is a plan prepared in advance in case a problem occurs.

Read the scenario

Read once for the facts. Then read again, noticing where you would need to make a decision.

Scenario: You are the operations manager at a mid-sized European distribution centre. Your warehouse handles inbound shipments from three overseas manufacturing sites and fulfils orders to roughly 40 industrial clients across the region.

This morning, you learn that a strike at a major port has stopped all container movements for at least five days. Three of your highest-value shipments are on vessels either currently at the port or due to arrive there within 48 hours. Two of these shipments are committed to clients with contractual delivery windows that will now be missed.

Your buffer stock will cover domestic orders for roughly four days. Your Managing Director wants a briefing within the hour. Your two affected clients will need formal notification today. Procurement is asking whether they should activate the alternative routing contingency, which costs approximately 40% more.

Quick comprehension

  1. How many parties need to be communicated with in the next 24 hours?
  2. What is the estimated duration of the disruption — and how confident should you be in that estimate?
  3. What trade-off is procurement asking you to authorise?
  4. At what point will the disruption begin to affect your own clients (not just inbound shipments)?

1. At least four: the MD (internal escalation), the two affected clients (external notification), and procurement (a routing decision). Depending on your structure, you may also need to brief sales and customer service.

2. "At least five days" — which should be treated as optimistic. Strikes rarely end exactly when first announced. Your communications should use language that allows for further extension ("at this stage", "subject to further updates").

3. Alternative routing — higher cost (40% more) in exchange for meeting the contractual window. A classic cost-vs-service trade-off that must go to the MD.

4. After approximately four days, once buffer stock is exhausted — meaning if the strike runs the full five days, you have a one-day gap minimum for domestic orders.

Discussion

  1. If you were the MD, what would you want in the first briefing — data, recommendations, or reassurance? Why?
  2. Which client would you contact first: the one you will almost certainly disappoint, or the one where there is still a chance of meeting the window?
  3. What information should you not share externally, even if a client asks directly?

Register — saying the hard thing, diplomatically

At C1 you already know the grammar. What separates a senior communicator from a mid-level one is register: the same information can come across as panicked, defensive, or measured — depending entirely on the phrasing.

Direct vs diplomatic — same message, different weight

Direct (avoid with clients)Diplomatic (professional register)
"Your shipment is late." "We are currently anticipating a delay to your shipment."
"It is not our fault." "The cause lies outside our direct operational control."
"We cannot deliver on time." "We are unlikely to be able to meet the original delivery window."
"I do not know when it will arrive." "We are not yet in a position to confirm a revised arrival date, but we expect to have clarity within 24 hours."
"You will have to wait." "We appreciate your patience while we work through the available options."
"I will tell you later." "I will revert with a full update by the end of the working day."
"This is going to cost more." "The alternative routing carries a cost implication, which we will need to discuss."

Phrase banks for specific moves

1. Opening a bad-news communication

I am writing to flag… I wanted to bring to your attention… We have become aware of a developing situation regarding… I am reaching out proactively to…

2. Softening the impact

There is a likelihood that… At this stage, we are anticipating… Subject to further updates… We are currently assessing the potential impact on…

3. Showing you are in control

We have already initiated… Our immediate priority is… We are actively exploring alternative options, including… The mitigation plan we are putting in place involves…

4. Committing to follow-up

I will revert with a full update by… I will keep you informed as the situation develops. Please consider this an interim update — a more detailed picture will follow. I remain available should you wish to discuss further.

5. Escalating internally (less softening needed)

I need to flag an emerging risk. This warrants your attention before the end of the day. I would welcome your steer on… There are two options on the table; I am recommending…

Practice — upgrade these sentences

Rewrite each direct sentence in a more diplomatic register. Try before revealing the model answer.

1. "The strike will probably ruin our whole month."

"The disruption is likely to have a material impact on our performance for the remainder of the month, which we are now modelling."

2. "Procurement messed up the backup plan."

"The contingency that procurement had pre-arranged has not activated as expected, and we are reviewing the reasons before deciding on next steps."

3. "I can't give you an answer right now."

"We are not yet in a position to confirm, but I will revert with a definitive response within the next few hours."

Role Play — three conversations, back to back

Use the vocabulary and phrases from the previous tabs. Aim to sound measured and in-control in every conversation, regardless of how the other side behaves.

Role-play timer (optional)
05:00

Scene 1 · Internal escalation to the MD (3 minutes)

Your role Operations manager. You have 3 minutes to brief the MD. Deliver: (a) what has happened, (b) immediate impact, (c) two options with a clear recommendation, (d) what you need from them.
Teacher plays The MD. Pressed for time. Wants decisions, not narrative. Will interrupt if you give too much background.
Target language "I need to flag…" / "Our immediate priority is…" / "There are two options on the table; I would recommend…" / "I would welcome your steer on…"

Scene 2 · External client call — first notification (4 minutes)

Your role Calling the client proactively, before they hear from someone else. You have a delay but not yet a revised date. The goal: deliver the news, protect the relationship, commit to a follow-up.
Teacher plays A demanding but fair long-term client. Will push for a guaranteed arrival date. Will express frustration. Expects a concrete next step before the call ends.
Target language "I am reaching out proactively…" / "We are currently anticipating…" / "We are actively exploring…" / "I will revert with a full update by…"

Scene 3 · Internal team stand-up (3 minutes)

Your role Lead a 3-minute stand-up with your warehouse and procurement team. Set the immediate priorities for the day. Assign actions. End with a clear check-in time.
Teacher plays A team member who raises a practical concern mid-briefing (e.g. "the back-up carrier has not confirmed"). You must respond without losing control of the meeting.
Target language "Our focus today is…" / "I need each of you to…" / "Let us reconvene at…" / "That is a valid point — can we park it and come back to it at…"

After the role-play — quick self-review

  1. Did you use at least 5 phrases from Tab 3?
  2. Did you over-apologise? (Once is professional; three times is defensive.)
  3. Did you commit to a specific follow-up time in every conversation?
  4. Did you stay in the passive / diplomatic register with the client, and the more direct register internally?

Reflection & take-aways

  1. Which of today's phrases could you start using in your working day tomorrow?
  2. Which register shift was most useful: escalating upwards, or explaining to clients? Why?
  3. Think of a real disruption you have dealt with — how would you phrase it differently in English now?
  4. Is diplomatic English culturally natural for you, or does it feel over-cautious compared to how people communicate in your first language?
  5. Name one situation where being too diplomatic would actually cause a problem.

Language to retire

If any of these are still in your writing, replace them this week:

  • "Very bad / very late / very difficult" — C1 writers quantify or specify: "materially delayed", "significant operational impact".
  • "I will tell you" — replace with "I will revert", "I will keep you informed", or "I will follow up".
  • "It is not possible" — replace with "it is not currently feasible", or reframe: "the more workable option is…".
