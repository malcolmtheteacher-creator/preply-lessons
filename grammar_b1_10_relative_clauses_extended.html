Extra Detail or Essential Information?

The same words — who, which — can do two different jobs, depending on a tiny pause.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "My cousin Elena, who moved to Japan last year, still sends us postcards."
  • "The people who live upstairs are incredibly noisy."
  • "My sister, who's just started a new job, is loving it."

In which sentences does the relative clause tell us which person we mean? In which does it just add a bonus fact about someone already clear? Say your answer before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Sentences 1 and 3 already name or identify the person ("Elena", "my sister") — the clause just adds extra information, and it sits between commas. Sentence 2 has no name at all — "who live upstairs" is the only thing that tells us which people we mean, so there's no comma, because the clause is essential.
[noun] + who/which/that + clause  →  essential, tells you WHICH one (no commas)
[noun], who/which + clause,  →  extra, bonus fact about someone already clear (commas)
One rule worth remembering: "that" only works in the no-comma version. You can't write "My cousin, that moved to Japan, ..." — after a comma, English always uses who or which, never that.

Whose, When and Where

Three more words for the same job: adding detail without starting a new sentence.

Spot the pattern

  • "That's the woman whose dog got lost in the park." (shows something belongs to her)
  • "I'll never forget the day when we got engaged." (a point in time)
  • "This is the restaurant where we had our first date." (a place)

What job does each bold word do? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
whose + noun shows possession — "the woman whose dog" = it's her dog. when follows a time word (day, moment, year). where follows a place word (café, restaurant, park) — and just like "who" and "which", the sentence continues completely normally straight after, with no extra preposition needed.
[noun] + whose + noun + verb  →  shows possession
[time noun] + when + clause  →  a point in time
[place noun] + where + clause  →  a place, no extra preposition

Keeping It Natural: Dropping the Word

Sometimes English lets you leave the connecting word out completely — but not always.

Spot the pattern

Three sentences you could say with or without the bracketed word:

  • "The photo (that) we took in Kyoto is my screensaver now."
  • "The friend (who) I told you about is visiting next month."
  • "The book (that) she recommended turned out to be brilliant."

Try reading each one without the bracketed word. Does it still work? What comes right after the gap in every one of these — is it a verb, or a completely different subject (we, I, she)?

Check the pattern CLICK
In every one, a new subject (we, I, she) comes right after the gap — the pronoun was only ever standing in for the object of the clause ("we took [it]", "I told you about [her]", "she recommended [it]"). When the pronoun is the object, you can drop it. Compare that to "the people who live upstairs" — there, "who" is itself the subject of "live", so there's nothing else to do the job. You can never drop it there.
"The waiter who served us was friendly."
who = subject → keep it
≠
"The jacket (that) I left on the train has gone."
that = object → droppable
This only ever happens in the no-comma (essential) version. After a comma, the word always stays: "My cousin, who moved to Japan, ..." can never become "My cousin, moved to Japan, ..." — that would turn into a different sentence altogether.
