The Broken Boiler

A fortnight without hot water, a landlord who says "soon" very warmly every time you ring, and a baby in the flat. Today's call has to get a date out of him — without turning him into someone who stops answering the phone.

The Scene 🎬

Fourteen days. You've been boiling kettles to wash a baby.

The boiler went on the second of the month. You rang Deepak that afternoon and he was lovely about it — genuinely sorry, said he'd get someone out. You rang again a week later and he was lovely about it again. Someone is coming, he said. Soon.

Nobody has come. And the difficulty is that Deepak isn't a bad landlord — he's a nice man with three flats and a full-time job who is quietly out of his depth, and every conversation you have with him is pleasant and ends with nothing happening. Being pleasant back has got you fourteen days of cold water.

So today's call has one job: come off the phone with a name, a day and a time. Not "soon". You also want to stay on good terms with him, because you live in his flat and you'd like to go on living in it.

  2. Is it possible to be firm with someone and still be liked by them? Or does one always cost the other?

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for chasing someone who is perfectly nice and completely useless — getting a date out of them without making an enemy. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The trick here is that your tone stays friendly while your content gets firmer. Most people do the opposite — they stay vague and let their voice get cold, which reads as passive aggression and makes the other person defensive. Keep the warmth in your voice all the way through and let the sentences do the work. You should sound like someone who likes him and is not going to hang up without a date.

📌 Establishing the facts

Phrase 1

Open with the timeline, flatly, before he starts explaining. No anger — just the fact that puts the whole conversation in proportion. Complete the missing word:

"I did ring about this a ___ ago."

"I did ring about this a fortnight ago."
A fortnight is two weeks — very common in British English and more pointed here than "two weeks", because it sounds like a proper unit of time rather than a count. The stressed did is doing the real work: it quietly answers the excuse he hasn't made yet, which is that he didn't know. You told him. Fourteen days ago.
Phrase 2

He's explained about the other flat and promised it's on his list. You need to sympathise and then put the fact straight back on the table. Which reply keeps him onside while refusing to move on?

A. "Well, that's not really my problem, is it."
B. "I know you've got a lot on. It's still not been looked at, I'm afraid."
C. "No, no, don't worry — whenever you can get to it."
B — "I know you've got a lot on. It's still not been looked at, I'm afraid."
A is true and useless: it makes him defensive and he'll spend the call justifying himself. C is what you've said the last three times, and it's why you're on day fourteen. B does both jobs in two short sentences — the first accepts his excuse completely, which stops him having to defend it, and the second returns to the only fact that matters. "Still" is the pivot word. "I'm afraid" keeps it apologetic in tone while the content doesn't budge an inch.
Phrase 3

He's been picturing a cold radiator. He needs to understand the actual scale of it — and there's one detail he's got wrong. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "There's no hot water at all, not just the heating."
The single most effective sentence in the call, and it isn't pressure — it's information. He had genuinely assumed it was only the radiators, which is a thing that can wait a fortnight; no hot water at all is not. Before you escalate anything, check whether the other person simply has the wrong picture. Very often the delay is a misunderstanding wearing the costume of neglect.

🍼 Raising the stakes, gently

Phrase 4

You have one genuinely powerful fact — the baby. Say it in a way that isn't emotional blackmail. Put the pieces in order:

but we've got a babyI'm not trying to be difficult,in the flat.
"I'm not trying to be difficult, but we've got a baby in the flat."
The first half is what makes the second half acceptable. Without it, "we've got a baby" lands as a card being played and he may feel manipulated; with it, it's a plain fact offered by someone who is visibly reluctant to make a fuss. "I'm not trying to be difficult" is one of the most useful openers in English for exactly this — it's how you say something forceful while denying any appetite for conflict.

📅 Turning "soon" into a day

Phrase 5

He's said "soon" for the fourth time. This is the sentence that has to break the pattern. Put the pieces in order:

what are we talking,When you say soon,roughly?
"When you say soon, what are we talking, roughly?"
Quiet, devastating, and completely polite. It quotes his own word back at him and asks him to define it, which he cannot do vaguely a second time without hearing how vague he's being. "Roughly" is the kindness in it — you're not demanding a contract, just a rough day — and paradoxically that's what makes people give you a real one. Use this any time somebody tells you "soon", "shortly", or "in due course".
Phrase 6

He's offered a vague "end of the week". Push it into this week rather than next — as a question, not an instruction. Complete the missing word:

"Could we get someone out this week ___ than next?"

"Could we get someone out this week rather than next?"
"Rather than" forces a choice between two specific options instead of leaving an open field. Notice also "could we" — the first person plural quietly makes it a shared problem you're solving together, which is much harder to resist than "can you". Small word, big difference: "can you get someone out" puts him on trial; "could we" puts you on the same side of it.

📧 Writing it down without going to war

Phrase 7

You want this in writing — but the moment an email sounds like evidence, a friendly landlord becomes a careful one. Which version gets it written down without changing the relationship?

A. "I'll be putting this in writing, for the record."
B. "Can I put that in an email, just so we've both got it?"
C. "I've been keeping a log of all of this, actually."
B — "Can I put that in an email, just so we've both got it?"
A and C both announce that you're building a case, and a landlord who thinks he's being built a case against stops being helpful and starts being careful. B gets you the identical piece of paper while sounding like admin. "So we've both got it" is the whole trick — it's framed as mutual convenience, and it's true: he'd genuinely rather not have to remember either.
Phrase 8

Say out loud that you don't want this to become formal — which is both true and reassuring. One word is missing, and it's part of a set phrase meaning take the official path:

"I'd rather not go down the formal ___, honestly."

"I'd rather not go down the formal route, honestly."
To go down a route is to take a particular course of action — usually one with consequences. Saying you'd rather not is genuinely disarming: you're telling him the official path exists, that you know how to use it, and that you'd prefer not to. That combination is far more persuasive than a threat, because it leaves him the dignified option and makes taking it feel like his idea.

🔒 Nailing it down

Phrase 9

Never come off the phone without the practical details, or you'll spend Friday not knowing whether to go out. Ask the two questions that turn a promise into an appointment. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Who's coming, and what time do you want me to be in?"
Two questions, and both of them assume it's happening — which is why they work. "Who's coming" gets you a name, and a named person is much harder to quietly not send than an anonymous "someone". "What time do you want me to be in" hands him a small task and makes the appointment real. Asking the practical questions is itself a form of pressure, and a very polite one.
Phrase 10

Finally — the consequence, in case Friday goes the way the last fortnight went. Which version protects you without wrecking the relationship?

A. "If it slips again I'll be contacting the council and withholding rent."
B. "If it slips again, I'll have to get someone in myself."
C. "It'd better actually happen this time."
B — "If it slips again, I'll have to get someone in myself."
A is a declaration of war on a man who has just agreed to help you, and you'd have to live with him for the rest of the tenancy. C is a schoolteacher's line — pure irritation, no consequence, easy to ignore. B is the strongest of the three precisely because it's the mildest: it isn't a punishment, it's just what will have to happen. "I'll have to" makes it sound reluctant and almost automatic — not something you're threatening, something you'd be left with.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

The hardest people to chase are not the rude ones. Rude is easy — it tells you where you stand and it frees you to be firm back. The hard ones are the warm, apologetic, genuinely well-meaning people who say sorry beautifully and then don't do the thing, because every conversation ends pleasantly and nothing changes.

What breaks the loop is almost never anger. It's specificity: a number, a day, a name, a written line. Vagueness is what lets a fortnight happen, and vagueness usually belongs to both people — he said "soon", and for three calls you accepted it.

And there's the thing underneath, which is that when you rent, the temperature of your own bath is somebody else's decision.
