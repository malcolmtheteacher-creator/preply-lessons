The Holiday Budget

Five friends, one trip, and a plan that's getting more expensive every time somebody opens a browser tab. You want to go. You just can't go at that price — and nobody has said a number out loud yet.

The Scene 🎬

It started as a week away. Somewhere warm, five of you, nothing fancy. And then, link by link, it grew: the villa with the pool, because it was only a bit more each; the Thursday flights, because Thursday suits everyone; the restaurant with the rooftop that somebody saw on Instagram and everybody agreed looked unreal. Nobody has done anything wrong. Every single upgrade was reasonable on its own. It's just that the total has quietly doubled and nobody has said so.

You want to go. That's the awkward part — this would be much easier if you didn't. You're not backing out, you're staying in, and you'd like the trip to cost about half what it currently costs. Which means being the one who slows it all down and says a number out loud, in a group where saying a number out loud feels like admitting something.

So you get Nadia on her own, before the deposits go in. She's the one who's been sending the links. She isn't rich either. She just hasn't sat down and added it up yet.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of the last thing you planned with friends. Did anyone ever say what they could actually spend — or did everyone just sort of go along with it and hope? Why is that so hard?
  2. Is money a normal thing to discuss with your friends, or a slightly private one? Does your answer change depending on who's got more of it?

The One Doing the Sums 🧮

You've added it up, twice, and it doesn't work. You're going on this trip — you just need it to cost less, and you'd rather say that now than spend the whole week quietly not ordering things.

The other voice

Sends the villa, sends the restaurant, means nothing by it. She hasn't checked the total either — and the moment she realises that, she's a bit embarrassed, which makes her defend the plan before she agrees with you.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for talking about money with people you like. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

How it should sound. Light, and a bit slow. The danger with money is that you've rehearsed it in your head for two days, so it comes out either too careful or too sharp, and either way it sounds like an accusation. Try to sound like it's just occurred to you, and leave gaps — the other person needs room to admit they haven't checked either, which is usually what's really going on.

💬 Opening the money conversation at all

Phrase 1

You're about to slow down something everyone's excited about. Which opener makes you sound like a friend rather than an auditor?

A. "We need to have a serious conversation about the finances."
B. "Can I be the boring one for a second and talk about money?"
C. "Has anyone actually thought about what this is costing?"
B — "Can I be the boring one for a second and talk about money?"
A sounds like a meeting, and C is a complaint wearing a question mark — it implies everyone else has been careless. B works because you take the hit yourself before anyone can hand it to you: you name the role ("the boring one"), you say it's temporary ("for a second"), and you ask permission, so the other person gets to say yes rather than feeling ambushed.
Phrase 2

The first thing they'll assume is that you're pulling out. Kill that idea in the same breath as you raise the problem. One word missing: "I'm in, definitely — it's just the number I'm ___ on."

"I'm in, definitely — it's just the number I'm stuck on."
To be stuck on something is to be unable to get past one particular part of it, which is exactly right here, because you're not weighing the trip up — you've decided, and one figure is blocking you. And putting "I'm in, definitely" first matters more than it looks, since it tells them this is a problem you want solved rather than an exit you're arranging.

🔢 Getting the real figures on the table

Phrase 3

Nobody has said what they can spend, so everyone is guessing upwards. Ask the group question that gets real limits out — without pointing at anyone. Put the pieces in order:

ceiling,What'sdo you think?actualeveryone's
"What's everyone's actual ceiling, do you think?"
A ceiling is the highest point you can go to — so it asks for the limit rather than the preference, which is the number that actually decides things. "Everyone's" spreads it across the group instead of interrogating one person, and "do you think?" turns it into speculation, so they can answer honestly without it counting as a declaration.
Phrase 4

Now you go first, because nobody else will. Which version actually moves the conversation forward?

A. "I'm not exactly made of money, you know."
B. "I could probably stretch a bit if I really had to."
C. "I can do about three hundred. Anything past that and I'm pretending."
C — "I can do about three hundred. Anything past that and I'm pretending."
A is a mood, not a figure, so nobody can plan around it, and B invites them to push you past your limit because you've just told them the limit isn't real. C gives them something to work with: a number, softened by "about" so it's still human, and then a second half that quietly closes the door. Pretending here means going along with a price you can't really pay while acting like you can — which is what most people do, and what naming the number saves you from.

🪶 Proposing cheaper without judging

Phrase 5

You've got a cheaper idea, but you don't want it to sound like a ruling. Float it so it's easy for anyone to say no to. Put the pieces in order:

the week after?Would it beto look atmad
"Would it be mad to look at the week after?"
"Would it be mad to…?" is a way of suggesting something while pre-emptively admitting it might be a terrible idea, so the other person can shoot it down without any awkwardness — which, oddly, makes them far more likely to agree. Note it's "look at", not "move to": you're proposing a glance, not a decision.
Phrase 6

She's defending the expensive thing, and you don't want to argue that it's rubbish — because it isn't, and you'd love it. Which line gets you out of the argument about taste altogether?

A. "Honestly? It's a bit of a rip-off for what it actually is."
B. "I could afford it, I just don't think we should be spending that."
C. "It's not that it's not worth it — it's just more than I've got."
C — "It's not that it's not worth it — it's just more than I've got."
A attacks the thing itself, which starts an argument you can't win and can't really mean, and B is simply untrue, so it will collapse the moment anyone looks at it. C works because it concedes everything except the money: you agree the restaurant is worth every penny, so there's nothing left to defend, and the only remaining fact is what's in your account, which nobody can argue you out of. It also leaves the people who can afford it feeling their choices haven't been sneered at.
Phrase 7

Two positions, both reasonable, neither budging. Offer the move that gives everybody most of what they want. Which one is it?

A. "What if we split the difference?"
B. "What if we just did the cheap option?"
C. "What if the ones who want it just pay for it?"
A — "What if we split the difference?"
To split the difference is to meet in the middle between two amounts, so it hands everyone a partial win and nobody a defeat. B is your position dressed up as a compromise, and C looks generous but actually sorts your friends into the ones who can and the ones who can't, which is the exact division you're trying to avoid.

🛡️ Protecting the people who haven't said anything

Phrase 8

You've said your number, and the silence around you is suspicious. Check whether you're actually alone in this, or just the only one talking. Put the pieces in order:

can I?thinking it,I can't bethe only one
"I can't be the only one thinking it, can I?"
It's a question you already know the answer to, and asking it anyway is the point, because the tag "can I?" hands the other person an easy way to join you. Almost nobody in a group like this is the only one thinking it — they're just the first one saying it, and this line invites the second one to arrive.
Phrase 9

Jay's gone quiet in the chat and you can guess why. Say something to Nadia that takes the pressure off anyone who hasn't spoken — without naming them as the person who's short of money. Say it out loud in your own words first.

e.g. "Nobody should have to say out loud that they can't afford it."
What makes this work is that it argues for a rule rather than for a person, so it never puts Jay in the position of being discussed. It also quietly explains the silence — going quiet isn't rudeness, it's what people do instead of confessing — and it makes the fix obvious, which is to put the numbers up front so that the plan is what's expensive, and no individual has to own it.

💛 Landing it warmly

Phrase 10

Close it so the whole thing feels like housekeeping between friends rather than a confrontation you've just won. Put the pieces in order:

than be weird about itHonestly, I'd ratherall week.sort the money now
"Honestly, I'd rather sort the money now than be weird about it all week."
To sort something is British English for dealing with it and getting it settled, and "be weird about it" names precisely what happens otherwise — the quiet counting, the not ordering a starter, the mood nobody can explain. Putting the two side by side makes this conversation look like the easy option, which is a kinder way to end than thanking anyone for putting up with you.

The Twist

💳 She was worse off than you

One more round, no script. Halfway through, Nadia goes quiet and then says it: the deposits have been going on a credit card she hasn't cleared since Christmas, and she's been sending the links because it was easier than admitting she'd stopped counting. So you're not the poorest person in this chat. You're just the only one who said it — and the conversation you'd carefully prepared, the one where you gently talk her down from a price she can happily afford, is now the wrong conversation entirely. Start the timer. Don't rescue her, don't lecture her about the card, and don't make her a project. Can you keep her dignity intact and still come out of it with a trip you can both actually pay for? One rule: the second you say "oh Nadia" in that voice, you've lost it.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said an actual number out loud, and I didn't apologise for it.
  • I made it clear I was staying in — nobody could mistake this for backing out.
  • I proposed something cheaper without suggesting the expensive thing was stupid.
  • I asked what other people could afford, instead of only announcing what I couldn't.
  • I left Nadia able to admit she hadn't checked either — I didn't make her defend the plan.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

We'll tell our friends about the state of our marriages and the results of our blood tests, and then go silent about what's in the current account. Money is more or less the last thing left that we don't say out loud, perhaps because it feels less like a fact about your circumstances than a score somebody's given you.

And a plan gets expensive in a very particular way — not through anyone's greed, but because nobody wants to be the first to flinch. Each upgrade is only a bit more than the last, everyone assumes everyone else is fine with it, and the silence keeps reading as agreement right up until the deposits go in.

Which means the cost of everyone's politeness lands on whoever has the least, and they pay it twice: once in money they didn't have, and once in the week they spent being careful about the bill. Somebody saying a number out loud is what stops that, and it doesn't have to be the person it's costing.

Two bigger questions

  1. Would you rather a friend told you honestly that they couldn't afford something, or quietly made an excuse and let you keep your image of them? Be honest — and does your answer match what you'd want them to do about you?
  2. In a group of friends with very different amounts of money, whose job is it to notice? The one with the most, the one with the least, or the one who happens to be organising it — and what does it cost each of them to be the one who speaks?
