Explaining Hard Ideas Simply

The Feynman Method · Analogy, Plain Language & Layered Explanation

When does an explanation actually click?

Most of us have had the experience — usually rare — of someone explaining a difficult idea so well that you suddenly understand it. Not memorise it. Not nod along to it. Understand it. The world rearranges itself slightly. You can’t go back to not knowing.

This lesson is about that move. What is the explainer doing differently? And how do you do it yourself?

1. Think of a moment when an explanation suddenly “clicked” for you. Maths, physics, history, anything. What made it work?

Most people, when they look back, find that the explanation that worked had at least three features: it started with something they already knew; it used a vivid comparison or image; and it left them feeling like the idea was simple all along, even though five minutes earlier it had seemed impossible.

2. Have you ever tried to explain something you thought you understood — and discovered halfway through that you actually didn’t?

(That moment is one of the most useful experiences in any field. It’s the topic of this entire lesson.)

3. Quick predictions — rank these from most to least useful when explaining a hard idea:

(a) Defining your terms precisely at the start

(b) Using a vivid analogy from everyday life

(c) Listing the five most important facts

(d) Asking the listener what they already know

Most expert teachers would rank: (d) first — you can’t build on a foundation you haven’t checked. (b) second — the right analogy does the work of three pages of definitions. (a) only matters once the listener can already see what the terms point to. (c) last — lists are how experts organise their own knowledge, but they’re rarely how anyone else acquires it.

Open question:

Of all your school subjects, which one had the best explainers? Which had the worst? What do you think made the difference?

Eight words to take into the reading

Four of these are tools of the trade for anyone who has to explain something difficult. The other four are the language of careful thinking — words you’ll need to talk about your own understanding precisely. All eight appear in the reading on the next tab.

an abstraction An idea separated from any concrete example of it. Mathematics is mostly abstraction; physics uses it; everyday life mostly avoids it.

In 1983, the BBC sat Richard Feynman down for an interview. The interviewer asked an apparently simple question: “Why do magnets repel each other?” Feynman did not answer. Instead, he spent seven minutes explaining why he could not answer in the way the question expected. The question, he said, sounded like it was asking for a deeper cause. But to give that, he would need to use the language of forces and fields — and the moment he did, the interviewer would ask why forces and fields work that way, and so on, until they reached an abstraction the interviewer simply did not have the framework to grasp. Feynman was not refusing to answer. He was making a point about the layered nature of explanation itself. It is not a chain of words; it is a chain of trust between depths.

This was Richard Feynman in his element. Nobel laureate in physics, professor at Caltech, one of the great minds of the twentieth century — and unusually, for a researcher of his stature, deeply committed to teaching. In the early 1960s he had taken time away from his research to teach a two-year introductory physics course for undergraduates. The lectures he gave, transcribed and published as The Feynman Lectures on Physics, are still in print sixty years later. They are not for non-specialists. But the way he taught — the moves he used to take a hard idea and make it land — became a small craft of its own.

The first move is to start where the listener already is. Feynman almost never opened with a definition. He opened with something the listener could already picture — a stretched rubber band, a swinging pendulum, water draining from a sink. Definitions, he said, are like packing material: they protect the idea, but they aren’t the idea. The second move is the analogy. Not a casual comparison, but one that imports the right structure from a familiar domain into an unfamiliar one. Feynman’s favourite was explaining heat as molecular jiggling — once you accept that heat is just things moving around faster, half of thermodynamics becomes intuitive instead of mysterious. The third move: strip the jargon. Specialist vocabulary signals expertise, but it tends to block understanding. If you must use a term, you have to earn it first by explaining what it points to in plain language.

The fourth move is the most demanding: test the explanation by trying to teach it. Feynman insisted that if you cannot explain something to a beginner, you have not really understood it yourself. You may have memorised it, you may be able to manipulate the equations, but the visceral understanding is missing. It would appear that the moment you stumble in front of an imaginary student is the moment your real comprehension ends. The fifth move is to keep the explanation layered. A good explanation gives a curious twelve-year-old enough to see the rough shape of the idea, and gives an expert enough to see that the precision is still there underneath, ready to be unpacked.

A specific example. At a children’s lecture in the 1960s, Feynman was asked why a rubber band gets warm when you stretch it suddenly. Most people — even most physicists — would reach for thermodynamics jargon: entropy, free energy, Helmholtz functions. Feynman reached for a string of pearls. Imagine the molecules in the rubber band as a long, tangled string of pearls, he said. When the rubber is at rest, the pearls are messy and disordered. When you stretch it, the pearls are forced into a straight line. Order goes up; disorder goes down; and the energy that was stored in their disordered jiggling has to go somewhere. It comes out as heat. The children — and the physicists in the room — got it. What Feynman had done, in effect, was to dress an abstraction in something concrete they could already see.

Why does this matter beyond physics? Because the same five moves work in any field where one person has to explain something complex to another. A doctor explaining a diagnosis. A lawyer explaining a contract. A historian explaining the causes of a war. A startup founder explaining their business model in a lift. The person who can take a difficult idea, hand it across the gap, and watch it land — not just be heard but actually grasped — owns the room. Arguably, it is the most portable skill in any thinking profession.

One last challenge, in Feynman’s own spirit. Take an idea you think you understand. Try to explain it, in plain English, to a curious twelve-year-old who asks good questions. Dissect your own explanation as you go. Where do you stumble? Where do you reach for jargon? Where do you wave your hands and say “it’s complicated”? It remains the case that wherever you stumble is where your understanding actually ends. The good news: now you know where to look.

One thing worth remembering

The technique now widely called the “Feynman method” was never formally taught by Feynman himself. He didn’t write a how-to guide. The method was reverse-engineered from watching him teach — particularly the lectures he gave at Caltech between 1961 and 1963. The five moves above are one common reconstruction. There are others.

The five moves — one at a time

Each of these is small. None of them are technical. The skill is in doing all five at once, under pressure, on a topic you actually understand.

1Start where they are

Open with something the listener can already picture. Don’t define your terms first — you don’t know yet what they need defined.

Don’tAn event horizon is the boundary in spacetime within which the gravitational pull is so strong that the escape velocity exceeds the speed of light.
DoImagine you’re falling toward a black hole. There’s a moment when, suddenly, no signal you send — no light, no radio — can ever reach the outside again. That moment is what we call the event horizon.

2Use a vivid analogy

The right analogy doesn’t just illustrate — it imports the right structure from a familiar domain. Heat is jiggling. Memory is filing. Black holes warp space the way a heavy ball warps a stretched bedsheet.

Don’tCompound interest is exponential growth where the interest accrued in each period is added to the principal for the calculation of subsequent interest.
DoCompound interest is what happens when your money has babies, and then those babies have babies. Slow at first; ridiculous later.

3Strip the jargon

Specialist vocabulary blocks understanding for outsiders. If you must use a term, earn it first by explaining what it points to in plain language. Then introduce the word as a label for what they already see.

Don’tThe placebo effect is mediated by descending modulation of nociceptive signalling via endogenous opioid release.
DoWhen you believe a treatment will work, your brain releases its own painkillers. Real ones. We call this the placebo effect.

4Test by trying to teach

The single most reliable test of your own understanding is to explain it to a beginner. Stumble = gap. Don’t hide the gap; investigate it.

Don’tIf asked: “why does that work?” → “It’s complicated. There are mathematical reasons.”
Do“Honestly, that’s where my own understanding gets thin. Let me think out loud…” (Then think out loud. The listener will respect this enormously.)

5Keep it layered

A good explanation works at multiple depths. The 12-year-old gets the shape; the expert gets the precision. The trick is to give the shape first, in plain language, and let the precision live just under the surface where it can be unpacked if needed.

Don’tTwo separate explanations — one wrong-but-simple, one right-but-impenetrable.
DoOne explanation that’s simple at the surface and accurate underneath. “Light is bent by gravity” (true at every level — just gets more precise the deeper you look).

Practice — rewrite the jargon

Each of these is a real sentence, written by an expert, that fails Move 3 (strip the jargon). Rewrite each so a curious 12-year-old could follow it — using analogy where useful.

1. “Photosynthesis is the metabolic conversion of light energy to chemical energy via the reduction of carbon dioxide using water as an electron donor.”

Plants build themselves out of sunlight. Specifically: they take light, water and air, and use the energy in the light to glue them into sugar. That’s photosynthesis. Almost everything you’ve ever eaten started this way.

2. “Quantum entanglement is a non-local correlation between particle states that persists irrespective of the spatial separation of the particles.”

Sometimes two particles get linked in a way that, when you measure one, you immediately know what you’d find if you measured the other — even if they’re a million miles apart. Nobody fully understands why. Einstein called it “spooky.”

3. “Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services in an economy over a period of time.”

Inflation is what happens when your money slowly buys less. The chocolate bar that cost £1 last year now costs £1.05; the price didn’t change because the chocolate got fancier — the money just got a little weaker.

4. “An algorithm is a finite sequence of well-defined, computer-implementable instructions to solve a class of problems or perform a computation.”

An algorithm is a recipe. Specifically, it’s a recipe a computer can follow, with no judgement of its own — just “do this, then this, then check if X, then this.” Sorting your music alphabetically? Algorithm. Finding the fastest route home? Algorithm.

This is the moment everything you’ve practised has to do real work.

Pick one of the four ideas below. You will speak about it for two minutes, as if explaining it to a curious twelve-year-old who asks good questions but knows no physics. The trick: you may not use a textbook diagram — only words.

While you speak, try to use:

Choose your topic

(a) Why ice floats on water. Most solids sink in their own liquid. Water is the strange one — and life on Earth depends on it.

(b) How GPS satellites work, and why they have to use Einstein’s relativity. Without it, your phone’s map would be wrong by about eleven kilometres after a single day.

(c) Why time slows near a black hole. A friend watching from far away would see your clock tick more and more slowly the closer you got to the horizon. (Callback to the previous lesson.)

(d) Why the sky is blue. Yes, it’s about the wavelength of light — but explain it without ever using the word “wavelength.”

After you speak

Three questions to answer aloud:

1. Which of the five moves was hardest? Why?

2. Did you stumble anywhere? What does that tell you about where your real understanding ends?

3. If you did it again, what one thing would you change?

To finish — bigger questions:

1. Which fields most reward this skill? Which least? Why might that be?

2. Have you ever met someone who could make difficult ideas feel obvious? What was different about them?

3. Is there an idea you’ve always thought you understood, but you suspect you’d struggle to explain to a curious 12-year-old?

4. Feynman said: “If you can’t explain it simply, you don’t understand it well enough.” Do you think that’s always true? Can you think of an exception?
