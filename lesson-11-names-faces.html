The envoy who knew everyone

Rome, a little over two thousand years ago. A foreign ambassador, Cineas, arrives from the court of King Pyrrhus of Epirus, who is at war with Rome and wants to talk. He has a problem that anyone who has been to a big party will recognise. The Senate has hundreds of members. Everyone expects to be greeted by name. And the ambassador has never met any of them.

The Roman writer Pliny the Elder, who collected examples of great memories in his Natural History, says that Cineas managed it. On the day after he arrived in Rome, he greeted all the senators, and all the knights, by name.

What was surprising about Cineas?
According to Pliny, he learned the names of all the senators and knights in Rome the day after arriving.
Talk about it. How do you feel when someone you have just met uses your name correctly? And when they forget it?

Pliny gives other examples. He says King Cyrus could call every soldier in his army by name. He says a Roman, Lucius Scipio, knew the names of all the citizens of Rome. He says King Mithridates, who ruled twenty-two peoples, could give judgments in each of their languages. Whether every detail is true, the stories show what people admired: a leader who knew your name.

A name is the most personal word there is. To remember it is a way of saying you matter to me. And to forget it, people tell us, is one of the most painful social mistakes.

Why do you think leaders in the old stories remembered names?
Knowing a person's name shows respect and builds loyalty. A name is the most personal word.
Talk about it. Tell about a time someone remembered your name when you did not expect it. How did it feel?

Why do we forget names so easily? Because when we are introduced, we are usually busy thinking about what to say, or how we look. The name comes in at one ear and goes out at the other. It is also an abstract word: Greg is not a picture, and a face with a new name on it is a bundle of unconnected facts.

The cure is to turn the name into a picture, and fasten that picture to something you notice in the face. It takes about five seconds, and it works because a name is no longer a sound but a scene.

Why do we forget names so easily?
We do not pay attention when introduced, and a name is an abstract word with nothing to hold it to the face.
Talk about it. What do you usually do in the first few seconds after someone tells you their name?

Five steps for a name

A name is a sound; a face is a picture. The method joins them in a scene.

  1. Hear it. Slow down. Look at the person, and ask them to repeat the name or spell it if you are not sure.
  2. Say it. Use it at once: 'Nice to meet you, Greg.' Then once or twice more in the conversation.
  3. Find one feature. Pick something about the face or hair that you notice first: ears, eyebrows, a smile, glasses. Pick a feature, not a judgement.
  4. Turn the name into a picture. A sound-alike or a meaning: Greg = a gremlin, Rose = a rose, Carmen = a car full of men.
  5. Join them, big and moving. Make the picture act on the feature, and exaggerate: a gremlin is swinging from Greg's huge ears.

Keep it kind

  • Pick a feature you could praise: the colour of the eyes, the wave of the hair, the shape of a smile.
  • Keep your pictures private: they are for your memory, not for saying.
  • Do not choose features that make you judge the person. A neutral thing, like glasses or hair, is enough.

Reviewing

That evening, run through the faces you met, one by one, and say the name picture for each. A name you review the same day will usually stay for a week. Review it again after a few days and you will probably have it for good. You can also place the pictures at places in a palace if you must keep a large number.

Say it aloud. Introduce yourself aloud to an imaginary stranger, then 'meet' Greg, Rose and Carmen and say your pictures.
