Chairing the Meeting

Half an hour, three items, and two problems sitting in the room. One of them won't stop talking. The other one won't start. Both of them are yours now.

The Scene 🎬

Ten to eleven on a Tuesday. Six people, a booked room, and for the first time the meeting is yours to run. Three items on the agenda, thirty minutes on the clock, and a decision that has to leave the room with somebody's name attached to it. Until today you've sat in these meetings. Today you own the shape of it.

Two problems are already in the chairs. Marcus is warm, generous and completely inexhaustible — he talks in long, pleasant loops that are always almost on topic, and he has genuinely no idea he's doing it. He is not a villain. He'd be horrified to know he'd taken nineteen minutes. And Yuki, at the far end, has the single best piece of information in this room and has said nothing at all. Yuki will not fight Marcus for the floor, and shouldn't have to. Nobody is being difficult. The meeting will simply go badly on its own, politely, unless someone shapes it.

Here's the thing about chairing that nobody warns you about: your job is not to be the cleverest person in the room. It's to make sure the room works. Open crisply. Cut Marcus off without humiliating him. Push a tangent somewhere else. Reach down the table and pull Yuki in. Test whether the silence in the room is agreement or just good manners. Then finish, on time, with names and dates on things.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of the best meeting you've ever sat in and the worst. What was the person at the front actually doing differently? Be concrete — not "they were good", but what they did with their mouth in the first two minutes.
  2. When someone talks for too long in a meeting you're running, what stops you from cutting in? Name the real reason, honestly — is it the English, or is it that you don't want to be the rude one?

Who's who

You
The New Chair 🗓️

Competent, well-liked, and about to discover that running a room is a different skill from being good in one. You have thirty minutes and no authority except the authority you take.

The other voice
Marcus 🗣️

Enthusiastic, likeable, endless. Every tangent he chases is interesting; none of them is the agenda. He isn't trying to dominate — he simply never notices the clock, and nobody has ever told him.

The other voice
Yuki 🔎

Closest to the work, quietest in the room. Knows the one fact that changes everything, and will only say it if someone deliberately makes space. Will not compete for that space.

First: read the conversation in the next tab — but read it for the cost. Every move the chair makes spends something socially: cutting Marcus off costs warmth, "let Yuki finish" costs comfort, "is anyone uneasy?" costs the safety of a quick yes. Nothing here is free. Watch where the chair chooses to spend, and what they buy with it.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap, so you've felt it from all three chairs. Notice how short the chair's lines are. The person running the room speaks least.

YouRight — thanks everyone. We've got half an hour and three things to get through — here's how I'd like to use it. Ten minutes on the switch to Redgate, ten on training, and I want ten left to agree the cutover date. Marcus, can you take us through the first one — three minutes?
MarcusHappily. So, Redgate. It's been a long road with them — I remember when we first looked at them, must have been 2019, completely different company back then, different management, half of them have gone now. Anyway: pricing's good, delivery windows are tighter than what we've got, and honestly while I'm on it, this whole thing raises the question of our returns process, because our returns process is a mess. I mean genuinely a mess. Last month I had a pallet come back three times. Three! And if we're changing supplier anyway, then surely the sensible thing is to look at the whole—
YouMarcus — let me jump in there, only because I want Yuki's view before we lose time. The returns thing: that's a real issue, but it's a different meeting. Can we park it? I'll put it top of next week's agenda with your name on it.
Marcus…Fair enough. Parked.
YouThank you. Yuki, you've been closest to this — what are we missing?
YukiI don't want to derail anything. But the tighter delivery windows — they depend on Redgate's—
MarcusOh, the windows are excellent, I've seen the figures myself, they're—
YouLet Yuki finish.
YukiThank you. They depend on their Leeds hub. Leeds shuts for rebuilding in January. From January to March, everything routes through Bristol instead, and Bristol adds two days. So if we cut over in January, we start a brand-new contract at our worst service level, in our busiest month.
You…Right. That changes item three, doesn't it. So what I'm hearing is — the switch itself is right, but January is precisely the wrong month to do it.
YukiYes. April costs us nothing at all.
MarcusAlthough — I'd say we could push Redgate on Bristol. I've got a good relationship with their ops director, I could probably—
YouMaybe. But then we're betting our busiest month on a favour. Where are we — is everyone happy, or is anyone uneasy?
MarcusHonestly? Uneasy about telling the board we've slipped. Not uneasy about the decision.
YouThat's useful — thank you. Because that's a message problem, not a plan problem. Marcus, you know the board best: would you draft the note? April cutover, with Yuki's Leeds dates in it, so it reads as a decision and not a delay. So that's you, Marcus, by Thursday — is that realistic?
MarcusThursday's fine. I'd enjoy that, actually.
YouGood. Yuki, could you send Marcus the hub dates tomorrow morning? Perfect. We said half an hour and we're at twenty-eight. Thank you — that was a good one.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Yuki's fact killed the January plan. Rebuild the chain out loud: what closes, what happens instead, what does it cost, and why does the month matter so much? Four links.

2. The chair asked whether anyone was uneasy — and Marcus said he was. Uneasy about what, exactly? And why did the chair treat that as good news rather than a problem?

3. Yuki almost never said the thing that changed the meeting. Twice, something nearly buried it. What were those two moments — and what did the chair have to spend, socially, to get that information into the room?

1. Leeds hub shuts for rebuilding in January → everything routes through Bristol until March → Bristol adds two days → so a January cutover launches a brand-new contract at the worst service level in the busiest month of the year. Not "January is risky" — a chain of four facts that only Yuki had.
2. Uneasy about telling the board they'd slipped — not about the decision itself. That's good news, because it separates cleanly: the plan is agreed, only the wording is hard. The chair named that ("a message problem, not a plan problem") and turned it straight into Marcus's job. Ask "is everyone happy?" and you'd never have found it.
3. First, Yuki said nothing at all while Marcus filled the room — so the chair had to ask directly. Second, Marcus talked over Yuki's opening sentence — so the chair had to stop him with three words. The cost: interrupting a colleague mid-flow twice, in public, and looking briefly like the unfriendly one. That's the price of the only fact that mattered.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that make a room fair and finish it on time. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. An interruption is 10% words and 90% warmth and speed. The same eight words — "let me jump in there for a second" — land as a slap or a favour depending entirely on how you say them. Fast and flat, eyes down: a slap. Warm, unhurried, with the reason attached ("only because I want Yuki's view"), it's a gift to the whole room, including the person you just stopped. Slow down. Smile with your voice. Give the reason. Then the hard move stops being a hard move.

🚪 Opening — set the shape before anyone talks

Phrase 1

First words of the meeting. You want to put the time, the number of items and your plan for both on the table in one breath — so the room knows the shape before anyone opens their mouth. Complete the chunk: "We've got half an hour and three things to get through — ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ ___."

"…here's how I'd like to use it."
Three moves in one sentence: the clock ("half an hour"), the load ("three things"), and — crucially — your plan for the two. "Here's how I'd like to use it" is softer than "here's the agenda" but does more work: "I'd like" keeps it human, while quietly claiming the right to decide. Say this in the first ten seconds and nobody has to be reminded of the time later — the time is already in the room.
Phrase 2

You're handing item one to a colleague. You want it to sound like an invitation — but with a fence around it. Which one gives him the floor and a limit, without sounding like a warning?

A. "Marcus, tell us about the first one, but please keep it short this time."
B. "Marcus, can you take us through the first one — three minutes?"
C. "Marcus, you've got three minutes and I'll be timing you."
B — "Marcus, can you take us through the first one — three minutes?"
Take us through means "walk us from the start to the end of it" — a friendly, professional way to ask someone to explain something in order. It flatters: it says you're the guide, we'll follow. Then "— three minutes?" is dropped on the end as a light question, not a rule. And here's the real value: you can only cut someone off fairly if you gave them a number at the start. A tells him off before he's spoken; C makes it a test.

✋ Keeping control, kindly

Phrase 3

Marcus is four minutes into his three and still going. You have to stop him — and he has to still like you afterwards. Put it in order:

only becausebefore we lose timeMarcus —I want Yuki's viewlet me jump in there,
"Marcus — let me jump in there, only because I want Yuki's view before we lose time."
To jump in is to enter a conversation someone else is already in the middle of. On its own it's an interruption. What makes it survivable is "only because" — those two words say I'm not stopping you, I'm serving the room, and they hand him a reason he can't take personally. Use his name first (it lands the brake softly), and put the reason on the end where he'll actually hear it. Interrupt without a reason and you're rude. Interrupt with one and you're doing your job.
Phrase 4

His tangent is a genuinely good point — just not today's. You need to move it out of the meeting without calling it worthless. Which line does that best?

A. "Let's not get into that now."
B. "That's a real issue, but it's a different meeting. Can we park it?"
C. "That's off topic, we'll come back to it another time maybe."
B — "That's a real issue, but it's a different meeting. Can we park it?"
Three parts, all load-bearing. "That's a real issue" — you agree with him, so he has nothing to fight. "It's a different meeting" — the problem isn't his point, it's the calendar; that's not a criticism, it's a category. And to park something means to leave it somewhere safe and come back to it — a car isn't thrown away when you park it. Asking "can we park it?" makes it a joint decision. A and C both mean "no" wearing a coat, and he'll hear it. And parking only works if you really do come back: say when.

🤝 Bringing people in

Phrase 5

The quietest person has the deepest knowledge. "Any thoughts, Yuki?" invites a polite "no". You want a question that's impossible to shrug off, because it hands her authority and asks for something specific. Complete it: "Yuki, ___ ___ ___ ___ to this — what ___ ___ ___?"

"Yuki, you've been closest to this — what are we missing?"
Two gifts in one line. "You've been closest to this" gives her standing in front of everyone — she's not being asked for an opinion, she's being asked as the expert. And "what are we missing?" assumes there is something and that it's the room's fault for not knowing it, not hers for holding it. A quiet person can't easily answer that with "nothing" — you've made speaking the polite option. Compare "any thoughts?", which makes silence the polite option.
Phrase 6

She's finally started — and four words in, someone talks over her. You have about half a second. There's no time for a sentence. In your own words: what do you say? Say it out loud before you reveal.

"Let Yuki finish."
Three words, and the bravest thing in this lesson. No softener, no "sorry", no reason attached — because there's no time and, this once, no need: everyone in the room already knows you're right. It costs you a moment of discomfort and it buys her the floor. Note who you spend the discomfort for: not yourself. If you never say this, the quietest person in your meetings never speaks, and you will never know what they knew.

⚖️ Testing agreement

Phrase 7

The room has gone quiet. That might be agreement — or it might be four people being polite. You get one question. Which one gets you the truer answer?

A. "Everyone happy with that?"
B. "Any objections?"
C. "Where are we — is everyone happy, or is anyone uneasy?"
C — "Where are we — is everyone happy, or is anyone uneasy?"
A and B both make silence the easy, polite answer — they ask for permission to move on, and of course they get it. C does something different: it puts uneasy on the table as an acceptable thing to be. "Uneasy" is a small, low-cost word — a feeling, not an accusation. Nobody has to declare war to say "I'm a bit uneasy", so they actually will. You're not asking for consent, you're inviting dissent — and dissent you didn't invite arrives three weeks later, in an email, when it's expensive.
Phrase 8

Several people have half-said the same thing. Before you go further, you want to put one clear sentence in the middle of the table — and let them correct it if it's wrong. Complete the chunk: "So ___ ___ ___ ___ is…"

"So what I'm hearing is…"
The most useful four words a chair owns. It's not "so we've decided" — it's a draft, offered for correction, which is why nobody bristles at it. But whoever says "so what I'm hearing is…" quietly writes the sentence everyone then argues from, and that's real power held in a soft hand. If you've got it wrong, someone fixes it — free. If you've got it right, the room has a decision it didn't know it had.

📌 Landing it

Phrase 9

An action needs a person, a date, and — the bit everyone forgets — that person actually agreeing. One line, all three. Put it in order:

is that realistic?by Thursday —So that's you,Marcus,
"So that's you, Marcus, by Thursday — is that realistic?"
Owner, deadline, consent — in eleven words. "That's you, Marcus" names him out loud in front of witnesses, so it's real. "By Thursday" is a date, not "soon" (nothing has ever happened by "soon"). And "is that realistic?" is the move most chairs skip: it gives him a genuine chance to say no now, in the room, instead of quietly missing it on Thursday. An action nobody agreed to out loud isn't an action; it's a hope.
Phrase 10

Twenty-eight minutes gone, everything agreed, everyone's still in their chairs. You want to close it so that people leave feeling it was worth the half hour — and so they turn up next time. In your own words: what are your last two sentences? Say them out loud before you reveal.

"We said half an hour and we're at twenty-eight. Thank you — that was a good one."
Finishing early is the single cheapest way to be trusted with a room, but only if you say it — otherwise nobody notices you kept your promise. "We said… and we're at…" points at the promise and at the clock, together. Then "that was a good one" gives the credit back to them. You spent thirty minutes interrupting people; you can afford eight words of thanks. That last line is why they'll give you the floor next time.

🔑 Make them yours

Not "a meeting" — your meeting, the real one that's coming. Pin these to it, and say each answer out loud until it's automatic.

Your next meeting: how long, how many items, and how do you want to spend the time? Write your actual opening sentence.

Who is your Marcus? Picture him mid-flow, minute four. Write the exact words you'll stop him with — including your reason.

Who is your Yuki — the person who knows most and says least? Write the question you'll hand them.

Your Turn 🎭

Now the room's yours. Speak every answer out loud FIRST — a real meeting won't wait while you craft the ideal sentence, and neither will Marcus. Use the timer.

Role-play timer: 4:00

Round 1 — you're chairing 🗓️

New meeting, new room. Twenty-five minutes, two items: the office move, and who covers the August rota. Marcus is here. So is Yuki. No looking back at the phrases — reach for them from memory.

MarcusRight, the office move. Well. Round 2 — swap: now you're Marcus 🗣️

This one teaches more than Round 1, and it's uncomfortable, which is the point. You're Marcus now: warm, enthusiastic, genuinely interested — and you have no idea you're doing anything wrong. Ramble properly. Chase the tangent. Enjoy yourself. Then get stopped, and notice exactly how it feels: which interruptions sting, which ones you barely mind, and what the chair did differently between the two.

The ChairThanks for coming, everyone. Twenty minutes, two items. Marcus, can you take us through the supplier review — three minutes?
The ChairMarcus. Marcus. We haven't got time for this.
The ChairMarcus — let me jump in there, only because I want to get to Yuki before we lose the time. Can I bring you back to the supplier review?
The ChairYuki, you've been closest to this — what are we missing?
The ChairSo that's you, Marcus, by Friday — is that realistic?
Now step out of Marcus for a second. Two people stopped you in exactly the same place. One made you defensive; one didn't. Say out loud what the difference actually was — then remember that your own Marcus feels it the same way you just did.
The Twist

🎯 You parked the wrong thing

Back in the chair, four minutes left, and it's going beautifully. Then Yuki says, quietly: "Can I say something? The tangent Marcus kept chasing — the filing. It's not a side issue. It's why the move keeps slipping. Everything he's been raising for a year is the actual reason we're here." He was right. You parked the one thing that mattered — and you parked it well, warmly, with a good reason, in front of everyone. Now you have four minutes, and you have to un-park it in public. Start the timer and do it out loud: say plainly that you got it wrong, give Marcus the credit where the room can hear it (he's been raising this for a year and nobody listened — including you, ten minutes ago), and decide with the time you've got whether this is a five-minute reopen or a meeting of its own with a date on it. One rule: you may not pretend you meant to do this. Being the chair means being the one who says "I got that wrong" first — and the room will trust you more, not less, for it.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said out loud. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I put the clock and the plan into the room in my first breath — before anyone else spoke.
  • I interrupted someone mid-flow, gave a reason, and he could still like me afterwards.
  • I made space for the quietest person, and protected it when someone talked over her.
  • I invited someone to be uneasy instead of fishing for a yes.
  • Nothing left the room without a name, a date, and that person saying yes out loud.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that'll cost you on Tuesday. Run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Every room decides, very quickly and mostly without noticing, whose voice counts. It rarely decides it on the evidence. It goes to whoever is loudest, quickest, most senior, most at home in the language, most comfortable taking up space — and none of those things is the same as being right.

Which is why the loudest voice in a meeting is so seldom the best-informed one. The person who has actually done the work is usually too close to it to speak in headlines, and too aware of what they don't know to sound certain. Marcus talks because he's interested. Yuki is quiet because she's careful. The room, left alone, will hear Marcus.

So chairing isn't a status job. It looks like one — you're at the front, you have the agenda — but it's a fairness job. You're not there to be right; you're there to make sure the room can find out what's right. And your real power isn't the agenda or the clock. It's this: you can hand the floor to someone who would never have taken it.

Every time you do, it costs you a little — a moment of awkwardness, someone's flow, the risk of looking unfriendly. That's the whole job. Spend it well.

Two bigger questions

  1. In the meetings you sit in, who ends up talking most — and is it the same person who knows most? If it isn't, what would actually have to change for that gap to close: a better chair, a different culture, or something in the quiet person themselves?
  2. A chair who interrupts, parks, and cuts people off is doing the job — and will be liked less for it than a chair who lets the meeting drift warmly over time. Is that a price worth paying? And who really pays it: the chair, or the six people who never got to speak?
