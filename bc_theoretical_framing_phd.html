Theoretical Framing

PhD Academic English · Allegiance · Departure

What theoretical framing actually does in a PhD

A theoretical framework is not a hat you put on at the beginning of the thesis and then forget about. It is the set of commitments that determines what you treat as data, how you interpret it, and what counts as an answer. Examiners read framing chapters with one main question: does this writer understand the tradition they have placed themselves inside?

The framing chapter therefore has to do three things at once: signal allegiance to a recognisable intellectual tradition, mark departure from parts of that tradition that don't serve your argument, and justify both moves in terms a reader from another camp could engage with.

The framing test

If your theoretical framing could be removed and replaced with a different framework without changing your analysis, the framework isn't actually doing work. A real framework shapes what you can see in your data — and what your data is allowed to mean.

What examiners are checking

  • Have you read the foundational figure carefully, or only the secondary literature on them?
  • Do you know the major debates within the tradition, or do you treat it as monolithic?
  • Can you say what the tradition cannot do, and have you explicitly addressed those limits?
  • Is your departure from the tradition principled, or just convenient?

The two-move structure: allegiance and departure

Almost every PhD theoretical framing chapter executes the same two moves in sequence. Get these right and the chapter does its work; get them wrong and the chapter reads as either uncritical discipleship or unprincipled cherry-picking.

MOVE 1 — ALLEGIANCE

Identify the tradition you draw on

Name a recognisable theoretical lineage. Show you understand it from the inside. Demonstrate engagement with at least one primary text — not just the textbook summary.

This study is situated within a broadly post-structuralist tradition, drawing in particular on Foucault's later work on governmentality (1977; 1991). Three features of this approach inform the analysis that follows...

The framework adopted here builds on practice theory as developed by Bourdieu (1977) and extended in more recent sociological work...

MOVE 2 — DEPARTURE

Mark where you depart from the tradition

State explicitly which parts of the tradition you do not adopt, and why. The departure should be principled — driven by your research question or by limitations the tradition itself has acknowledged.

While this study draws on Foucault's analytic of governmentality, it departs from his account in one important respect: where Foucault tends to read regimes of governance as systemic, the present analysis treats them as locally contested...

This research takes Bourdieu's concept of habitus as its starting point but resists the determinism that some readings ascribe to it. Following recent reformulations (Reay, 2017; Friedman, 2020), I treat habitus as...

Why both moves matter

❌ Allegiance without departure

Reads as discipleship. The writer appears to have absorbed a tradition uncritically. The viva question will be: "Where does Foucault not help you?" and you will have no answer.

❌ Departure without allegiance

Reads as cherry-picking. The writer takes one concept from a tradition without engaging with the framework that gives that concept meaning. The viva question will be: "Why are you using this concept at all if you don't accept the framework around it?"

The analysis that follows draws on Bourdieu's (1977) practice theory, and in particular on his concepts of habitus and field. Two features make this framework especially productive for the present study: its insistence that practice is shaped by structures the actor has internalised, and its refusal to reduce action to either pure agency or pure determination. At the same time, this study departs from Bourdieu's account in two respects. First, where Bourdieu's analyses tend to emphasise reproduction, the present research is concerned with moments where habitus is disrupted rather than reproduced. Second, Bourdieu's framework was developed in the context of relatively stable national fields; the present study examines a digitally-mediated and transnational context in which the boundaries of "the field" are themselves in question. These departures are therefore not corrections to Bourdieu but extensions of his framework into conditions he did not fully theorise.

The specific language of theoretical positioning

Theoretical framing has its own vocabulary of moves. These are the verbs and phrases that signal sophistication to the reader.

VERBS OF ALLEGIANCE

How to claim a tradition

This study is situated within / draws on / builds on / follows / takes its starting point from...

The analytic framework adopted here derives from...

I follow [thinker] in treating X as Y.

VERBS OF DEPARTURE

How to mark distance without rejection

This study departs from / diverges from / takes issue with / complicates / resists / extends beyond...

While I draw on X, I do not adopt Y.

Following recent reformulations (e.g., ...), I treat X not as Y but as Z.

VERBS OF EXTENSION

How to claim you're advancing the tradition, not abandoning it

This study extends / develops / operationalises / refines / brings into conversation...

Building on these developments, I propose that...

QUALIFIED CLAIMS

The hedging that protects you in the viva

This is not to suggest that... but rather that...

While I do not claim that X is universal, I do contend that in the contexts examined here...

This study makes no claim to resolve the longstanding debate over X. Its more modest aim is...

How to draw on a thinker without parroting them

One of the most common signals of an underdeveloped framing chapter is what might be called parrot mode: the writer uses the tradition's vocabulary fluently but cannot say in their own words what the concepts mean or what they do.

The viva will catch this. Examiners ask: "In your own words, what is habitus?" or "How would you define governmentality without using Foucault's phrasing?" The student who has only memorised the vocabulary cannot answer.

The "translation test"

For every theoretical concept you cite, write a one-sentence definition in your own words — not paraphrasing the original, but explaining what the concept does. If you can't, you don't yet own the concept.

❌ Parrot definition

"Habitus refers to systems of durable, transposable dispositions, structured structures predisposed to function as structuring structures (Bourdieu, 1977)."

Problem: This is Bourdieu's own phrasing recycled. The writer hasn't translated it into their own understanding. A viva examiner will ask: "What does that actually mean for what you can see in your data?"

✅ Owned definition

"Habitus, in this study, refers to the patterns of taste, perception and action that someone has internalised so deeply they feel like personal preferences rather than the marks of a social position. The concept allows me to analyse moments where a participant's stated rationale for a choice is at odds with the trajectory their wider practice reveals."

Why it works: the writer has translated the concept into their own working understanding, and made clear what it lets them see in their data.

The methodological consequence rule

Every theoretical concept you adopt must connect to a methodological consequence. "This means in practice that I will treat X as Y when I encounter it in my data." Concepts that don't have this kind of operational pay-off are decorative — and examiners can tell.

Audit your framing

Rewrite one definition

Take the most parroted-feeling definition in your draft. Rewrite it in your own words, with an explicit statement of what the concept lets you see in your data.

The viva-readiness check:

If your examiner asked, today: "What can your framework not do? What kinds of question is it badly suited to answer?" — could you give a confident, specific answer? If not, you are not yet ready to defend it.
