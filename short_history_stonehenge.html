Stonehenge

Five thousand years old, and still keeping its secret.

Today: you'll tell the story of a five-thousand-year-old mystery, and speculate about what it means — using the passive voice and modals of deduction.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Stonehenge is prehistoric — it was built long before anyone in Britain could read or write, so it left us no records at all.
prehistoric (adj) — from the time before written history existed.
2. It is a monument: a great structure deliberately built to last, to mark something important and to be seen for thousands of years.
a monument (n) — a large structure built to commemorate something or to endure as a landmark.
3. To build it, people had to erect stones weighing many tonnes — and first to haul them, by sheer muscle, across miles of open country.
to erect (v) — to raise something up into a standing position. to haul (v) — to drag or pull something heavy with great effort.
4. The largest stones were cut from a quarry — a place where rock is dug out of the ground — some distance away.
a quarry (n) — an open pit or site from which stone is extracted.
5. The stones appear to align with the sun: stand in the centre at midsummer, and the light rises directly along the main axis.
to align (with) (v) — to be arranged in a straight line with something else.
6. That sunrise marks the solstice — the longest day of the year, when the sun reaches its highest and furthest point before turning back.
the solstice (n) — midsummer or midwinter; the day of longest or shortest daylight.
7. To the people who raised it, the place was almost certainly sacred — holy ground, set apart for ceremony and for the gods.
sacred (adj) — holy; connected with religion and deserving deep respect.
8. It was also a burial ground: the cremated remains of many people were laid there, making it a kind of vast Stone-Age cemetery.
a burial ground (n) — a place where the dead are laid to rest; a cemetery.
9. How it was built is genuinely baffling — every explanation seems to raise a new question that no one can quite answer.
baffling (adj) — impossible to understand or explain; deeply puzzling.
10. With no records to go on, all we have is theory and speculation — ideas put forward without proof, argued back and forth for centuries.
a theory (n) — a proposed explanation. speculation (n) — forming opinions without firm evidence; guessing.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A ring of giants

Out on Salisbury Plain, in southern England, stands a great ring of standing stones. It is called Stonehenge, and it is one of the most famous prehistoric monuments on Earth. It is also one of the oldest things human hands have made and left standing: around five thousand years old. It was not thrown up in a single season but built in stages, beginning around 3100 BC, with the famous circle of giant stones raised between about 2600 and 2400 BC.

To grasp how old that is, hold this in your mind: when the stones were being erected, the great pyramids of Egypt were either not yet built or were freshly new. There was no writing in Britain, no metal tools, no wheel. And yet here, on an open plain, a people we can barely picture decided to raise a monument that would outlast every empire that came after them — and, against all the odds, it has.

What you see today is only part of what once stood. But even now, the scale stops people in their tracks. These are not boulders that happened to lie there; they were chosen, shaped, dragged into place and stood upright on purpose, by people who left us no word to explain why.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where is Stonehenge, and roughly how old is it?

2. What does the writer ask you to picture, to understand just how ancient it is?

1. On Salisbury Plain in southern England; around 5,000 years old, built in stages from about 3100 BC, with the great circle raised between roughly 2600 and 2400 BC.

2. That when the stones were being raised, the Egyptian pyramids were not yet built or were brand new — and Britain had no writing, no metal tools and no wheel.

Discuss:
  • The people who built it had no writing, no metal and no wheel — yet they raised something that has outlasted every empire since. What does that make you think about how we measure "advanced" civilisations?

Part 2 — Stones that should not be there

Look closely and you find not one kind of stone but two. The huge uprights — the "sarsens" — are the giants of the monument, some weighing up to twenty-five tonnes. These were brought from about twenty-six kilometres away. Heavy enough. But it is the smaller stones, the "bluestones," that turn the puzzle into a genuine mystery. They weigh only two to four tonnes each — and they were carried from the Preseli Hills, in Wales, around two hundred and thirty kilometres away.

Two hundred and thirty kilometres. By people with no wheel and no metal tools. How it was done is still argued over to this day. The stones were almost certainly dragged on wooden sledges and rollers, and perhaps floated part of the way on rafts. But every theory leaves something unexplained, and the honest answer is that we do not fully know. It is baffling — and it has been baffling clever people for a very long time.

And this is the part that should give you pause. They did not have to bring those bluestones so far. There was stone closer to hand. Something made it worth hauling rock the length of a country, by muscle alone, over years. Whatever Stonehenge was, it mattered enough for that.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What are the two kinds of stone, and where did each come from?

2. Why is the journey of the bluestones the real mystery?

1. The huge "sarsens" (up to 25 tonnes), brought from about 26 km away; and the smaller "bluestones" (2–4 tonnes), carried from the Preseli Hills in Wales, around 230 km away.

2. They were hauled some 230 km by people with no wheel and no metal tools — probably on sledges, rollers and perhaps rafts — when closer stone was available, so something must have made the effort worth it.

Discuss:
  • They went to extraordinary effort to bring specific stones from far away, when nearer stone would have done. What might make a particular place — or material — feel that important to people?

Part 3 — What was it for?

And here is the question that no one can close: what was Stonehenge actually for? Because the people who built it left no writing, we have only theory and speculation. Was it a temple? A giant calendar in stone, aligned with the sun so that it catches the sunrise at the solstice? A place of healing, where the sick came in hope of a cure? Or a symbol of unity, a great shared project that bound together the scattered peoples of ancient Britain? Each idea has its supporters; none of them can be proven.

But there is one thing we do know for certain. Stonehenge was a burial ground. The cremated remains of perhaps a hundred and fifty people have been found there — making it the largest late-Stone-Age cemetery in Britain. So at the very least, it was a place to bring the dead: sacred ground, set apart, returned to again and again across the centuries.

That is what makes Stonehenge so quietly powerful. We can stand inside it, measure it, date its stones to the year — and still not know what was in the minds of the people who raised it. Five thousand years on, it keeps its secret. And perhaps that is exactly why we keep coming back to it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name three of the theories about what Stonehenge was for.

2. What is the one thing we actually know for certain?

1. Any three of: a temple; a giant calendar aligned with the sun at the solstices; a place of healing; a symbol of unity for the scattered peoples of ancient Britain.

2. That it was a burial ground — the cremated remains of perhaps 150 people were found there, making it the largest late-Stone-Age cemetery in Britain.

Discuss:
  • We can date every stone, yet we still can't say what it meant to the people who built it. Does knowing the facts but not the meaning make Stonehenge more fascinating, or more frustrating?

The grammar this story runs on: the passive & modals of deduction

This is a story with no known author. We know what was done, but rarely who did it — and we're forced to guess at why. Two pieces of grammar do exactly that work.

The two tools:
• The passive — was / were + past participle — when the action matters more than the doer, or the doer is unknown: "the stones were dragged across the plain," "the monument was built in stages."
• Modals of deduction (past) — must have / might have / can't have + past participle — for how sure you are about something in the past: "it must have been a temple" (I'm certain), "it might have been a calendar" (it's possible), "it can't have been easy" (I'm certain it wasn't).

1. The passive — make the action the subject

Turn each active sentence into the passive. The doer is unknown or unimportant, so let the action take centre stage.

1. People built the monument in stages. → The monument in stages.

2. They dragged the bluestones from Wales. → The bluestones from Wales.

3. They raised the great circle around 2500 BC. → The great circle around 2500 BC.

4. They found the remains of 150 people there. → The remains of 150 people there.

1. was built

2. were dragged

3. was raised

4. were found

2. Modals of deduction — how sure are you?

Fill each gap with must have, might have or can't have + the past participle, to match the level of certainty in brackets.

1. The builders had no wheel, so moving the stones (certain — not) easy. → it easy.

2. The site aligns with the solstice, so it (possible) a kind of calendar.

3. They hauled stones 230 km, so the place (certain — yes) deeply important to them.

4. Some say it was a place of healing — well, it (possible), but we can't prove it.

1. can't have been (you are certain it wasn't)

2. might have been (it's possible)

3. must have been (you are certain it was)

4. might have been (it's possible)

3. Put them together — speculate about Stonehenge

Fill each gap with the passive or a modal of deduction, whichever the meaning needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

The bluestones (carry) all the way from Wales. Nobody knows exactly how, but they (possible / move) on sledges and rafts. Whatever the method, it (certain — not / be) quick. The site (use) as a burial ground, and it (certain / be) sacred to the people who built it — though what else it (possible / be) for, we may never know.

were carried (passive) · might have been moved (possibility) · can't have been (certain it wasn't) · was used (passive) · must have been (certainty) · might have been (possibility)

Notice how the passive states what we know was done, while the modals carry your level of certainty about the unknowns.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • An odd object you once found — a rusted key, a coin, a carving — that you never explained: "it must have belonged to…"
  • A ruined building or abandoned house near where you grew up: "it might have been a…", "it can't have been left empty by choice"
  • The Nazca Lines in Peru — vast figures only visible from the sky: who were they made for, and why?

  • A photograph in your family with no caption — strangers, a place no one recognises: speculate about who they were
  • Easter Island's giant stone heads — how were they moved, and what were they built to do?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Stonehenge took generations to build, for reasons we still don't understand. What would it take for a society today to start a project no one alive would see finished?
  2. We've dated it, measured it, dug beneath it — and the meaning still escapes us. Are there some things about the past we should accept we'll simply never know?
  3. Millions visit Stonehenge every year and stand quietly before it. What is it, do you think, that people are really going to see?
