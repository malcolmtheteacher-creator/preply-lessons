The Ulysses Contract

C1 Level · A 25-minute lesson on the rules HR sets in advance to hold under pressure later

Rules set in advance

Two and a half thousand years ago, Homer's Ulysses had himself bound to the mast of his ship so that he could safely hear the sirens' song without leaping to his death. He understood two things at once: that the song would overwhelm him, and that his future self could not be trusted to resist it. So he set the rule in advance, in the calm moment, and lashed himself to it. Almost every serious HR policy you have ever designed runs on the same principle.

Shape of the lesson: 5 min opening · 3 min vocab · 5 min reading · 5 min grammar · 7 min speaking.

One paragraph. Then we talk.

Read aloud, slowly. Pause at each comma.

The Norwegian philosopher Jon Elster, writing in 1979, treated the Ulysses contract as a curious philosophical paradox: the present self deliberately constraining the future self. For an HR professional, the picture looks rather different. Almost every serious HR rule, on inspection, IS a Ulysses contract scaled up — a structured interview format set in advance, a hard line on what is discussed at which stage of recruitment, a binding policy on conflicts of interest, a ring-fenced grievance protocol. Each of these is the calm-moment version of the organisation lashing itself to the mast, and each exists precisely because, when the moment of pressure arrives, the team will be talked into an exception they will later regret. The skill of senior HR is partly the skill of designing those contracts well; the harder skill, and the more political, is holding them when an exception is being requested under sympathetic, urgent, and entirely plausible cover.

Show Answer
A binding HR policy is the institution doing the same thing Ulysses did personally — setting a hard rule in advance, when judgement is calm and pressure is low, so that the rule will hold later when judgement is clouded and pressure is high. The "lashing to the mast" is the organisational equivalent of Ulysses' physical binding: a constraint chosen freely now, precisely so that the future cannot undo it casually.

Formal hypotheticals — the language of HR defence

When you defend an HR policy in a meeting, the register lifts. If we weaken this… becomes Were we to weaken this… — same meaning, more weight. Three structures do most of the work.

The move: drop the if; invert the auxiliary in front of the subject. If we were to… → Were we to…   If the policy hadn't been in place… → Had the policy not been in place…   If anyone asks… → Should anyone ask…

Three forms, three moments in an HR conversation

1. Were we to… — for a hypothetical present/future weakening of the policy.

Were we to grant this exception, we would be reopening the question every quarter.

2. Had the policy not… — for defending a policy by reference to a past situation it prevented.

Had the framework not been in place, the dispute would have been much harder to manage.

3. Should anyone… — for procedural defence; calm, formal, slightly distancing.

Should anyone ask for an exception, refer them to the policy document.

One focused speaking task

Time remaining: 5:00
Defend the policy

Pick one real binding HR policy currently operating in your organisation — from recruitment, performance review, conflict of interest, or grievance/dispute resolution — that a colleague has, recently or hypothetically, asked you to relax for an "exceptional" case. Make the case for keeping the policy as it stands, even if, privately, you have some sympathy with the request. Speak as you would in a real meeting: measured, professional, conceding what must be conceded but holding the line on what matters.

Cover:

  1. What is the policy, and what was it designed to prevent?
  2. What is the "exception under sympathetic cover" that's being requested?
  3. Where does the request hit you hardest — and why is that exactly where the policy is most necessary?
  4. How do you say "no" in a way that the requesting colleague can hear, and that protects the working relationship?
