LECTURE TRANSCRIPT

Good afternoon. Today I'd like to talk about what is sometimes called the lost art of wayfinding — the system of perception by which our ancestors navigated long before any instrument was invented.

The story really begins, for most people, in 1976, with a voyage made by a traditional Polynesian canoe called the Hōkūleʻa. The vessel sailed two thousand five hundred miles from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti without a chart or a compass. Its master navigator, Mau Piailug, used only the stars, the patterns of the ocean swell, and the behaviour of seabirds. The voyage was, in part, a deliberate experiment, designed to prove that the great Pacific migrations were the product of skill rather than accident.

Now, what is interesting is that this kind of navigation isn't a single trick. It is a layered system of perception. The night sky provides a fixed framework — Polynesian navigators memorised what they called a star compass of around two hundred and twenty rising and setting points. By day, the angle of the sun gives bearings. The direction and frequency of the ocean swell carries information about distant land that the eye alone cannot see. Even the behaviour of certain seabirds — those that return to land at night — can be read as a kind of arrow.

Compare this to what most of us now do. A small device in our pocket tells us where we are, and a second one tells us where to go next. Neither asks us to remember the route or attend to landmarks. Research carried out on London taxi drivers — who, you may know, learn an extraordinary mental map of the city called the Knowledge — has shown measurable changes in the hippocampus, the brain region responsible for spatial memory. When the work is taken over by a satellite, that region appears, conversely, to shrink.

What follows from this — and here, of course, opinions divide — is what some commentators now call the case for navigational fitness. They argue that a faculty which is never exercised cannot be reliably called upon, even in emergencies. Whether this is a serious concern or simply nostalgia for an older world is a matter we will return to in the seminar.

Academic Reading · one passage, three task types

Finding North Without a Compass

Read the passage and then answer the questions that follow. In a real IELTS test you would have about 20 minutes for this length of passage.

A On a bright morning in May 1976, a traditionally built Polynesian canoe known as the Hōkūleʻa set sail from Hawaiʻi for Tahiti. The journey would cover 2,500 miles of open ocean. There was no chart on board, no compass, no GPS. The vessel's navigator, a Micronesian master named Mau Piailug, had agreed to make the voyage using only the techniques his people had used for over a thousand years: reading the sky, the swell of the sea, and the flight of birds. To the surprise of many academic observers, the canoe arrived precisely where it had intended to go.

B The voyage was not, primarily, a feat of endurance. It was an argument. For most of the twentieth century, Western scholarship had tended to dismiss the great Pacific migrations as a string of lucky accidents — windblown survivors drifting between islands rather than purposeful travel. Piailug's success forced that view to be reconsidered. Wayfinding, it turned out, was real, learnable and astonishingly precise.

C The system itself is a layered one. The night sky provides the most stable element: Polynesian navigators memorise what they call a "star compass" of around 220 rising and setting points around the horizon. By day, the angle of the sun gives bearings. Wind direction and the long ocean swell each contribute another layer; even the behaviour of certain seabirds — those that fly out to fish at dawn and return to land at dusk — can be read as a kind of directional arrow. No element on its own is conclusive. The navigator's skill lies in weighing all of them at once, accepting that they sometimes disagree, and forming a judgement.

D Compare this to the way most people in industrialised societies now find their way. A device in the pocket says where we are; another in the car says where we should go. Neither asks the user to attend to landmarks, remember sequences of turns, or keep an internal sense of north. The skill of wayfinding has not died, exactly. It has been quietly handed over to satellites.

E Whether this handover has come at a cost is contested. Research carried out on London taxi drivers, who must learn an enormous mental map of the city's 25,000 streets and 20,000 landmarks before they are licensed, has produced striking findings. Using brain imaging, scientists have shown that the hippocampus — the region responsible for spatial memory — is measurably enlarged in experienced taxi drivers. Some studies have suggested that the reverse is also true: where spatial work is taken over by external aids, the relevant brain regions shrink. The findings remain disputed in detail, but the broad pattern is consistent.

F In recent years, a small but determined movement has begun to make the case for what its supporters call "navigational fitness". Its proponents are not technophobes. They use modern tools, but argue that a faculty which is never exercised eventually cannot be relied on, even when external systems fail. Their argument is essentially one of resilience: that the same logic which leads us to keep paper maps on board ocean-going vessels should lead us to keep some of the perceptual skills alive in everyday life.

G None of this means that GPS is a mistake. It is, in most respects, a remarkable tool: cheap, fast, and accurate to within metres. But like any tool, its benefits are paid for by the capacities it replaces. The interesting question, in the end, is not whether to use the new technology, but how to remain, in its absence, capable of finding the way ourselves.

Discussion essay

Question

Others argue that schools should focus only on the skills that students will actually use in their future lives.

Task 2 time: 40:00

Discussion essay structure (4 paragraphs)

  • Introduction: paraphrase the question. Briefly indicate that you will discuss both views before giving your own opinion.
  • Body paragraph 1 — View A: the case for keeping traditional skills in schools. Reasons + one example.
  • Body paragraph 2 — View B: the case for focusing only on future-relevant skills. Reasons + one example.
  • Conclusion: state clearly which view you find more convincing, and why. (You should give your opinion somewhere — either built across the body paragraphs or in the conclusion.)

Useful chunks for the discussion essay

  • Introducing view A: "On the one hand, those in favour of teaching traditional skills would argue that…"
  • Supporting view A: "Such skills are not merely practical; they also…" / "One clear example would be…"
  • Introducing view B: "On the other hand, it is sometimes argued that…"
  • Supporting view B: "From this perspective, school time should be devoted to…"
  • Conceding: "There is undoubtedly some force to this position; however, …"
  • Stating your opinion: "In my view… / Personally, I find the first argument more convincing, primarily because…"
  • Conclusion: "To conclude, while…, on balance…"

Higher-band grammar to reach for

  • Conditionals: "If schools were to abandon such skills altogether, students would lose…"
  • Concessive clauses: "Although digital tools have undoubtedly made navigation easier, …"
  • Inversion: "Not only does map-reading develop spatial reasoning, it also…"
  • Cleft sentences: "What such skills offer that machines cannot is…"
  • Nominalisation: "the gradual erosion of practical competence", "the case for resilience"
