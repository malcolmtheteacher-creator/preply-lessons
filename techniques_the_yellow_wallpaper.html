SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B2/C1 · 50 min

The Yellow Wallpaper

Charlotte Perkins Gilman (1892) — a woman is shut in a room to be "cured", forbidden to write, and left alone with the wallpaper

Read in three parts

This is a woman's secret journal. Her physician husband, John, has prescribed a "rest cure": no work, no writing, no company — only rest in a room she did not choose. She writes anyway, in snatches, when no one is watching. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. Watch the entries grow shorter and the line between the room and her mind grow thinner. The story handles a real and serious experience of illness; read it with care.

Part 1 — The house and the rest cure

It is very seldom that mere ordinary people like John and myself secure ancestral halls for the summer. A colonial mansion, a hereditary estate, I would say a haunted house, and reach the height of romantic felicity — but that would be asking too much of fate!

John is practical in the extreme. He has no patience with faith, an intense horror of superstition, and he scoffs openly at any talk of things not to be felt and seen and put down in figures. John is a physician, and perhaps — (I would not say it to a living soul, of course, but this is dead paper and a great relief to my mind) — perhaps that is one reason I do not get well faster. You see, he does not believe I am sick! And what can one do?

If a physician of high standing, and one's own husband, assures friends and relatives that there is really nothing the matter with one but temporary nervous depression — a slight hysterical tendency — what is one to do? My brother is also a physician, and also of high standing, and he says the same thing. So I take phosphates or phosphites — whichever it is — and tonics, and journeys, and air, and exercise, and am absolutely forbidden to "work" until I am well again. Personally, I disagree with their ideas. Personally, I believe that congenial work, with excitement and change, would do me good. But what is one to do?

I did write for a while in spite of them; but it does exhaust me a good deal — having to be so sly about it, or else meet with heavy opposition. The most beautiful place! It is quite alone, standing well back from the road, quite three miles from the village.

I don't like our room a bit. I wanted one downstairs that opened on the piazza, but John would not hear of it. The paint and paper look as if a boys' school had used it. It is stripped off — the paper — in great patches all around the head of my bed, about as far as I can reach, and in a great place on the other side of the room low down. I never saw a worse paper in my life. One of those sprawling flamboyant patterns committing every artistic sin.

It is dull enough to confuse the eye in following, pronounced enough to constantly irritate and provoke study, and when you follow the lame uncertain curves for a little distance they suddenly commit suicide — plunge off at outrageous angles, destroy themselves in unheard of contradictions. The color is repellent, almost revolting; a smouldering unclean yellow, strangely faded by the slow-turning sunlight.

Pause and discuss:
  • "He does not believe I am sick!" The cure forbids the one thing — writing — that she says helps her. What does it tell us that her treatment is the silencing of her own voice?
  • Three times she asks "what is one to do?" Who holds all the authority in this household, and how is it justified?
  • She describes the wallpaper as if it were alive and self-destructive. Why might Gilman make her see the paper this way so early, before anything has "happened"?

Part 2 — The wallpaper takes hold

We have been here two weeks, and I haven't felt like writing before, since that first day. I am sitting by the window now, up in this atrocious nursery, and there is nothing to hinder my writing as much as I please, save lack of strength. John is away all day, and even some nights when his cases are serious. I am glad my case is not serious! But these nervous troubles are dreadfully depressing.

John does not know how much I really suffer. He knows there is no reason to suffer, and that satisfies him. Of course it is only nervousness. It does weigh on me so not to do my duty in any way! I meant to be such a help to John, such a real rest and comfort, and here I am a comparative burden already!

I'm getting really fond of the room in spite of the wallpaper. Perhaps because of the wallpaper. It dwells in my mind so! I lie here on this great immovable bed — it is nailed down, I believe — and follow that pattern about by the hour. It is as good as gymnastics, I assure you. I start, we'll say, at the bottom, down in the corner over there where it has not been touched, and I determine for the thousandth time that I will follow that pointless pattern to some sort of a conclusion.

I know a little of the principle of design, and I know this thing was not arranged on any laws of radiation, or alternation, or repetition, or symmetry, or anything else that I ever heard of. It is repeated, of course, by the breadths, but not otherwise. Looked at in one way each breadth stands alone; the bloated curves and flourishes — a kind of "debased Romanesque" with delirium tremens — go waddling up and down in isolated columns of fatuity.

But, on the other hand, they connect diagonally, and the sprawling outlines run off in great slanting waves of optic horror, like a lot of wallowing seaweeds in full chase. The whole thing goes horizontally, too, at least it seems so, and I exhaust myself in trying to distinguish the order of its going in that direction.

There are things in that paper that nobody knows but me, or ever will. Behind that outside pattern the dim shapes get clearer every day. It is always the same shape, only very numerous. And it is like a woman stooping down and creeping about behind that pattern. I don't like it a bit. I wonder — I begin to think — I wish John would take me away from here!

Pause and discuss:
  • "John does not know how much I really suffer… that satisfies him." What is the danger of being told, by someone who loves you, that your distress is not real?
  • With nothing to do and no one to talk to, she pours all her trained intelligence into reading the wallpaper. How does enforced idleness turn into obsession?
  • The "dim shapes" become "a woman stooping down and creeping about behind that pattern." Whom do you think the trapped woman might represent?

Part 3 — The woman behind the pattern, and the ending

The front pattern does move — and no wonder! The woman behind shakes it! Sometimes I think there are a great many women behind, and sometimes only one, and she crawls around fast, and her crawling shakes it all over. Then in the very bright spots she keeps still, and in the very shady spots she just takes hold of the bars and shakes them hard. And she is all the time trying to climb through. But nobody could climb through that pattern — it strangles so.

I have watched her sometimes away off in the open country, creeping as fast as a cloud shadow in a high wind. If only that top pattern could be gotten off from the under one! I mean to try it, little by little. I have found out another funny thing, but I shan't tell it this time! It does not do to trust people too much.

As soon as it was moonlight and that poor thing began to crawl and shake the pattern, I got up and ran to help her. I pulled and she shook, I shook and she pulled, and before morning we had peeled off yards of that paper. I am getting angry enough to do something desperate. To jump out of the window would be admirable exercise, but the bars are too strong even to try.

This is the last day, but it is enough. John is to stay in town over night, and won't be out until this evening. I have locked the door and thrown the key down into the front path. I don't want to go out, and I don't want to have anybody come in, till John comes. I want to astonish him. I've got a rope up here that even Jennie did not find. If that woman does get out, and tries to get away, I can tie her!

I am securely fastened now by my well-hidden rope — you don't get me out in the road there! It is so pleasant to be out in this great room and creep around as I please! I don't want to go outside. I won't, even if Jennie asks me to. For outside you have to creep on the ground, and everything is green instead of yellow. But here I can creep smoothly on the floor, and my shoulder just fits in that long smooch around the wall, so I cannot lose my way.

Why, there's John at the door! It is no use, young man, you can't open it! How he does call and pound! Now he's crying for an axe. It would be a shame to break down that beautiful door!

"John dear!" said I in the gentlest voice. "The key is down by the front steps, under a plantain leaf!"

That silenced him for a few moments. Then he said — very quietly indeed: "Open the door, my darling!"

"I can't," said I. "The key is down by the front door under a plantain leaf!"

And then I said it again, several times, very gently and slowly, and said it so often that he had to go and see, and he got it of course, and came in. He stopped short by the door.

"What is the matter?" he cried. "For God's sake, what are you doing!"

I kept on creeping just the same, but I looked at him over my shoulder.

"I've got out at last," said I, "in spite of you and Jane! And I've pulled off most of the paper, so you can't put me back!"

Now why should that man have fainted? But he did, and right across my path by the wall, so that I had to creep over him every time!

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • By the end she no longer pities the trapped woman — she is her, creeping the room. Read her last lines again. Is this a breakdown, an escape, or a strange, terrible kind of freedom?
  • "In spite of you and Jane!" — most readers take "Jane" to be the narrator's own first name. What does it mean that she now speaks of herself as someone she has left behind?
  • The husband faints and she "creeps over him every time." After a whole story of him controlling her, what has the final image quietly reversed?
  • Your turn: Have you ever been told that something was being done "for your own good" by someone who would not listen to how it actually felt? How did that silencing affect you?

Grammar — Aspect: Continuous & Perfect Continuous

The narrator's collapse is written almost entirely in the language of ongoing, unfinished, changing things: she is getting worse, the shapes get clearer every day, the woman had been creeping for hours behind the pattern. That is the work of aspect — not when something happens, but how we view it: as a complete event, or as something still in progress, or as something that has gone on for a stretch of time.

1. Continuous (present & past) — an action in progress, or a state that is changing

Form: be + -ing. Use the present continuous for what is happening now or around now, and especially for a state that is changing: "I am getting worse." Use the past continuous for an action in progress at a past moment, often interrupted: "The woman was creeping when the moonlight fell across the wall."

1. "I think I worse," she writes; the change is happening now. (get — present continuous)

2. The dim shapes clearer every single day. (become — present continuous, changing state)

3. When John came home, the narrator the pattern by the hour. (follow — past continuous, action in progress)

4. The woman behind the paper the bars as the moon rose. (shake — past continuous)

1. am getting (present continuous: be + -ing, for a changing state)

2. are becoming (present continuous for a gradual change; "are getting" also acceptable)

3. was following (past continuous: an action in progress at a past moment)

4. was shaking (past continuous for an ongoing past action)

2. Perfect continuous (present & past) — duration up to a point

Form: have/has been + -ing (present) or had been + -ing (past). It stresses how long something has been going on, leading up to "now" or up to a past moment. Present: "I have been watching it for hours." Past: "She had been creeping long before I understood what I saw." Often used with for and since.

1. "I this paper for hours," she admits. (watch — present perfect continuous, with "for")

2. We in this house since the start of summer. (stay — present perfect continuous, with "since")

3. By the time John returned, the woman behind the pattern all night. (creep — past perfect continuous)

4. She was exhausted because she the paper off the wall. (pull — past perfect continuous)

1. have been watching (present perfect continuous: have been + -ing, duration up to now)

2. have been staying (present perfect continuous with "since" + start point)

3. had been creeping (past perfect continuous: duration up to a past moment)

4. had been pulling (past perfect continuous explaining a past result/state)

3. Stative vs dynamic — why some verbs resist the continuous

Some verbs name a state, not an action: know, believe, seem, contain, want, understand, mean. States simply are — so they normally stay in the simple form and resist -ing. Compare the narrator's "John does not believe I am sick" (state — simple) with "the paper is moving" (action — continuous). 2. is moving (dynamic action happening now — continuous)

What Gilman does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are the reason a story published in 1892 still unsettles a reader and still feels like an argument about who gets believed.

1. The unreliable, deteriorating narrator — the voice we outgrow

We see the whole story through one mind, and that mind is coming apart. Early on she is sharp, witty, self-aware; by the end she reports tying herself to the wall in a tone of cheerful calm. Gilman destabilises our trust so gradually that we cannot name the moment it breaks. The narrator never tells us she is unwell — the prose betrays what she cannot see, so that we come to know more than she does and, finally, more than she will admit.

2. The diary / epistolary form — short, broken entries that mirror a mind

The story is built as a secret journal, written in stolen moments. Gilman fragments it into short entries that grow shorter, more clipped, more dashed-off as the narrator declines; the white space between entries enacts the gaps opening in her grip on things. Because it is "dead paper," she confesses what she would tell "no living soul" — so the form itself licenses the intimacy that makes the horror land.

3. Symbolism — the wallpaper and the trapped woman

The hideous yellow paper, with its bars and its creeping figure, embodies the narrator's confinement: a woman shut behind a pattern she did not choose, trying to climb through and being strangled by it. As the story goes on, the symbol collapses into its meaning — the trapped woman is the narrator — so that freeing "her" and losing herself become the same act. The paper externalises an inner state we could not otherwise watch directly.

4. Irony — the cure that destroys, the love that confines

The deepest move is structural irony. The "rest cure" is meant to heal her; it is the very thing that undoes her, because it strips away work, voice and company. John's control is loving — he calls her "my darling," he means to protect her — and Gilman lets that tenderness indict itself: the gentler the cage, the harder it is to name as a cage. The final image, in which the controlled wife creeps over her fainted husband, inverts the whole order of the house.

Task A · Retell the story in your own words (3 minutes)

Before we discuss it, tell the story back to me as if I had never read it — in your own words, not line by line. Aim for about eight to ten sentences.

As you go, use at least five words from the Vocabulary tab, and tell it using continuous and perfect-continuous aspect from the Grammar tab — describe what was going on ("she was following the pattern by the hour") and how long things had been happening ("she had been watching the paper for weeks"). These beats will help you: a woman is brought to a lonely house and shut in an upstairs room for a "rest cure" · she is forbidden to write, so she keeps a secret journal · with nothing to do, she becomes obsessed with the hideous yellow wallpaper · she starts to see a woman creeping behind the pattern · she has been peeling the paper for days, and on the last night she frees — and becomes — the woman, creeping over her fainted husband.

Task B · Controlled "for your own good" (4 minutes)

John overrules his wife at every turn, always insisting it is for her health. Describe a time when someone decided something for you, or for someone close to you, "for your own good" — a parent, a partner, a doctor, a manager. Take it in three steps:

  1. what was decided, and the reason given for overruling you;
  2. how it actually felt to have your own view set aside;
  3. whether, looking back, the control protected you or harmed you — or both.

Use at least one perfect-continuous form ("they had been telling me for years that…") and one continuous form ("while it was happening, I was feeling…"), plus two effect verbs from the Technique tab.

Task C · A space that affected your mind (3 minutes)

The narrator's room — its bars, its nailed-down bed, its strangling paper — slowly reshapes how she thinks. Describe a real place that worked on your mind: a cramped office, a hospital ward, a childhood bedroom, a long lockdown indoors.

Talk me through what the space was doing to you over time — what you kept noticing, what you started to dwell on, how your mood was shifting the longer you stayed. Lean on the continuous for the change as it unfolded ("the longer I was sitting there, the more I was…") and the perfect continuous for how long it had been going on.

Task D · Noticing a gradual change (4 minutes)

The most frightening thing in the story is how slowly the narrator changes — so slowly she cannot see it. Tell me about a gradual change you have noticed, either in yourself or in someone you know well: a drift in mood, a growing habit, a quiet loss of confidence, a slow recovery.

Describe the change as a process still unfolding, not a finished fact. Use the present perfect continuous for the duration ("she has been withdrawing for months", "I have been feeling steadier lately") and the present continuous for the direction of travel ("things are getting better / harder"). End by saying what you think the change is moving towards.

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The narrator is harmed most by people who love her and believe they are helping. Why can well-meant control be harder to resist than open cruelty?
  2. Gilman based the story on her own "rest cure." Does knowing it grew from real experience change how you read the ending?
  3. The whole story is one woman's private journal, and no one in the house ever reads it. Who, then, is she writing to — and why does that matter?
  4. If the narrator could be heard and believed early on — given work, company, a voice — do you think the ending could have been different? What does the story ask of how we treat people in distress today?
