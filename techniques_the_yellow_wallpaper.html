The Yellow Wallpaper

Charlotte Perkins Gilman — a summer of enforced rest, a barred room, and a pattern that will not stay still.

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. In the 1880s a fashionable doctor might prescribe a rest cure: weeks lying in bed with no visitors, no books and no work of any kind.
a rest cure (n) — a real 19th-century treatment of enforced bed rest and total inactivity, prescribed especially to women for "nervous" illness.
2. The physician examined her for barely five minutes, wrote out a prescription, and assured the whole family that nothing was seriously wrong.
a physician (n) — a doctor, especially one of high professional standing.
3. After months of confinement in a single room, even the corridor outside felt to him like open country.
confinement (n) — the state of being shut in or kept in a restricted space; the loss of freedom to move.
4. Late at night something seemed to creep along the landing — moving slowly, silently, low against the floor.
to creep (v) — to move slowly and quietly, low or close to the ground, often in a way that unsettles.
5. She found the colour of the room so repellent that she could hardly stay in it; it made her feel faintly sick.
repellent (adj) — causing strong dislike or disgust; driving you away.
6. What began as idle curiosity grew into an obsession: from morning to night she could think of nothing else.
an obsession (n) — a thought or interest that fills the mind so completely you cannot turn away from it.
7. The old hotel was decorated in a flamboyant style — gilded curls, loud colours, patterns on every surface, far too much of everything.
flamboyant (adj) — strikingly bold, showy or elaborate, often to the point of excess.
8. In her fever she slipped into delirium, talking to people who were not there and watching shapes move across the walls.
delirium (n) — a disturbed state of mind marked by confusion, restlessness and sometimes visions of things that are not there.
9. The fire had died down to a smouldering glow — no flame any more, just a dull, unclean orange that would not quite go out.
smouldering (adj) — burning slowly without flame; (of a colour or a feeling) glowing dully and refusing to go out.
10. The ivy had wound itself so tightly around the young tree that it threatened to strangle it altogether.
to strangle (v) — to choke by squeezing the throat; figuratively, to smother something so it cannot grow or escape.

The story, in three parts

What you are about to read is a woman's secret journal, written in stolen moments during one summer. Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The rest cure

It is very seldom that mere ordinary people like John and myself secure ancestral halls for the summer. A colonial mansion, a hereditary estate — I would say a haunted house, and reach the height of romantic felicity — but that would be asking too much of fate!

John is practical in the extreme. He scoffs openly at any talk of things not to be felt and seen and put down in figures. John is a physician, and perhaps — (I would not say it to a living soul, of course, but this is dead paper and a great relief to my mind) — perhaps that is one reason I do not get well faster. You see, he does not believe I am sick! And what can one do?

If a physician of high standing, and one's own husband, assures friends and relatives that there is really nothing the matter with one but temporary nervous depression, what is one to do? I am absolutely forbidden to "work" until I am well again. Personally, I believe that congenial work, with excitement and change, would do me good. But what is one to do? I did write for a while in spite of them; but it does exhaust me a good deal — having to be so sly about it, or else meet with heavy opposition.

I don't like our room a bit. I wanted one downstairs that opened on the piazza, but John would not hear of it. It was a nursery first, I should judge, for the windows are barred for little children. The paper is stripped off in great patches all around the head of my bed. I never saw a worse paper in my life: one of those sprawling flamboyant patterns committing every artistic sin. The colour is repellent, almost revolting; a smouldering unclean yellow, strangely faded by the slow-turning sunlight.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. According to the narrator herself, why isn't she getting well faster?

2. What room has she been given, and what does she notice about it?

1. Her husband John — a physician — does not believe she is sick, and she is forbidden the "work" (writing) she believes would actually do her good. She writes anyway, in secret.

2. An upstairs room that was once a nursery: barred windows, and a torn, hideous yellow wallpaper whose colour she finds repellent.

Discuss:
  • Her prescribed cure forbids the one thing she says helps her — writing. In a household like this one, who decides what counts as "sick" and what counts as "well" — and what happens to the person who disagrees?

Part 2 — The wallpaper takes hold

We have been here two weeks, and I haven't felt like writing before, since that first day. I am sitting by the window now, up in this atrocious nursery. John is away all day, and even some nights when his cases are serious. I am glad my case is not serious! But these nervous troubles are dreadfully depressing.

John does not know how much I really suffer. He knows there is no reason to suffer, and that satisfies him. It does weigh on me so not to do my duty in any way! I meant to be such a help to John, such a real rest and comfort, and here I am a comparative burden already!

I'm getting really fond of the room in spite of the wallpaper. Perhaps because of the wallpaper. It dwells in my mind so! I lie here on this great immovable bed — it is nailed down, I believe — and follow that pattern about by the hour. I start at the bottom, down in the corner over there where it has not been touched, and I determine for the thousandth time that I will follow that pointless pattern to some sort of a conclusion.

The sprawling outlines run off in great slanting waves of optic horror, like a lot of wallowing seaweeds in full chase. I exhaust myself in trying to distinguish the order of its going.

There are things in that paper that nobody knows but me, or ever will. Behind that outside pattern the dim shapes get clearer every day. It is always the same shape, only very numerous. And it is like a woman stooping down and creeping about behind that pattern. I don't like it a bit. I wonder — I begin to think — I wish John would take me away from here!

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does John know about her suffering — and what does he not know?

2. What has she begun to see behind the outside pattern?

1. He "knows there is no reason to suffer, and that satisfies him" — he does not know how much she really suffers, and does not ask.

2. Dim shapes that get clearer every day — always the same shape — like a woman stooping down and creeping about behind the pattern.

Discuss:
  • With no work, no company and nothing to do, she pours all her trained intelligence into the wallpaper. Is the obsession a symptom of her illness — or the mind's last way of keeping itself busy?

Part 3 — The last day

The front pattern does move — and no wonder! The woman behind shakes it! Sometimes I think there are a great many women behind, and sometimes only one, and she crawls around fast, and her crawling shakes it all over. She is all the time trying to climb through. But nobody could climb through that pattern — it strangles so.

As soon as it was moonlight and that poor thing began to crawl and shake the pattern, I got up and ran to help her. I pulled and she shook, I shook and she pulled, and before morning we had peeled off yards of that paper.

This is the last day, but it is enough. John is to stay in town over night, and won't be out until this evening. I have locked the door and thrown the key down into the front path. It is so pleasant to be out in this great room and creep around as I please! I don't want to go outside. For outside you have to creep on the ground, and everything is green instead of yellow. But here I can creep smoothly on the floor, and my shoulder just fits in that long smooch around the wall, so I cannot lose my way.

Why, there's John at the door! It is no use, young man, you can't open it! "John dear!" said I in the gentlest voice. "The key is down by the front steps, under a plantain leaf!" I said it again, several times, very gently and slowly, so often that he had to go and see, and he got it of course, and came in. He stopped short by the door.

"What is the matter?" he cried. "For God's sake, what are you doing!"

I kept on creeping just the same, but I looked at him over my shoulder. "I've got out at last," said I, "in spite of you and Jane! And I've pulled off most of the paper, so you can't put me back!"

Now why should that man have fainted? But he did, and right across my path by the wall, so that I had to creep over him every time!

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What have she and the woman behind the pattern been doing to the wallpaper, and why?

2. What does John find when he finally opens the door — and how does the story end?

1. By moonlight they have been peeling the paper off together — she pulls and the woman shakes — to free the woman trapped behind the pattern.

2. His wife creeping around the room, shoulder to the wall. She tells him she has "got out at last, in spite of you and Jane" — and John faints, so that she has to creep over him every time round.

Discuss:
  • "In spite of you and Jane!" — most readers take "Jane" to be the narrator's own name. After a whole story of being managed and overruled, what has she escaped from — and what has the escape cost? Is the last image a defeat, a victory, or somehow both?

Grammar — Continuous & Perfect Continuous

This story is written almost entirely in the language of unfinished, ongoing, changing things: the shapes are getting clearer, she has been watching the paper for hours, the woman had been creeping all night. That is aspect — not when something happens, but how we see it: complete, or still in motion.

The continuous — be + -ing: an action in progress, or a state that is changing.
• Present: "I am sitting by the window now." · "The pattern is changing — the shapes are getting clearer every day."
• Past — in progress at a past moment, often interrupted: "When John opened the door, his wife was creeping around the room."
The perfect continuous — have/has been + -ing, had been + -ing: duration up to a point, often with for and since.
• Up to now: "I have been lying here for hours, watching it." · "We have been staying here since June."
• Up to a past moment: "By morning, they had been peeling the paper all night."
One warning: state verbs resist -ing. Know, believe, seem, want name states, not actions — so it's "John does not believe I am sick", never "is not believing".

1. Complete each sentence with one of these verbs, in the present continuous or the present perfect continuous: lie · change · stay · shake

1. "I here for hours," she writes, "watching the pattern move." (duration up to now)

2. The paper — the shapes behind it get clearer every day. (a change in progress)

3. We in this house since the beginning of June. (duration up to now)

4. Look at the moonlight on the wall — the woman the bars again. (happening right now)

1. have been lying (present perfect continuous — duration up to now, with "for hours")

2. is changing (present continuous — a change still in progress)

3. have been staying (present perfect continuous — "since" marks the starting point)

4. is shaking (present continuous — happening at this moment)

2. State or action? Put each bracketed verb into the present simple or the present continuous

2. At this very moment she (creep) around the edge of the room.

3. "I (know) that paper has a secret nobody else will ever find."

4. The front pattern (move) — she is certain of it now.

2. is creeping (an action in progress now)

3. Complete the retelling of the ending

Put each bracketed verb into the past continuous or the past perfect continuous. Then read the finished paragraph aloud.

By the last morning, she and the woman behind the pattern (peel) the paper all night, and yards of it lay on the floor. When John finally opened the door, his wife (creep) around the room with her shoulder against the wall. She (watch) that pattern for weeks — and now, she told him, she had got out at last. had been peeling · was creeping · had been watching

The perfect continuous measures how long something had been going on before the moment we see it — which is why the ending feels less like a shock and more like something that had been coming all summer.

Going deeper — take the idea further

You have the story and the words. Now push the ideas outward — into history, medicine and society. Do all of this out loud. There are no right answers here, only good reasons.

Useful language for building an argument: "What's striking is…", "You could argue that…", "It's not X that matters, it's Y…", "Seen from today, …", "That may be true, but…".
1 · The cure that made things worse

The rest cure was real: in the 1880s, women diagnosed with "nervous" conditions were ordered to bed for weeks — no reading, no writing, no visitors — while men with the same symptoms were often sent riding and hunting in the open air. Why might a treatment look perfectly sensible to everyone except the patient? Think of other moments in history when the official cure did more harm than the illness — and how anyone finally noticed.

2 · Who gets believed?

In the story, a physician's confident verdict outweighs the patient's own account of herself — and her brother, also a doctor, "says the same thing." In any society: whose reports of their own pain tend to be believed, and whose tend to be politely dismissed? What gives a voice authority — a title, a tone, a gender, a uniform? How much of that has changed since 1892, and how much only looks changed?

3 · Writing as survival

Forbidden to write, the narrator writes anyway — "this is dead paper and a great relief to my mind." People in confinement have always done this: prisoners' diaries, sailors' logs, letters never sent. What does putting an experience into words actually do for the person writing? Is creative work a luxury for comfortable times, or a necessity that people reach for precisely when things are worst? Give examples from history, books or film.

4 · The story that changed medicine

Gilman had been prescribed the rest cure herself. She sent this story to the famous physician who had prescribed it — and years later wrote that he had changed his treatment after reading it. Where argument and complaint had failed, a piece of fiction worked. Why can a story get past defences that facts cannot? What other works of fiction — books or films — have actually changed a law, a practice or a public mind?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Everyone who harms the narrator believes they are helping her. Why is well-meant control so much harder to resist — and to name — than open cruelty?
  2. The whole story is a journal that no one in the house ever reads. Who, then, is she writing to — and why does the writing still matter?
  3. Gilman wrote the story to protest a treatment she had been given herself — and the treatment changed. What can a story do that a scientific argument cannot?
