The invisible gorilla: true or false?

In 1999, two psychologists filmed students passing basketballs and asked viewers to count the passes. Here are six claims about that study and its follow-ups. Decide which are true.

1. Viewers who counted the passes by the team in white often missed a person in a gorilla suit who walked through the middle of the screen.
True. About half of the viewers did not see it.
2. People who know about the gorilla experiment are protected from missing the next unexpected thing.
False. Knowing about the effect does not remove it.
3. In a 2013 study, most of twenty expert radiologists missed a picture of a gorilla placed on a lung scan.
True. Most of them did not report it.
4. The gorilla on the scan was many times bigger than the small nodules they were looking for.
True. It was roughly the size of a matchbox, many times larger than a typical nodule.
5. The radiologists who missed the gorilla had never looked at that part of the scan.
False. Eye-tracking showed that many of them had looked straight at it.
6. The harder the counting task was, the more people noticed the gorilla.
False. The harder the task, the fewer people noticed it.
Talk about it:
  • Which of these results surprises you most, and why?
  • If looking at something is not enough to see it, what is?

The evidence, in three steps

Before each part, make a prediction out loud. Then read, and check yourself.

Part 1 — The experiment

Before you read: Viewers are told to count passes. What do you predict will happen to the thing nobody asked them to watch for?

In 1999, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris at Harvard University filmed six students passing basketballs, three in white shirts and three in black. Viewers were asked to count how many passes the white team made. Halfway through, a person in a gorilla suit walked into the scene, faced the camera, beat their chest and walked out again, remaining on screen for about nine seconds.

Roughly half of the participants never noticed it. Asked afterwards whether they had seen anything unusual, many said no, and some were so certain that they asked to see the video again. When they did, they could not believe the gorilla had been there all along.

Part 2 — Looking is not seeing

Before you read: If attention decides what we notice, what might that mean for witnesses, drivers or doctors?

The effect is called inattentional blindness, and it shows that attention does not simply filter what we see; it largely decides what we are aware of. Having been told to concentrate on one task, participants did not register the gorilla at all, even when it was large and dark and in the centre of the picture. The more demanding the task and the more similar the gorilla was to the team being tracked, the more often it was missed.

A related finding is change blindness. In an experiment by Simons and Daniel Levin, a researcher stopped a pedestrian to ask for directions. While they talked, two workers carrying a door walked between them, and during that moment the researcher was swapped for a different person. About half of the pedestrians continued giving directions without noticing.

New words in this part — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

Researchers call it inattentional blindness: failing to notice a fully visible object because attention is focused elsewhere.
inattentional blindness (n) — failing to see something clearly in view because your attention is on something else.

Part 3 — The cost in real life

Before you read: Experts do this every day. Would you expect experts to miss something unexpected? Why?

The consequences are not confined to the laboratory. In 2013, researchers asked twenty radiologists to look for cancer nodules in CT scans. On the final scan, a picture of a gorilla about the size of a matchbox had been added. Eighty-three per cent of the radiologists missed it, even though eye-tracking showed that most of them had looked directly at it.

The same limit is behind accidents on the road. Drivers who are talking on a phone, including hands-free, tend to miss things that they are looking straight at. The lesson is not that people are careless. It is that attention has a limited capacity, so being confident that you would notice is no protection.

Participle clauses

Participle clauses make a sentence shorter and more formal. They are useful for explaining causes and for describing what was happening at the same time.

-ing clause (active, same time or reason): Concentrating on the instrument, the pilot missed the plane.
-ed clause (passive meaning): Told to look for nodules, the radiologists missed the gorilla.
having + -ed (earlier action): Having been told to count, viewers ignored everything else.
The subject of the participle must be the subject of the main clause. Never Walking to work, the rain started.

Would they notice?

Five real-life situations. Say whether the person would notice, and use a participle clause in your explanation. Check the research last. You do not have to match it.

1. A witness to a robbery was watching the robber's gun. Did she remember the robber's face?-ing clause
Weapon focus. Often, no: attention narrows on the weapon, so other details are poorly remembered.
2. A pilot was concentrating on an instrument. Did he see a plane on the runway?-ed clause
Selective attention. There are real cases like this; attention to one display can reduce awareness of the outside.
3. A shopper checking her phone walks through a crowd. Does she see a person she knows?-ing clause
Divided attention. Quite possibly not; she may also say 'I looked straight at them'.
4. A security guard is told to look for one specific person on a monitor. Will he notice something else unusual?-ed clause
The task decides. Probably not: people find what they look for and miss what they don't expect.
5. Does telling people about the gorilla make them notice the next unexpected thing?having + -ed
Awareness is not protection. Knowing that it exists does not remove the effect; people still miss the next surprise.
