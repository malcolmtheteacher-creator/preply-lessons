The Invisible Gorilla

If half of us can look straight at a gorilla and not see it, what else are we missing?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The experiment

In 1999, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris at Harvard University filmed six students passing basketballs, three in white shirts and three in black. Viewers were asked to count how many passes the white team made. Halfway through, a person in a gorilla suit walked into the scene, faced the camera, beat their chest and walked out again, remaining on screen for about nine seconds.

Roughly half of the participants never noticed it. Asked afterwards whether they had seen anything unusual, many said no, and some were so certain that they asked to see the video again. When they did, they could not believe the gorilla had been there all along.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were viewers asked to do?

2. How did participants react afterwards?

1. Count the passes made by the white team.

2. About half had not noticed the gorilla; some were so certain that nothing unusual had happened that they asked to see it again.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think people are so confident that they would notice something unusual?

Part 2 — Looking is not seeing

The effect is called inattentional blindness, and it shows that attention does not simply filter what we see; it largely decides what we are aware of. Having been told to concentrate on one task, participants did not register the gorilla at all, even when it was large and dark and in the centre of the picture. The more demanding the task and the more similar the gorilla was to the team being tracked, the more often it was missed.

A related finding is change blindness. In an experiment by Simons and Daniel Levin, a researcher stopped a pedestrian to ask for directions. While they talked, two workers carrying a door walked between them, and during that moment the researcher was swapped for a different person. About half of the pedestrians continued giving directions without noticing.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does inattentional blindness show?

2. What happened in the experiment about directions?

1. That attention largely decides what we are aware of, and we can miss even obvious things.

2. The researcher was swapped for another person behind a door, and about half of the pedestrians did not notice.

Discuss:
  • If our awareness is built by attention, how reliable is a witness's memory of what they saw?

Part 3 — The cost in real life

The consequences are not confined to the laboratory. In 2013, researchers asked twenty radiologists to look for cancer nodules in CT scans. On the final scan, a picture of a gorilla about the size of a matchbox had been added. Eighty-three per cent of the radiologists missed it, even though eye-tracking showed that most of them had looked directly at it.

The same limit is behind accidents on the road. Drivers who are talking on a phone, including hands-free, tend to miss things that they are looking straight at. The lesson is not that people are careless. It is that attention has a limited capacity, so being confident that you would notice is no protection.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened in the radiology experiment?

2. What is the lesson?

1. Eighty-three per cent of radiologists missed a gorilla on the scan, though most had looked at it.

2. Attention has limited capacity; being confident that you would notice offers no protection.

Discuss:
  • Which jobs should be redesigned because of what we know about attention? How?

Grammar — participle clauses

Participle clauses let you link two ideas in one sentence, without a conjunction and a repeated subject. They are common in written English and in careful speaking.

Three forms:
• -ing clause (active, same time): "Focusing on the ball, most people missed the gorilla." (= Because they focused…)
• -ed clause (passive): "Asked afterwards, many said no." (= When they were asked…)
• having + past participle (earlier action): "Having counted the passes, he found the right number."
The rule: the subject of the participle clause must be the same as the subject of the main clause. "Walking down the street, the lamp post hit me" is wrong because the lamp post isn't walking! Use Walking down the street, I hit a lamp post.
Add while or after for clarity: While watching the video…

Would you have noticed?

Five situations. Say whether you think the person would notice and why, then reveal.

1. A witness to a robbery was watching the robber's gun. Did she remember the robber's face?
Weapon focus. Often, no: attention narrows on the weapon, so other details are poorly remembered.
2. A pilot was concentrating on an instrument. Did he see a plane on the runway?
Selective attention. There are real cases like this; attention to one display can reduce awareness of the outside.
3. A shopper checking her phone walks through a crowd. Does she see a person she knows?
Divided attention. Quite possibly not; she may also say 'I looked straight at them'.
4. A security guard is told to look for one specific person on a monitor. Will he notice something else unusual?
The task decides. Probably not: people find what they look for and miss what they don't expect.
5. Does telling people about the gorilla make them notice the next unexpected thing?
Awareness is not protection. Knowing that it exists does not remove the effect; people still miss the next surprise.
