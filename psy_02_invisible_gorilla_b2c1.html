The invisible gorilla: true or false?

In 1999, two psychologists filmed students passing basketballs and asked viewers to count the passes. Here are six claims about that study and its follow-ups. Decide which are true.

1. Viewers who counted the passes by the team in white often missed a person in a gorilla suit who walked through the middle of the screen.
True. About half of the viewers did not see it.
2. People who know about the gorilla experiment are protected from missing the next unexpected thing.
False. Knowing about the effect does not remove it.
3. In a 2013 study, most of twenty-four expert radiologists missed a picture of a gorilla placed on a lung scan.
True. Twenty of the twenty-four did not report it.
4. The gorilla on the scan was many times bigger than the small nodules they were looking for.
True. It was roughly the size of a matchbox, many times larger than a typical nodule.
5. The radiologists who missed the gorilla had never looked at that part of the scan.
False. Eye-tracking showed that most of them had looked straight at it.
6. The harder the counting task was, the more people noticed the gorilla.
False. The harder the task, the fewer people noticed it.
Talk about it:
  • Which of these results surprises you most, and why?
  • If looking at something is not enough to see it, what is?

The evidence, in three steps

Part 1 — The experiment

Before you read: Viewers are told to count passes. What do you predict will happen to the thing nobody asked them to watch for, and how will they react when they are told about it?

In 1999, Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris at Harvard University filmed six students passing basketballs, three in white shirts and three in black. Viewers were asked to count how many passes the white team made. Halfway through, a person in a gorilla suit walked into the scene, faced the camera, beat their chest and walked out again, remaining on screen for about nine seconds.

Roughly half of the participants never noticed it. Asked afterwards whether they had seen anything unusual, many said no, and some were so certain that they asked to see the video again. When they did, they could not believe the gorilla had been there all along.

all along (phrase) — from the very beginning and the whole time since. Example: "I searched the house for my keys, and they were in my coat pocket all along."

Part 2 — Looking is not seeing

Before you read: What do you think makes an unexpected object easier or harder to miss? And would you notice if the stranger you were talking to was suddenly replaced by someone else?

The effect is called inattentional blindness, and it shows that attention does not simply filter what we see; it largely decides what we are aware of. Having been told to concentrate on one task, participants did not register the gorilla at all, even when it was large and dark and in the centre of the picture. The more demanding the task and the more similar the gorilla was to the team being tracked, the more often it was missed.

A related finding is change blindness. In an experiment by Simons and Daniel Levin, a researcher stopped a pedestrian to ask for directions. While they talked, two workers carrying a door walked between them, and during that moment the researcher was swapped for a different person. About half of the pedestrians continued giving directions without noticing.

Words from this part — what does each word in bold mean here? Say your idea, then reveal.

“The effect is called inattentional blindness, and it shows that attention does not simply filter what we see; it largely decides what we are aware of.”
inattentional blindness (n) — failing to see something in plain view because your attention is focused on something else. Example: "Inattentional blindness may explain why a driver checking the satnav fails to see a cyclist right ahead."
“Having been told to concentrate on one task, participants did not register the gorilla at all, even when it was large and dark and in the centre of the picture.”
register (v) — to notice something consciously, so that you become aware of it. Example: "I heard my name in the announcement, but it didn't register until I was on the train."
“The more demanding the task and the more similar the gorilla was to the team being tracked, the more often it was missed.”
demanding (adj) — needing a lot of effort, skill or concentration. Example: "It's a demanding job, so she's exhausted by Friday."
Your call:
  • Looking is not the same as seeing. Give a real-life example.

Part 3 — The cost in real life

Before you read: Would you expect experts, such as doctors reading scans, to miss something unexpected? Why, or why not?

The consequences are not confined to the laboratory. In 2013, researchers asked twenty-four radiologists to look for cancer nodules in CT scans. On the final scan, a picture of a gorilla about the size of a matchbox had been added. Eighty-three per cent of the radiologists missed it, even though eye-tracking showed that most of them had looked directly at it.

The same limit is behind accidents on the road. Drivers who are talking on a phone, including hands-free, tend to miss things that they are looking straight at. The lesson is not that people are careless. It is that attention has a limited capacity, so being confident that you would notice is no protection.

Words from this part — what does each word in bold mean here? Say your idea, then reveal.

“The consequences are not confined to the laboratory.”
be confined to (phrase) — to exist or happen only in one place, group or situation. Example: "The problem isn't confined to big cities; small towns have it too."
“It is that attention has a limited capacity, so being confident that you would notice is no protection.”
capacity (n) — the amount that something or someone can hold or deal with. Example: "After three meetings in a row, I'd reached my capacity for new information."
Your call:
  • If experts miss large, obvious things, who should we blame: the expert, or the system they work in?

Participle clauses

Participle clauses make a sentence shorter and more formal. They are useful for explaining causes and for describing what was happening at the same time.

-ing clause (active, same time or reason): Focusing on the ball, most viewers missed the gorilla.
-ed clause (passive meaning): Asked afterwards, many viewers said they had seen nothing unusual.
having + -ed (earlier action): Having been told to count, viewers ignored everything else.
The subject of the participle must be the subject of the main clause. Never Walking to work, the rain started.

Build it

Rewrite each one as a single sentence that starts with a participle clause. Type it, say it, then reveal a model.

1. Because he was concentrating on the instrument, the pilot missed the plane on the runway. Use an -ing clause. → …, the pilot missed the plane on the runway.
Concentrating on the instrument, the pilot missed the plane on the runway.
2. While she was checking her phone, she walked straight past a friend. Use an -ing clause. → …, she walked straight past a friend.
Checking her phone, she walked straight past a friend. (or: While checking her phone, …)
3. The radiologists had been told to look for nodules. They missed the gorilla. Use an -ed clause. → …, the radiologists missed the gorilla.
Told to look for nodules, the radiologists missed the gorilla.
4. The viewers counted the passes. After that, they were asked whether they had seen anything unusual. Use having + past participle. → …, the viewers were asked whether they had seen anything unusual.
Having counted the passes, the viewers were asked whether they had seen anything unusual.

Would they notice?

Five real-life situations. Say whether the person would notice, and use the participle clause shown in your explanation. Then compare your view with the research or with one idea. You do not have to match it.

1. A witness to a robbery is watching the robber's gun. Would she notice the details of his face? -ing clause
Weapon focus. Studies suggest often not: attention narrows on the weapon, so witnesses remember the face and other details less well.
2. A pilot landing in a flight simulator is concentrating on the display in front of him. Would he notice a plane blocking the runway? -ing clause
Selective attention. In flight-simulator studies, some experienced pilots using a head-up display did not see a plane on the runway and continued to land.
3. A woman absorbed in her phone is walking across a busy square. Would she notice something strange, such as a clown on a unicycle? -ed clause
Divided attention. In one study, only about a quarter of people walking across a square while using a phone noticed a clown riding a unicycle near them.
4. A security guard has been told to watch for one particular person on a monitor. Would he notice something else unusual? -ed clause
Idea. Probably not. Told to look for one thing, people tend to find what they are looking for and miss what they don't expect.
5. Someone who knows all about the gorilla experiment watches a new version of the video. Would they notice the next unexpected event? having + -ed
Awareness is not protection. In a 2010 follow-up, people who knew the original video spotted the gorilla, but they were no better at noticing other unexpected changes in the same video.
