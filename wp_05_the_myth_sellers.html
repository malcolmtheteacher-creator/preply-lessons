The Hook

What if someone told you there is no truth — only persuasion?

The Pre-Socratics we have met — Thales, Heraclitus, Parmenides, Democritus — all believed that truth existed and could be discovered through reason. But in the 5th century BC, new voices emerged in Athens. The Sophists were travelling teachers who charged high fees to teach the art of winning arguments. And they taught that the person with the most skillful argument was the person with the truth.

This is not a small claim. This is a revolution. If truth is whatever you can persuade others to believe, then philosophy ceases to be about discovering a fixed reality and becomes about power. In this lesson, we see that crisis, and we see why it demanded a response.

II. The Story — Truth for Sale

In the second half of the 5th century BC, Athens was at the height of its power. Democracy was flourishing. The empire was rich. But it needed something new: citizens who could speak persuasively in the assembly and the courts. Into this moment came the Sophists.

Who Were the Sophists?

The word sophist originally meant "wise man" or "expert." But the Sophists were something unprecedented: the first professional educators. They travelled from city to city — particularly to Athens — and charged substantial fees to teach rhetoric: the art of persuasion, of winning arguments, of moving an audience.

The Pre-Socratics had been driven by wonder. They asked questions for their own sake. The Sophists were more practical. They asked: what knowledge is worth paying for? And their answer was: the knowledge that wins.

The sophists are those who make their living by their wisdom. — Plato, Protagoras 312c

Protagoras (c. 490–420 BC)

The most famous Sophist was Protagoras of Abdera. He was wealthy, influential, and taught in Athens for nearly forty years. Plato's dialogue Protagoras portrays a lavish dinner at his honour, attended by the city's most ambitious young men, all eager to pay for his instruction.

Protagoras's most famous claim was deceptively simple:

Man is the measure of all things — of the things that are, that they are; of the things that are not, that they are not. — Protagoras, Fragment B1

What does this mean? It means: there is no objective reality independent of the observer. What is true for you is true for you. What is true for me is true for me. There is no court of appeal beyond individual perception and belief. Truth is not something discovered; it is something each person measures or determines for themselves.

This is called relativism — the doctrine that truth is relative to the individual or the culture. Protagoras was not denying that we have experiences; he was denying that our experiences correspond to anything objective.

A modern parallel: "That's your truth, but it's not my truth." This phrase echoes Protagoras. Modern defenders of the Sophists have argued that he was onto something profound: that perspective matters, that knowledge is always situated, that there is no view from nowhere. The Sophists have been partially rehabilitated by modern scholars as serious philosophers, not mere charlatans. But the question remains: can we say that the Holocaust happened if we deny that any objective truth exists?

Gorgias (c. 485–375 BC)

Gorgias of Leontini was another celebrated Sophist, famous for his impromptu speeches and his ornate, poetic style. Unlike Protagoras, Gorgias focused almost entirely on rhetoric — the technique of persuasion — rather than philosophy.

But Gorgias made one famous philosophical claim, preserved in a lost work called On Nature. According to the ancient commentator Sextus Empiricus, Gorgias argued:

Nothing exists. If something existed, it could not be known. If it could be known, it could not be communicated. — Gorgias, reconstructed from Sextus Empiricus, Against the Mathematicians VII.65

This is a reductio ad absurdum — a logical trap set to catch his opponents. Gorgias was not seriously claiming that nothing exists. Rather, he was showing that the Pre-Socratics could not defend their claims about being and knowledge without contradiction. If Parmenides says "being is," how does he know that? How does he communicate it? Gorgias proves they cannot escape these traps.

What matters for Gorgias is not truth about the world, but the power of the word itself. Rhetoric, he believed, was the highest art — the art of moving souls through language alone.

The Sophists' Contribution and Danger

Why They Mattered

The Sophists democratized education. Before them, wisdom was a privilege of the wealthy or the naturally talented. The Sophists said: you can learn to speak persuasively, to argue effectively, to win. This was liberating. It meant that any ambitious young man could acquire the skills he needed to succeed in democratic Athens.

They also shifted philosophy's focus. The Pre-Socratics asked about nature. The Sophists asked about humans: how do we live together? What is justice? What is courage? What is excellence? This turn to ethics and politics was crucial. Philosophy could not remain only a study of matter and change; it had to become a study of how to live.

Why They Were Feared

If truth is relative, and if persuasion is the greatest power, then might makes right. A good lawyer can make a guilty man innocent by the sheer force of his rhetoric. A skilled politician can convince the city to go to war by appealing to emotions rather than reason. The Sophists offered a toolkit for truth-telling and lie-telling alike. They did not distinguish between them.

In Plato's dialogues, Socrates is often outraged by the Sophists, not because they are wrong about everything, but because they have divorced knowledge from virtue. You can know how to persuade without knowing whether persuasion serves justice or injustice. You can win an argument without serving truth.

III. The Ideas — The Sophist Challenge

The Sophists present philosophy with a crisis and a choice. Let's break this into three interlocking ideas.

Idea 1: Relativism

There is no absolute truth — only perspective.

Protagoras argued that what is true for one person may be false for another, and both are correct. A food tastes sweet to a healthy person, bitter to a sick person. Neither perception is more true than the other; the food simply is sweet and bitter relative to the perceiver.

If this is so, then the Pre-Socratics' search for the fundamental nature of reality is futile. There is no nature in itself, only nature as it appears to a particular observer, in a particular culture, at a particular time. Truth is historical, cultural, personal.

The unresolved question: Can we say that relativism itself is only relatively true? Or is it absolutely true that there is no absolute truth? The Sophists never escape this paradox.

Idea 2: The Power of Rhetoric

Persuasion is the highest art. The best argument is the one that wins.

If there is no objective truth to appeal to, then what matters is not correspondence to reality but effect. A speech is good if it persuades. A lawyer is excellent if his client walks free. A politician is skilled if the city votes as he wishes.

Gorgias makes this claim explicitly: rhetoric is supreme because it can move any audience to believe anything. And this is not a weakness; it is the definition of power. The Sophist does not ask "is this argument true?" He asks "does this argument work?"

This has a troubling corollary: if rhetoric can make the worse argument appear better, then truth becomes irrelevant. The Sophist who learns Gorgias's techniques can defend the guilty and condemn the innocent. And the techniques are morally neutral; they care not for the justice of the cause.

Idea 3: The Human Turn

Philosophy must leave nature and address the human world: law, justice, politics, virtue.

The Pre-Socratics had been asking "what is everything made of?" The Sophists pivot: "how should humans live together?" This is not a rejection of earlier philosophy; it is a redirection. The Sophists recognized that humans care more about living well than about understanding atoms.

But this human turn comes with a risk. Once you focus on what appears to humans rather than what is in reality, you are in danger of treating morality and law as mere social conventions, not as truths about how we ought to live. And if law is conventional rather than natural, then it can be questioned, challenged, subverted by those clever enough to see through the pretense.

The Crisis

Think before clicking: The Sophists have solved a practical problem (how to teach persuasion) but created a philosophical crisis. If truth is relative, how do we ground morality? How do we distinguish justice from injustice? How do we justify laws if they are merely conventions?

This crisis demands an answer. Someone must show that truth is real, that justice is grounded in something more than persuasion, that the examined life is worth living. In the next lesson, we meet the man who took on that task: Socrates. He believed the Sophists were dead wrong. And he paid for that belief with his life.

But notice: The Sophists' question — "how do we know anything?" — has never been fully answered. Even after Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle, the question echoes. Descartes will wrestle with it. Kant will wrestle with it. Postmodern philosophers will claim the Sophists were right. The problem of relativism is one of philosophy's deepest and most enduring riddles.

The 5 Images for Location 5

1. A marketplace stall with “TRUTH FOR SALE” signs in many colours — different-coloured truths arranged on shelves, customers browsing and buying whichever truth matches their perspective. Gold truth, red truth, silver truth. Each one is right for the person who buys it.

2. A man staring into a mirror that shows a different face each time he glances — Protagoras, no longer sure which face is his. Is the mirror lying? Or is he himself multiple selves?

3. A speaker on a marble platform, his words becoming visible as stones that rise and weep — Gorgias, whose rhetoric is so powerful it makes the impossible happen. Rivers flow backward at his command. Giants weep like children.

4. A courtroom where the guilty walk free and the innocent are condemned — the lawyer-sophist has won his case through pure oratory. Truth was never consulted. Only rhetoric mattered.

5. A compass spinning wildly, unable to find north — no fixed moral direction in this relativist world. Every direction is equally valid. The compass is useless because there is no true north.

What the Sophists Left Unresolved

The Sophists posed a devastating question: if truth is only what you can persuade others to believe, then how is truth different from falsehood? How is justice different from injustice? They believed the answer: there is no difference. Truth and justice are just useful fictions.

But Socrates refused this answer. He believed — with absolute conviction — that truth is real and that a life devoted to pursuing it is the only life worth living. The tension between these two views will run through the entire rest of this course. Modern philosophy will keep asking the Sophist question. Can we know anything with certainty? Or is perspective all we have? The Sophists may have been wrong, but they were not foolish. Their question is still open.
