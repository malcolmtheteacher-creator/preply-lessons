The Company That Changed Its Mind

In 2014 Microsoft was rich, feared and falling behind. A new chief executive started with the culture — and the company became one of the most valuable in the world.

Easier version: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll work through a turnaround that its leader describes as a change of culture rather than strategy — and you'll practise nominalisation, the way board papers and strategy documents turn actions into nouns: the decision to…, a sharp reduction in…, our commitment to…
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • How would you describe the culture of an organisation you know well in three words? Would the people at the top choose the same three?
  • Can a leader really change a culture — or only change the people, the rules and the incentives and hope culture follows?
  • Have you ever worked with a system that made colleagues compete against each other? What did it do to cooperation?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Windows and Office produced enormous, reliable profits year after year, even while the company failed in new markets. Every other project was paid for by these two cash cows.
a cash cow (n) — a mature product or business that generates steady profit with little new investment. The risk: a company protects the cow instead of building what will replace it.
2. The Windows division, the Office division and the server division each had their own goals, budgets and bosses, and they rarely shared anything. Teams worked in silos.
a silo (n) — a part of an organisation that works in isolation from the others. Silo mentality, to break down silos, siloed teams. Originally a tall tower for storing grain.
3. Releasing Office on the iPad might have made people less likely to buy a Windows tablet. Some managers feared the new product would cannibalise the old one.
to cannibalise (v, US: cannibalize) — to take sales away from another product made by the same company. Noun: cannibalisation. The strategic question is always: if we don't cannibalise ourselves, will a competitor do it for us?

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: recurring revenue · headcount · capex. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means money a company spends on long-term physical assets such as buildings, equipment and data centres?
capex (n) — short for capital expenditure. Contrast opex (operating expenditure): day-to-day running costs such as salaries and rent. “Capex on data centres doubled.”
2. Which term means income that arrives regularly and predictably, for example from subscriptions, instead of from one-off sales?
recurring revenue (n) — the reason investors value subscription businesses so highly. Related: annual recurring revenue (ARR), the subscription model, churn (customers who cancel).
3. Which term means the total number of people employed by an organisation?
headcount (n) — to reduce headcount, a headcount freeze. Note the register: it sounds neutral in a board paper and cold in front of the people it describes.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Every year, managers had to place a fixed percentage of their team at the bottom of a performance curve, whatever the team had achieved. The system, called forced ranking, was abandoned in 2013. Forced ranking is…
  (a) promoting staff only by length of service   (b) rating employees against each other so that some must always receive low ratings   (c) letting employees rate their own managers
(b) — forced ranking (n), also stack ranking or rank and yank (informal). Supporters say it removes weak performers; critics say it makes colleagues compete instead of cooperate.
2. Developers, device makers, business partners and customers all built their work around the company's tools. Leaving would be difficult, because the whole ecosystem depended on them. An ecosystem in business is…
  (a) the company's environmental policy   (b) the physical network of offices a company owns   (c) the network of partners, products and users that grow around a platform and depend on each other
(c) — an ecosystem (n): borrowed from biology. A developer ecosystem, to lock customers into an ecosystem. The stronger the ecosystem, the harder it is for customers to switch.

D · Collocation & word building

1. Software whose code anyone may read, change and share is called open ___. Complete the term, and explain why a company that sells software might still support it.
open source (n/adj) — open-source software, to open-source a project. Companies support it because developers use it, it builds goodwill, and money can be made on services, hosting and tools around it.
2. The total value of all a company's shares on the stock market is its market capital___. Complete the noun, and give the short form people use in conversation.
market capitalisation (n, US: capitalization) — share price × number of shares. Short form: market cap. “Its market cap passed a trillion dollars.” Verb family: capital → capitalise → capitalisation.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — Rich and falling behind

When Steve Ballmer became chief executive of Microsoft in 2000, the company dominated personal computing. Windows ran on most of the world's computers, and Office was the standard tool of office work. Over the next fourteen years, profits and revenue grew strongly. Yet the share price hardly moved for more than a decade, because investors could see what the profits hid: Microsoft was losing the new markets.

Google took internet search. Apple, and then Google's Android, took the smartphone. Microsoft's own phone software won only a small share, and its 2012 version of Windows, redesigned for touchscreens, was poorly received by many users. In 2013 it agreed to buy Nokia's phone business for around seven billion dollars — a very late, very expensive attempt to catch up.

Inside the company, many employees and former employees described a culture of internal competition. Divisions protected their own products, and a performance system of forced ranking required managers to give some people low ratings every year, whatever the team had achieved. Critics argued that it encouraged staff to compete with colleagues rather than cooperate. The system was dropped in late 2013. A few months later, in February 2014, Ballmer was replaced by Satya Nadella, an engineer who had joined Microsoft in 1992 and had been running its fast-growing cloud and server business.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the share price stay flat even though profits were rising?

2. What features of the internal culture were criticised before 2014?

1. Investors saw that the profits came mainly from Windows and Office, while Microsoft was losing the important new markets — search and smartphones — so they did not expect strong future growth.

2. Divisions worked in silos and protected their own products, and forced ranking made employees compete against each other because some had to receive low ratings every year.

Discuss:
  • Profitable and falling behind at the same time: which numbers in your organisation would show that — and which numbers would hide it?

Part 2 — Mind before strategy

Nadella's early decisions were striking because they helped rivals' products. Within weeks, Microsoft released Office for the iPad — something the company had delayed for years, partly to protect Windows tablets. Microsoft software began appearing on Android and Apple devices. The company that had once treated the open-source operating system Linux as a threat began supporting it on its own cloud platform, Azure. The message was that Microsoft's future lay in the cloud and in services people used everywhere, not in forcing them onto Windows.

There were painful decisions too. In 2015 Microsoft wrote down most of the value of the Nokia purchase, at a cost of more than seven billion dollars, and cut around 7,800 jobs, mainly in the phone business. Meanwhile the company changed how it sold software: instead of licences bought once every few years, Office increasingly became a monthly or annual subscription, giving Microsoft more predictable, recurring revenue.

Nadella described his main project as cultural. Drawing on the psychologist Carol Dweck's research on the growth mindset, he argued that the company should value curiosity and learning over proving that it was always right. The idea was tested early: at a conference on women in computing in 2014, Nadella suggested women should trust the system rather than ask for pay rises, and later the same day publicly said he had been wrong. Microsoft then bought companies popular with the people who had long distrusted it — the game Minecraft, the professional network LinkedIn and the developer platform GitHub — and largely allowed them to keep running as they were.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Give two decisions from Nadella's early years that supported rival platforms.

2. How did the way Microsoft sold Office change, and why did investors like it?

1. Any two of: releasing Office on the iPad; making Microsoft software available on Android and Apple devices; supporting Linux on Azure.

2. It moved from one-off licences towards subscriptions, which provide regular, predictable recurring revenue that investors value highly.

Discuss:
  • Microsoft started helping its rivals' devices so that its own services could be everywhere. What would it take for your organisation to work with a competitor's platform instead of against it?

Part 3 — Worth trillions, and the next test

The results were dramatic. Azure became one of the two largest cloud platforms in the world. Microsoft's market capitalisation, around 300 billion dollars when Nadella took over, passed one trillion dollars in 2019 and later three trillion. The company's reputation among software developers changed from suspicion to something much closer to respect.

Then Microsoft made its largest bet. It invested in the artificial-intelligence company OpenAI, starting in 2019 and eventually committing more than ten billion dollars, and it built AI assistants into Windows, Office and its cloud products. Its capital expenditure on data centres rose to tens of billions of dollars a year. In July 2025 its market capitalisation briefly passed four trillion dollars. The relationship with OpenAI brought its own risks: when OpenAI's board suddenly removed its chief executive in 2023, Microsoft had enormous exposure to a company it did not control.

At the same time, the company that talked about empathy and learning was cutting jobs in years of record profits — around ten thousand in 2023, and many thousands more in 2025 — as it moved money towards AI. Supporters say that is exactly what a learning company does: it moves resources to where the future is. Critics ask whether the culture story was always easier to tell while the company was growing. And some analysts argue that the turnaround owed as much to the cloud boom, and to the loyalty of business customers to Office, as to any change of mindset. The question is still open: did the culture change the company — or did the market?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the growth in market capitalisation suggest about how investors saw the company after 2014?

2. What are the two opposite ways of reading the job cuts in years of record profit?

1. That they believed Microsoft had found strong new sources of future growth — especially the cloud and later AI — in contrast to the flat share price of the previous decade.

2. Supporters see them as a learning organisation moving resources towards the future (AI); critics see a gap between the language of empathy and the reality of cutting jobs while profits are high, and question whether the culture story is only easy when business is growing.

Discuss:
  • When an organisation says one thing about its culture and then makes a decision that seems to contradict it, what do employees believe — the words or the decision? Give an example you have seen.

Grammar — nominalisation

Strategy documents, annual reports and board papers are full of nouns where speech would use verbs: not we decided but the decision, not costs fell sharply but a sharp fall in costs. This is nominalisation. It makes writing more compact, more formal and more impersonal — which is exactly why it is powerful, and exactly why it can hide who did what.

1 · Verb → noun, adverb → adjective
• “We reduced costs sharply.” → “There was a sharp reduction in costs.”
• “The team responded quickly.” → “The team's rapid response…”
• Common pairs: decide → decision, fail → failure, grow → growth, invest → investment, expand → expansion, improve → improvement, commit → commitment, analyse → analysis.
2 · Adjective → noun
• “The market is volatile.” → “The volatility of the market…”
• able → ability, complex → complexity, flexible → flexibility, aware → awareness, efficient → efficiency.
3 · The prepositions that come with the nouns
• an increase / decrease / decline / rise / fall in (the thing that changes) — a rise of 10% (the amount)
• commitment to · emphasis on · investment in · demand for · the impact of X on Y

Two warnings. Too many nouns create fog: “the implementation of the optimisation of the reallocation of resources”. And nominalisation removes the person: “the reduction in headcount” does not say who decided it. In a board paper that may be fine. In front of the people affected, use verbs and say who.

1. Make the noun

Complete the second sentence with the noun form of the word in bold. Write one word.

1. Customers adopted cloud services quickly. → The rapid of cloud services by customers changed the industry.

2. The company was reluctant to release software for rival devices. → The company's to release software for rival devices cost it years.

3. Revenue from subscriptions grew strongly. → There was strong in revenue from subscriptions.

4. Microsoft acquired GitHub in 2018. → Microsoft's of GitHub in 2018 surprised many developers.

1. adoption   2. reluctance   3. growth   4. acquisition

Notice what else changed: quickly became rapid, and strongly became strong. The adverb follows the verb into its new form.

2. The preposition after the noun

Write the one preposition that follows each noun here.

1. The results showed a steep decline sales of phones.

2. The board confirmed its long-term commitment open-source software.

3. The new leadership placed much greater emphasis learning from mistakes.

4. Heavy investment data centres reduced short-term profits.

1. in   2. to   3. on   4. in

Learn these as chunks, not as rules: decline in, commitment to, emphasis on, investment in.

3. The board paper — complete it, then read it aloud

Turn a spoken update into the opening of a board paper. Complete each gap with the noun form of the word in brackets. Then read it aloud, and compare it with how you would say the same thing to a team.

“Since the change of leadership, the (decide) to release our core products on competing platforms has led to a significant (expand) of our customer base. The (abandon) of forced ranking was followed by a marked (improve) in collaboration between divisions. The (fail — plural) of the earlier phone strategy have now been recognised in the accounts, and our (commit) to the cloud remains unchanged.”

decision · expansion · abandonment · improvement · failures · commitment

Now say it to a team using verbs: “We decided to…, so more customers…, We stopped ranking people against each other, and…”. Which version sounds more honest? Which is more precise?

Going deeper

A note on the figures: market values, investments and job numbers are rounded and taken from public reporting; valuations change daily, and this is very much a live case.

What the case actually tests. The first reading is the one the company itself tells: the turnaround began in the mind. A chief executive changed what the organisation rewarded — learning over being right, cooperation over internal rivalry — and the strategic decisions followed from that: supporting rival platforms, embracing open source, buying communities that distrusted Microsoft and leaving them alone. On this reading, the growth mindset was not decoration; it was the permission that allowed a proud company to admit that Windows was no longer the centre of everything.

The second reading is structural. The cloud market was exploding in exactly these years, and Azure had been started under the previous leadership. Business customers' dependence on Office and Windows gave Microsoft a huge base to sell subscriptions to. Forced ranking had already been dropped before the new chief executive arrived. On this reading, Nadella's great skill was recognising where the company's real advantages lay and removing the pride that prevented it from using them — impressive, but not a cultural miracle. Culture is the story that is easiest to tell afterwards, and hardest to measure.

The third point is the test that is happening now. A culture built around growth and learning is easy to believe while the company is growing and hiring. Large job cuts in record-profit years, and enormous AI spending with uncertain returns, put the language under pressure. If the culture was real, it should show in how those decisions are explained and carried out. If it was a story, the gap between words and decisions will become visible to employees long before it becomes visible to investors.
Talk it through:
  • Culture or market? Take one side and argue it with at least two facts from the case — then name the one fact that most weakens your side.
  • Which decision in your organisation's recent history would only have been possible after a change in culture? And which was simply forced by the market?
  • Microsoft stopped ranking employees against each other. What does your organisation's performance system actually reward, whatever its values statement says?
  • When a company cuts jobs while profits are at a record, what explanation would you, as an employee, accept as honest?

Present this case to a board that believes its own company needs a culture change. Structure it: the profitable but falling-behind decade → silos and forced ranking → the new chief executive → supporting rival platforms → the Nokia write-down and job cuts → subscriptions and the cloud → the growth mindset → the trillion-dollar results → the AI bet → the open question.

Finish with one sentence on what your board should take from it. Use at least five key terms (cash cow, silo, cannibalise, recurring revenue, headcount, capex, forced ranking, ecosystem, open source, market capitalisation), and use at least three nominalisations.

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

You are a senior leader at a company-wide meeting. An employee stands up and says: “You talk about empathy and a learning culture. This year the company made record profits and cut thousands of jobs. Isn't ‘culture’ just the story you tell while the spreadsheet decides?”

Answer properly: take the question seriously, do not hide behind nouns, explain the decision in plain verbs, and say clearly what the culture should mean in a moment like this. Then do it again in ninety seconds — the compression is the exercise.

Task C · Four rooms, one change (4 minutes)

Take one change in how an organisation works — real or invented — and announce it four ways, one minute each. Notice when you reach for nouns and when you need verbs.

  • To the board: the rationale, the cost and the expected impact, in formal written style.
  • To the teams affected: what is happening, who decided, and what it means for them.
  • To long-standing customers: why this is good for them, even if it looks like you are helping a competitor.
  • To a partner who has never trusted you: what is genuinely different now, and how they can test it.

Prefer not to use your own organisation? Take one of these instead:

  • A bank opening its app so that rival financial companies can offer services inside it.
  • A carmaker allowing competitors' cars to use its charging network.
  • A hospital group ending a system that ranks doctors against each other.
  • A newspaper moving from advertising income to paid subscriptions.
