Reading Habits at University

Academic reading is fundamentally different from leisure reading. As a B2 student moving toward university study, you'll encounter dense texts, specialized terminology, and complex arguments. Understanding your reading patterns is the first step to developing efficiency.

Common Reading Challenges

  • Trying to read every word equally
  • Encountering unfamiliar vocabulary and stopping
  • Losing focus on main ideas in long passages
  • Spending hours reading without retaining information
  • Not having a clear purpose before reading

Why Reading Purpose Matters

Before opening any academic text, ask yourself: Why am I reading this? Are you reading to:

  • Get an overview of a topic?
  • Find specific information?
  • Deeply understand an argument?
  • Prepare for an exam or essay?

Your purpose determines which reading strategy you'll use.

Vocabulary in Context

University texts often contain discipline-specific vocabulary and complex sentence structures. Rather than stopping to look up every unknown word, B2 readers learn to extract meaning from context.

Strategy: Guess from Context

When you encounter an unfamiliar word, look at surrounding sentences. Does the context give you clues about meaning? Can you understand the main idea without knowing that exact word? Often, you can.

The SQ3R Method

A systematic approach to reading academic texts deeply and retaining information. Perfect for textbooks, research articles, and complex arguments.

S — Survey

Spend 5-10 minutes looking at the text without reading word-for-word. Read the title, abstract, headings, first and last paragraphs. Look at graphs, tables, and images. This gives your brain a framework for the detailed reading that follows.

Q — Question

Turn headings into questions. If a section is titled "Climate Change Impacts," ask yourself "What are the impacts of climate change?" This activates your brain and gives you a reading purpose.

R — Read

Now read the text actively, looking for answers to your questions. Take notes. Highlight sparingly. Engage with the material.

R — Recite

After each section, close the text and summarize what you've learned. Can you answer your questions? If not, reread that section.

R — Review

After finishing, review your notes. Test yourself on main points. Try to explain the argument to someone else.

Three Reading Speeds

Skilled academic readers choose different speeds for different purposes.

1. Skimming (5-10 minutes for 10 pages)

Purpose: Get the main idea quickly

How: Read introduction and conclusion. Read topic sentences of each paragraph. Skip examples and detailed evidence.

Best for: Getting an overview before deciding if you need to read deeply; background reading for essays

2. Scanning (2-5 minutes)

Purpose: Find specific information

How: Move your eyes down the page looking for a specific word or idea. Don't read everything.

Best for: Looking up statistics, definitions, or quotes; using reference materials

3. Deep Reading (20+ minutes for dense material)

Purpose: Understand complex arguments thoroughly

How: Read every sentence. Take detailed notes. Stop and think. Reread difficult passages. Look up important unfamiliar words.

Best for: Core texts for your degree; preparation for critical analysis; sources you'll cite

Key Insight: You don't read everything at the same speed. Expert readers adjust their speed based on purpose and text difficulty. Building this flexibility is a core B2 academic skill.

Practice: Reading an Academic Passage

Read the passage below using the skimming strategy. You have 3 minutes. Focus on understanding the main argument, not every detail.

The Evolution of Remote Work: A Post-Pandemic Perspective

The COVID-19 pandemic fundamentally altered workplace dynamics across the globe. What began as a temporary emergency measure—moving employees to work from home—has evolved into a permanent restructuring of how organizations approach productivity and employee engagement. Research from the Global Workplace Institute suggests that 70% of workers now expect flexible working arrangements as standard, representing a significant shift in employment expectations.

This transformation has cascading effects on urban infrastructure, commuting patterns, and real estate markets. Metropolitan areas traditionally built around office density now face declining demand for commercial property. Simultaneously, technology companies based in Silicon Valley report improved employee retention when offering remote-first policies. Yet challenges persist: collaboration suffers in purely asynchronous environments, mental health impacts of isolation have become apparent, and the digital divide creates inequality for workers without reliable internet access or dedicated workspace.

Organizations now grapple with a fundamental question: how to balance flexibility with connection? Hybrid models—combining remote and in-office work—have emerged as a compromise, though implementation varies dramatically. Some companies mandate three days in the office; others provide complete flexibility. The evidence suggests that cookie-cutter approaches fail; successful remote work policies must align with organizational culture, sector requirements, and employee needs.

Your Reading Reflection

Take a few minutes to think about your own reading habits and goals.

Reflection Questions

What type of academic reading is most challenging for you?

Dense theoretical texts? Science papers with jargon? Long articles? Identifying this helps you choose strategies. For instance, scientific papers might benefit from reading the abstract, methods, and conclusion first, skipping the detailed results initially.

How often do you currently skim vs. read deeply?

B2 students often default to deep reading for everything, which wastes time. Skilled readers skim 80% of their reading. Challenge yourself this week: skim two sources before deep reading one core text.

Which reading strategy will you try first?

If you're preparing for exams or essays, try SQ3R on one textbook chapter. If you need to review sources quickly, practice skimming and scanning. Set a specific goal: "I will skim 5 sources this week rather than reading them all deeply."

Action Steps

  1. Identify your next academic reading task (essay research, exam prep, course reading)
  2. Choose a strategy based on your purpose (skim/scan for overview, SQ3R for deep understanding)
  3. Set a time limit—this forces you to stay focused
  4. Take notes in your own words as you read
  5. Review your notes before moving to the next source

Remember: At university, you'll read 50+ pages per week in some courses. Efficiency in reading is not laziness—it's essential time management. Developing flexible reading strategies now will serve you throughout your academic career.
