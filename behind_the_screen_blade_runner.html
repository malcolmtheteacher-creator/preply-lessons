The film, in three parts

Ridley Scott's Blade Runner (1982), based on Philip K. Dick's 1968 novel Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?. Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A test built to catch a lie the body can't tell

Los Angeles, 2019: a sunless, rain-soaked megacity lit by giant neon advertisements. Corporations have built "replicants" — bioengineered beings, artificial in origin but otherwise indistinguishable from humans, used as slave labour on off-world colonies. Replicants are banned from Earth on pain of death. When a small group escapes and reaches the city, Rick Deckard — a retired "blade runner," a police hunter whose job is to track down and "retire" (a chillingly polite word for kill) rogue replicants — is pulled back into the job.

Before he even leaves headquarters, Deckard is shown the tool of his trade: the Voight-Kampff test, a machine that watches a subject's eyes and involuntary reactions while an examiner asks strange, emotionally loaded questions. He demonstrates it on Rachael, an assistant at the Tyrell Corporation who believes herself entirely human. It takes far more than the usual number of questions to catch her out. Rachael, it turns out, doesn't know what she is — she was given a complete set of implanted memories, a real childhood that never actually happened to her.

Deckard is unsettled. If a fabricated life can feel this real from the inside, how confident can anyone be about where the line between human and replicant actually falls?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is a blade runner, and what is the Voight-Kampff test used for?

2. Why does Rachael's test unsettle Deckard so much?

1. A blade runner is a police hunter who tracks down and kills escaped replicants; the Voight-Kampff test uses involuntary physical responses to questions to expose whether someone is really human.

2. Rachael passes almost as human and doesn't know she's a replicant herself — her memories, though false, feel completely authentic to her.

Discuss:
  • If Rachael's memories feel real and shape who she is, does it matter that they were implanted rather than lived? What would you say makes a memory "real"?

Part 2 — The hunt

Deckard tracks the fugitive replicants one by one through the city's underworld. He corners and retires Zhora, a replicant working as an exotic dancer, in a brutal chase through crowded streets. Soon after, Leon — one of the group's more volatile members — nearly kills Deckard in retaliation; Rachael, torn between what she is and what she has started to feel, shoots Leon to save Deckard's life. The line she is supposed to stand on the wrong side of keeps blurring.

Meanwhile, the group's leader, Roy Batty, finally reaches the one person who can help him: Eldon Tyrell, the corporation's founder and, in effect, his maker. Roy's demand is desperate and simple — replicants like him are built with only a four-year lifespan, and his time is almost up. He begs for more life. Tyrell, with something close to genuine regret, tells him it isn't possible; the flaw is built too deep into his design to undo. Roy's fury and grief boil over, and he kills Tyrell.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does Rachael shoot Leon?

2. What does Roy ask Tyrell for, and how does Tyrell respond?

1. Leon is about to kill Deckard, and Rachael saves him — even though doing so means acting against her own kind.

2. Roy begs Tyrell for a longer lifespan; Tyrell says the four-year limit can't be undone, and Roy kills him in grief and rage.

Discuss:
  • Roy wants more life more desperately than almost any human character in the film. Does that desperation make him more human, or does it just prove he was built to imitate human feeling perfectly?

Part 3 — Tears in rain

The final confrontation between Deckard and Roy plays out across a derelict building's rooftops in the pouring rain. Roy, his four years finally running out, is faster and stronger than Deckard, and could easily let him fall to his death. Instead, at the last second, Roy hauls Deckard back onto the roof and saves him. It is not what anyone — least of all Deckard — expected from the "monster" he was sent to destroy.

As his systems fail, Roy sits in the rain and speaks quietly about the extraordinary things he has seen in his short life — sights no human will ever witness, all of it about to vanish with him, unrecorded and unmourned. Then he dies. Deckard is left staring at what remains: a being built to expire on schedule, who chose, in his final moment, to give a life rather than take one.

Deckard leaves the city with Rachael, whose own limited lifespan is never fully explained. Throughout the film — and made explicit in the Director's Cut, through a recurring dream of a unicorn and a small origami unicorn left for him by a colleague — there's a quiet, unresolved suggestion that Deckard himself might not be entirely certain what he is either.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Roy do in his final moment, and why is it surprising?

2. What detail hints that Deckard might not be fully human himself?

1. Instead of letting Deckard fall to his death, Roy saves him — an act of mercy from the "replicant" Deckard was sent to kill.

2. A recurring unicorn dream and an origami unicorn left by a colleague suggest Deckard's own memories may be implanted too, though the film never confirms it.

Discuss:
  • By the end, who behaved more "human" — Roy, a replicant, or Deckard, a man hunting his own kind? Does the ambiguity about Deckard change how you feel about the whole story?

Grammar — appearance vs reality: seem, appear & as if

No film needs this language more than Blade Runner. Every character is judged by how they seem — and the whole plot depends on the gap between how something appears and what it actually is.

seem / appear + to-infinitive: used to say how something looks or feels, without stating it as a hard fact. "She seems to remember a childhood she never had." "He appears to trust her completely."
look like / sound like + noun or clause: comparing appearance or sound to something else. "That question sounds like a trap." "He looks like an ordinary man."
as if / as though + clause: describing manner — the way someone behaves, as if a certain (often untrue) thing were the case. Use were for something unreal or hypothetical: "She talks as if she were human."
Feel the difference:
• seem/appear to + infinitive gives a cautious opinion about a whole person or situation.
• look like/sound like compares one thing to another using your senses.
• as if/as though describes the manner of an action — how someone does something, often implying it isn't really true.

The Real Story — the real test for telling minds apart

The Voight-Kampff test is fiction, invented by Philip K. Dick for his 1968 novel. But it has a real, much older ancestor — and the philosophical question underneath it is one nobody has ever fully settled.

🧠 The Turing Test — a machine's real ancestor to the Voight-Kampff test

In 1950, the British mathematician Alan Turing published a paper called "Computing Machinery and Intelligence" in the journal Mind. Rather than trying to answer the vague question "can machines think?", Turing proposed a practical test he called the Imitation Game — now known as the Turing Test. A human judge holds a typed conversation with two hidden partners, one human and one machine, and tries to work out which is which. If the machine's answers can't reliably be told apart from the human's, Turing argued, we should take its claim to "think" seriously.

The Voight-Kampff test in Blade Runner is a dramatic descendant of this idea, with a twist: instead of judging words, it watches the body — pupil dilation, blush response, breathing — for the involuntary signs of genuine empathy that (in the story) only humans possess.

Are we our memories? A very old philosophical question

Rachael's crisis — discovering her whole remembered childhood was implanted — dramatises a real debate in philosophy about personal identity. In 1690, the philosopher John Locke argued that what makes you the same person over time isn't your body but the continuous chain of memory and consciousness connecting your past selves to your present one. If Locke is right, and memory is what identity is built from, then a being with a coherent, felt set of memories has a real claim to being a "self" — even if those memories were, in Rachael's case, manufactured rather than lived.

This connects to an older puzzle called the problem of other minds: since we can never directly experience another being's inner life, how can any of us truly know that someone else — human, animal or machine — really feels anything at all, rather than simply behaving as if they do? The Voight-Kampff test is Blade Runner's fictional attempt to solve exactly that unsolvable problem.

A debate that is still very much alive

This isn't only a period piece from the 1980s. As real artificial intelligence systems produce increasingly fluent, human-like responses, philosophers and scientists are seriously debating what has been called the "problem of other AI minds" — whether we could ever have good grounds for believing a machine is genuinely conscious, rather than just extremely convincing. Most current philosophical opinion leans towards caution: convincing behaviour, even very convincing behaviour, is not proof of an inner life. This is genuinely unresolved — nobody, including leading philosophers and AI researchers, currently has a settled answer.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, did you know the Voight-Kampff test had a real scientific ancestor in the Turing Test? Does that change how you see the film?
  • If Locke is right that memory makes the person, does it matter whether those memories were lived or implanted?
  • Do you think we will ever be able to prove, one way or the other, whether a machine truly feels something? Why, or why not?
