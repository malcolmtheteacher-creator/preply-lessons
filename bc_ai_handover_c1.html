The Company That Hired the People Back

A payments company announced that its AI assistant was doing the work of seven hundred people. A year later its chief executive was hiring humans again.

All the cases

Today: you'll follow a company that went all in on AI and then adjusted, learn a simple grid for deciding which tasks to automate, and practise describing change over time in a spoken explanation.
Before we start — talk these through:

  • Think of a customer-service experience you had with an automatic system. At what point did you want to speak to a person?
  • Is it a sign of weakness when a company reverses a big decision? Why, or why not?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — Seven hundred people's worth

Klarna is a Swedish company that lets customers buy now and pay later. It has millions of customers, and they ask a great many questions: where is my order, why was I charged, how do I change a date. In February 2024 the company published a figure that travelled around the business world. Its new AI assistant, it said, had handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month. That was about three out of every four customer chats, and Klarna said the work was equal to that of seven hundred full-time agents.

The assistant worked in more than thirty languages, around the clock. Klarna said customer satisfaction was similar to what people had given human agents and that the time to solve a problem had fallen sharply. The chief executive, Sebastian Siemiatkowski, spoke about the result often and with obvious pride.

It is worth noticing one word in the announcement: "equal to". The seven hundred was a company's own estimate of workload, not a list of people who had lost their jobs. Klarna's numbers of staff had been falling anyway, by about a fifth, because the company had stopped hiring and people had left.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Klarna say about its AI assistant in February 2024?

2. What does "equal to seven hundred agents" actually mean?

3. Why had the number of Klarna employees fallen?

1. That in its first month it handled about 2.3 million chats, around three-quarters of the total, with the work of seven hundred full-time agents.

2. It was the company's own estimate of the amount of work, not a count of people dismissed.

3. The company had stopped hiring and people had left without being replaced, which is called attrition.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think a company would want to describe its AI results in terms of the number of people it replaced?

Part 2 — A different tone

In 2025 the story changed. In an interview with Bloomberg, Siemiatkowski said that Klarna was hiring human customer-service workers again. He admitted that the company had put too much weight on cost, and that this had produced work of lower quality. Cheaper, he said, was not the same as good enough.

The new plan was not a return to the old system. Klarna intended to build a pool of remote workers, a little like the model used by Uber, that customers could reach when they wanted a person. The company recruited students and people who already loved the product, and it said it wanted to be sure that a human was always available on request.

Klarna itself rejected the idea of a retreat. It described the change as two tracks running together: automation for scale, people for quality.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the chief executive admit in 2025?

2. What kind of workforce did Klarna plan to build?

3. How did Klarna describe the change?

1. That the company had focused too much on cost, which had made the quality lower.

2. A pool of remote workers, a bit like Uber's model, who customers could reach on request.

3. As two tracks together: AI for scale and humans for quality, not a retreat from automation.

Discuss:
  • Is it more impressive to announce a success loudly or to say publicly that you got the balance wrong? Which would you trust more?

Part 3 — What the rule might be

Reports of the reversal vary. Some outlets wrote that the company had laid off seven hundred people and then regretted it, which Klarna's own account does not support, and some described customer satisfaction as having collapsed, for which the evidence is thin. The safest summary is the plainest one: a company that moved very fast to automate found that some conversations needed a person, and it adjusted.

What should be taken from that? Nobody would argue that a chatbot should not tell a customer where a parcel is. Almost everybody would want a human when the problem is unusual or the stakes are high, such as a fraud case or a dispute about a large bill. The skill lies in deciding which is which before the customer has to complain.

That is a decision a small consultancy can make for its clients in an afternoon, with a pen and one simple grid, and it is much cheaper than finding out the hard way.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why should you be careful with some reports about this story?

1. Some outlets say 700 people were laid off or that satisfaction collapsed, claims the company's own account and the evidence do not clearly support.

2. When the problem is unusual, or when a lot is at stake, such as fraud or a dispute about a large bill.

3. By using a simple grid to decide which tasks to automate and which to keep with people, before problems appear.

How the tool works

Before you automate a task, put it on a grid with two questions. How routine is it, meaning does it come up all the time in the same shape? And what is the cost if it goes wrong? The answers tell you who should do it, a machine, a person, or the two of them together. The Handover Rule is simple: automate the routine and cheap, keep a person for the unusual and the costly, and make the human door easy to find.

Using it, step by step

  1. List the tasks. Write down every type of question or job, not the department. Where is my order. Why was I charged twice. I think my account was hacked.
  2. Place each one on the grid. Ask how often it comes up in the same shape, and what it costs when it goes wrong, in money, in trust or in safety.
  3. Decide who does what. Top left: let the machine do it. Bottom left: let it help while a person decides. Right-hand column: keep a person involved, and in the bottom right keep a person in charge.
  4. Build the handover. A customer should be able to reach a human in one step, without arguing with the bot. If I type "person", I get a person. Then measure what happens for a month.

Try it yourself

Each task could be handled by a customer-service tool. Where does it belong on the grid? Choose, then check.

1. A customer asks what time your office opens on Monday.
2. A customer says a payment left their account that they did not make.
3. A customer asks for a copy of last month's invoice.
4. A customer wants to change the date of a very large order.
5. A customer writes a long complaint that mixes three different problems.
6. A customer asks whether a charge is correct on a loan with unusual terms.
7. A customer asks where a parcel is.
8. A customer writes with a question nobody on the team has seen before and the tone is calm.

1. Automate — routine, and nothing much is lost if it is slightly wrong.

2. Person only — unusual and costly. Trust is at stake and the answer cannot be generic.

3. Automate — same shape every time, low risk.

4. Automate and check — routine in form, but the stakes are higher, so a person reviews it.

5. AI helps, person decides — the tool can summarise and sort the issues; a person chooses the response.

6. Person only — unusual and expensive if it is wrong.

7. Automate — the classic routine question.

8. AI helps, person decides — unusual but low stakes, so a tool can suggest while a person chooses.

Where the tool breaks

The grid is a starting point. Your own data will move some tasks. Check what customers actually ask before you decide which are routine.

The handover is part of the product. A bot that makes the human hard to find saves money for a month and costs trust for years.

It works for internal tasks too. The same grid helps with first drafts, scheduling and research: routine and low stakes go to the tool; unusual and costly stay with you.

Talk it through:
  • Which task in your own work would be hardest to place on the grid?
  • Is it ever right for a company to make the human option deliberately hard to reach?

Language — Describing change over time

Stories about companies are mostly stories about change. A small set of structures lets you say what was true then, what is true now, and what stayed the same.

used to, no longer, still: Klarna used to rely on agents for every chat. It no longer relies on the bot alone. It still uses AI for routine questions. Used to is only for the past. No longer and still go before the main verb, but after be.
Past, then present, with a time marker: In 2024 the company was announcing its savings. By 2025 it was hiring people. Since then it has described the approach as two tracks. Since goes with the present perfect, and by + a date goes with a state or event at that point.
Verbs of change: rise, fall, drop, reverse, shift, scale back, expand. Staff numbers fell by about a fifth. The company scaled back its AI plans. It reversed its position.
