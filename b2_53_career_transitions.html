Career Transitions & Changes

B2 Level · Career Development

Why People Change Careers

Career changes are more common than ever. People leave jobs for many different reasons—sometimes by choice, sometimes by circumstance.

Reflect: Have you ever considered changing careers? What would make you consider it? What are you looking for in a career?

Common Reasons for Career Changes

Seeking Growth & New Challenges

After five years as a marketing manager, you've mastered your role. You're no longer learning. You want to move into product management or start your own business. You're not leaving because of problems—you're leaving because you want more challenge and growth.

Better Work-Life Balance

You work 60-hour weeks in investment banking. You're stressed, never see your family, and your health is suffering. You want a career that's demanding but more sustainable. You're willing to take a lower salary for better quality of life.

Changing Priorities

You spent ten years climbing the corporate ladder. Then you have a child. Your priorities shift. You want flexibility, remote work options, and time with your family. Your old career path doesn't fit your life anymore.

Forced Redundancy

Your company downsizes. Your position is eliminated. You didn't choose to leave, but you're facing it. This is actually an opportunity to think about what you really want to do, not just find the next similar job.

Following Your Passion

You spent years in accounting because it was stable and paid well. But you've always loved teaching. At 35, you decide to go back to school to become a high school teacher. It's a risk financially, but it feels right.

Wrong Fit

You thought sales was your dream job. Six months in, you realize you hate the pressure and the constant competition with colleagues. The culture doesn't match your values. You need to find work that aligns with who you are.

Think about this: Which of these scenarios feels most relatable to you? Or do you have a different reason for considering a career change?

The Myth of the Linear Career

Your parents probably had one career. You had maybe two or three. Your children will likely have five or six. This is normal. Career paths are no longer straight lines—they're more like zigzags or spirals. You might go from finance to tech to nonprofits. You might return to school at 40. You might take a sabbatical. All of this is increasingly common.

Career Transition Vocabulary

Master these eight key terms to discuss career changes professionally and confidently.

ResignationThe formal act of leaving a job; officially quitting your position
Notice PeriodThe amount of time (usually 2 weeks, 1 month, or 3 months) you must work after telling your employer you're leaving
Transferable SkillsAbilities and experience you developed in one job that can be used in a different role or industry
Career PivotA significant change in career direction; moving to a completely different field or industry
SabbaticalA period of time off work, usually several months or a year, for rest, travel, or personal projects
RedundancyThe termination of employment because the job or position is no longer needed (also called a "layoff")
SeverancePay and benefits given to an employee when they're fired or laid off, usually as compensation
UpskillingLearning new skills or improving existing ones to make yourself more employable or capable in your field

Phrases for Discussing Career Changes

Explaining Your Career Gap

"After [previous job], I took time to reassess my priorities." This is honest and shows intention, not laziness.
"I was laid off when the company restructured. I used that time to upskill in [new area]." Own the situation and show how you used the time productively.
"I took a sabbatical to travel and gain perspective on what I want from my career." Shows self-awareness and intentionality.
"I went back to university to study [subject] because I wanted to make a career pivot." Shows investment in your future and clear direction.

Framing Your Career Transition Positively

"My background in [old field] gives me a unique perspective in [new field]." Positions your different experience as an advantage.
"I'm not running from my previous role—I'm running toward this new challenge." Shows this is a pull, not a push. You want this, not just escaping.
"I've realized I want to work in an industry where I feel my values are aligned." Shows integrity and self-knowledge.
"My skills in [previous job] have prepared me well for [new role]." Connects your past to your future. Highlight transferable skills.
"I'm excited about this opportunity because it combines two things I'm passionate about: [A] and [B]." Shows enthusiasm and clear thinking about your direction.

In Job Interviews - Addressing Your Career Change

"I'm making this transition because I want to grow in [specific area], and your company does exactly that." Shows you're not just job-hopping. You have a clear goal.
"While my title changed, the core skills I developed—[skill], [skill], [skill]—are directly applicable here." Don't let a different job title scare them. Connect the dots.
"This might look like a big change, but I've been building toward this for [timeframe]." Shows this isn't impulsive. You've been thinking about it.

Three Challenging Scenarios

Here are realistic situations you might face. Read each one, then try responding before revealing the suggested approach.

Scenario 1: Telling Your Boss You're Leaving

The Situation: You've been in your job for three years. You've decided to move to a company that's offering you a better salary, growth opportunity, and work-life balance. Your boss has been supportive, but you know they'll be disappointed because you're a strong performer and hard to replace.

What do you say?

"I've made the difficult decision to move on to a new opportunity. I want to give you the full notice period and ensure a smooth transition. I'm grateful for everything I've learned here, and I want to make sure your team isn't left in a difficult position. Can we discuss a transition plan?"

Why this works:

  • You're respectful of their position
  • You emphasize the relationship and gratitude
  • You focus on solutions, not problems
  • You're clear and professional, not apologetic
  • You offer to help with the transition

Scenario 2: Explaining a Career Gap in an Interview

The Situation: You were laid off 8 months ago. You took 3 months to recover and think. Then you took 5 months doing freelance work, volunteering, and preparing for this new opportunity. The interviewer asks: "I see you've been out of full-time work for about 8 months. What were you doing?"

What do you say?

"I was laid off when the company went through restructuring. Rather than rushing into the next job, I took time to think carefully about what I wanted. I did freelance consulting work, which strengthened my skills in [area], and I volunteered with [organization], which gave me experience in [area]. That time really clarified what I'm looking for—which is why I'm so excited about this role."

Why this works:

  • You own the situation (layoff) without complaining
  • You show the gap was intentional and productive
  • You highlight specific skills developed during that time
  • You connect it to why you're interested in THIS job
  • You end on a positive, forward-looking note

Scenario 3: Negotiating Your Notice Period

The Situation: You work in a senior role that typically requires 3 months notice. But your new company wants you to start in 4 weeks. Your current employer has asked for the standard 3 months. You respect your employer and don't want to leave them in a difficult position, but the new opportunity has a start date they can't move.

What do you say to your current employer?

"I understand the importance of a full transition period, and I'm committed to ensuring your team is supported. However, my new employer has a fixed start date in 4 weeks. Can we work together on a transition plan for the time I have? I'm happy to [document processes, train my replacement, be available remotely after I leave, etc.]."

Why this works:

  • You acknowledge the constraint and show respect
  • You're clear about what you can control (the hard date)
  • You offer concrete solutions
  • You show flexibility by offering to help beyond the 4 weeks if needed
  • You're professional and solution-focused, not demanding

Planning Your Career Transition

Whether you're planning a major change or just exploring options, thinking strategically about your career is important. Use this exercise to clarify your thinking.

Reflection Questions

Think about these questions and write your responses below:

  • What are you good at? What do people often ask you for help with?
  • What kind of work energizes you? What drains you?
  • What are your non-negotiable values? (Money, impact, flexibility, learning, status, helping others, creativity?)
  • If you didn't need the money, what would you do?
  • In 5 years, where do you want to be?

Write Your Career Transition Story

Imagine you're in an interview 2 years from now. You've made a significant change in your career. Write how you would explain this transition to an interviewer. Focus on:

  • Why you decided to make the change
  • What you've learned in the transition
  • How your previous experience prepared you
  • Why this new path excites you

Key Takeaways

Career Changes Are Normal

You're not abandoning anything. You're evolving. Employers know this. They expect it. Focus on making thoughtful choices, not staying in one place forever.

Frame It Positively

Don't say, "I hated my last job." Say, "I'm excited about this new opportunity because..." Always focus on what you're moving toward, not what you're leaving.

Honor Your History

Show gratitude for your previous roles. You learned something valuable. That experience helped you get here. Even if the job was difficult, find something positive to say.

Build Your Narrative

Your career doesn't need to be a straight line. But it does need to make sense. You should be able to explain why each move made sense at the time. That narrative is powerful.

Invest in Transferable Skills

Leadership, communication, project management, problem-solving—these work in any industry. As you build your career, always develop skills that travel with you, not just technical skills for one industry.

Plan, But Stay Flexible

Have a sense of direction, but stay open to opportunities. Sometimes the best career moves come from unexpected places. Be intentional but also adaptable.

Next Steps

• Set up informational interviews with people in fields you're curious about

• Take a course or certification to upskill in an area that interests you

• Update your LinkedIn to reflect the full range of your transferable skills

• Identify mentors who've made successful career transitions

• Start having honest conversations about what you want from your career
