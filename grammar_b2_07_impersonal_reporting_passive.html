Warm-up

SPEAK

Have a go, out loud, before anything else: when you want to pass on gossip you haven't checked yourself, how do you say it without repeating "nobody's confirmed this" every single time?

Listen in on this catch-up

Jo: Did you hear about the Fletchers?

Priya: No — what's happened?

Jo: It's said they're finally selling that big house on the corner.

Priya: Really? I heard the husband's got a new job somewhere.

Jo: Yeah — he's said to have already handed in his notice.

Look at Jo's last two lines. Two different ways of passing on news without saying who actually told her. Can you spot both? Say them out loud before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "It's said they're finally selling..." — it is said (that) + a full sentence, for a claim or rumour with no one person at the centre.
2. "he's said to have already handed in his notice" — a person + is said to have + done something, for news about one specific person that's already happened.

Master these and you can gossip, follow transfer rumours, and share local news exactly like a native speaker does — without ever naming your source.
Today: three ways of passing on rumours and news without naming your source — it is said/thought/believed that..., someone is said/thought to be..., and someone is said/thought to have been / to have done... — so you can talk about gossip, transfers, and local news the way people actually do.

What's Being Said

The impersonal way to pass on a rumour or a general belief — nobody specific gets the blame for starting it.

Spot the pattern

  • "It's said that the new café owner used to be a chef in London."
  • "It's thought that the roadworks won't finish before spring."
  • "It's rumoured that the football club are about to sign a new striker."

What comes straight after "it is"? And what always follows the reporting verb? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
it + is/'s + said / thought / believed / reported / rumoured + that + a full sentence. You're reporting the claim itself, not making it about one person. In speech, "that" is often dropped — "it's said the café's changing hands" sounds just as natural.
it + is + said / thought / believed / reported / rumoured + that + [subject + verb]
One trap: this only works with a real clause after "that". You can't force a dummy "it" sentence like the weather into the personal to-infinitive pattern — "it is said to be foggy tonight" isn't standard English. These need the full clause instead: "it is said that it will be foggy tonight."

Quick check

1. Nobody's confirmed anything, but there's a rumour going round that the corner shop's closing down. Report it, starting with it, using rumoured:

" the corner shop is closing down."

2. Everyone in the village says the vicar's retiring in the summer, though there's been no official announcement. Report it, using believed:

" the vicar's retiring in the summer."

What He's Said To Be

Now put a specific person or thing first — for something true about them right now.

Spot the pattern

  • "The new dentist's said to be excellent."
  • "That flat above the chemist's is thought to be haunted, apparently."
  • "The twins next door are believed to be identical — even their own mother mixes them up."

What comes straight after "is/are said/thought/believed"? And where did "that" go? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
[subject] + is/are + said / thought / believed / rumoured + to + be (no "that", no repeated subject). This describes a state or quality true right now. Turn the impersonal form round: "it is thought that flat is haunted" becomes — put the flat first — "that flat is thought to be haunted."
[subject] + is / are + said / thought / believed / rumoured + to be / to have / to live / to know...  (a state now)
The classic slip: adding "that" after the personal subject — "he is said that he is rich" doubles up the subject and breaks the pattern. Drop "that", go straight to the infinitive: "he is said to be rich."

Quick check

1. Everyone in the office reckons the new boss is really strict, though nobody's actually tested it yet. Report it about the boss, using said + be:

"The new boss really strict."

2. Word around the village is that the shop owner speaks five languages. Report it, using believed + speak:

"The shop owner five languages."

What He's Said To Have Done

Same personal pattern, but for something that's already happened — not a state now, an event that's over.

Spot the difference

  • "He's said to have turned down a move to a bigger club last summer."
  • "The couple next door are thought to have already exchanged contracts on the house."
  • "She's rumoured to have quit her job months ago, though she still turns up every day."

Compare "he's said to be rich" with "he's said to have made his fortune in property." Which one's a state, and which one's an event that's over? Say it, then check.

Check the idea CLICK
to be / to have + noun = a state true now. to have + past participle = something that already happened, finished, before now. "He's said to be rich" (the state) vs "he's said to have made his fortune in the nineties" (the event that produced it).
[subject] + is / are + said / thought / believed / rumoured + to have + past participle  (already happened)
Don't drop the "have" — "he is said to leave last year" doesn't fit the time reference at all. Once the event is finished, the infinitive must be perfect: "he is said to have left."

Quick check

1. Word's going round that the old shopkeeper retired last month, though nobody's seen a leaving do. Report it, using said + retire:

"He last month."

2. The striker only signed in January, but he's already unhappy — rumour has it he's asked for a transfer. Report it, using rumoured + ask:

"He for a transfer already."

What Do You Remember?

Before we go on — from earlier lessons

1. Somebody stole your bike outside the station last night. Put it in the passive, starting with "My bike":

"My bike outside the station last night."

2. What's the difference between the passive "was built" and "has been built"? Say it, then check.

Check the idea CLICK
"Was built" is simple past passive — a finished action, often with a stated or implied time. "Has been built" is present perfect passive — it connects the completed action to now, and often no specific time is given because the result still matters more than the date.
