II. Where Comics Came From

Comics didn't appear out of nowhere. They evolved over decades from newspaper strips, pulp magazines, and a long tradition of visual storytelling that stretches back centuries.

The Newspaper Strips (1895–1930s)

The Yellow Kid (1895) — Created by Richard F. Outcault for the New York World. Often cited as the first comic strip character. His yellow nightshirt carried text, and the strip pioneered the use of speech balloons and sequential panels.

The Yellow Kid appeared in Joseph Pulitzer's New York World newspaper. He was so popular that rival publisher William Randolph Hearst hired Outcault away, and both papers ran competing versions. This feud between Pulitzer and Hearst became so intense that people started calling their sensationalist journalism "yellow journalism" — named after the Yellow Kid himself.

Language Focus: Describing Origins & Influence

"Comics originated in / grew out of / evolved from newspaper strips."

"The Yellow Kid is widely considered to be / is often cited as the first..."

From Strips to Books (1933–1937)

The first "comic books" were simply newspaper strips reprinted in a magazine format. In 1933, a printing salesman named Maxwell Gaines realised he could fold tabloid-sized pages in half, stitch them together, and sell them for 10 cents. This format — the pamphlet-style comic book — would become the industry standard for decades.

Key Fact

The first comic book with all-original content (not reprints) was New Fun #1 in 1935, published by Malcolm Wheeler-Nicholson — the founder of what would eventually become DC Comics.

The Big Bang: Superman (1938)

Two Jewish teenagers from Cleveland, Ohio — Jerry Siegel (writer) and Joe Shuster (artist) — had been shopping their creation around to publishers for years. Nobody wanted it. Then in June 1938, Superman appeared on the cover of Action Comics #1 and everything changed.

Superman was the ultimate immigrant story. Created by two sons of Jewish immigrants, he was an alien from a destroyed world who found a new home and used his gifts to protect others. In a nation of immigrants, this resonated deeply. — Bradford Wright, Comic Book Nation

Why did Superman matter so much? Try to explain before revealing the answer.

Historical context matters: In 1938, America was emerging from the Great Depression and watching the rise of fascism in Europe. Superman offered a fantasy of absolute moral clarity — a powerful being who chose to protect the weak. He was wish-fulfilment for an anxious generation.

Cultural significance: Superman invented the entire superhero genre. Before him, comic books existed but had no defining character type. After him, a flood of imitators appeared: Batman (1939), Wonder Woman (1941), Captain America (1941).

III. The Ages of Comics

Comic book historians divide the medium's history into distinct "ages" — each defined by a different cultural mood, artistic style, and relationship with censorship.

The Golden Age

Superman's debut triggered an explosion. Within three years, superheroes dominated the newsstands. During World War 2, comic books became essential entertainment for soldiers — 44% of men in military training camps read comics regularly. Captain America famously punched Hitler on his very first cover in March 1941 — nine months before the US entered the war.

Sheena, Queen of the Jungle #4 (1942) — One of the Golden Age's many genres beyond superheroes. Sheena was actually one of the first female protagonists in comics, predating Wonder Woman's regular series.

But then came the backlash.

The Day Comics Nearly Died

The Comics Code Authority seal — From 1954 to 2011, this stamp appeared on virtually every comic book sold in America. No retailer would stock a comic without it.

What the Code banned:

❌ No vampires, werewolves, or zombies

❌ No "disrespect for authority"

❌ No sympathetic criminals

❌ No horror or excessive violence

❌ "Good must always triumph over evil"

In 1954, psychiatrist Fredric Wertham published Seduction of the Innocent, claiming comic books caused juvenile delinquency. He testified before a US Senate subcommittee. The result? The comics industry created the Comics Code Authority — a self-censorship body so strict it nearly killed the medium. Entire genres (horror, crime, romance) were wiped out overnight.

Language Focus: Cause & Consequence

"As a result of / As a consequence of the Code, horror comics disappeared."

"This led to / resulted in / brought about a dramatic decline in..."

"The Code had the effect of / ended up stifling creativity for decades."

The Marvel Revolution

Stan Lee at the White House, 2008 — The man who revolutionised comics. With artists Jack Kirby and Steve Ditko, Lee created Spider-Man, the X-Men, the Fantastic Four, the Hulk, Iron Man, Thor, and Black Panther — all between 1961 and 1966.

In the early 1960s, Stan Lee was 39 years old, bored, and about to quit comics. His wife Joan told him to write one comic exactly the way he wanted to before leaving. That comic was The Fantastic Four (1961) — and it changed everything.

Lee's revolution was simple but radical: make superheroes human. Spider-Man was a broke teenager with acne. The X-Men were outsiders feared and hated for being different (a clear metaphor for racial prejudice). The Hulk was a scientist cursed by his own power. For the first time, readers could see themselves in their heroes.

Why was this such a big deal? Think about what DC's heroes (Superman, Batman) were like compared to Marvel's new creations. What was different?

DC heroes were godlike, perfect, untouchable. Superman had no real personal problems. Batman was a billionaire. Marvel heroes had rent to pay, relationship troubles, self-doubt, and moral complexity. This made them feel real — and it attracted an older, more sophisticated readership, including college students.

Social Issues & Underground Comix

By the 1970s, comics were tackling real-world issues. A famous 1971 Spider-Man story showed Harry Osborn addicted to drugs — published without the Comics Code Authority seal because the Code banned any depiction of drugs, even anti-drug messages. This directly led to the Code being revised.

Meanwhile, the underground comix movement (note the "x" spelling) rejected mainstream comics entirely. Artists like R. Crumb created uncensored, often shocking work sold in head shops and alternative bookstores. These were comics for adults, by adults, with no Code and no rules.

The Graphic Novel Revolution

1986 was the year everything changed again. Three works appeared that proved comics could be serious literature:

🕳

Watchmen (1986)

Alan Moore & Dave Gibbons deconstructed the superhero myth. What if superheroes were real — and deeply flawed? Named one of TIME Magazine's 100 greatest English-language novels.

🐸

Maus (1986)

Art Spiegelman told his father's Holocaust survival story using mice as Jews and cats as Nazis. In 1992 it won the Pulitzer Prize — the first and only comic to do so.

🦇

The Dark Knight Returns (1986)

Frank Miller reimagined Batman as a brutal, ageing vigilante. It redefined what superhero stories could be and directly influenced every Batman film since.

🌏

The Rise of Manga

Meanwhile, Japanese manga was becoming a global force. By the 2000s, manga outsold American comics in many markets. Dragon Ball, Naruto, and One Piece reached billions of readers.

Why "Graphic Novel"?

The term was popularised by Will Eisner when he published A Contract with God (1978). He wanted bookshops to stock his work alongside "real" literature, not file it with children's comics. The label was partly a marketing strategy — but it worked. It gave comics cultural legitimacy.

V. Your Memory Palace Room

This lesson's Memory Palace room is a vintage comic book shop. Walk through it with me. There are 5 vivid scenes at 5 anchor points. Each one encodes key facts from the lesson.

Scene 1 · The Door

A grinning bald kid in a yellow nightshirt is blocking the entrance

As you push open the shop door, you see him — the Yellow Kid himself, grinning maniacally, his nightshirt covered in scrawled text. Behind him, newspapers are flying everywhere in a whirlwind. You can smell the printer's ink. He's shouting about "yellow journalism" and pointing at a sign that reads "1895: Where it all began."

Encodes: The Yellow Kid (1895), birth of newspaper comic strips, connection to "yellow journalism."

Scene 2 · The Spinner Rack

A man in a red cape is lifting the spinner rack over his head

The old metal spinner rack is groaning, loaded with comics — and a figure in a bright red cape is lifting the entire thing above his head with one hand. With his other hand he's catching falling copies of Action Comics #1. The price tag reads "10¢" but it keeps flickering to "$3.2 MILLION." Two teenage boys watch from behind — one writing, one drawing. Cleveland, Ohio. 1938.

Encodes: Superman, Action Comics #1, Siegel & Shuster, the incredible value change, 1938.

Scene 3 · The Counter

A stern man in a suit is stamping "APPROVED" onto everything

Behind the counter stands a humourless man in a grey suit, wielding a massive rubber stamp. He's stamping "APPROVED" on some comics and hurling others into a blazing metal bin. Horror comics, crime comics, romance comics — all going up in flames. Above him hangs the Comics Code Authority seal, enormous and unmissable. The year 1954 is carved into the counter. You can hear the Senate subcommittee in the background.

Encodes: Comics Code Authority (1954), Fredric Wertham, Senate hearings, genres destroyed by censorship.

Scene 4 · The Back Wall Shelves

A teenager in a spider costume is arguing with his landlord

The back wall shelves are ALIVE. A broke teenager in a homemade spider suit is trying to put comics on the shelf while his landlord shouts about unpaid rent. Next to him, a group of misfit teenagers with strange powers huddle together, rejected by society. An old man with sunglasses and a moustache — Stan Lee — watches them all and grins. A sign reads: "1961: Flawed heroes. Real problems. The Marvel Revolution."

Encodes: Spider-Man, X-Men, Stan Lee, Marvel's revolution of flawed heroes, 1960s Silver Age.

Scene 5 · The Window Display

Three books sit in the window, glowing like holy relics

In the front window, three books are displayed on velvet stands, each glowing with golden light. The first shows a blood-spattered smiley face (Watchmen). The second shows a mouse in a concentration camp (Maus). The third shows an ageing knight silhouetted against lightning (The Dark Knight Returns). A brass plaque beneath reads: "1986: The Year Comics Became Literature." Above them, a Pulitzer Prize certificate gleams.

Encodes: Watchmen, Maus, The Dark Knight Returns, 1986 as the graphic novel turning point, Pulitzer Prize.
