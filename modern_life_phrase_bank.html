Your Phrase Toolkit

Every phrase you meet in the Modern Life role plays, gathered in one place and sorted not by situation but by what it does — so when you need to say no, be honest, or stay close, you know exactly where to reach.

How to use it. Don't read it like a list — raid it. Before your next real message, call or awkward conversation, find the job you need doing below, borrow the phrase, and say it out loud until it feels like yours. The same few moves — saying no kindly, telling the plain truth, heading off guilt, staying warm — work almost anywhere.

🫸 Getting ahead of an awkward conversation

Opening gently, before a plan or a problem hardens.

"Can I be totally honest with you for a sec?"
Asks permission to be honest and keeps it warm and personal — the other person leans in instead of bracing themselves.
The Group Chat
"…before this snowballs any further."
Flags that you're speaking up early, while things can still change — not derailing something already decided.
The Group Chat

🙅 Saying no — warmly and clearly

Declining this one thing without disappearing from the friendship.

"I'm going to have to sit this one out."
"Sit one out" = skip a single round, not quit for good. Warm, reluctant, and clearly just about this time.
The Group Chat
"The truth is, it's a bit out of my budget right now."
Owns the real reason plainly and lightly. A true reason beats an elaborate excuse almost every time.
The Group Chat

🛟 Heading off the guilt

Stopping people from "fixing" your no — and stopping it becoming a drama.

"Please don't let me hold anyone back."
Warm but firm — it actively protects everyone else's plan so your no doesn't reshape their day.
The Group Chat
"I'd hate for this to become a thing."
"A thing" = a drama everyone has to tiptoe around. Lightly asks the group to let it go, without a lecture.
The Group Chat

🤝 Turning a no into a smaller yes

Showing you're still in — just moving the plan, not skipping it.

"Here's what I can absolutely do…"
Bright and definite. Following a no with a confident yes proves you're still fully in the friendship.
The Group Chat

💛 Staying close — and a warm close

Making sure you're still one of the group, and ending on their joy, not your absence.

"Keep me in the loop for everything else, though."
"In the loop" = kept informed and included. Says "I'm out of the trip, not out of the friendship."
The Group Chat
"I'm genuinely made up for you — go and have the best time."
"Made up for you" is warm British English for thrilled for someone. Leaves them hearing your happiness for them.
The Group Chat

🫱 Raising something sensitive — gently

Opening a delicate subject so it doesn't sound like an attack.

"Can I raise something — and it's honestly not a criticism?"
Names what you're NOT doing (attacking), so they relax and listen instead of bracing to defend themselves.
The Screen-Time Talk
"Tiny favour — genuinely no big deal."
Shrinks a request down before you make it, so a boundary lands light rather than heavy.
A Photo of You

🪞 Owning your share & saying how you feel

Disarming defensiveness by taking some of it on yourself — and using "I feel", not "you always".

"I'm just as bad, I know."
Takes the blame off them and makes it a shared problem, not an accusation — nobody has to get defensive.
The Screen-Time Talk
"It's not about the phone — it's that I miss you."
Goes past the habit to the feeling underneath. "I miss you" can't be argued with the way "you're always on your phone" can.
The Screen-Time Talk

🎯 Kind, useful criticism

Honest feedback that helps rather than wounds — specific, balanced, and warm.

"Most of it was lovely — one thing let it down."
Balances the good with the off, so the criticism sounds fair, not like an attack. Then be specific about the one thing.
The Honest Review
"I only mention it because I want this place to do brilliantly."
Frames the criticism as support — you're on their side. Turns feedback from a complaint into a gift.
The Honest Review

🌉 Reconnecting & making it real

Crossing a long silence, and turning "we should catch up" into an actual plan.

"I know it's been way too long — that's on me as much as anyone."
Names the gap and owns your share without grovelling — no guilt-trip, no long apology.
Back in Touch
"Let's not do the 'we must catch up' thing and then not — are you free on the 20th?"
Kills the vague promise by naming a real date on the spot. The person who makes the plan keeps the friendship.
Back in Touch

🚧 Holding a boundary, warmly

Asking for what you need — and standing by it without a fight or a lecture.

"Would you mind taking that one down?"
A clear, direct request wrapped in "would you mind" — polite, but it actually asks for the thing.
A Photo of You
"It's nothing you did — I just keep my [face / the kids / my nights out] off there."
Explains your why simply, without a privacy lecture, and reassures them they haven't done anything wrong.
A Photo of You
"Honestly, no drama at all — I just prefer it that way."
Holds the boundary lightly if they push back. You don't owe a big justification for a simple preference.
A Photo of You

🎙️ Having an opinion & defending it

Reviewing anything out loud — a clear verdict, honest hedging, agreeing and disagreeing well.

"So, properly — what did you actually make of it?"
Asks for the real, unfiltered opinion, not the polite public version. "To make of something" = to think of it.
The Collab
"For me, it's a masterpiece — flaws and all."
A bold verdict that stays believable — a big compliment plus an honest admission ("flaws and all" = even with its faults).
The Collab
"It's a bit overrated, if I'm honest."
Criticise the hype without sounding like a hater — "a bit" and "if I'm honest" keep it fair, not an attack.
The Collab
"That's where we differ — I actually loved the ending."
Disagree warmly by naming it as a difference of taste, not a right-or-wrong — so nobody feels attacked.
The Collab
"Yeah, I'm totally with you on that."
Agree with energy. Cousins: "A hundred percent", "You're spot on", "Couldn't agree more".
The Collab

🧩 Sorting something out together

Turning "your problem" into "our problem" before anyone digs in.

"Can we work out a system for the days we're both here?"
Opens with a shared problem and a shared fix, so nobody has to defend themselves first.
Working From Home
"It's nobody's fault — the flat just wasn't built for two offices."
Blames the circumstances rather than the person. Takes the sting out before you've even said what's wrong.
Working From Home
"What does your week actually look like?"
Ask what they need before you say what you want. It also gets you the facts you'll build the plan out of.
Working From Home
"Would it work if…?"
Floats an idea instead of planting a flag. Easy for them to improve, easy for you to drop.
Working From Home
"I'm easy either way."
Hands them a real choice. Only say it when it's true — it's generous, not a shrug.
Working From Home
"Can we try it for a couple of weeks and see how it feels?"
Makes it a trial rather than a treaty. Nobody has to be sure, so nobody has to argue.
Working From Home
"The only thing I'd really need to protect is…"
Names your one hard need after giving ground everywhere else — which is exactly why it gets granted.
Working From Home
"If it's not working, say — don't just put up with it."
Books the review in advance, so a quiet resentment has somewhere to go.
Working From Home
"I'd rather sort it now than let it fester."
Says why you're raising it at all: not because it's huge, but because small things left alone stop being small.
Working From Home

🧱 Holding your ground with a company

Being immovable about the outcome and kind to the human on the phone.

"Let me give you the order number and exactly what's happened."
Hands them what they need to help you. You sound organised, which makes you hard to lose.
The Delivery Gone Wrong
"None of this is your fault, I know — but I do need it sorted."
Lifts the blame off the person and puts the problem back on the table. Kind and immovable at once.
The Delivery Gone Wrong
"What I'd like is a replacement, here, by Saturday."
A thing, a place, a date. Vague asks get vague answers; a specific one has to be answered.
The Delivery Gone Wrong
"I appreciate that, but that doesn't really work for me."
Declines the offer without rejecting the person. The kindest possible no is still a no.
The Delivery Gone Wrong
"I understand. What I need is still…"
The broken record: same sentence, same tone, every time they offer you something else. Their script runs out; you don't.
The Delivery Gone Wrong
"Can you give me a date rather than 'up to ten working days'?"
Turns a fog into a promise, or exposes that there wasn't one. Either way you learn something.
The Delivery Gone Wrong
"Is there someone who can authorise that?"
Goes up a level without punishing the person in front of you — it asks about the system, not about them.
The Delivery Gone Wrong
"So just so I'm clear — what happens next, and when?"
Makes them say the plan out loud, which catches the gap between what you agreed and what they typed.
The Delivery Gone Wrong
"Can I take a reference for this call?"
Makes a promise survive the moment somebody else picks up the phone.
The Delivery Gone Wrong

👂 Listening, instead of fixing

Being useful to someone who's struggling — mostly by not rushing to help.

"Tell me what actually set it off."
Opens the door on the real thing rather than the thing they led with.
The Comparison Trap
"So it's not the house itself — it's that you feel behind."
Says their feeling back to them in plainer words. People often can't see it until they hear it.
The Comparison Trap
"That sounds genuinely horrible — and it makes complete sense you'd feel that."
Validates the feeling without having to agree the thought behind it is true.
The Comparison Trap
"I'm not going to tell you it's all fake. You know that already."
Names the useless comfort and refuses it — which is what proves you're actually listening.
The Comparison Trap
"For what it's worth, I do this too. More than I'd admit."
Lowers the stakes and gets you off the high ground, without hijacking their turn.
The Comparison Trap
"Do you want me to just listen, or do you want me to be useful?"
Asks permission before helping. Most bad advice is good advice given to someone who wanted company instead.
The Comparison Trap
"What would 'on track' even look like for you?"
A question instead of a lecture. It lets them find the hole in it themselves.
The Comparison Trap
"Whose life are you actually measuring yourself against?"
Makes the comparison say its own name out loud, which is usually enough.
The Comparison Trap
"Ring me next time instead of scrolling. Any hour."
Offers a real, specific alternative. Vague warmth reassures nobody.
The Comparison Trap

💷 Talking about money without dying

Naming a number out loud — to friends, which is the hardest room there is.

"Can I be the boring one for a second and talk about money?"
Says the awkward thing about itself first, which somehow makes it much less awkward.
The Holiday Budget
"I'm in, definitely — it's just the number I'm stuck on."
Separates wanting to come from being able to afford it, so nobody hears it as backing out.
The Holiday Budget
"What's everyone's actual ceiling, do you think?"
Asks for other people's limits before announcing yours. "Actual" is the word that gets a real answer.
The Holiday Budget
"I can do about three hundred. Anything past that and I'm pretending."
A real number, said plainly. It's very hard to argue with a number and very easy to argue with a mood.
The Holiday Budget
"It's not that it's not worth it — it's just more than I've got."
Declines the price without sneering at the thing, so nobody with more money feels judged.
The Holiday Budget
"I can't be the only one thinking it, can I?"
Opens a door for everyone else who's been quiet. Usually you weren't the only one.
The Holiday Budget
"Don't take this the wrong way — it's not a big thing."
Lowers the temperature before you've said anything, so they hear the sentence instead of bracing against it.
The Money You Lent
"That two hundred from back in March — where are we with that?"
Names the fact and asks a question, with no accusation anywhere in it.
The Money You Lent
"'Soon' is fine — I'd just rather have a date."
Accepts their promise and quietly refuses its vagueness in the same breath.
The Money You Lent
"It's not the money. It's that I've started keeping score, and I hate it."
The true reason, which moves people in a way the money never does.
The Money You Lent
"Are you alright, though? Genuinely."
Puts the friendship back in the room once the business is done.
The Money You Lent

🚪 The people you're stuck with

Complaining to someone you'll see every week for years.

"Sorry to be that neighbour."
Names the role you're afraid of playing, which is how you avoid playing it.
The Neighbour's Noise
"This is probably going to sound worse than I mean it."
Warns them your tone might slip before it does, and buys forgiveness in advance.
The Neighbour's Noise
"I'm not asking you to stop having people over. Honestly."
Rules out the thing they're dreading, so they can hear what you're actually asking.
The Neighbour's Noise
"Would midnight be doable, do you think?"
A question, not a demand — and a specific one, so there's something to say yes to.
The Neighbour's Noise
"What would work for you?"
Hands them the solution to author. People keep the arrangements they invented.
The Neighbour's Noise
"If it's a one-off, forget I said anything."
Gives them a dignified exit, which is what stops a neighbour becoming an enemy.
The Neighbour's Noise
"You'd tell me if I was doing it to you, wouldn't you?"
Makes it mutual rather than a complaint from above. The tag question does the work.
The Neighbour's Noise
