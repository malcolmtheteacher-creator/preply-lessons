At the Train Station

The ticket queue is long, the departure board is lying to you, and your train is about to vanish. Time to keep your head and talk your way home.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

It's a grey Tuesday morning in a station the size of a cathedral. Announcements echo, pigeons loiter under the roof, and you've got a train to Durham to catch — where someone is expecting you, and where a second train, a connection you booked weeks ago, will not wait around for anyone.

You need three things and you need them fast: a ticket, the right platform, and — as it turns out — a plan B, because the neat little journey on your screen is about to fall apart. Getting home on time today won't be about panicking. It'll be about how you keep your cool.

Behind the glass at the ticket office: a clerk who has watched a thousand timetables collapse and calmed a thousand flustered travellers — unhurried, dryly kind, and, if you stay pleasant, entirely on your side.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did a train, bus or flight last let you down? What went wrong — and did you handle it well, or did you rather lose the plot?
  2. Some people can be told "your train is cancelled" and stay perfectly calm; others erupt. Which are you — and does staying calm actually get you further, or just get you walked over?

Who's who

You
The Traveller 🎒

Somewhere to be, someone waiting, a connection you can't miss. Polite, quick-thinking, and determined to reach Durham today — one way or another.

The other voice
The Booking Clerk 🎫

Seen every delay and heard every complaint twice. Knows the routes, the rules and the refunds — and which of them will bend for someone who asks nicely.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the softeners, the hedges, and the moment bad news is met with a level head instead of a raised voice.

ClerkMorning! Where are we sending you today?
YouMorning — I'm after a single to Durham, if that's possible. And I'll be honest, I'm not even sure I'm in the right queue.
ClerkYou're in the right queue, don't worry. Single to Durham, off-peak — that'll be forty-two pounds fifty.
YouContactless, if that's alright. Am I right in thinking the next one goes from platform four?
ClerkPlatform four it is — the 10:15, calling at York and Darlington. You've got twenty minutes, so no need to sprint just yet.
YouPerfect. And just to be on the safe side — it is running on time, isn't it? I've a connection at the other end I really can't afford to miss.
ClerkLet me just… ah. Now you've said that. I'm sorry — it's flashed up as cancelled this very second. Signalling fault up the line. I promise I only see it as you ask.
YouOh, you're joking. Right — so where does that leave me? Is there any way of getting to Durham today at all?
ClerkThere is, but it's the scenic route, I won't lie to you. Take the 10:40 to York from platform two, change there, and pick up the Newcastle train — that one stops at Durham. Gets you in about an hour later than you'd hoped.
YouSo if I've understood you correctly: the 10:40 to York, change, then anything heading for Newcastle. And my ticket still covers all that?
ClerkIt does — your ticket's valid on the diverted route, no extra charge. Just keep it to hand for the barriers.
YouThat's a relief. And since the cancellation's your end, not mine — am I entitled to claim the delay back?
ClerkYou are. Anything over fifteen minutes and you can put in for Delay Repay. Shall I jot the reference down for you, or would you rather I printed the form?
YouWould it be possible to have it in writing, if it's no trouble? You've turned a small disaster into a manageable one — thank you, honestly.
ClerkAll part of the service. Go and grab yourself a coffee — platform two, and don't let the York train leave without you. That one they'll run bang on time, just to spite you.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. "Now you've said that." There's a small, wry irony in the timing of when the cancellation appears. What is it — and why does the clerk bother to say "I promise I only see it as you ask"?

2. The direct train dies, yet the traveller still reaches Durham the same day. What exactly is the alternative route the clerk offers — and does it cost the traveller anything extra?

3. The traveller leaves the desk with something in their hand that turns a ruined plan into a claim. What is it, and what makes them eligible for it?

1. The train flashes up as cancelled at the exact moment the traveller says they can't afford to miss their connection — as if the asking jinxed it. The clerk adds "I only see it as you ask" to make clear they weren't hiding it: the bad news and their knowledge of it arrived together, so it isn't their fault or a stitch-up.
2. Take the 10:40 to York from platform two, change there, and pick up the Newcastle train, which stops at Durham — arriving about an hour later than planned. It costs nothing extra: the ticket is valid on the diverted route.
3. A Delay Repay form (in writing). They're eligible because the delay is more than fifteen minutes and the cancellation is the railway's fault, not theirs.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that keep you calm, polite and firmly in the game when the timetable turns against you — at this station and everywhere else. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚪 Opening softly

Phrase 1

You've reached the front of the queue and you're not even sure it's the right desk. You want to ask for a single to Durham without sounding like you're barking an order. Which opening lands best?

A. "Single to Durham."
B. "Give me a single to Durham."
C. "I'm after a single to Durham, if that's possible."
C — "I'm after a single to Durham, if that's possible."
"I'm after…" is a warm, everyday British way of saying "I'd like…". Tag on "…if that's possible" and you hand the other person the power, which makes almost any request easier to grant.
Phrase 2

You think the next train leaves from platform four — but you'd rather check than assume, and rather sound tentative than bossy. There's a four-word opener that floats your guess as a question: "___ ___ ___ ___ the next one goes from platform four?"

"Am I right in thinking…?"
The perfect hedge for checking something you half-know without claiming to be certain: "Am I right in thinking the shop shuts at six?" If you're wrong, no one loses face.

🎣 Asking smoothly

Phrase 3

You're lost in a station the size of an aircraft hangar and need someone to point you to your platform — as gently as possible. Put the pieces in order:

in the right direction?would youpointing memind
"Would you mind pointing me in the right direction?"
"Would you mind + -ing" makes any request feel light and optional. Watch the grammar too: mind pointing, never "mind to point".
Phrase 4

You need to be sure the train's on time — a missed connection would sink your whole day — but you don't want to sound anxious or accusing. Complete the five-word hedge you tuck in before the question: "___ ___ ___ ___ ___, is it running on time?"

"Just to be on the safe side, is it running on time?"
"Just to be on the safe side…" frames your double-check as sensible caution rather than distrust — so the other person doesn't feel they're being doubted.

🌧️ When it all goes wrong

Phrase 5

The clerk has just told you your train is cancelled. A beginner freezes or snaps. You stay level and hand the problem straight back — "so… what now?" — without a flicker of anger. Which line?

A. "This is absolutely ridiculous."
B. "So where does that leave me?"
C. "What am I supposed to do now?"
B — "So where does that leave me?"
Calm, adult, and it invites the other person to solve the problem with you. A and C both leak frustration onto the one person who can actually help.
Phrase 6

You refuse to give up on reaching Durham today. Ask whether ANY route still exists — the phrasing quietly pleads without whining. Build the sentence:

of gettingis thereto Durham todayany wayat all?
"Is there any way of getting to Durham today at all?"
"Is there any way of + -ing… at all?" is negotiation gold: it asks the other person to go hunting for a solution, and the little "at all" reaches for even the slimmest chance.

🛤️ Sorting out plan B

Phrase 7

The clerk has rattled off a fiddly alternative — change here, pick up that train there. Before you trust your legs to it, you reflect the whole plan back to check you've got it. Complete the opening chunk: "___ ___ ___ ___ ___ ___, I change at York?"

"So if I've understood you correctly, I change at York?"
Repeating a complicated plan back in your own words catches mistakes before they cost you a train. Cousin phrase: "So what you're saying is…?"
Phrase 8

The cancellation is the railway's fault, not yours, and you want to raise the question of money back — firmly, but without starting a row. Which line is firm AND polite?

A. "I want my money back."
B. "Am I entitled to claim the delay back?"
C. "You owe me a refund."
B — "Am I entitled to claim the delay back?"
"Am I entitled to…?" asserts your right calmly, by asking about it rather than demanding it. "Entitled to" is the confident, grown-up word for "allowed to have" — it assumes a fair system, not a fight.

🌟 One last request — and a proper thank-you

Phrase 9

You'd rather the refund claim was printed than scribbled on a scrap of paper — but it's an extra ask, so you cushion it. Complete the four-word softener: "___ ___ ___ ___ have that in writing?"

"Would it be possible to have that in writing?"
"Would it be possible to…?" is the all-purpose polite request — heavier than "can I", lighter than begging. "…in writing" quietly protects you if the claim is ever questioned.
Phrase 10

The clerk has rescued your entire morning. Which thank-you will they still be thinking about at their lunch break?

A. "Thanks."
B. "Thank you for your assistance."
C. "You've turned a small disaster into a manageable one — thank you, honestly."
C — "You've turned a small disaster into a manageable one — thank you, honestly."
It names exactly what they did, and "honestly" adds real warmth. A is bare; B reads like a form letter. Specific gratitude is the kind that lands and gets remembered.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole trip out loud using all ten, in order — soft opening → smooth questions → the bad news → plan B → last favour → goodbye. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

🌩️ The last train home

One more round — no script, no safety net. It's Christmas Eve, the last train to Durham is due to leave in ten minutes, and the departure board has just flicked to CANCELLED. You HAVE to be home tonight; someone is waiting. Outside there's a single taxi that two other stranded passengers also want; there may or may not be a rail-replacement bus; a supervisor might authorise a hotel; and a stranger on the platform, going almost your way, has a car. Start the timer and talk your way home: explore every angle — share the taxi, split the fare, ask about the bus, appeal to the supervisor, accept the lift from the stranger. Who gets the taxi if there's only one? One rule: the moment you turn rude or start pulling rank, doors close — people help people they like.

The Echo 🕰️

In the summer of 1914, the platforms of Britain, France and Germany filled with young men boarding trains they were sure would carry them to a short, glorious war — home by Christmas, everyone said.

The timetable was wrong. By December they were dug into frozen trenches a few hundred yards apart — until, one Christmas, the guns simply stopped, and enemies climbed out to shake hands, swap cigarettes and kick a ball about in the space between.

A cancelled train forces strangers to wait together on a cold platform and, now and then, to actually talk to one another. For a few hours in 1914, a whole war was cancelled — and the men discovered they rather liked each other.

Two bigger questions

  1. A delay throws strangers together — a platform, a waiting room, a replacement bus — and now and then you end up talking to someone you'd never otherwise have met. When has an interruption to your plans led to an unexpectedly human moment?
  2. The Christmas Truce happened because ordinary men, for a few hours, chose to ignore the orders they'd been handed. How much of getting along with strangers — at a ticket desk, on a stalled platform, or in a trench — comes down to simply deciding to be human first?
