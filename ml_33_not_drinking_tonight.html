Not Drinking Tonight

It's Marta's birthday, the table is loud, and you're not drinking tonight — maybe this month, maybe from now on; that part's your business. What's about to be everyone else's business, apparently, is your glass. Between the toast and the taxi, you'll decline roughly nine drinks. Here's how to do it smiling.

The Scene 🎬

The strangest social rule nobody wrote down: order a lime and soda, and suddenly everyone's a lawyer demanding your reasons.

Your reasons are your own — health, sleep, training, medication, money, a January experiment, or simply not fancying it. Tonight it doesn't matter which, and that's the first thing to know: you are not obliged to table your reasons, and the fastest way to a long boring argument is offering them up for cross-examination.

What you'll actually face at Marta's birthday is a taxonomy of pushers, none of them villains. Dario, the cheerful waiter of peer pressure: "go on, ONE, it's a birthday!" Priya, the detective: "wait, why? Are you on antibiotics? Are you PREGNANT?" And Big Tom, the philosopher, two beers in: "you know what your problem is — you don't trust yourself to have fun." Underneath all three runs the same current: your empty-of-alcohol glass reads, to some drinkers, as a quiet comment on their full one. It isn't. You'll need to say so, lightly, without saying it.

The whole craft is tone: cheerful, brief, unbudging. No sermons (nobody's drinking is your business either), no apologies (you've done nothing), no elaborate cover stories (they collapse by round three). A relaxed "not for me tonight" repeated with total good humour outlasts every campaign ever mounted against it — usually by round two, always by round three.

  2. Which is harder in your culture: refusing a drink, refusing food, or refusing an invitation? What does the difficulty protect?

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for declining a drink — and for holding any private choice against a friendly, relentless table. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. One rule above all: the same warm tone at decline number one and decline number nine. The moment you sharpen, it becomes a thing; the moment you apologise, it becomes negotiable; the moment you explain at length, it becomes a debate. Cheerful. Brief. Unbudging. Repeat.

🥂 The first declines

Phrase 1

The job: the basic no, with no crack in it. Which version?

A. "Oh — um, I probably shouldn't… well, maybe just a small one, if everyone's…"
B. "Not for me tonight, thanks."
C. "No. I don't drink. Alcohol is a poison, actually — there was a documentary—"
B — "Not for me tonight, thanks."
A is a door left ajar, and Dario lives in ajar doors — "probably" and "maybe" are invitations with extra steps. C wins the argument and loses the table; nobody at a birthday ordered a documentary. B is complete: cheerful, final, reason-free. "Tonight" is quietly clever — it makes the decline about now, not a lifestyle up for review, even if it is one. Five words. No crack.

Take it anywhere: "Not for me tonight, thanks." Dessert, karaoke, the group bet, the second bottle — the all-purpose warm no.
Phrase 2

The job: giving your hands and glass a job. Complete it:

"What I ___ take is a lime and soda — in a wine glass if it makes the photos better."

"What I WILL take is a lime and soda."
The counter-order is half the battle: an empty hand at a table is a vacuum every pourer feels obliged to fill, while a full glass — any full glass — reads as "sorted, move along". Ordering it cheerfully in the same breath as the no converts you from a problem to be fixed into a person with a drink. The wine-glass joke does the last bit: it says you're IN the party, just running different fuel.

Take it anywhere: "What I will take is ___." Every no lands softer with a yes riding beside it.
Phrase 3

The job: the birthday/occasion pressure — "it doesn't even count!". Complete it:

"I'll ___ louder than anyone — just with bubbles of the non-negotiable kind."

"I'll toast louder than anyone — just with different bubbles."
Occasion-pushers aren't really offering wine; they're checking you're IN — that the birthday matters to you, that you're not above the moment. So answer the real question: full-volume participation, glass contents irrelevant. Once Dario hears the toast is safe, the Rioja stops mattering. Join harder, drink nothing: it defuses almost every ceremony objection ever mounted.

Take it anywhere: "I'm all in — just with a different glass." Weddings, leaving dos, championship nights: separate the joining from the substance.
Phrase 4

The job: shutting the detective down kindly. Complete it:

"No ___, honestly — I'm just not drinking at the moment. Nothing to detect."

"No story — I'm just not drinking at the moment."
Priya's not malicious; she's a narrative economy, and your glass is an unexplained plot point. "No story" answers the actual hunger — there's nothing here for the collection — while "at the moment" declines to schedule a future debate about forever. Crucially, You gives ONE fact and no reasons: reasons are hooks, and every hook gets fished. Then the pivot: "tell me about the flat." Detectives can be redirected onto richer cases. Theirs.

Take it anywhere: "No story — just a thing I'm doing at the moment." Diets, budgets, dating breaks, quiet months of every kind.

🛡️ The campaign continues

Phrase 5

The job: the deep move — separating the decision from the drink. Put the pieces in order:

it's that I've decided,It's not about the one glass —and I'm happy.
"It's not about the one glass — it's that I've decided, and I'm happy."
The pusher's entire argument is that one glass is small — and they're right, which is why arguing quantities loses. Move the ground: the glass is tiny, the DECISION is yours, and it's already made. "And I'm happy" is the door-closer — there's no problem here to solve, no suffering to rescue you from. People argue with rules and regulations all night; they rarely argue with someone's settled contentment.

Take it anywhere: "It's not about the ___ — I've decided, and I'm happy." The sentence that ends relitigation of any small-looking choice.
Phrase 6

The job: answering "your empty glass judges us all". Complete it:

"My glass isn't saying anything about ___. It's just ___ than yours."

"My glass isn't saying anything about yours — it's just quieter than yours."
Big Tom said the honest thing drunk people say: the sober glass feels like a mirror. The rescue is naming the fear and retiring it with a joke — no sermon (which would prove his point), no defensiveness (ditto), just: my choice isn't a comment on yours. The laugh does the diplomatic work; the sentence underneath does the real work. Most glass-hostility is exactly this fear, and it dissolves the moment it's kindly named.

Take it anywhere: "My ___ isn't a comment on yours." Diets at dinner parties, budgets among spenders, early nights among owls.
Phrase 7

The job: the standing answer, for the third wave. Complete it:

"Ask me again in an ___ — the answer's the same, but I'll be three legs up at darts by then."

"Ask me again in an hour — the answer's the same."
The pre-emptive strike on the whole rest of the evening: it tells the table the no has no expiry time, no weakening curve, no "later when you've relaxed". Delivered with the darts joke, it's not a wall — it's a standing arrangement, cheerfully published. Most campaigns end here, because campaigns run on the hope of eventual yes, and you've just cancelled the hope while keeping the party.

Take it anywhere: "Ask me again later — same answer, better mood." For every no that will otherwise be re-asked hourly.
Phrase 8

The job: de-escalating the whole topic in seven words. Complete it:

"Don't make it a ___ and it won't be a ___."

"Don't make it a thing and it won't be a thing."
Said with a grin, this is the social physics of the evening stated as law: the glass only becomes an event if someone hosts the event. Pair it with evidence — "I'm having a great night, look, this is my great-night face" — and the last worry (that you're secretly suffering, that the party is diminished) evaporates. The table wants to know everything's fine. Show them fine.

Take it anywhere: "Don't make it a thing and it won't be one." Haircuts, breakups, career changes — the anti-drama clause for any table.

🎯 Winning the evening

Phrase 9

The job: converting the difference into a gift. Complete it:

"I'll ___, then — everyone wins."

"I'll drive, then — everyone wins."
The masterstroke of the sober evening: turn your difference into the table's luck. The driver, the cake-guardian, the one who remembers where Tom left his jacket, the keeper of the aux cable — suddenly your lime and soda is infrastructure, and infrastructure doesn't get argued with. "Everyone wins" isn't spin; it's the actual accounting, and tables are quick at that maths once it's shown.

Take it anywhere: "I'll ___, then — everyone wins." Convert any difference into the group's advantage and watch the pressure die of gratitude.
Phrase 10

The job: the closing declaration — present till the end. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Happy birthday, Marta — my glass and I love you, and both of us are staying till the end."
The deepest fear under all the pushing — Dario's, Tom's, everyone's — is that the not-drinking you is a leaving-early, half-there, no-fun you. The close answers it with a promise: full presence, last-taxi membership, love declared. Then keep it: be the one still dancing at midnight holding bubbles of the non-negotiable kind. One evening of that and the table never pushes again — you've proved the only thing they actually needed proved.

Take it anywhere: "I'm staying till the end." The sentence that retires the pressure permanently — backed by staying till the end.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1429, a teenage girl walked into the French court and declined, politely and completely, every script written for her. Peasant girls did not address kings; she did. Women did not wear armour or lead armies; she would. At every table she ever sat at — court, camp, and finally courtroom — Joan of Arc faced the same chorus in different clothes: go on, be sensible, do what everyone does, who do you think you are?

What's striking in the trial records isn't defiance — it's her tone. Asked the same trap questions for weeks, she gave the same calm answers, without sharpening, without apologising, without ever tabling her private reasons for cross-examination. "Asked why she wore men's dress, she answered that it was her own affair." Pressed again: the same answer, delivered — the notaries recorded — with composure that unsettled her questioners more than any argument. The campaign against her assumed eventual surrender; she had simply cancelled the hope of it, and kept her seat.

Nobody at Marta's birthday is a Burgundian inquisitor, and a lime and soda is not Orléans. But the mechanics of holding a private choice against a insistent room haven't changed in six centuries: the same warm answer, every round, reasons your own, presence undiminished. Tables, like tribunals, eventually move on to someone who wobbles.
