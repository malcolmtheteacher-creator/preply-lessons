Would You Plug In?

If a machine could give you a perfect life that felt completely real, would you take it, and what does your answer reveal?

Today: you'll explain what, besides feeling good, makes a life worth living, and you'll master the structures for talking about situations that are imaginary or unreal.

The ideas, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The offer

In 1974 the American philosopher Robert Nozick published a thought experiment that is still argued about. Imagine, he said, a machine that could give you any experience you wanted. Super-clever scientists would stimulate your brain so that you felt you were writing a great novel, making a friend, or reading a fascinating book. All the time you would be floating in a tank, with electrodes attached to your head.

Should you plug into this machine for life, pre-programming your life's experiences? You would not know you were in the tank; you would be certain it was real. Nozick expected most readers to refuse, and he used the refusal to ask why. If what matters is how our life feels from the inside, there is no reason to say no.

He suggested three reasons. We want to do certain things, not merely have the experience of doing them. We want to be a certain kind of person, and a floating blob has no character. And we want to stay in contact with a reality deeper than anything a human being has made.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the machine offer, and what is the catch?

2. What three reasons did Nozick suggest for refusing?

1. Any experience you want, felt as completely real, but you would be floating in a tank with electrodes, and you would not know it.

2. We want to do things, not only seem to do them; we want to be a certain kind of person; and we want contact with a deeper reality.

Part 2 — The test of happiness

The machine is a test for a very old idea called hedonism: that pleasure is the only thing good in itself. For Jeremy Bentham, writing around 1800, the right choice was always the one that produced the most happiness. If experiences are all that matter, the machine wins easily, and a refusal looks like a mistake.

But the case is slippery. Researchers have asked people the question in several forms and found that the wording matters. In one study, the philosopher Felipe De Brigard suggested that people react differently when told they are already in the machine and offered the chance to leave. Many who said no to plugging in also said no to unplugging, which suggests that we simply prefer whatever we are used to, and that our intuition may be a habit rather than a principle.

That is a serious objection. If our answer changes with the framing, can we trust it as evidence about what is valuable? Or does the very fact that so many people feel uneasy tell us something, even if we cannot say exactly what?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What idea does the machine test?

2. What did De Brigard's variation suggest about people's answers?

1. Hedonism, the view that pleasure or happiness is the only thing good in itself.

2. That the wording changes the answer: many prefer whatever they are used to, so the refusal may be a habit, not a principle.

Discuss:
  • If your answer changes with the framing, does that make it worthless, or does it show something about how we think?

Part 3 — The machines we already have

No one has built Nozick's tank, but we have built its cousins. Games that reward us on a schedule, feeds designed to keep us looking, and virtual worlds that get better every year. The 1999 film The Matrix turned the idea into a choice between a red pill and a blue pill: bitter truth or comfortable fiction.

The question is no longer only whether to plug in, but how far we already have. Every curated photograph, every edited memory, every confident story we tell about ourselves is a small step towards an experience that feels better than the facts. Perhaps the real lesson of the machine is not that illusion is always wrong, but that we care about the difference between feeling that something happened and having it happen.

And if that is true, then reality matters to us even when it hurts. That may be the most surprising thing the thought experiment shows.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What choice does The Matrix offer?

2. What might the machine really show about what we value?

1. Between a red pill, which shows the bitter truth, and a blue pill, which keeps a comfortable fiction.

2. That we care about the difference between seeming and being, between feeling that something happened and its actually happening.

Discuss:
  • Which everyday technology comes closest to Nozick's machine, and where would you draw the line?

Grammar — unreal conditionals and wishes

To talk about situations that are imaginary, impossible or contrary to fact, English uses the past tense for present meaning. This is the shape of thought experiments, and it is what lets you invite a listener into one.

Four structures:
• If + past simple, would + base verb — "If I lived in the tank, I would never know." (After if, were is used for all persons in careful English: "If I were you…")
• Suppose / Imagine + past simple — "Suppose the machine offered you a perfect life."
• I wish / If only + past simple — present regret: "I wish I could be sure." "If only it were simpler!"
• would rather / as if + past simple — "I would rather you told me the truth." "He talks as if he knew everything."
Feel the difference:
• The past tense here does not mean past time. It means distance from reality.
• I would rather + base verb when the subject is the same ("I would rather live"), but I would rather + subject + past when it changes ("I would rather you stayed").
• wish + would is for annoyance at a habit: "I wish you would stop."

Would you still plug in?

Five variations of the machine. Say what you would choose and why, then reveal what each version tests.

1. The machine is only for one year, as a holiday, and you remember everything afterwards. Do you plug in?
It tests whether the objection is about reality or about permanence. Many people who refuse a lifetime accept a year, which suggests they value a life with the real world in it, not that every simulation is wrong.
2. Everyone you love can plug in with you, and they will all be happy and fully aware it is a simulation. Do you plug in?
It tests the role of shared reality. If other real minds are inside, some of the objection about contact with other people disappears, but the objection about doing things is still there.
3. You are told you are already in the machine, and you can leave for a harder, real life. Do you leave?
It tests habit against principle. If you say you would stay, you may be showing preference for the familiar, which is the point De Brigard raised.
4. The machine gives you the feeling of finishing a marathon, with no training. Do you want it?
It tests effort. The feeling is worth much less when it does not come from something you did, which supports Nozick's first reason.
5. Your real life is full of pain and loneliness. Does your answer change?
It tests how strong the objection is. Many people say it becomes harder to refuse, which suggests that reality matters, but not infinitely.

A thought experiment

Imagine this:
  • If you could be certain that no one would ever tell you the truth about your life, and that everything you believed was a kind and comforting story, would you want to know? Why, or why not?
  • Design the rule you would put on the machine to make it acceptable to you: what would it have to guarantee?

Build the strongest case for the other side

Argue against yourself:
  • If you would refuse the machine, argue for one minute that you should plug in. If you would plug in, argue that you should not. Use at least one unreal conditional.
