The Bad Recommendation

'Use my guy — he's brilliant.' Four words, said over coffee, meant with your whole heart. Four months later your friend has a half-finished kitchen, a builder who's stopped answering, and a very careful voice when she mentions it. Your guy. Your recommendation. Your move.

The Scene 🎬

A recommendation is a small loan of your reputation. Sometimes the borrower defaults — and the debt lands between you and a friend.

Milan renovated your bathroom two years ago: on time, on budget, tidy, lovely with the neighbours. So when Rosa mentioned her kitchen, you didn't hesitate — "use my guy." What you didn't know: your job was small and simple; hers is big and complicated. Milan took on more than he handles well, took a second project mid-way, and has now gone quiet with her kitchen at the plaster-and-dangling-wires stage. Rosa has been polite about it for three weeks — a pointed, careful politeness that anyone fluent in friendship can hear screaming.

Here's what most people do, and it's fatal: defend the recommendation. "He was great for me!" — true, useless, and heard as taking his side. The other fatal move: vanishing from the mess entirely — "well, your contract's with him" — technically correct, and the end of something between you.

The third way is the whole lesson: get INTO the middle on your friend's side. Hear all of it unsoftened, own the vouching without absurdly owning the plastering, and then spend the one currency you genuinely have that she doesn't: your relationship with Milan. He answers your calls. That's worth more than any apology — and today it gets used.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for when your recommendation goes wrong — builders, restaurants, jobs, people. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The register is ally, not defendant: you're not on trial for the recommendation, and you're certainly not Milan's lawyer. Every sentence should move you further INTO the mess on your friend's side — the opposite of the instinct, which is to explain your way out of it.

🚪 Walking into the mess

Phrase 1

The job: raising it first — you, not her. Complete it:

"I heard how bad it's got, and I'm so sorry it was ___ ___."

"I'm so sorry it was my guy."
The recommender who waits to be confronted has already lost a week of friendship — Rosa was three weeks into dreading the conversation YOU should have started. "My guy" is the phrase doing the work: it re-attaches your name to the mess voluntarily, which is precisely what she'd been too polite to do. Arrive with pastries, arrive uninvited, but arrive — the front step beats the phone for this one.

Take it anywhere: "I heard, and I'm sorry it was my ___." My guy, my restaurant, my idea, my introduction. Claim the connection before it's claimed for you.
Phrase 2

The job: unlocking the unsoftened version. Complete it:

"Tell me all of it — and don't ___ it because he came from me."

"Don't soften it because he came from me."
Your friend has been editing the disaster to protect your feelings — that's what the careful politeness IS. This sentence cancels the editing licence explicitly, and you may need to cancel it twice ("I'm not here for the polite version") before the dam goes. What comes out will sting, and some of it will glance off you — "my friend said he was brilliant" — let it land. The unsoftened version is the price of being useful.

Take it anywhere: "Don't soften it because ___ came from me." Recommendations, introductions, ideas you championed — cancel the kindness filter.
Phrase 3

The job: owning your share — precisely, not theatrically. Which version?

A. "This is all my fault. I feel terrible. I basically wrecked your kitchen."
B. "I recommended him in good faith — but that doesn't fix your kitchen."
C. "Well, to be fair, he was fantastic for us. Maybe your job spooked him?"
B — "I recommended him in good faith — but that doesn't fix your kitchen."
C is the fatal defence: true, irrelevant, and heard as "you're the problem". A drowns her disaster in your feelings and forces her to comfort YOU — the mess's second insult. B holds both facts without flinching: the recommendation was honest, and honesty is not a plumbing service. Own the vouching, not the plastering. The boundary is what makes the ownership credible.

Take it anywhere: "I did ___ in good faith — but that doesn't fix ___." The precise apology for every honest mistake with consequences.
Phrase 4

The job: the caveat you skipped, delivered late but delivered. Complete it:

"I should have said: he was great for ___ job — which was ___. That caveat was mine to give."

"He was great for MY job — which was small. That caveat was mine to give, and I didn't give it."
The honest anatomy of most failed recommendations: not a lie, but an over-extension — one bathroom's worth of evidence stretched across a kitchen-sized claim. Naming the gap does two things: it explains the disaster without excusing anyone, and — hear Rosa — "that actually helps," because it converts "my friend misjudged him" into "my friend's data was smaller than my project." It also writes your future policy: say what you actually know, always.

Take it anywhere: "It was great for MY ___, which was ___ — I should have said so." The late caveat, for every recommendation you ever oversold.

🔧 Becoming useful

Phrase 5

The job: from feelings to facts. Complete it:

"What's still ___ — money, work, or both? Walk me through where it stands."

"What's still outstanding — money, work, or both?"
The pivot from sympathy to usefulness, and the question that turned Rosa's fifteen-week fog into a two-line problem: unconnected units, and two thousand pounds of invisible worktops. You can't make a single useful call until the mess has a shape — and shaping it is itself a gift; she'd stopped being able to see it whole. Sympathy opens the conversation. This question opens the rescue.

Take it anywhere: "What's still outstanding — money, work, or both?" Every mess you're helping with: get the inventory before the strategy.
Phrase 6

The job: spending the asset only you hold. Put the pieces in order:

He'll answer me faster than you —I'll call him myself, today.so let's use it.that's not fair, but it's true,
"I'll call him myself, today. He'll answer me faster than you — that's not fair, but it's true, so let's use it."
THE move of the whole lesson. The recommender holds a currency the wronged friend doesn't: relationship, leverage, the builder's hope of future referrals — "he thinks you're his best advert." Naming the unfairness while deploying it is what keeps it clean. And "today" matters: leverage promised for someday is sympathy wearing a hard hat.

Take it anywhere: "They'll answer me faster — not fair, but true, so let's use it." Spend your access on the person your recommendation cost.
Phrase 7

The job: the call's asks — checkable, not hopeful. Complete it:

"Dates in writing for finishing — and ___ the worktops are ordered. An invoice, not a ___."

"Dates in writing, and proof the worktops are ordered — an invoice, not a promise."
Fifteen weeks of "next week, promise" is what promises are worth here; the rescue call must ask for things that exist on paper. Dates in writing. The stone merchant's invoice. Photographs of ordered slabs. Every ask should pass one test: can Rosa hold it in her hand? You've met this rule at the car-hire desk and the retention line — it's the same rule, wearing plaster dust.

Take it anywhere: "In writing — an invoice, not a promise." For every rescue negotiation with someone who's been promising for weeks.

🤝 Guaranteeing the friendship

Phrase 8

The job: the warranty on your vouching. Complete it:

"I'm not ___ now it's gone wrong. I'm in this until your taps run."

"I'm not disappearing now it's gone wrong — I'm in this until it's fixed."
The sentence that defines what your word is worth. Most recommendations are fair-weather instruments — warmly given, quietly abandoned at the first invoice dispute. Declaring the warranty ("until your taps run") converts yours into something rarer, and it's what Rosa actually needed more than apology: not a sorry friend, an enlisted one. Note the escalation clause attached: two weeks, then the trade association together. A guarantee with no teeth is a greeting card.

Take it anywhere: "I'm not disappearing now it's gone wrong." Say it, then be findable in week fifteen.
Phrase 9

The job: checking the friendship's damage directly. Complete it:

"Has this ___ us? Genuine question — I'd rather know."

"Has this damaged us? Genuine question — I'd rather know."
The bravest sentence of the morning, and the one almost nobody risks — we'd rather wonder for a year than ask for a minute. Rosa had been "dreading telling you in case it broke something"; the direct question let her check the beam and find it holding — "weirdly, it's the opposite, as of four minutes ago." Ask it only after the useful work is promised; asked too early it's fishing for absolution. Asked at the right moment, it's maintenance on the thing that actually matters.

Take it anywhere: "Has this damaged us? I'd rather know." After every mess that involved your name — the friendship audit, out loud.
Phrase 10

The job: the policy you take away. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Next time I'll say what I actually know — and I'll stand behind it."
The whole lesson, packed for travel. Caveats attached: the size of your evidence stated with the praise — "great for my small job; never seen him do a big one." Guarantee included: your willingness to stand in the mess if it comes. If a recommendation isn't worth guaranteeing, soften it to what it really is — "I liked him, but my sample size is one bathroom" — which is honest, useful, and keeps your word at full value for the times you spend it whole.

Take it anywhere: "I'll say what I know, and stand behind it." The terms for everything you'll ever vouch for.

The Echo 🕰️

It was the most confident recommendation of its age. The Hindenburg was the safe way to cross the Atlantic — faster than ships, more refined than aeroplanes, with a dining room, a smoking lounge, and a spotless record the Zeppelin company recommended to the world with total sincerity. Tickets were sold on trust: we've flown it for years; it was great for us. And it was — right up until May 6th, 1937, when it burned in Lakehurst, New Jersey, in front of the newsreel cameras, in thirty-four seconds.

What history remembers less: the recommendation had always carried a hidden caveat nobody voiced. The airships had been great — on hydrogen the engineers had never wanted, denied the safe helium they'd designed for, scaled beyond the conditions of their good record. The evidence was smaller than the claim, and the men doing the vouching knew the gap and rounded it off — the way we all do, over coffee, saying "use my guy, he's brilliant."

Your builder's collapse burns slower than a zeppelin, and nobody films it. But the anatomy is identical: an honest record, over-extended past its evidence, recommended without its caveats — and afterwards, the only question that matters for the people who trusted you: does your word show up at the wreck? The Zeppelin company's didn't; it argued causes for decades. Yours can be on the front step by Saturday, with pastries.
