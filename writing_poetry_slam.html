Warm-up — Poetry for the Stage

Up to now we've been writing poetry for the PAGE — poems someone reads silently to themselves. Today we look at poetry written for the STAGE — poems written to be performed aloud in front of a live audience.

The technical name is SLAM POETRY, or SPOKEN WORD. They are closely related: spoken word is the broader category, slam is the competitive performance form within it.

How it started

In 1984, an American construction worker called Marc Smith got tired of poetry readings being dry, polite and elitist. He started running events at a Chicago bar where poets would perform their work in front of an audience — and the audience would score each performance out of 10. The winning poet got a small cash prize. The losers got booed.

That competitive framing transformed poetry. Suddenly the audience mattered. Lines too clever to land aloud lost. Lines that hit the heart won. A new kind of poem evolved — short, personal, rhythmic, repetitive, and direct. Slam.

Why slam matters even if you never enter a competition

Three reasons:

  1. Slam teaches you to write for an audience, not just yourself. Every choice has to land.
  2. Slam is the most accessible form of poetry. No degree needed, no rhyme rules to learn — just the willingness to mean what you say.
  3. The techniques transfer. Direct address, the volta, the closing beat — these tools work in any kind of poem.
Is slam different from "real" poetry?

No. Slam IS real poetry. The split between "page poets" and "stage poets" is artificial — many of the most respected modern poets (Patricia Smith, Saul Williams, Kate Tempest, Kae Tempest, Andrea Gibson) work in both worlds. The form is just optimised for a different reader: a listening human rather than a silent one.

Vocabulary — Eight Slam Tools

These are the eight techniques you'll find in almost every strong slam piece. Tap to reveal each.

Slam
Tap for definition
A poem written to be performed aloud at a poetry-slam competition. Short (2–3 minutes), personal, rhythmic, scored by the audience.
Chicago, 1984 — Marc Smith.
The hook
Tap for definition
The opening line — the line that pulls the audience in and tells them: this poem is worth listening to. Slam openings are short, declarative, and personal.
e.g. "I have my father's hands."
Personal stakes
Tap for definition
The subject of a slam piece. Slam works when the speaker has skin in the game — identity, family, grief, fear, injustice. Detached observation does not score.
e.g. a poem about your actual father, not "fathers in general".
Direct address
Tap for definition
A line where the speaker breaks the fourth wall and speaks directly to the audience. Pulls them into the poem as participants.
e.g. "You — yes, you in the back — have you ever..."
Anaphora
Tap for definition
The same word or phrase repeated at the START of consecutive lines. The rhythmic engine of slam.
e.g. "I do not have his patience. / I do not have his silence. / I do not have his way of..."
Code-switching
Tap for definition
Switching between languages, dialects, or registers within the poem. Lets the speaker bring the texture of their actual voice into the work.
e.g. dropping a regional phrase or another language inside an English poem.
Volta
Tap for definition
The TURN. A single line where the poem pivots — usually marked by "but," "until," "and then," "now," or "here is what I never learned."
A poem without a volta is a list. A poem with one has shape.
Closing beat
Tap for definition
A short final line — two or three words — delivered after a deliberate pause following the main ending. The moment the audience erupts.
e.g. "Still." / "Watch me." / "Soon."

Reading — "Hands"

Read this aloud, slowly, as if to an audience. Notice how each move is doing a different job.

Hands
I have my father's hands.
The same square palms,
the same crooked thumb that he broke playing rugby in '69
and never got fixed.
The same lines. The same map.
But I will tell you what I do NOT have.
I do not have his patience.
I do not have his silence.
I do not have his way of looking at the sea
for an entire afternoon
without saying a single word.
You — yes, you in the back, the one looking at your phone —
have you ever sat with someone you loved in total silence
and felt full?
He used to call me "wee man."
In Coleraine that meant: hurry up.
In Coleraine that meant: I love you.
In Coleraine that meant: never leave.
I learned three things from him.
One: how to whistle.
Two: how to stand in a doorway.
Three: how to leave a room without saying goodbye.
And here is what I never learned —
how to stay.
I have my father's hands.
I am still working out what to do with them.
[ pause ]
Still.

What's in this poem?

The eight slam tools — where they appear
  • Slam form — short, personal, free verse, written for the voice.
  • The hook — "I have my father's hands." Five words. The audience is already in.
  • Personal stakes — the speaker's actual father, actual hands, actual loss. Not "fathers in general."
  • Direct address — "You — yes, you in the back, the one looking at your phone — have you ever..." The audience can answer in their head. They become part of the poem.
  • Anaphora — three lines opening with "I do not have his...". Four lines opening with "In Coleraine that meant...". The rhythmic engine.
  • Code-switching — "wee man" — a regional/dialect phrase that brings the speaker's real voice into the poem.
  • The volta — "And here is what I never learned — / how to stay." The poem pivots from inheritance to loss. Without this turn, the poem is a list of memories. With it, it has shape.
  • The closing beat — "Still." One word, after the pause. The audience expects the poem to be over after "what to do with them." Instead they get one more word — and that one word is the moment the room reacts.

Discussion

  • Why does the poem open with "I have my father's hands"? What does it set up?
  • What changes when the speaker shifts from "I" to "You — yes, you in the back"?
  • The "Coleraine" stanza could have just listed three meanings of "wee man." Why repeat "In Coleraine that meant" three times?
  • What does the volta — "how to stay" — actually mean about the speaker?
  • Why does the closing beat work? What would change if the poem ended on "what to do with them"?

The closing beat — a short final fragment after a deliberate pause. The audience thinks the poem is over after "what to do with them." Then they get one more word — and that's the moment they react.
Bonus — find the code-switching

"He used to call me wee man." The Ulster-Scots / Northern Irish phrase brings a specific dialect — and therefore a specific PERSON — into the poem. Code-switching doesn't require switching to a different language; switching to a different REGISTER or DIALECT works the same way.

Then the poem doubles down by repeating "In Coleraine that meant…" — naming the specific place. This is the texture of the speaker's real voice landing in the poem.

Your Turn — Write a Slam Piece

Pick a PERSON in your life who shaped you. A parent, grandparent, sibling, teacher, friend, ex. Someone whose name you wouldn't have to think twice about. We're going to write a slam piece about them using today's tools.

Writing timer
15 minutes. Don't edit while you write.
15:00
Stanza 1 — The hook
ONE short line. Declarative. Personal. Tells the audience this is a poem about THIS person and nothing else. Something like "I have my mother's tongue" or "My brother taught me how to lie."
Stanza 2 — Anaphora about what you DO have / are / inherited from them
Three or four lines starting with the same phrase. "I do not have their ___" or "I have their ___" or "She gave me ___".
Stanza 3 — Direct address to the audience
Break the fourth wall. Ask the audience a real question they can answer in their head. Make them complicit in what you're saying.
Stanza 4 — Code-switching (or a phrase only they used)
Include a word or phrase from a different language, dialect, or register. Could be a regional saying, a family in-joke, a phrase in another language. Then say what it meant — three different things it meant. Like the Coleraine stanza.
Stanza 5 — The volta
The turn. One or two lines starting with "But here is what...", "And here is what I never...", "Until I learned...", or "Now I know...". This is where the poem pivots.
Stanza 6 — Return + closing beat
Return to the opening line (or echo it). End with one line of normal length, a pause, and then TWO OR THREE WORDS as the closing beat.

Free Write — And Then Perform

Slam pieces are written to be performed. So the final exercise has two parts: write it, then SAY it aloud.

Your prompt
Write a slam piece about something you care about that you've never written about before. Could be a place, a moment, a belief, a regret, an act of resistance, an act of love. About 30–40 lines. Free verse.

Try to use at least FIVE of today's eight tools: hook, personal stakes, direct address, anaphora, code-switching, volta, closing beat.
Free-write timer
20 minutes to write. Then read aloud.
20:00

Now the test — perform it

Stand up. Find a clear bit of floor. Read your poem aloud at a steady performance pace — not too fast, not too slow. Mark on your draft:

  1. Where did you stumble? Those lines are too long or awkwardly broken.
  2. Where did you feel the energy DIP? Those moments need an image, a turn, or a cut.
  3. Where did you feel the energy LAND? Protect those lines — they're working.
  4. Did you hold the pause before the closing beat? Try it again, longer. Count "one Mississippi" in your head.
