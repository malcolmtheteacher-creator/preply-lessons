Does the Method Matter?

Many kinds of therapy help people. Is one better, or is something else more important?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — A strange idea

In 1936, a psychologist called Saul Rosenzweig made a surprising idea. In the story 'Alice in Wonderland', there is a race with a dodo bird. At the end, the dodo says: 'Everybody has won, and all must have prizes.' Rosenzweig said that this is also true for therapy: every kind of therapy wins.

This idea is called the 'Dodo bird verdict'. It says that different therapies, for example talking therapy, behaviour therapy and others, often give a similar result.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where does the name 'Dodo bird' come from?

2. What does the idea say?

1. From the story 'Alice in Wonderland'.

2. That different therapies often give similar results.

Discuss:
  • Do you think this is true? Why?

Part 2 — What the research says

In 1977, two researchers looked at 375 studies. They found that people who had therapy were usually better off than people who did not. In the average case, the client who had therapy was better off than about 75% of people who did not.

Many later studies agreed: most therapies work, and most are about equal. Some therapies are better for some problems. For example, for fears and panic, therapies with exposure and practice often work very well. But for many problems, the differences between therapies are small.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many studies did the 1977 researchers look at?

2. What did they find?

1. 375.

2. People who had therapy were usually better off than people who did not.

Discuss:
  • If most methods give similar results, how should a client choose a therapist?

Part 3 — The common factors

If the method is not the most important thing, what is? Researchers talk about 'common factors', things that all good therapies share. The most studied is the relationship between therapist and client, called the 'alliance'. In more than 300 studies, a good alliance was linked to a better outcome.

Other common factors are: hope, a clear explanation of the problem, and a plan that the client believes in. Also, some therapists get better results than other therapists, even with the same method. Some researchers say this means: train people in the relationship, not only in the method. Other researchers say that the method still matters, too.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the 'alliance'?

2. Name one other common factor.

1. The relationship between the therapist and the client.

2. Hope, a clear explanation, or a plan the client believes in.

Discuss:
  • Is it possible to teach a student to be good at relationships? How?

Grammar — quantifiers

Use quantifiers to say how many or how much. They are very useful for describing results.

With plural nouns (many):
• all / most / many / some / a few / few / none of the studies
• Most therapies work. / Some therapies are better for panic. / A few studies disagree. (a few = some)
• Few studies disagree. (few = almost none)
With uncountable nouns (much):
• a lot of / much / some / a little / little / no research
• There is a lot of research on alliance. / There is little research on this.
• Use of the with a group: most of the clients, some of the studies.

What helps?

Five cases. Say what you think helps the client and why. Then reveal an idea.

1. A client says: 'My therapist listens very well, but I don't think the method is right for me.' What should happen?
Idea. Talk about it. The client's belief in the plan is important. The therapist can explain, or change the plan.
2. Two therapists use the same method. One gets better results. Why?
Idea. Research suggests that things like empathy, flexibility and how well the therapist builds an alliance are important.
3. A client has a very strong fear of flying. Does the method matter more?
Idea. For specific fears, methods with exposure and practice often work well, so the method matters.
4. A client says the therapist does not understand her. What can the therapist do?
Idea. Ask about it. Researchers say that asking for feedback can improve the outcome.
5. Is it enough to teach students only one method?
Idea. Many people say they also need relationship skills and the skill of reading how the client is doing.
