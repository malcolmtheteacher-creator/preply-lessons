Millions of Muslims have done exactly that. They've memorised the entire Qur'an. It takes years of study and practice. Today you'll discover why this is so important to Islamic faith and how it connects to the Qur'an's unique claim to be God's exact words.

The Story of Revelation

Muslims believe the Qur'an is the direct, unchanged word of Allah (God), revealed to Prophet Muhammad. Here's how it happened:

The Timeline of Revelation

610 CE: Muhammad, a merchant in Mecca, Arabia, is meditating in a cave on Mount Hira when Angel Jibril (Gabriel) appears to him. The Angel commands: "Read!" This is the first revelation.

610-632 CE: Over 23 years, Muhammad continues to receive revelations from Allah through Jibril. He recites them to his followers, who memorise them and write them on whatever materials are available (leather, palm leaves, parchment, bone).

632 CE: Muhammad dies. The revelations are complete. There are now thousands of people who have memorised the Qur'an (huffaz, plural of hafiz).

650s CE: Under Caliph Uthman, about 20 years after Muhammad's death, the scattered writings are compiled into one official standard text to prevent differences in how the Qur'an is read.

Key belief: Muslims believe that the Qur'an is not Muhammad's words or interpretation—it is Allah's exact words. Muhammad was the vessel through which God spoke. The Qur'an is eternally preserved, perfect, and unchanged since the time of revelation.

Key Terms You Need to Know

Qur'an: The Islamic holy text, believed by Muslims to be the direct word of Allah (God)
Revelation (Wahy): Allah's communication of divine truth to Muhammad through the Angel Jibril
Jibril (Gabriel): The Angel through whom Allah revealed the Qur'an to Muhammad
Surah: A chapter of the Qur'an. There are 114 surahs
Ayah: A verse of the Qur'an. There are approximately 6,236 ayahs total
Hafiz (pl. Huffaz): A Muslim who has memorised the entire Qur'an word-for-word. It's considered a great honour and achievement
Tajweed: The rules and art of beautiful, correct recitation of the Qur'an. Each letter is pronounced precisely
Arabic: The original language of the Qur'an. Muslims believe the Qur'an can only truly be the Qur'an in Arabic

Structure of the Qur'an

114 Surahs, Arranged Longest to Shortest

The Qur'an is not arranged chronologically (in the order Muhammad received them). Instead, it's arranged roughly from longest chapter to shortest.

Surah 1 (Al-Fatihah, "The Opening"): This is the shortest surah but it's placed first. It's considered the most important. Every Muslim recites it in every prayer five times a day.

Why arranged this way? Muslims believe this arrangement is also part of God's design. It makes the text memorable and balanced.

Why Arabic Matters: A Fundamental Difference

The Qur'an's Relationship to Language

Islamic belief: The Qur'an can ONLY be the Qur'an in Arabic. The words, the rhythm, the meaning—all are divinely preserved in Arabic. Translations into English, French, Urdu, etc. are considered interpretations, not the true Qur'an.

Why? Muslims say that the Qur'an's beauty, precision, and meaning are inseparable from its Arabic. The language itself is seen as miraculous. No translation can capture this.

Consequence: Millions of Muslims worldwide speak Arabic poorly or not at all. But they still learn to recite the Qur'an in Arabic. Many memorise it without fully understanding every word—the sound, the recitation (tajweed), is part of the reverence.

How This Differs from the Bible:

The Christian Bible was written in Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek. But Christianity sees no problem with translations. In fact, most Christians read the Bible in their own language (English, Spanish, Chinese, etc.). The Bible's authority doesn't depend on the original language—it depends on the content.

Islam is different. The Qur'an's authority is inseparable from its Arabic. This is a fundamental theological claim.

How the Qur'an Is Treated: Physical Respect

Placed Highest

The Qur'an is always placed on the highest shelf in a home. Never placed on the floor or below other books.

Wrapped and Protected

Often wrapped in cloth to protect it from dirt. A copy in a mosque is treated with ceremonial care.

Wudu (Ablution) Before Touching

Many Muslims perform ritual washing (wudu) before touching the Qur'an. Hands are purified before contact with the holy text.

Never Disrespected

Never sat upon, written in, or treated carelessly. Insulting or desecrating the Qur'an is considered a grave sin and causes serious offence in Muslim communities.

Beautiful Calligraphy

Copies of the Qur'an are often illuminated with beautiful Arabic calligraphy and art. It's treated as a precious object.

Recitation as Worship

The Qur'an is meant to be recited aloud and heard. Even non-Arabic speakers often listen to recordings of Qur'an recitation as a form of worship.

How the Qur'an Is Used in Islamic Life

Recited in Every Prayer (Salah)

Muslims pray five times a day. Each prayer includes recitation of Qur'anic verses, starting with Surah Al-Fatihah. Over a Muslim's lifetime, the Qur'an is recited thousands of times.

Memorised (Hafiz)

Some Muslims dedicate years to memorising the entire Qur'an. They become hafiz, which is a tremendous achievement. Some Muslims do this while also going to school or work. Many hafiz can recite the Qur'an from memory in prayer and teach others.

Studied in Madrasah

Islamic schools (madrasah) teach the Qur'an intensively. Students learn Arabic, the meaning of passages, how to recite beautifully (tajweed), and Islamic law derived from the Qur'an.

Source of Shariah (Law)

Islamic law is based primarily on the Qur'an. Muslim scholars interpret the Qur'an to understand how to live, what's permitted (halal) and forbidden (haram), and how society should be organized.

Recited at Births, Deaths, and Weddings

Major life events include Qur'an recitation. At a birth, the Adhan (call to prayer) including Qur'anic phrases is whispered in the baby's ear. At death, the Qur'an is recited. At weddings, the couple listens to the Qur'an.

Listened To for Spiritual Healing

Many Muslims listen to recordings of the Qur'an being beautifully recited. This is seen as a form of spiritual comfort and connection to God.

Amazing fact: Over 10 million people alive today have memorised the entire Qur'an. That's 6,236+ verses, over 77,000 words, in Arabic. No other book comes close to this level of memorisation. This is unique to Islam.

Activity 2: Surah Al-Fatihah (The Opening)

Below is the first and most important surah of the Qur'an in Arabic (transliterated) and English translation. This is recited in every Muslim prayer.

Surah Al-Fatihah (1:1-7)

Bismillahir rahmanir rahim
In the name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful

Alhamdulillahi rabbi l-alamin
Praise be to Allah, Lord of all the worlds

Ar-Rahmanir rahim
The Most Gracious, the Most Merciful

Maliki yawmi d-din
Master of the Day of Judgment

Iyyaka na'budu wa iyyaka nasta'in
You alone do we worship, and You alone do we ask for help

Ihdina s-sirata l-mustaqim
Guide us on the straight path

Sirata l-ladhina an'amta 'alayhim ghayri l-magdhubi 'alayhim wa la d-dallin
The path of those upon whom You have bestowed favor, not of those who have earned Your anger, nor of those who go astray

Questions About Surah Al-Fatihah:

Q1: What do the opening words tell you about Muslim beliefs?

The surah begins by invoking Allah's mercy and grace. This shows that Muslims see God as both powerful and merciful. Every prayer begins by remembering these attributes of God.

Q2: What is the central request in this surah?

"Guide us on the straight path." The surah is about asking God for guidance in life. Muslims ask Allah to help them stay on the right path and avoid going astray.

Q3: Why do you think this surah is recited five times a day in prayer?

This surah contains the core of Islamic belief: praise of God, recognition of God's power, and a request for guidance. Reciting it five times a day keeps these values at the centre of a Muslim's life.

Discussion Questions

Pick at least 2 and write 3-4 sentences for each:

Question 1: Why might memorising the entire Qur'an be so important to Muslims?

Think about what memorisation means: the text lives inside you. You become part of preserving it. You live it every day. What does that suggest about the relationship between a Muslim and their sacred text?

Question 2: If a sacred text can only truly exist in one language, what does that mean for non-Arabic speakers?

Billions of Muslims speak Urdu, Bengali, Malay, Indonesian, Swahili, etc. They may not understand Arabic well. What's gained and lost by insisting the Qur'an must be in Arabic?

Question 3: Is a text more sacred if it has ONE author or MANY authors?

Muslims say the Qur'an has one author: Allah. Christians say the Bible has many human authors but one divine inspiration. Which claim makes a text more sacred? Does it matter?

Question 4: A Muslim and a Christian both say their text is "the word of God." Do they mean the same thing?

For Muslims: Allah's literal words, direct, unchanged. For Christians: God-inspired but human, interpreted, evolving. Are they talking about the same concept?

The Big Question

"The Qur'an's claim to be perfectly preserved makes it more authoritative than the Bible."

Decide: Do you agree or disagree? Then explore both sides.

Arguments FOR (Why someone might agree):

  • If a text is God's direct words, preserved perfectly unchanged since revelation, that's a stronger claim than "divinely inspired but written by humans."
  • The Qur'an has been memorised by millions, creating multiple backups. If every written copy were lost, hafiz could reconstruct it perfectly. The Bible can't claim that.
  • Muslims have consistent belief in the Qur'an across all traditions (Sunni, Shi'a, Sufi, etc.). The same text. The Bible has multiple translations and interpretations.
  • The Qur'an's arrangement, language, and content are unchangeable. This gives it absolute authority in Islam.

Arguments AGAINST (Why someone might disagree):

  • "Perfectly preserved" is a claim, not a fact. Both the Bible and Qur'an have been copied by hand for centuries. Small variations exist in both (though minimal).
  • For Christians, the claim isn't that the words are perfect—it's that the message is divinely inspired. Maybe that's a stronger claim in a different way.
  • The Qur'an's authority comes from Muslims believing in it. The Bible's authority comes from Christians believing in it. Authority doesn't depend on preservation—it depends on faith.
  • Millions of Christians also take the Bible as absolute, unquestionable authority. The difference isn't really about preservation—it's about interpretation.
Your turn: Which side is more convincing to you? Write 3-4 sentences explaining your view.
