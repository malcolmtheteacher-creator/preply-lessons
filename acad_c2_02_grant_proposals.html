What Just Happened?

You've identified that grant writing is not about research design—it's about reviewer confidence, risk assessment, and funding agency priorities. The best research doesn't win grants. The clearest, most credible pitch to the right funder wins.

In this lesson, you'll learn to architect a proposal that moves reviewers from skeptical to convinced. You'll learn what each section actually needs to do.

Your Moves

These are the strategic language moves that distinguish funded proposals from rejected ones.

1 The Need Statement +

You don't describe the problem. You build a case that the problem is urgent, specific, and solvable. You make the funder feel that not funding you leaves a real gap.

When to use it:

In the first section of every proposal. When you're justifying why this research matters now.

Weak need statement

"Climate change is a major global challenge affecting millions of people."

Strong need statement

"Current climate adaptation models fail to account for rapid rural migration in Southeast Asia. The gap leaves 40 million small-scale farmers without actionable guidance. This 3-year study will provide location-specific protocols that [funder name] has signaled as a priority."

2 Objectives That Reviewers Believe +

You state objectives that are measurable, achievable in the timeframe, and directly connected to the need. Reviewers read objectives wondering: "Can they actually do this?"

When to use it:

When writing your specific aims or objectives section. When you're framing what success looks like.

Vague objective

"To understand the impact of X on Y across different contexts."

Credible objective

"To identify which of five adaptation strategies increases yield sustainability in regions experiencing both drought and flooding (Objective 1), and to develop region-specific implementation guides (Objective 2) that can be deployed by existing extension networks."

3 Methodology Under Scrutiny +

You choose methods not because they're theoretically elegant, but because they're the most efficient and credible path to answering the question. You anticipate the reviewer asking: "Why this method, not that one?"

When to use it:

In the methodology section. When you're choosing between approaches.

Justification that doesn't convince

"We will use qualitative interviews because they provide rich data."

Justification that builds confidence

"We initially considered RCTs but discovered no existing variation in X to exploit. We chose quasi-experimental design with matched comparison groups because: (1) we can use existing administrative data (reducing cost), (2) our preliminary work identified 12 matched sites, and (3) this design gives reviewers what they need—comparison groups and baseline data."

4 Demonstrating Feasibility +

Feasibility is everything. You show: you've done this before (or similar), you have partnerships locked in, you have access, your timeline is realistic, and you have a team that can execute.

When to use it:

In the methodology and team sections. When reviewers are assessing risk.

Feasibility move

"Our team has completed two prior studies in this region (Year 1, Year 2). We have signed MOUs from the Ministry of Agriculture and five community partners. Our database already contains baseline data from 8,000 farmers. Timeline: months 1-4 will refine instruments; months 5-30 will conduct field work; months 31-36 will analyze and disseminate."

5 Impact and Dissemination +

You separate intellectual impact (publications, theory advancement) from practical impact (what will change, who will use this). You name specific audiences and mechanisms.

When to use it:

In dedicated impact sections. When you're explaining why this matters beyond academia.

Weak impact framing

"Results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications and conferences."

Strong impact framing

"Intellectual: 8-10 publications in high-impact journals; advancement of X theory. Practical: findings will be embedded in the Ministry's new extension curriculum (reaching 50,000 farmers/year). Policy: briefing for the National Planning Commission will inform climate adaptation funding allocations."

6 Budget Justification +

Every line item tells a story. You don't just list costs—you explain why each cost is necessary and cost-effective. You show you're not asking for anything extraneous.

When to use it:

In budget narrative sections. When justifying any significant expense.

Budget move

"RAs (2 FTE): $45k/year. We budget for university-scale salary + benefits because research-quality data requires trained data collectors. These two RAs will manage field operations across 12 sites, ensuring quality control. We could not achieve objectives 1 and 2 without on-site presence."

Your Situation

You're pitching a 3-year research project to a competitive funding committee. You have 5 minutes for your verbal pitch. The committee will then ask questions. Your proposal is strong on intellectual merit but the committee wants to understand feasibility and impact. You know they fund 12 projects per cycle out of 200+ applications.

Your Task: Prepare Your Pitch

You have 4 minutes to prepare. Think through:

  • How do you open in a way that makes them feel the urgency?
  • What's the ONE sentence that summarizes what you'll discover?
  • Why should they believe you can actually do this in 3 years?
  • What's the biggest risk, and how are you mitigating it?
4:00

Now Speak: Your 5-Minute Pitch

Deliver your pitch to the committee. Your goal: they leave thinking "this person has thought deeply about this problem, can actually execute it, and will deliver something we care about."

What Your Pitch Should Show

Clear need Do they understand why this matters right now?
Realistic scope Does your project fit in 3 years, or does it feel overambitious?
Credibility signals Have you done related work? Do you have access? Partnerships?
Specific outcomes Can they see what success looks like?

Testing Your Moves

Each challenge puts you in a specific funder-interaction. You'll have 45-90 seconds to respond. Press the button below to reveal challenges one at a time.

Challenge 1: The Feasibility Question

A panelist asks: "Your project touches three countries, uses mixed methods, and involves 15 partner organizations. How will you manage quality control across such a complex operation in three years?"

You have 90 seconds: Answer this directly. Show them you've thought about this—it's the main risk.

1:30

Challenge 2: The Impact Pushback

Panelist: "You talk about publications and journal articles. That's standard academic output. What makes you think policy makers will actually care about this research?"

You have 60 seconds: You need to show real impact pathways, not generic dissemination plans.

1:00

Challenge 3: The Budget Challenge

Panelist: "Your budget is 15% higher than the average proposal for this program. Can you justify the additional cost?"

You have 60 seconds: Don't apologize. Explain what you get for that cost that others don't.

1:00

Challenge 4: The Scope Creep Question

Panelist: "Your methodology section mentions several exploratory analyses beyond your main objectives. How will you stay focused?"

You have 60 seconds: Reassure them that you understand the risk and have a strategy.

1:00

Challenge 5: The Team Capacity Question

Panelist: "I see you're director on three other funded projects right now. Why should we believe you'll prioritize this one?"

You have 60 seconds: This is about trust and management structure. Use it to show how you've planned for leadership.
