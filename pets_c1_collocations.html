Real Stories

Dr. Frans de Waal, primatologist: "The question isn't whether animals have emotions - the evidence is overwhelming that they do. The question is why we spent so long denying it."

Think about it...

We call them "fur babies." We throw them birthday parties. We feel guilty leaving them alone. We spend more on their food than our own. Have we gone too far - or have we finally got it right?

Collocations are words that naturally go together. Native speakers don't say "make a deep bond" - they say "form a deep bond." Today we'll look at 5 collocations for discussing the complex world of pets and animal relationships.

1. form a deep bond (with)

▼
Meaning
To develop a strong emotional connection with someone or something over time. Implies genuine attachment, not superficial liking.
Form
form (not make/create/build) + a deep bond + with + person/animal

Also: form a close bond, form an unbreakable bond, form a strong bond
"Children who grow up with dogs often form deep bonds with them that last into adulthood."
"It took months, but eventually the rescue cat formed a bond with its new owner."
Register Note
Neutral to formal. Common in psychology, animal behavior discussions, and emotional conversations. Avoids the casual "get close to."

Now You Try

Tell me about a bond you've formed - with a pet, a person, or even a place. What made it "deep"?

2. meet someone's needs

▼
Meaning
To provide what someone requires - physically, emotionally, or otherwise. Often used when discussing whether something/someone is adequate or sufficient.
Form
meet (not satisfy/fulfill in casual contexts) + someone's needs

Also: meet the needs of, fail to meet needs, struggle to meet needs
"Can a studio apartment really meet the needs of a large breed dog?"
"Many first-time owners don't realize how difficult it is to meet a parrot's social needs."
Register Note
Slightly formal. Very common in welfare discussions, healthcare, education, and animal rights contexts. More objective than "make someone happy."

Now You Try

Think of an animal (exotic pet, zoo animal, farm animal). Can humans realistically meet its needs in captivity? Why or why not?

3. exhibit signs of

▼
Meaning
To show evidence or symptoms of something (usually a condition, emotion, or state). More observational and analytical than "show."
Form
exhibit + signs of + noun (stress/anxiety/intelligence/affection)

Also: exhibit symptoms of, exhibit behavior, exhibit characteristics of
"Dogs left alone for 8+ hours often exhibit signs of separation anxiety."
"Elephants in captivity frequently exhibit signs of psychological distress."
Register Note
Formal/academic. Common in scientific writing, medical contexts, and analytical discussion. Sounds more authoritative than "show signs of."

Now You Try

Have you ever noticed an animal exhibiting signs of a human-like emotion? What did you see, and what did you conclude?

4. blur the line between

▼
Meaning
To make the distinction between two things unclear or less defined. Often used when discussing categories that were once separate but are now mixing.
Form
blur the line (not "the border" or "the edge") + between + X and Y

Also: the line becomes blurred, where do you draw the line?, cross the line
"Pet insurance, pet therapists, pet nutritionists - we've really blurred the line between pet and child."
"Social media has blurred the line between private pet ownership and public performance."
Register Note
Neutral. Very useful for analytical discussion and debate. Common in essays, opinion pieces, and thoughtful conversation about social trends.

Now You Try

What's something in modern life where you think we've blurred the line too much? (Could be pets, work/life, online/offline, etc.)

5. weigh up the implications

▼
Meaning
To carefully consider the possible consequences or effects of something before making a decision. Implies thoughtful, balanced analysis.
Form
weigh up (British) / weigh (American) + the implications (of something)

Also: weigh up the pros and cons, weigh up the options, weigh up the risks
"Before buying an exotic pet, people should weigh up the implications for the animal's welfare."
"We need to weigh up the implications of treating animals as commodities."
Register Note
Formal. Excellent for ethical discussions, business decisions, and academic debate. Shows you're thinking critically rather than emotionally.

Now You Try

What's a decision you made recently where you had to weigh up the implications carefully? What factors did you consider?

Quick Review: 5 Collocations

  1. form a deep bond with - develop strong emotional connection
  2. meet someone's needs - provide what's required
  3. exhibit signs of - show evidence/symptoms of
  4. blur the line between - make distinction unclear
  5. weigh up the implications - consider consequences carefully

Discussion Time

Choose 2-3 topics that interest you. Your teacher will ask follow-up questions!

1
Can humans truly form a deep bond with an animal, or are we just projecting human emotions onto them?
Think about: What evidence would prove it's a "real" bond?
2
A city apartment, a busy owner who works 10-hour days... Can this lifestyle ever truly meet a dog's needs?
Think about: Physical needs vs. emotional needs
3
When a dog exhibits signs of joy seeing its owner, is it love - or just conditioned behavior for food and shelter?
Think about: Does the distinction matter?
4
Pet spas. Pet psychologists. Pet birthday parties. Have we blurred the line between pet and child too much?
Think about: Is this harmless fun or something more concerning?
5
If we weigh up the implications of keeping animals as pets - the breeding industry, the carbon footprint, the welfare issues - is pet ownership actually ethical?
Think about: Playing devil's advocate
6
"Pet influencers" earn millions. Their animals live in luxury but perform constantly. Is this exploitation, or are these the luckiest pets alive?
Think about: Can an animal consent to being "famous"?
