The Great Wall of China

Not one wall but many — built, lost and rebuilt across two thousand years.

Today: you'll tell the story of how the Great Wall was built and rebuilt over centuries, separate the facts from the famous myths, and discuss what walls are really for — using the present perfect with for and since to say how long the Wall has stood, alongside the language of building and defence.

Key words — guess first, then sort

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. After the earthquake, the old harbour town was slowly rebuilt, stone by stone, much as it had stood for centuries.
to rebuild (v) — to build something again, often after it has fallen into ruin or been destroyed.
2. Hundreds of thousands of labourers — peasants, soldiers and prisoners — were sent north to carry stone and pile up earth.
a labourer (n) — a worker who does hard physical work, usually with their hands.
3. The villagers tipped damp soil between two wooden boards and pounded it down again and again, until the rammed earth stood as solid as a low stone wall.
rammed earth (n) — a building material made by pressing layers of damp soil tightly together until they harden.
4. Behind its thick stone fortification, the little town felt safe; raiders who reached the gates found the high walls too costly to storm.
a fortification (n) — a wall, tower or other structure built to defend a place against attack.
5. The nomads of the great grasslands followed their herds across open country, carrying their homes with them rather than settling in one place.
a nomad (n) — a member of a people who move from place to place rather than living in one settled home. (adj: nomadic)
6. From the watchtowers along the top, soldiers could see for miles and warn the next post of any movement on the horizon.
a watchtower (n) — a tall tower from which guards keep watch over the surrounding land.
7. On the night the ships were sighted, a beacon blazed on the headland, its flames warning the whole coast of danger.
a beacon (n) — a fire or light set on a high place as a signal, often to warn of danger.
8. Past the last farm the road petered out and the wild frontier began, where the maps grew vague and few travellers dared to go.
a frontier (n) — the edge of a country or settled area, especially where it meets a wilder or rival region.
9. Everyone in the village swore the old castle was haunted, yet when a curious traveller looked into it, the whole tale turned out to be a myth.
a myth (n) — a widely believed story or idea that is not actually true. (adj: mythical)
10. Grandmother kept the old recipes, the songs and the carved chest as part of the family's heritage, and now my children will guard them too.
heritage (n) — the buildings, traditions and history passed down from the past that a people value and protect.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The first emperor's wall

The first thing to understand about the Great Wall is that there was never just one. For centuries before it became famous, rival Chinese states had thrown up walls of their own along their borders, mostly long ridges of rammed earth meant to slow a raid. Then, around 220 BC, the man who had conquered them all — Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor of a unified China — ordered these scattered defences to be joined into a single line facing north.

The purpose was clear enough. Beyond the frontier lay the steppe, and on it the nomads: fast, mounted peoples whose raids the settled farms of the empire could never quite predict. A continuous wall, manned by soldiers, was meant to slow them down and give the empire warning. But the human cost of building it at such speed was enormous. Hundreds of thousands of labourers — conscripted peasants, soldiers and convicts — were marched north to dig, carry and build, and many never came home.

That suffering left its mark on the country's memory. One of China's most enduring folk tales tells of Meng Jiangnü, a young wife whose husband is taken away to work on the wall and dies there; her weeping, the legend says, brings a whole section crashing down. It is a story, not a record — but it captures something true about how the wall was remembered: as a wonder, and as a grief.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is it misleading to talk about "the" Great Wall as a single thing built at one time?

2. What did Qin Shi Huang actually do around 220 BC, and why?

1. Many separate walls were built over a very long period; earlier states had built their own border walls, and what we call "the" wall is really many walls from different times joined and rebuilt.

2. He ordered the older, scattered walls to be connected into one continuous northern line, to slow and warn against raids by the nomads of the steppe — built at huge human cost.

Discuss:
  • The wall is remembered both as a wonder and as a place of suffering. Can a thing be magnificent and terrible at the same time — and does knowing its cost change how we should see it?

Part 2 — Many walls, many dynasties

Qin Shi Huang's wall did not last, and neither did the dynasties that followed. Over the next eighteen centuries the wall was abandoned, repaired, moved and rebuilt many times, by emperors who each had their own enemies in the north and their own ideas about where the line should run. The wall we picture today — the broad, grey ribbon of brick and stone winding over green hills — is almost all the work of one late dynasty, the Ming, who rebuilt it between the fourteenth and seventeenth centuries.

The Ming were rebuilding for a reason: not long before, China had been conquered and ruled by the Mongols, and the new dynasty was determined never to let it happen again. So they built in earnest. Their wall was faced in brick and cut stone rather than bare earth, and it carried a whole defensive system on its back — watchtowers set within sight of one another, garrison forts, and fortified gates controlling who and what could pass.

That last point matters more than the fighting. For much of its life the wall was less a battlefield than a border post. It marked the frontier, regulated trade along the routes that crossed it, and let officials tax the goods and watch the people moving between the empire and the steppe. And when danger did appear, the wall could speak: a chain of beacons carried fire by night and smoke by day, passing a warning from tower to tower far faster than any rider.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which dynasty built most of the famous brick-and-stone wall we see today, and what had frightened them into doing it?

2. Apart from fighting, name two everyday jobs the wall did along the frontier.

1. The Ming dynasty (14th–17th centuries) built most of the surviving brick-and-stone wall; they had just been ruled by the Mongols and were determined to keep the northern peoples out.

2. Any two of: marking and guarding the frontier; controlling and taxing trade; watching and controlling the movement of people; signalling warnings by beacon along the line.

Discuss:
  • The wall was as much a customs post and signalling line as a battlement. Does that make it more or less impressive than the "giant fortress" we usually imagine?

Part 3 — The wall and its myths

No structure on earth has gathered more myths than the Great Wall, and two are worth correcting because almost everyone believes them. The first is that the wall is the only human-made object visible from space with the naked eye. It is not. The wall is enormously long, but only a few metres wide and much the same colour as the land around it; astronauts from many missions, including China's own first man in space, have reported that they simply could not pick it out. Length is not the same as visibility.

The second myth is darker: the idea that the bones of dead workers were used as building material, mixed into the wall itself. This too is false. There is no archaeological evidence of bodies inside the wall, and no bone has ever been found built into it. The grim truth is sadder and simpler — many labourers did die during construction, and some were buried in the ground nearby, which is almost certainly how the legend began.

Strip the myths away and what remains is astonishing enough. Counting every section ever built and rebuilt, the walls run for well over twenty thousand kilometres. Most stretches are now crumbling, unguarded and slowly returning to the hills; a few restored sections are visited by millions of people every year. The wall that was built to keep the world out has ended up drawing the world in.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why can't the wall actually be seen from space with the naked eye?

2. Where does the "bodies in the wall" story come from, and what is the real explanation?

1. It is very long but only a few metres wide and a similar colour to the surrounding land, so it can't be picked out by eye; astronauts (including China's first) have confirmed they couldn't see it.

2. No bodies or bones have been found inside the wall — it's a myth. Many workers did die and some were buried in the ground nearby, which is most likely how the legend started.

Discuss:
  • The "visible from space" and "bodies in the wall" stories are repeated everywhere, yet both are false. Why do you think some myths stick so firmly — and is it really worth the effort to correct them?

The grammar this story runs on: how long? Present perfect with for / since

The whole story is really about duration — something that began long ago and is still true now. "The Wall has stood for more than two thousand years." "It has protected China's frontier since the third century BC." When a situation starts in the past and continues up to the present moment, English reaches for the present perfect — and the Wall is the perfect subject for it.

How it works: the present perfect is has / have + past participle (has stood, have lasted, has been rebuilt). We use it for something that began in the past and is still going on now:
• The Wall has stood for more than two thousand years. (it began long ago — and it's still standing today)
FOR + a length of time (how long the period is): for two thousand years, for centuries, for a long time.
SINCE + a starting point (when it began): since the third century BC, since the Ming dynasty, since 1987.
To ask: How long has the Wall stood? — "How long…?" + present perfect.
Present perfect passive (has / have been + past participle) — when the focus is on the Wall, not the builders: Much of it has been rebuilt many times. · Several sections have been restored for tourists.

1. For or since?

Fill each gap with for (a length of time) or since (a starting point). Read each one aloud.

1. The Wall has stood more than two thousand years.

2. It has guarded the northern frontier the third century BC.

3. The Ming watchtowers have looked out over the hills the fourteenth century.

4. Tourists have visited the restored sections decades.

1. for (a length of time — "two thousand years")

2. since (a starting point — "the third century BC")

3. since (a starting point — "the fourteenth century")

4. for (a length of time — "decades")

2. Put the verb into the present perfect

Use has / have + past participle to show the situation has continued up to now. Keep the first words to start you off.

1. (stand) The Wall on these hills for over two thousand years.

2. (exist) Walls along this frontier since long before the first emperor.

3. (draw) In recent times, the Wall millions of visitors a year.

1. has stood

2. have existed

3. has drawn

3. Present perfect passive — focus on the Wall

The Wall stays the subject; the builders disappear. Put each verb into has / have been + past participle.

1. (rebuild) Much of the Wall many times over the centuries.

2. (restore) A few sections for the millions who visit.

3. (abandon) Long stretches and left to crumble back into the hills.

1. has been rebuilt

2. have been restored

3. have been abandoned

4. Ask "How long…?"

Write the question, then answer it with for or since. Try them aloud as a mini-dialogue.

2. (this section / be restored) ? — It since the 1980s.
