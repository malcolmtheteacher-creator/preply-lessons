A Question to Hold

Who resists? When a powerful empire arrives with legions and conquest in mind, not all people surrender. This lesson shows us three great resisters: Caractacus and his tribes, fighting for independence; Boadicea, rising in fury after Roman cruelty; and the spirit of Britain itself, which Rome wanted to rule but never fully to break.

Marshall tells their stories with sympathy and drama. But each resister has a different fate — and that difference teaches us something about power, hope, and how nations remember their heroes.

II. Caesar's Expeditions (55–54 BC)

Julius Caesar was fighting in Gaul (modern France) when he first heard of the island across the sea.

Julius Cæsar had been fighting in Gaul, or France as we now call it. While there, he heard of the little island with white cliffs over the sea. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter II

In the summer of 55 BC, Caesar crossed the Channel with his legions. Marshall captures the drama of that moment:

After sailing along the coast, Caesar found a good place to land and turned his vessels inshore. But the great galleys required so much water that they could not come quite close to land. Seeing this, Caesar told his soldiers to jump into the water. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter II

The soldiers hesitated. The Britons had rushed to meet them on the shore.

The soldiers hesitated, as the Britons had rushed into the water to meet them and the Romans did not like fighting in the sea. The fierce Britons, urging their horses into the waves, greeted the enemy with loud shouts. Every inch of the shore was known to them, and they galloped through the water without fear. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter II

But Caesar's soldiers were disciplined. They formed ranks in the water and pushed the Britons back. The first landing was a Roman success — but it would not lead to permanent conquest. Caesar fought for a few weeks, then sailed home to Gaul. He returned the following year (54 BC) for a larger expedition, but again, he eventually withdrew.

The historian's note: Caesar's expeditions were brief raids rather than conquest. He was fighting in Gaul and wanted to prevent British tribes from aiding the Gallic resistance. The Britons' fierce defence impressed even the Roman writer Tacitus. But Britain wasn't worth the Roman effort—not yet. It would take nearly a century more, and a different emperor, to change that calculation.

III. Claudius, Caractacus & Boadicea (43–61 AD)

The Real Conquest Arrives: Claudius, 43 AD

For nearly a century after Caesar, Rome left Britain alone. But in 43 AD, the Emperor Claudius decided to do what Caesar could not. He sent an army under the general Aulus Plautius, and this time, the Romans came to stay.

Marshall's account emphasizes the shock of the invasion:

And so the Romans came to Britain and conquered it. The Britons fought hard, but the Romans, with their discipline and their wonderful armour, were more than a match for the wild tribes. One by one the British tribes were conquered, and the Romans made roads and built towns. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter III

Caractacus: Defiance Before the Senate

For years, a British king named Caractacus resisted the Roman advance. He fought from the hills and forests of Wales, refusing to accept Roman rule. When he was finally captured and brought before the Emperor Claudius in Rome itself, he made one of history's great speeches of defiance.

I am as nobly born as you, he said proudly to Claudius. I had men and horses, lands and great riches. Was it wonderful that I wished to keep them? You fight to gain possession of the whole world and make all men your slaves, but I fought for my own land and for freedom. Kill me now and people will think little of you: but if you grant me my life, all men will know that you are not only powerful but merciful. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter III

Claudius was moved. He pardoned Caractacus, who lived out his days in Rome. Marshall thus ends the Caractacus story with mercy rather than tragedy.

The historian's note: Caractacus (Caratacus in Welsh history) genuinely did resist Rome for nearly a decade, and his speech to Claudius is recorded by the Roman historian Tacitus in almost exactly the words Marshall uses. However, the text is fragmentary, and we cannot know precisely what was said—only that it impressed the Romans enough to spare his life. Caractacus was indeed pardoned and lived in Rome, though some sources suggest he may have plotted escape. His fame lay in his resistance, not his submission.

Boadicea's Rebellion: The Queen's Revenge (61 AD)

Some eighteen years later, a new queen rose against Rome. Her name was Boadicea, and she had been treated with terrible cruelty by the Romans. Her tribe, the Iceni, had been allies of Rome—but Rome betrayed them.

Marshall tells it with passionate intensity:

After Boadicea had been so cruelly and unjustly treated, she burned with anger against the Romans. She stirred up the people and called them to war. Thousands of men flocked to her banner. They were mad with rage and longing for revenge. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter V

Boadicea led them southward, burning the Roman settlements as she went. She utterly destroyed the towns of London (Londinium) and Verulamium (St. Albans). Marshall captures her famous words to her troops:

It is better to die with honour than to live in slavery. I am a woman, but I would rather die than yield. Will you follow me, men? — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter V

Boadicea's rebellion seemed unstoppable. But the Roman governor, Suetonius Paulinus, gathered his forces and met her in battle. The Romans, despite being vastly outnumbered, formed a tight formation and fought with disciplined ferocity. The Britons, fighting in a less organized way, were defeated.

Boadicea was captured. Marshall treats her end with dignity:

Yes, dear one, said Boadicea gently. I too will drink, and we shall meet again. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter V

(She took her own life rather than face Roman captivity.)

The historian's note: Boadicea (or Boudicca) is now one of Britain's most celebrated resisters, but we know her almost entirely through Roman accounts. The British left no written records of her. What we know comes from Tacitus and Cassius Dio, writing decades or centuries after events. Marshall's account follows the classical sources closely. The rebellion was real, the destruction was real, and the Romans won a decisive victory—but the woman herself remains partly legend, transmitted through her enemies' eyes.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on. There are no "right answers" — only your own thinking.

Was Caesar afraid?

Marshall says the Roman soldiers "hesitated" before jumping into the water to meet the Britons. What does that moment tell us about the power of defenders fighting on their own land?

Caractacus's mercy

Caractacus appealed to Claudius's mercy. Was this wisdom or defeat? How do you use words when you've lost a war?

Why Boadicea matters

Boadicea lost. But Britain remembers her as a hero. Why do we celebrate someone who fought for a losing cause?

The gap between empires

Caesar couldn't conquer Britain. A century later, Claudius did. What changed? Power? Organization? British unity collapsing? All of the above?

Marshall's sympathy

Marshall writes about British resistance with clear sympathy and drama. But she's writing in 1905, when Britain itself is an empire. Did she see herself in Caractacus and Boadicea?

The real and the told

What we know about Boadicea comes only from Roman writers. We have no British account of her own words. How does that change what we can claim to know?

2. Three Resisters

Name the three great figures who fought against Roman power, and describe what made each one significant:

Julius Caesar — crossed the Channel twice (55, 54 BC) but did not conquer. The Britons' fierce defence impressed the Romans but also held them back.

Caractacus — fought Roman expansion from the Welsh hills for nearly a decade. Captured, he appealed to Claudius's mercy and was spared. His defiance became legendary.

Boadicea — queen of the Iceni, she led a massive rebellion in 61 AD after Roman cruelty. She burned Londinium and Verulamium but was defeated by Suetonius Paulinus. Rather than be captured, she died by her own hand.
