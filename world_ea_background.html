East Asia: Three Countries, Three Cultures

People say “Asian culture” as if it were one thing. East Asia alone holds very different languages, writing systems and governments.

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The Tang dynasty ruled China for almost three centuries.
dynasty (n) — a series of rulers from the same family. Example: “The Tang dynasty ruled China for almost three centuries.”
2. Japanese is written with more than one script.
script (n) — a system of written symbols. Example: “Japanese is written with more than one script.”
3. Mandarin is the official language of mainland China and Taiwan.
Mandarin (n) — the most widely spoken form of Chinese. Example: “Mandarin is the official language of mainland China and Taiwan.”
4. Korea sits on a peninsula between China and Japan.
peninsula (n) — a long piece of land almost surrounded by water. Example: “Korea sits on a peninsula between China and Japan.”
5. Ideas from Confucianism spread across the region over many centuries.
Confucianism (n) — a system of ideas about family, duty and respect, from the thinker Confucius. Example: “Ideas from Confucianism spread across the region over many centuries.”
6. Japan is an archipelago of about 14,000 islands.
archipelago (n) — a group of many islands. Example: “Japan is an archipelago of about 14,000 islands.”
7. People in different parts of China speak different dialects, some so different that speakers cannot understand each other.
dialect (n) — a form of a language spoken in one area. Example: “People in different parts of China speak different dialects, some so different that speakers cannot understand each other.”
8. Old tensions still affect how the three countries see each other.
tension (n) — a feeling of worry and unfriendliness between groups. Example: “Old tensions still affect how the three countries see each other.”

The stereotype: “China, Japan and Korea are basically the same culture.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A big region, many places

East Asia usually means China, Japan, South Korea, North Korea, Mongolia and Taiwan, plus the Chinese regions of Hong Kong and Macau. About 1.6 billion people live there — roughly one person in five on Earth. China alone has more than 1.4 billion, Japan about 124 million and South Korea about 51 million. The land is just as varied: Japan is an archipelago, Korea is a peninsula, and China is a country the size of a continent, with deserts, mountains and some of the biggest cities in the world.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. About what share of the world's population lives in East Asia?

2. What kind of geography does Japan have?

1. About one person in five.

2. It is an archipelago — a group of islands.

Discuss:
  • Which of these places could you point to on a map without looking?

Part 2 — Shared roots

For many centuries China was the region's cultural centre. Chinese characters, Buddhism and Confucian ideas about family and duty travelled to Korea and Japan, along with tea, paper and styles of building. But each country changed what it received. Korea created its own alphabet, Hangul, in the 1440s. Japan developed kana to write Japanese alongside Chinese characters. The result is three writing systems that look nothing alike.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which country created Hangul?

2. What did Japan develop to write Japanese alongside Chinese characters?

1. Korea.

2. Kana.

Discuss:
  • Why might a country change something it borrows instead of copying it exactly?

Part 3 — Very different today

Today the differences are easy to see. Chinese, Japanese and Korean sound very different, and inside China, Mandarin and Cantonese are so far apart that speakers cannot understand each other. The political systems differ too: China is governed by a single party, Japan is a parliamentary democracy with an emperor as a symbol, and South Korea is a presidential democracy. History adds tension, because wars and disputes still shape how the countries see each other. “Asian culture” hides all of this.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Can speakers of Mandarin and Cantonese easily understand each other?

2. What kind of political system does Japan have?

1. No — the two are very different.

2. A parliamentary democracy with an emperor as a symbol.

Discuss:
  • What is lost when people say “Asian culture” instead of naming a country?

Grammar — Relative Clauses — Who, Which, Whose, Where

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
A relative clause gives extra information about a noun.
• who for people: “a thinker who lived 2,500 years ago”
• which / that for things: “a script which looks nothing like Chinese”
• whose for belonging: “a country whose writing system is an alphabet”
• where for places: “a peninsula where one people is divided into two countries”

Going deeper

The idea of one “Asian culture” comes partly from outside. When travellers, traders and later films described the region, they often grouped very different places together and noticed whatever looked unfamiliar. Inside the region, people usually think first of their own country, city or region — a Cantonese speaker from Hong Kong, someone from Busan, a person from Osaka. Shared ideas such as respect for elders or the value of education are real, but many other cultures share them too.

Worth noticing: Relative clauses: who (people), which / that (things), whose (belonging), where (places), when (times).
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Name the country. Instead of “Asian culture” or “in Asia, people…”, say which country, city or group you mean. It shows you know the difference, and people generally like being seen as themselves, not as a region.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Do you prefer to be described by your region or by your country — or by your city?
  • Which neighbouring countries in your part of the world are often treated as one?
  • How do languages and writing shape the way people see themselves?
