The Bet

Anton Chekhov (1889) — a banker and a lawyer wager two million on fifteen years of solitude

Easier version available: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Read in four parts

Chekhov opens at the end — an old banker on a sleepless night — and then walks us back to the wager that has poisoned fifteen years of his life. This is a graded retelling. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The twist is in the letter. Do not skip ahead.

Part 1 — The wager

Fifteen years ago, the banker had given a party, and the talk had turned to capital punishment. Most of the guests disapproved of it. The death sentence, they argued, was immoral and out of date; it would be better to replace it with imprisonment for life.

"I don't agree," said the banker, who was then young and hot-tempered. "I have tried neither, but if I had to choose, I would certainly choose the death sentence. To kill a man at one stroke is more merciful than to kill him slowly over many years."

A young lawyer among the guests disagreed sharply.

"Both are immoral, since both take away life. But if I had to choose between the death penalty and imprisonment for life, I would choose to live. To live somehow is better than not to live at all."

The banker, carried away, struck the table. "It's a lie! I bet you two million you wouldn't stay in solitary confinement for even five years." The young man's eyes flashed. "If you mean it seriously, I'll take the bet — but I'll stay not five years but fifteen." And so the reckless wager was sealed: two million roubles against fifteen years of a man's freedom.

Pause and discuss:
  • Both men make the bet in a moment of heat. Which of them, at this point, do you think is being more foolish?
  • The lawyer raises the stake himself — five years to fifteen — out of pride. Have you ever doubled down on something to prove a point?
  • Chekhov calls the wager "wild" and "senseless" even as it is being made. Why tell us the verdict so early?

Part 2 — The years of confinement

The lawyer was shut in a lodge in the banker's garden, cut off from all human contact. He could receive books, wine, a piano, anything in writing — but no visitors, no voices, no glimpse of the outside world. If he left the lodge even two minutes before the agreed moment, the banker was freed from paying.

In the first year the lawyer suffered terribly from loneliness and boredom. In the second he gave up wine and asked only for the classics. By the middle years he was studying languages, philosophy, history — six hundred volumes in four years. Then he read the Gospel, and then theology, and then everything at once: chemistry, medicine, Byron, Shakespeare. He wrote a letter and asked that experts be found to test his learning in six languages; when they confirmed it, he wept. In the last two years he read enormously and without system, an old man's unguided hunger for everything the mind can hold.

Pause and discuss:
  • Trace the lawyer's reading: novels, then languages, then scripture, then everything. What journey is Chekhov drawing through the choice of books?
  • The man is a prisoner, yet in some sense he is becoming freer than his jailer. How?

Part 3 — The banker's ruin

It is now the night before the fifteen years end, and the old banker paces his study. He is no longer rich. Reckless speculation has all but ruined him, and the two million he must pay at noon tomorrow will finish him completely. "Why didn't the man die?" he thinks. "He is only forty. He will take my last penny, marry, enjoy life, and I shall look on like a beggar."

A terrible idea takes hold of him. If the prisoner were dead, no one would suspect; the watchman has gone, the night is wild with storm. The banker takes the key, crosses the dark garden, and lets himself into the lodge to murder the man who has cost him everything. By the lamp he sees the prisoner asleep at the table — a skeleton, with long hair and a yellow face — and beside him, a sheet of paper covered in writing.

Pause and discuss:
  • The banker who once argued life was too cruel to take is now ready to take one. What has fifteen years done to him?
  • Chekhov gives the banker a perfect opportunity — storm, no witnesses, a sleeping victim. Why make the crime so easy?

Part 4 — The letter

The banker picks up the sheet and reads. The prisoner has written his farewell. In fifteen years of books, he writes, he has lived: he has hunted in African forests, loved women, climbed mountains, heard the songs of poets and the arguments of philosophers — all in his mind, all from his chair. And it has taught him to despise everything the world calls good.

"You have lost your reason and taken the wrong path. You take lies for truth and ugliness for beauty. I despise your books, your wisdom, your blessings of the world. To prove to you how I despise the life you live, I renounce the two million I once dreamed of. To free myself, I shall leave here five hours before the appointed time, and so break the agreement."

The banker reads it, kisses the strange man on the head, and goes out weeping — ashamed as he has never been ashamed in his life. He can hardly sleep for tears. And the next morning the watchman comes running: the prisoner has climbed out of the window and walked away into the dawn, five hours early, and disappeared. The banker goes to the lodge, takes the letter so that no awkward questions will be asked, and locks it in his safe.

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • The lawyer wins the bet and throws away the prize with five hours to spare. Has he won, or lost, or moved past the whole frame of winning?
  • The banker keeps the letter locked in his safe. What does that small final action tell us about whether he has truly changed?
  • Both men set out to prove a point about the value of a life. By the end, what has each of them actually proved?
  • Your turn: Was the lawyer's renunciation noble, or was it the broken logic of a man the solitude has unhinged? Defend your reading.

The grammar this story teaches itself

"The Bet" is built on roads not taken. The grammar of the road not taken is the grammar of the unreal past: wish / if only for regret, the third conditional for a past that never happened, and the mixed conditional for a past choice that still shapes the present.

1. wish / if only — naming the regret

For regret about the past, use wish / if only + past perfect. ("The banker wishes he had never made the bet.") For regret about the present, use wish / if only + past simple. ("He wishes he were still rich.")

1. The banker wishes he (never / propose) the wager.

2. "If only I (not / speak) so rashly that night," he thinks.

3. He wishes he (be) the calm man he was fifteen years ago.

4. The lawyer does not wish he (keep) the money — he is glad to throw it away.

1. had never proposed

2. had not spoken

3. were / was (present regret — past simple; "were" is the careful form)

4. had kept

2. Third conditional — the past that never happened

Pattern: If + past perfect, would have + past participle. Use it to talk about a different past and its different result — a result that never came to be.

1. If the banker (not / boast) about capital punishment, the bet (never / happen).

2. If the lawyer (leave) one day early, he (lose) the bet outright.

3. If the banker (murder) the prisoner, he (become) a criminal as well as a fool.

4. The prisoner (not / change) so deeply if he (have) company.

1. had not boasted / would never have happened

2. had left / would have lost

3. had murdered / would have become

4. would not have changed / had had

3. Mixed conditional — a past cause with a present result

Pattern: If + past perfect, would + base verb (now). Use it when a past action explains a present state. ("If the banker hadn't gambled his fortune away, he wouldn't be ruined now.")

1. If the banker (not / make) that bet, he (not / be) terrified tonight.

2. If the lawyer (waste) those years, he (not / despise) wealth as he does now.

3. If the banker (invest) wisely, he (be) able to pay the two million easily today.

1. had not made / would not be

2. had wasted / would not despise

3. had invested / would be

What Chekhov does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are why a story this short can hold a whole philosophy of life.

1. Starting at the end — the frame

Chekhov opens on the final night and then flashes back to the wager. We read the fifteen years already knowing they are nearly over, which charges every detail with the question: what will the man be like when he comes out?

2. The reversal — who is really imprisoned

The lawyer is the prisoner, yet he grows free while the banker, at liberty, grows trapped by debt and dread. Chekhov inverts the two positions so quietly that we only notice the swap when the banker creeps in to commit murder.

3. The letter — character delivered as a document

Instead of telling us what fifteen years did to the lawyer, Chekhov hands us the man's own letter and lets it speak for him. The renunciation arrives in the prisoner's voice, not the narrator's, which makes it land as confession rather than summary.

4. The locked safe — the undercut ending

After all that grandeur of renunciation, the banker quietly pockets the letter and locks it away. Chekhov undercuts the noble climax with a small, self-protective act, and refuses to tell us whether the banker is redeemed. The ambiguity is the point.

Spot-the-technique — quick MCQ

The prisoner grows freer in his cell while the free banker grows more trapped. What is this technique?
Chekhov gives us the prisoner's renunciation as a letter in the man's own words, rather than describing it himself. Why?
The story ends with the banker locking the letter in his safe. What does this final detail achieve?

Three speaking tasks

Do all three. Each one builds on the last. The timer is for your benefit — it forces you to fill the time rather than escape it. Use the conditional structures and effect verbs from the previous tabs.

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · Two Sets of Regrets (4 minutes)

Speak first as the banker, then as the lawyer, on the final night. Give each man two regrets and two things he does not regret. The banker might say, "If only I had never struck that table…"; the lawyer might say, "I don't regret a single year."

Use at least three wish / if only sentences and at least one third conditional. Keep the two voices clearly different.

Task B · Your Own Wager (4 minutes)

Describe a "bet" you once made with yourself or someone else — a commitment, a dare, a promise to stick something out. Tell what you staked, how it went, and what you would do differently.

Build the second half on mixed conditionals: "If I hadn't taken that on, I wouldn't be… today." Show how one past choice still shapes your present.

Task C · Defend the Renunciation (4 minutes)

The lawyer walks away from two million with five hours to spare. For two minutes, argue that this is the noblest act in the story — a man who has genuinely outgrown the prize. For two more, argue the opposite: that it is the damaged logic of someone fifteen years of isolation has broken, and that throwing away the money helps no one.

Use cleft sentences ("What he is really rejecting is…") and at least one structure of concession ("Even if he is sincere, …").

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The banker argued that slow imprisonment is crueller than execution. By the end, has the story proved him right, or wrong?
  2. The lawyer says fifteen years of books let him live more fully than free men do. Is a life lived entirely in the mind a real life?
  3. Both men gambled with a human life — one with his freedom, one with his fortune. Whose stake was higher?
  4. If you were offered the lawyer's deal today — fifteen years alone, anything to read, two million at the end — would you take it? At what price would you?
