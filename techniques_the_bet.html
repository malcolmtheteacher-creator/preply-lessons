TECHNIQUES IN ACTION · B2/C1 · 50 min

The Bet

Anton Chekhov (1889) — a contract, fifteen years, and a discovery worth more than two million rubles

Read in three parts

Read each chunk, pause, talk it through, then continue. The story is short — about 2,400 words — but each section earns a discussion.

Part 1 — The party and the bet

It is a dark autumn night. An old banker is walking up and down his study, remembering a party he gave fifteen years ago. There were many intelligent people there, and a great deal of interesting conversation. Among other things they talked about capital punishment.

Most of the guests disapproved of it. They thought it cruel, immoral, and unsuitable for a Christian state. Some of them believed life imprisonment was even worse — that it kills a man slowly, while execution kills him at once.

"I don't agree with you," said the banker. "Capital punishment is more moral and more humane than imprisonment. Execution kills instantly; life imprisonment kills slowly. Which executioner is more humane — the one who kills you in a few minutes, or the one who drags the life out of you over many years?"

A young lawyer of twenty-five was asked his opinion. "Both are equally immoral," he said, "but if I had to choose, I would choose the second. To live somehow is better than not to live at all."

A heated debate followed. The banker, who was younger then and full of energy, suddenly struck the table with his fist and shouted at the young lawyer: "It's a lie! I bet you two million you wouldn't last five years in solitary confinement!"

"If you mean it seriously," the lawyer replied, "I bet I'll stay not five but fifteen."

"Fifteen? Done! Gentlemen, I stake two million."

"Agreed. You stake your millions and I stake my freedom."

Pause and discuss:
  • If you were the lawyer's legal advisor, what clauses would you add to the contract?
  • Who has the better deal at this moment — the banker or the lawyer? Why?
  • The banker says "It's a lie!" What is he actually reacting to?

Part 2 — Fifteen years pass

The lawyer was placed in one of the lodges in the banker's garden. It was agreed that he would not leave the lodge, see human beings, hear voices, or receive letters or newspapers. He was allowed a musical instrument, books, wine, and tobacco. He could communicate with the outside world only by a small window — silently, in writing.

In the first year of confinement, the lawyer suffered terribly from loneliness. He played the piano day and night. He refused wine and tobacco. He read light novels — comedies, love stories, crime fiction.

In the second year, the piano was silent and he asked only for the classics.

In the fifth year, music was heard again. The lawyer asked for wine. Those who watched him through the window said he did nothing but eat, drink, and lie on his bed. He often wept.

In the second half of the sixth year, he began studying languages, philosophy, and history with great enthusiasm. In four years he learned six languages. He wrote a letter to the banker asking him to test his knowledge by giving the same passage to translators in six different countries. If they all agreed the translations were correct, the banker should fire a shot in the garden. The shot was fired.

In the tenth year, the lawyer sat motionless at the table and read only the New Testament. The banker found it strange that a man who had mastered six hundred volumes of learning should now spend a year reading one thin, easy-to-understand book.

In the last two years, the prisoner read enormously. He read chemistry, medicine, novels, philosophy, theology — all at once. His reading was the reading of a man swimming in the sea among the wreckage of his ship, snatching at one spar after another to save his life.

Pause and discuss:
  • At which year is the lawyer "winning"? At which year is the banker?
  • What kind of man is being built inside that lodge?
  • Meanwhile, what do you think is happening to the banker outside?

Part 3 — The night before the bet ends

The old banker remembered all this and thought: "Tomorrow at twelve o'clock he will regain his freedom. By our agreement, I must pay him two million. If I pay, it is all over — I am ruined forever."

Fifteen years ago his millions had been plentiful. Now he was afraid to ask himself which were more — his debts or his assets. Reckless gambling on the stock exchange and wild speculation had reduced his fortune. From a proud, fearless millionaire he had become a banker of middling rank, trembling at every rise and fall in his investments.

"That cursed bet!" muttered the old man, clutching his head in despair. "Why didn't the man die? He is only forty now. He will take my last penny from me, marry, enjoy life, gamble on the Exchange, while I shall look at him with envy like a beggar..."

The clock struck three. The banker listened: everyone was asleep in the house. Quietly he took the key of the lodge from his safe and went out into the garden. It was dark and cold. Rain was falling.

He approached the lodge. He looked through the small window. The lawyer was sitting at the table. Only his back, the hair on his head, and his hands were visible. Open books lay on the table, on the two chairs, and on the carpet near the table.

On the table in front of the lawyer lay a sheet of paper with something written on it in a fine hand.

The banker opened the door and read:

"Tomorrow at twelve o'clock I regain my freedom and the right to associate with people. But before I leave this room and see the sun, I think it necessary to say a few words to you. With a clear conscience, and before God who beholds me, I declare to you that I despise freedom, life, health, and all that your books call the blessings of the world... For fifteen years I have studied earthly life. It is true I have not seen the earth or its people, but in your books I have drunk fragrant wine, sung songs, hunted in the forests... Your books have given me wisdom. All that the restless human thought has created in the centuries is compressed into a small lump in my brain. I know I am wiser than you all. And I despise your books, I despise all the blessings of this world and all its wisdom. Everything is worthless, false, hollow, and deceiving like a mirage... To prove to you in deed how I despise all that you live by, I renounce the two million of which I once dreamed as of paradise and which I now scorn. To deprive myself of the right to receive them, I shall come out from here five hours before the agreed time, and so break the contract."

When the banker had read this, he put the paper on the table, kissed the strange man on the head, and went out of the lodge, weeping. At no other time, even after heavy losses on the Exchange, had he felt such contempt for himself.

The next morning the watchman ran in, pale with excitement, and told him he had seen the lawyer climb out of the window into the garden and disappear. The banker went at once with his servants to the lodge and made sure of the flight of his prisoner. To avoid unnecessary talk, he took the paper containing the renunciation, and on his return locked it in his safe.

Final discussion:
  • Who won the bet?
  • What did fifteen years actually cost the banker, and what did they actually buy the lawyer?
  • The banker locks the letter in his safe and tells no one. Why?
  • You sign contracts that pay out over years. Has any of yours ever reframed itself the way this one did?

The bet is built on conditionals

Every contract is a chain of conditionals. The Bet is the perfect text for drilling them because the entire story is a conditional structure.

The three conditionals you'll use today

First conditional — real future possibility:
"If you last fifteen years, I will pay you two million."

Second conditional — hypothetical present/future:
"If I were in his place, I would walk out after a week."

Third conditional — counterfactual past:
"If the banker had paid on time, he would have been ruined."

Gap-fill — complete each sentence about the story

1. If the lawyer (give up) in the first year, he (lose) nothing.

2. If the banker (pay) the two million tomorrow, he (be) ruined.

3. If the lawyer (not read) those books, he (not change) the way he did.

4. The banker thinks: "If only the lawyer (die) before tomorrow!"

5. If I (be) the lawyer, I (renounce) the money too.

1. had given up / would have lost (3rd conditional — counterfactual past)

2. pays / will be (1st conditional — real future)

3. had not read / would not have changed (3rd conditional)

4. would die / had died (wish / regret — 2nd or 3rd)

5. were / would renounce (2nd conditional — hypothetical)

Modal verbs of speculation — what's happening inside the lodge?

Use must / might / could / can't + have + past participle to speculate about what happened.

1. At year five, he wept often. He (lose) hope.

2. At year ten, he read only the New Testament. He (search) for something.

3. He learned six languages in four years. He (work) every waking hour.

4. He climbed out the window five hours early. He (want) to break the contract publicly.

1. must have been losing / must have lost

2. might / could have been searching

3. must have worked / must have been working

4. can't have wanted (the letter says the opposite — he wanted to break the contract privately by forfeiting)

What Chekhov does — and the verbs to describe it

Three moves to spot, and three effect verbs to use when you talk about them.

1. Time compression

Chekhov gives us fifteen years in about five hundred words. He picks one detail per year — the piano, the wine, the classics, the New Testament. The story compresses time and distils change.

2. The pivot

The story pivots at the banker's discovery of the letter. Up to that point, the reader and the banker share the same question: will the lawyer make it? After it, the question becomes: what has fifteen years actually done to him?

3. The reframe

The letter reframes the entire bet. The lawyer doesn't win — he declines to win. He has discovered something more valuable than two million rubles, and he despises both himself and the banker for ever caring about the money.

Effect verbs to use in your analysis

compresses distils pivots reframes unsettles repositions renounces destabilises
