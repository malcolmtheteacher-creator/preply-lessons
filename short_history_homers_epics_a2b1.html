The story, in three parts

Two very old poems, and how they came to a cinema today. Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — The Iliad: a war at Troy

The first poem is called the Iliad. It is a story about a war. For nearly ten years, a Greek army sat outside the walls of a city called Troy. They wanted to take the city back after a Trojan prince took a Greek queen, Helen.

The best Greek fighter was a hero called Achilles. He was very strong, but also very proud. One day the Greek king, Agamemnon, took something from Achilles. Achilles was so angry that he stopped fighting and stayed in his tent. Without him, the Greeks began to lose.

Then Achilles' best friend went to fight — and a brave Trojan prince, Hector, killed him. Now Achilles was full of sadness and anger. He went back to the war and killed Hector. But at the end, Hector's old father came to Achilles and asked for his son's body. And Achilles cried with him. The Iliad does not show the end of the war. It stops here, with a quiet, sad moment.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Achilles stop fighting?

2. How does the Iliad end?

1. The king Agamemnon took something from him, so he was very angry and stayed in his tent.

2. It ends after Achilles kills Hector. Hector's father asks for his son's body, and Achilles cries with him. The war is not finished in the poem.

Talk about it:
  • The poem starts with anger, not with a happy hero. Why do you think an old story begins like this?

Part 2 — The Odyssey: the long way home

The second poem is called the Odyssey. Now the war is over. A clever Greek hero, Odysseus, wants to go home to his small island, Ithaca. The journey should be short. But it takes him ten more years!

On the way, Odysseus meets many dangers. He meets a giant with only one eye, and he must escape from him. He meets a woman with magic who turns his men into pigs. He sails past monsters in the sea. A beautiful woman keeps him on her island for years and says, "Stay here, and never die." But Odysseus says no. He wants to go home more than he wants to live for ever.

At home, his wife Penelope waits for him. Many men say, "Odysseus is dead. Marry me!" But she is clever, like her husband. She says she will choose a man when she finishes making a big cloth — and every night she quietly takes the cloth apart again. At last Odysseus comes home. He does not win with his body; he wins because he is patient and waits for the right moment.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name two dangers that Odysseus meets on his journey.

2. How does Penelope keep the other men waiting for so long?

1. Any two of: a giant with one eye, a woman with magic who turns men into pigs, monsters in the sea, a woman who keeps him on her island.

2. She says she will choose a man when she finishes making a big cloth — but every night she takes her work apart again.

Talk about it:
  • Odysseus can live for ever, but he says no and goes home. Would you say yes or no? Why?

Part 3 — An old story, a new film

These poems are about 3,000 years old — much older than most stories we know. A poet called Homer is the name we give to them. But we are not sure who Homer really was. Maybe one person, maybe many people. For a very long time, no one wrote the poems down. People sang them and remembered them.

Was there a real war at Troy? We are not sure. But long ago, people found the ruins of a real old city in Turkey, in the same place as the story. So maybe the poems remember something true.

Today, the story is still very popular. In 2026, the famous director Christopher Nolan made a big film of the Odyssey. It was one of the most expensive films ever, with famous actors like Matt Damon. Many people went to see it. After 3,000 years, people still love this story — first as a song, then as a book, and now as a film.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was Homer, and why are we not sure about him?

2. What did Christopher Nolan do in 2026?

1. Homer is the name we give to the poet of the two poems. We are not sure who he was, because the poems are very old and people sang them for a long time before writing them down.

2. He made a big, expensive film of the Odyssey, with famous actors like Matt Damon. Many people went to see it.

Talk about it:
  • Is it good to make a new film of a very old story? Why, or why not?

Grammar: comparatives & superlatives

This story is full of "more" and "most": the oldest story, a bigger ship, the most famous hero. Here is how to say it.

Comparative — compare two things. Use -er + than for short words, and more + than for long words: "Troy is older than Rome." "This film is more exciting than the book."
Superlative — one thing above all the others. Use the -est for short words, and the most for long words: "It is the oldest story I know." "He is the most famous hero."
Careful — some words are different:
• good → better than → the best
• bad → worse than → the worst
• far → further/farther than → the furthest/farthest

1. Comparative or superlative?

Read the sentence and complete it. Use the word in brackets.

1. The journey home was than the war. (long)

2. Odysseus was one of heroes in all the stories. (clever — superlative)

3. A giant is much than a normal man. (big)

1. longer (long → longer than)

2. the cleverest (one of the cleverest heroes)

3. bigger (big → bigger than)

2. Make the comparative or superlative

Change the word in brackets. Then read the sentence out loud.

1. The Iliad is (famous — superlative) war poem in the world.

2. For me, the book is (good) than the film.

3. Troy is (old) than many famous cities.

1. the most famous (long word → the most)

2. better (good → better than)

3. older (old → older than)

3. Tell a short part of the story

Fill each gap with a comparative or superlative. Read it aloud when you finish.

The Odyssey is one of (old) stories in the world. The journey home was much (hard) than Odysseus thought. Many people say it is (exciting — superlative) adventure story ever.

the oldest · harder · the most exciting

Short words: -er / the -est. Long words: more / the most.
