Understanding Topic Sentences

A topic sentence tells the reader the main idea of a paragraph in one clear statement. It comes first and everything else in the paragraph supports it.

The Function of a Topic Sentence

  • Introduce the main point
  • Control what belongs in the paragraph
  • Guide the reader forward
  • Make promises the rest of the paragraph will keep

Example: Exercise Timing and Sleep

When you exercise matters just as much as how often you exercise. Our circadian rhythm — the body's natural 24-hour cycle — controls everything from digestion to energy levels. High-intensity exercise in the evening can disrupt sleep quality because it raises heart rate and body temperature when your body is preparing to rest. For this reason, fitness experts increasingly recommend exercising in the morning or early afternoon to align with your body's natural rhythm and guarantee better sleep at night.

What makes this a strong topic sentence?

  • It's specific (not "exercise is good")
  • It promises the paragraph will compare timing
  • Every sentence that follows supports this exact idea
  • The vocabulary is clear and sophisticated

3 Your mood can often affect what you choose to eat, but your food choices can actually change your mood.

Correct Order: 3 → 1 → 2

Sentence 1 is support (it explains how this works).

Sentence 2 is evidence (it gives specific examples).

Notice how the weakest position — "your mood affects food" — is turned into the strongest position: "food affects your mood," which is more persuasive and more interesting.

Paragraph Structure: The Three-Layer Model

Layer 1: Topic Sentence (The Main Idea)

Opens the paragraph. Makes one clear claim about food or health.

Example: "Processed foods are convenient, but they carry hidden costs to our health."

Layer 2: Supporting Sentences (Explanation & Evidence)

Develops the topic sentence. Answers "Why?" or "How?" Builds the case with details, facts, or reasoning.

Example: "Many processed foods contain hidden sugars and high levels of saturated fat designed to make them taste appealing but difficult to resist."

Layer 3: Evidence & Examples (Concrete Details)

Strengthens the argument with specific examples, research, or real-world situations.

Example: "A typical shop-bought yoghurt, marketed as a health food, can contain as much sugar as a chocolate bar — 20 grams or more."

Diabetes: A Case Study

Read these three paragraphs about the link between diet and diabetes. After each one, identify the topic sentence and underline the key vocabulary.

Paragraph 1: The prevalence of type 2 diabetes has increased dramatically over the last 50 years, particularly in countries where processed foods dominate the diet. While genetics play a role, research shows that lifestyle factors — especially eating habits — are the primary driver. Countries that have shifted toward Western diets high in processed foods, refined sugars, and saturated fats show corresponding spikes in diabetes cases. This suggests that what we eat is the most controllable factor in preventing the disease.

Topic sentence: "The prevalence of type 2 diabetes has increased dramatically over the last 50 years, particularly in countries where processed foods dominate the diet."

Key vocabulary: prevalence, type 2 diabetes, processed foods, refined sugars, saturated fats

Paragraph 2: Whole grains and foods rich in fiber help regulate blood sugar by slowing the rate at which sugars enter the bloodstream. This prevents the dramatic spikes and crashes that force the pancreas to work harder and harder. People who eat more whole grains, vegetables, and legumes have significantly lower rates of type 2 diabetes. In contrast, diets based on white bread, sugary cereals, and processed snacks leave people vulnerable.

Topic sentence: "Whole grains and foods rich in fiber help regulate blood sugar by slowing the rate at which sugars enter the bloodstream."

Key vocabulary: whole grains, fiber, blood sugar, legumes

Paragraph 3: The good news is that type 2 diabetes is largely preventable through dietary choices. You don't need expensive supplements or complicated rules — simply replacing processed foods with whole foods, limiting hidden sugars, and eating a variety of vegetables can dramatically reduce your risk. Even people with a family history of diabetes can avoid the disease by building healthy eating habits early. This means the power to prevent diabetes lies in your hands and on your plate.

Topic sentence: "The good news is that type 2 diabetes is largely preventable through dietary choices."

Key vocabulary: preventable, processed foods, whole foods, hidden sugars

Notice the Pattern

Each paragraph has a clear topic sentence that makes one argument. The sentences after it don't wander — they all stay focused on proving that one idea. This is what makes persuasive writing powerful.

Your Writing Task

Write a magazine article on healthy eating (180-200 words) using proper paragraph structure. You're trying to persuade readers that changing their eating habits matters.

Writing Time
3:00

Planning Template

1
Choose Your Main Argument
What's your main claim about healthy eating? (Example: "Fast food is designed to be addictive," or "Investing in healthy food now saves money on medical bills later")
2
Write Three Topic Sentences
One for each paragraph. Make sure each one is specific and introduces a different aspect of your argument.
3
Gather Evidence
For each paragraph, note: How does this topic sentence connect to my main argument? What facts or examples support it?
4
Use Vocabulary
Aim to use at least 3-4 health vocabulary words (circadian rhythm, processed food, saturated fat, whole grains, prevalence, diabetes, hidden sugar, immune system).
5
Check Your Structure
Each paragraph should have: a topic sentence + supporting details + evidence. Avoid rambling or changing the subject mid-paragraph.

Success Criteria

  • Relevant ideas: Every sentence directly supports your main argument about healthy eating
  • Logical paragraphs: Each paragraph has one clear main idea and stays on topic
  • Topic sentences developed: Each topic sentence is explained with examples, evidence, or reasoning
  • Neutral, persuasive style: You're convincing, not preaching or being aggressive
  • Range of vocabulary: You use specific health terminology, not just "good" and "bad"
  • 180-200 words: On target with length requirements

Writing to Persuade: The Challenge

Persuasive writing about health is tricky. You want to convince without sounding preachy. You want to be accurate without overwhelming readers with data. You want to inspire change without making people feel guilty. In your article, did you find moments where these tensions appeared?

Real-World Writing

Magazine articles, health blogs, social media posts, and advertisements all try to persuade us about food. When you read nutrition advice online, how do you tell the difference between genuine information and marketing? What signals make a health claim seem credible or suspicious?
