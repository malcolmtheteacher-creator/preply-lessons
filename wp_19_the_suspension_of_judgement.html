A Philosophical Problem

We have travelled far. The Milesians asked what is everything made of? Plato said, retreat to eternal forms. Aristotle said, reason about essences. The Stoics said, control your judgments. But now we face a deeper problem: Can we ever be certain of anything? If every argument has an equally strong counter-argument, how can we know anything at all? And if we cannot know, should we keep believing? Or should we do something more radical: suspend belief entirely?

II. The Story — A Journey to India, a Return to Doubt

Pyrrho of Elis (c. 365–275 BC)

Pyrrho was born in Elis, in the Peloponnese. He became a painter, then a philosopher. At some point, he joined Alexander the Great's expedition to India—perhaps as a teacher, perhaps simply as a companion seeking wisdom.

In India, Pyrrho encountered the gymnosophists—wandering ascetic philosophers, some of them naked, practicing extreme simplicity. He was fascinated. He returned to Greece—like Epicurus returning from his travels—but with a profoundly different conclusion than his older contemporary.

The Epoche: Suspension of Judgment

Pyrrho's key insight was this: for every argument, there is an equally strong counter-argument. This is called isostheneia—the equal strength of opposing arguments. Given this perfect balance, the wise response is epoché—suspension of judgment.

When things are equally balanced, our opinions about them appear to be unfounded. For if someone has a valid ground for believing one side to be better than the other, then indeed it would be better. But since we find such grounds on both sides, we suspend judgment. — Pyrrho (reconstructed from Sextus Empiricus)

Notice what this means: you don't believe that skepticism is true. You don't affirm anything. You simply—in the face of equal arguments—refrain from judgment. And surprisingly, this refusal brings ataraxia—peace, tranquility. The same word Epicurus used! But achieved differently: not through pleasure, but through the cessation of the struggle to believe.

A legend about Pyrrho: The stories told of Pyrrho suggest he achieved such perfect indifference that he would walk in front of carts, climb cliffs, and ignore obstacles—until his attendants had to guide him to safety. He was not suicidal. He simply did not judge danger to be bad, so his fear system did not activate. We might suspect these stories are exaggerations. But they capture something true: Pyrrho achieved a kind of liberation from conviction that verged on the inhuman.

Later Skepticism

After Pyrrho, skepticism evolved through two main lineages:

Academic Skepticism (Arcesilaus and Carneades in Plato's Academy): These thinkers held that knowledge is impossible, but they still acted based on probability. They suspended judgment on theoretical matters while remaining practically engaged.

Pyrrhonian Skepticism (Sextus Empiricus, 2nd century AD): Sextus preserved and systematized Pyrrho's approach. He argued that skepticism is not a dogma but a way of life—a method for achieving peace through the abandonment of the search for truth.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Skeptical Arsenal

Sextus provides a collection of "tropes" (modes, arguments) that show why we should suspend judgment:

The Trope of Contradiction: For every assertion, an equally strong counter-assertion can be made.

The Trope of Infinite Regress: To justify any belief, you need another belief to support it, which needs another, and so on forever.

The Trope of Relativity: All judgments are relative to the perceiver. What appears good to one appears bad to another.

The Trope of Hypothesis: Some claims rest on unproven assumptions.

These are powerful arguments. They anticipate modern philosophical problems—the regress problem in epistemology, relativism, the problem of the criterion. But they lead to a conclusion many find troubling: we cannot know anything with certainty, so we should stop trying.

III. The Ideas — Three Pillars of Skeptical Thought

Idea 1: Epoché (Suspension of Judgment)

For every claim, there is an equally strong counter-claim. Therefore, suspend judgment.

This is not mere agnosticism ("I don't know"). It is active suspension. You actively refrain from affirming anything. You don't say "I believe X might be true but I'm not sure." You say "I withhold judgment entirely." This is intellectually honest. It is also liberating—because it frees you from the exhausting demand to have opinions on everything.

Idea 2: Ataraxia Through Skepticism

Peace comes not from having answers but from stopping the search for them.

This parallels Epicureanism but inverts it. Epicurus said: simplify your desires, enjoy what you have, stop seeking more. The Skeptic says: stop seeking certainty, abandon the demand to know, and you will find peace. Both arrive at tranquility. The Epicurean through pleasure, the Skeptic through the cessation of intellectual struggle.

But here is a tension: Is the Skeptic really at peace? Or is the Skeptic simply more aware of the groundlessness of everything and has learned to live with that awareness? That might feel like peace, or it might feel like vertigo.

Idea 3: Isostheneia (Equal Strength of Arguments)

For any argument in favor of X, an equally strong argument against X can be constructed.

This is more radical than it sounds. It claims that no argument is definitively stronger than its opposite. Everything is perspectival. Everything is dependent on unproven assumptions. The Skeptic presents "tropes"—logical weapons—that neutralize any claim to certainty. In the perfect balance, judgment suspends.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Unresolved Question

Skepticism is intellectually honest. But here is what it cannot answer: How do you live without conviction?

You might suspend judgment on abstract metaphysical claims. But what about practical matters? If your house is on fire, do you suspend judgment about whether to leave? If someone needs help, do you suspend judgment about whether to help? The Skeptic's response is pragmatic: yes, act according to appearance and convention. Act as if things matter, even though you have suspended judgment about whether they truly matter.

But this feels like a dodge. Either things matter or they don't. Either your help can genuinely improve someone's condition or it cannot. The Skeptic seems to be sneaking back in conviction through the back door while claiming to have abandoned it through the front.

Also: What about love, justice, and meaning? These require conviction. They require you to judge that something is worth the risk. The Skeptic can achieve tranquility by suspending judgment on all these matters. But is that tranquility or anesthesia? Is it wisdom or just sophisticated escapism?

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to think through carefully.

Can you live without conviction?

The Skeptic suspends judgment on everything. But if you truly had no convictions, how would you choose between right and wrong? How would you love? How would you act?

Is Skepticism really true?

Skepticism claims that for every argument there is an equally strong counter-argument. But isn't the claim that for every argument there is an equally strong counter-argument itself open to counter-argument?

Tranquility through surrender

The Skeptic achieves peace by giving up the search for truth. But is that peace or just numbness? Is it wisdom or sophisticated escapism?

The legend of Pyrrho

The stories say Pyrrho walked in front of carts, climbed cliffs, ignored obstacles—indifferent to danger. Are these stories cautionary tales or celebrations of his philosophy?

What about justice?

The Skeptic suspends judgment on all matters. But some things demand judgment. If you see injustice, can you really withhold judgment and remain passive?

Skepticism and science

Modern science rests on conviction—that the universe follows laws, that reason can discover those laws. What would skepticism do to science?

Where does Stoicism fail?

We have just left the Stoics. They demanded self-mastery and virtue. Does the Skeptic's response to this—radical doubt and withdrawal—solve the problems Stoicism left unresolved?
