Listen in on this conversation

Tom: Where are my keys? I can't find them anywhere!

Amy: They're on the table, next to your bag.

Tom: They're not there...

Amy: Then look under the newspaper — you always put them there!

Notice something? Amy uses three different little words — on, next to, under — for three different positions. Why not just use one word for all of them? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
Because each word paints a different picture: on the table (resting on top), next to the bag (beside it), under the newspaper (hidden below it). English has a small set of these words for place, and mixing them up really can confuse people — "in the table" doesn't mean the same thing as "on the table"! The same little words also tell us when something happens, not just where it is.
Today: in, on, at (plus a few position words like next to, under and behind) so you can say exactly where things are — and the same three words again for saying when things happen. Useful for describing your home and arranging to meet someone.

Where Is It?

Talking about the exact position of things — in a house, in a bag, on the street.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "Your passport is in the top drawer."
  • "The lamp is on the desk, next to the window."
  • "Let's meet at the bus stop, behind the supermarket."

Look at in, on and at above. What's the difference — when do we use each one? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
in = inside an enclosed space (a drawer, a box, a room, a bag, a city)
on = on top of a flat surface (a desk, a table, a shelf, a wall)
at = an exact point or place (a bus stop, a door, a corner, a station)
Notice next to (beside the window) and behind (at the back of the supermarket) too — they name an exact position, but they don't need this in/on/at decision; they just mean what they say.
in = inside a space → in the kitchen, in a box, in London
on = on a surface → on the table, on a shelf, on the wall
at = an exact point → at the door, at the station, at the corner
next to = beside · under = below · behind = at the back of

When Is It?

The same three words — in, on, at — also tell us WHEN something happens.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "The train leaves at half past six."
  • "We're having a party on Saturday."
  • "I was born in July."

Same three words as before, but now about time. What's the pattern? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
at = an exact clock time (at six o'clock, at half past six, at midnight, at noon)
on = a day or a date (on Saturday, on Monday, on 3rd July, on my birthday)
in = a longer period of time (in July, in summer, in the morning, in 2024)
at = exact clock time → at 6 o'clock, at noon, at midnight
on = a day or date → on Monday, on 3rd July, on my birthday
in = a longer period → in July, in summer, in the morning, in 2024
Careful: we say in the morning, in the afternoon, in the evening — but at night. And when a day and a part of the day appear together, the day wins: on Monday morning, on Friday evening (not "in Friday evening").

In, On or At?

Real sentences mix place and time together. Same three words, same three ideas — you just notice which one you need.

Spot the pattern

Three sentences with place AND time:

  • "I'll meet you at the café at three o'clock."
  • "The keys are on the kitchen table, and we're leaving on Tuesday."
  • "She lives in Berlin, and her flight is in the morning."

In each sentence, in/on/at is used twice — once for place, once for time. Do they follow the same three ideas as before? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Yes — exactly the same three ideas, just applied twice in one sentence: at = an exact point (a café) AND an exact clock time (three o'clock). on = a surface (the table) AND a day (Tuesday). in = an enclosed space (Berlin, a big one!) AND a longer period (the morning).
AT → exact point (a door, a café) + exact clock time (6 o'clock, noon)
ON → surface (a table, a shelf) + day/date (Monday, 3rd July)
IN → enclosed space (a room, a city) + longer period (July, the morning, 2024)
