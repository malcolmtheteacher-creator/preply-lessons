Key Words

thesis statement (n) — the line in an introduction that gives your position and the reasons to come. Example: "Her thesis statement made it clear she disagreed, and why."
topic sentence (n) — the opening line of a paragraph that announces its one main idea. Example: "Each topic sentence should match a reason promised in the introduction."
paraphrase (v) — express the same meaning in different words. Example: "Paraphrase the question rather than copying it."
substantiate (v) — support a claim with evidence or a concrete case. Example: "He made a bold claim but did nothing to substantiate it."
digress (v) — move away from the main subject. Example: "The second paragraph digresses into the history of the railway."
reiterate (v) — say again, usually to make a point clear. Example: "The conclusion should reiterate your position, not introduce a new one."
cohesion (n) — the way sentences are connected so that a text reads as one piece. Example: "Words like 'this' and 'such' give a paragraph cohesion."
over-generalise (v) — claim something is true in every case when it is only true in some. Example: "Saying 'all teenagers hate reading' is to over-generalise."

The main reason I welcome frequent career changes is that they allow people to correct early mistakes. Most of us choose a profession at eighteen or twenty, long before we know our own strengths, and a labour market that permits a second attempt wastes far less talent. An accountant who retrains as a nurse at thirty-five, for instance, brings both financial discipline and genuine motivation to a hospital that needs both. Mobility of this kind benefits society as much as the individual, since people who have chosen their work twice tend to do it well.
