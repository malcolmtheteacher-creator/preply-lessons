Making Opposites: Prefixes

Small pieces at the FRONT of a word that change its meaning.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday complaints:

  • "This wifi is so unreliable — it keeps cutting out."
  • "I completely disagree with that decision."
  • "Sorry, I misheard you — can you say that again?"

Before you check: what job do "un-", "dis-" and "mis-" do at the start of each word?

Check the pattern CLICK
Each one flips or changes the meaning of the word after it: un- = not/opposite, dis- = the opposite of doing something, mis- = wrongly or badly. Add one to the front and you don't need a new word at all.
un- = not / opposite → unfair, unsure
in- = not → invisible, informal
im- = not (before m or p) → impolite, immature
il- = not (before l) → illegal
ir- = not (before r) → irregular
dis- = opposite of an action/state → disagree, dislike
mis- = wrongly / badly → misunderstand, misheard
re- = again → reopen, rewrite

From "you can" to "you can't"

"This behaviour is legal."
→
"This behaviour is illegal."

Notice the spelling change: "in-" becomes "il-" before a word starting with "l" — it's easier to say. The same happens before "m", "p" and "r".

Naming Things: Noun Endings

Endings that turn a verb or adjective into a noun — a thing, an action, a quality, or a person.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday nouns:

  • "The company announced a big improvement in service."
  • "Her kindness to strangers is well known."
  • "The manager praised her quick decision."

What did each word start as, before the ending was added? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
improve (verb) + -ment = improvement; kind (adjective) + -ness = kindness; decide (verb) + -sion = decision. The ending turns an action or a quality into a thing you can talk about, count, or discuss.
verb + -ment = the result/action → pay → payment
adjective + -ness = the quality → dark → darkness
verb + -tion / -sion = the action/process → invite → invitation
verb + -er / -or = the person who does it → teach → teacher, act → actor
adjective + -ity = the quality → able → ability

From action to thing

"They discussed the problem for hours."
→
"There was a long discussion about the problem."

The verb "discuss" becomes the noun "discussion" — now it can be the subject of a sentence, something you can describe as "long" or "useful".

Describing Things: Adjective & Adverb Endings

Endings that build adjectives (describing a thing) and adverbs (describing how something happens).

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "The market was full of colourful stalls."
  • "The instructions were completely useless — I still don't understand them."
  • "He answered every question honestly."

What job does each ending do? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
-ful = full of (colour + ful); -less = without (use + less); -ly turns an adjective into an adverb, describing HOW something is done (honest + ly). The first two describe things; the last describes actions.
noun + -ful = full of / having → peace → peaceful
noun + -less = without → harm → harmless
verb + -able / -ible = can be done → wash → washable
verb + -ive = tends to / has the quality of → attract → attractive
noun + -ous = full of / having → fame → famous
adjective + -ly = in that way, describes a verb → quick → quickly

Which word class does the gap need?

Before you form the word, check what job the gap is doing:

  • Does it describe a thing (often after "be", or before a noun)? → adjective.
  • Does it describe how an action happens? → adverb, usually -ly.
  • Is it the name of a thing, a quality, or a person itself? → noun.

Try it: "The trip was completely ___ (SUCCESS)." What job does the gap do?

Check your reasoning CLICK
The gap comes right after "was" and describes the trip — that's an adjective job. success → successful. "The trip was completely successful."

From instruction to description

"You can wash this jacket in the machine."
→
"This jacket is washable."
