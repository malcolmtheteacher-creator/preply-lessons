Nikola Tesla

The inventor who imagined the modern electrical world — and died almost forgotten.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of a brilliant mind who saw the future before anyone else, and discuss why some visionaries die unrewarded — using the language of invention and the grammar of looking forward from the past.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Tesla was a true visionary — he could picture a wireless, electric world decades before the technology to build it even existed.
a visionary (n) — someone with original ideas about what the future could be like, far ahead of their own time.
2. He didn't just improve old machines; he pioneered whole new fields, doing things nobody had ever attempted before.
to pioneer (v) — to be the first to develop or explore something new. (noun: a pioneer)
3. Tesla's alternating current could be sent over huge distances along thin wires — something Edison's system simply could not do.
alternating current (AC) (n) — a type of electrical current that regularly reverses direction, making it efficient to send over long distances.
4. The two men became fierce rivals, each determined that his own system, and not the other's, would power the world.
a rival (n) — a person competing against you for the same prize or goal. (noun: rivalry)
5. To harness the power of Niagara Falls, engineers built huge generators that turned falling water into electricity for whole cities.
to harness (v) — to control a natural force and put it to useful work.
6. Tesla held hundreds of patents — official documents giving him, and only him, the legal right to his inventions.
a patent (n) — a legal right that protects an invention so that others cannot copy it without permission.
7. His grandest dream was to transmit electricity through the air itself, with no wires at all, to anywhere on Earth.
to transmit (v) — to send something (power, a signal, information) from one place to another.
8. When the money ran out, the great tower project collapsed, and Tesla's wireless dream fell apart completely.
to collapse (v) — to fail suddenly and completely; to fall down or fall apart.
9. In his final years Tesla grew increasingly eccentric — counting his steps, fearing germs, and feeding pigeons as though they were old friends.
eccentric (adj) — behaving in a strange, unusual way that other people find odd. (noun: eccentricity)
10. Long after his death, his reputation was revived, and the lonely old inventor was finally celebrated as a genius.
to revive (a reputation) (v) — to bring something back to life, attention or popularity after a long decline.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — From a Croatian village to Edison's New York

Nikola Tesla was born in 1856 in Smiljan, a small village in what is now Croatia but was then part of the Austrian Empire. His family were ethnic Serbs; his father was a priest, and his mother, though she never went to school, built clever household devices of her own. From childhood Tesla showed a strange and dazzling mind: he could perform complex calculations instantly and could picture entire machines in his head, fully formed, before he ever drew them.

He studied engineering and physics but left without finishing a degree, then worked in the new electrical industry in Europe. There, an idea took hold of him that would define his life — a motor driven not by direct current but by alternating current. In 1884 he emigrated to the United States with almost nothing in his pockets, and went to work for Thomas Edison, the most famous inventor in America.

It did not last. Edison had built his empire on direct current (DC), and had little interest in the younger man's strange new ideas. The two fell out — by Tesla's account, over a promised payment that never came — and within a year Tesla had walked away. For a time he was so poor that he dug ditches for a living. But he was certain his current would one day light the world, and he refused to give the dream up.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did Tesla come from, and what kind of mind did he have as a child?

2. Why did Tesla and Edison fall out so quickly?

1. He was born in 1856 in Smiljan (now in Croatia) into an ethnic Serb family. As a child he had an extraordinary mind — instant calculations and the ability to picture whole machines fully formed in his head.

2. Edison's empire was built on direct current and he had little interest in Tesla's alternating-current ideas; they also clashed over a promised payment Tesla said never came, so Tesla left within a year.

Discuss:
  • Tesla arrived in a new country with almost nothing, sure that his idea would change the world. What does it take to keep believing in an idea when no one else will pay for it?

Part 2 — The War of the Currents

Free of Edison, Tesla developed his great invention: the alternating-current induction motor, a machine that could turn AC electricity into useful work. The genius of AC was that it could be sent at high voltage along thin wires across great distances, then stepped down for safe use — exactly what Edison's DC could not do. The industrialist George Westinghouse saw the potential, bought the rights to Tesla's patents, and put his company behind the new system.

What followed became known as the "War of the Currents." Edison fought back hard, insisting that alternating current was lethally dangerous, and staging grim public demonstrations to frighten people away from it. But the physics was on Tesla's side. The decisive moment came at Niagara Falls: in the 1890s, engineers chose Tesla's AC system to harness the enormous power of the falling water and send electricity all the way to the city of Buffalo. It worked. Tesla had been sure the world would one day run on his current, and now, visibly, it did.

Tesla's mind raced on far ahead of everyone else's. He built the high-voltage "Tesla coil," experimented with radio and X-rays, and in 1898 astonished a New York crowd by steering a small boat across a pool using only a wireless remote control — something most onlookers could barely believe was real. He was no longer just an inventor; he was a showman of the future, demonstrating tomorrow's world a century early.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the "War of the Currents," and what decided it?

2. Name two things, besides AC power, that Tesla pioneered.

1. It was the battle between Tesla's alternating current (backed by Westinghouse) and Edison's direct current to power the world. AC's ability to travel long distances won; the turning point was the Niagara Falls project of the 1890s, which used AC to power Buffalo.

2. Any two of: the Tesla coil, radio experiments, X-rays, and a wireless remote-controlled boat (demonstrated in 1898).

Discuss:
  • Edison tried to win the argument by scaring people rather than by improving his technology. Why do good ideas sometimes need a fight to win — and is the best idea always the one that wins?

Part 3 — The tower, the fall and the long echo

Tesla's ambition kept growing. On Long Island he began building Wardenclyffe Tower, a vast structure meant to transmit not just messages but electrical power itself through the air, wirelessly, to anywhere on the planet — free energy for the whole world. He believed wireless power was going to change everything. But the science was unproven, his backer J. P. Morgan lost faith and stopped the money, and the project collapsed. The unfinished tower was eventually torn down for scrap.

From there Tesla's life slowly came apart. Other inventors moved faster and patented his ideas first; his money drained away. He grew ever more eccentric — terrified of germs, obsessive about numbers, and famously devoted to the pigeons he fed in New York's parks, one of which he said he loved like a person. He spent his last years living quietly in a hotel, increasingly alone. When he died in January 1943, in Room 3327 of the New Yorker Hotel, he was largely forgotten and close to penniless.

And yet the story did not end there. In the decades after his death, Tesla's reputation was revived. The world realised that the lonely man in the hotel had imagined the wireless, electrical age long before it arrived. Today his name is on power systems, on a unit of magnetic measurement, on one of the most famous car companies on Earth — and on the imaginations of people everywhere who admire the dreamer who saw too far ahead of his time.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Wardenclyffe Tower meant to do, and why did it fail?

2. How did Tesla die — and what happened to his reputation afterwards?

1. It was meant to transmit electrical power (and messages) wirelessly through the air to anywhere on Earth. It failed because the science was unproven and his backer, J. P. Morgan, withdrew the funding, so the project collapsed.

2. He died in 1943, largely forgotten and almost penniless, in a New York hotel. In the decades afterwards his reputation was revived, and he is now celebrated worldwide as a visionary.

Discuss:
  • Tesla died poor and forgotten, then became world-famous decades later. Is that a tragedy, a triumph, or both? What does it say about how we judge people in their own lifetime?

The grammar this story runs on: the future in the past

Tesla's life is one long story of looking forward — but we are telling it from the past. To say what someone, back then, expected to happen next, English has a special set of forms: the future in the past.

The three tools:
• was/were going to — a past intention or expectation: "he believed wireless power was going to change everything."
• would — a prediction made from a past point of view: "Tesla was sure the world would one day run on his current."
• was/were about to — something on the very edge of happening: "the project was about to collapse."

1. was/were going to — past plans and expectations

Use this for what someone intended or expected at the time — whether or not it actually happened.

1. Tesla was certain that his motor (change) industry forever.

2. Morgan had promised the money, so Tesla believed the tower (be) built.

3. The young inventor was sure he (light) the whole world with alternating current.

1. was going to change

2. was going to be

3. was going to light

2. would — predicting from a past point of view

When you are telling the past and you reach for what someone then believed about the future, use would + verb.

1. Tesla was sure the world (one day / run) on his current.

2. He dreamed of a tower that (send) free power across the planet.

3. Few people guessed that, decades later, his reputation (return).

1. would one day run

2. would send

3. would return

3. Put them together — narrate a paragraph

Fill each gap with the future-in-the-past form the meaning needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

By 1901 Tesla was sure that wireless power (going to / transform) the world. He believed his tower (one day / light) cities for free, and he told Morgan the work (going to / succeed). But the money was about to run out — the great project (about to / collapse), and the future he had imagined (never / arrive) in his lifetime.

was going to transform · would one day light · was going to succeed · was about to collapse · would never arrive

Notice how each form points forward from a moment in the past: an intention, a prediction, and an event right on the edge of happening.
