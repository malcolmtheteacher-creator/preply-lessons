Today: who, which, that and where — so you can describe people, things and places in longer, more natural sentences.

Describing People: "...who..."

Adding extra information about a person, right inside the sentence.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences about people:

  • "She's the neighbour who feeds our cat when we're away."
  • "I've got a friend who works at the hospital."
  • "That's the man who fixed our boiler last month."

Before you check: what comes right after "who" in every sentence?

Check the pattern CLICK
who + a verb (feeds, works, fixed) — straight away, no extra pronoun. "Who" already means "the neighbour" or "the man", so English never repeats it: not "who he feeds", just "who feeds".
person + who + verb ...

From two sentences to one

"I have a colleague. She speaks four languages."
→
"I have a colleague who speaks four languages."

The second sentence disappears — "who" does its job instead, and the two ideas become one natural sentence.

Describing Things: "...which..." / "...that..."

The same idea, but for objects, places you can't visit yet, and other things.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences about things:

  • "That's the film which won the prize."
  • "This is the café that serves the best coffee in town."
  • "I bought a bag which has a broken zip."

Same question: what comes right after "which" or "that"?

Check the pattern CLICK
A verb, straight away — same as "who". The only difference is the word itself: which or that point back to a thing, never a person.
thing + which / that + verb ...
Useful to know: "that" is flexible — in everyday conversation it can replace both "which" (things) and "who" (people): "the man that fixed our boiler" sounds completely natural. But "which" only ever works for things, never for people.

From two sentences to one

"I've got a phone. It has a cracked screen."
→
"I've got a phone which has a cracked screen."

Describing Places: "...where..."

For talking about a place without repeating "in it" or "at it".

Spot the pattern

  • "That's the café where we met."
  • "This is the school where I studied."
  • "It's the park where the market happens on Sundays."

What's different here — is there a preposition like "in" or "at" anywhere after "where"?

Check the pattern CLICK
No — where already means "in that place" or "at that place", so no extra preposition is needed. After "where", the sentence continues completely normally: subject + verb.
place + where + subject + verb ... (no extra preposition)

From two sentences to one

"I work in this building."
→
"This is the building where I work."

"In" disappears completely — "where" is already doing that job.
