Today's aim: Today we're working on explaining why two observers can disagree about time without either being wrong, so you can state precisely what is relative in relativity and what is not.

Words you'll need

relative (adj) — depending on who is measuring or on what you compare it with. Example: "Speed is relative: it depends on what you measure it against."
absolute (adj) — the same for everyone, whatever their position or motion. Example: "Is there an absolute standard of time?"
observer (n) — a person or instrument that measures events. Example: "Two observers moving differently can record different times."
simultaneous (adj) — happening at exactly the same time. Example: "The two flashes seemed simultaneous to the person on the platform."
frame (n) — the point of view, with its own motion, from which measurements are made. Example: "In the train's frame, the clock is at rest."
dilation (n) — a stretching or expanding; in physics, the slowing of a moving clock. Example: "Time dilation has been confirmed with atomic clocks."
invariant (adj) — not changing, whatever the observer. Example: "The speed of light is invariant."
paradox (n) — a situation that seems impossible or self-contradictory but may be true. Example: "The twin paradox seems to contradict itself until you compare the journeys."
causal (adj) — relating to cause and effect. Example: "A causal order is one on which every observer agrees."
synchronise (v) — to make clocks show the same time. Example: "You cannot synchronise distant clocks without sending a signal."

The hook: two clocks, one journey

In 1971, scientists flew atomic clocks around the world on airliners and compared them with identical clocks left on the ground. The travelling clocks came back showing slightly different times, by tens to hundreds of billionths of a second, just as Einstein's theory predicts.

Two identical clocks, one journey, two different totals. Neither clock was faulty. So what, exactly, is "time"?

Say it aloud: Before you read on, say what it means for a clock to be "right". Right according to whom?

Roughly: each clock correctly records the time along its own path. The surprise is that there is no single "universal now" that every clock should agree with. The next tabs show why, and which things everyone does still agree on.

Statements that compare measurements across different motions (speed, length, timing of distant events) can differ. Statements about the events themselves, or the order of cause and effect, are agreed by everyone. The borderline case: each clock's own elapsed time along its journey is agreed, but two different journeys can give two different totals.

Three ideas, in small steps

Idea 1 · The speed of light is the same for everyone

Measured by anyone, whatever their own motion, light in a vacuum travels at about 300,000 kilometres per second. If you chase a beam of light, it still recedes from you at that speed. Careful experiments, beginning with Michelson and Morley in 1887, found no sign of any other value.

Idea 2 · Moving clocks run slow

Picture a clock made of a light pulse bouncing between two mirrors. If the clock moves sideways, the pulse must travel a longer diagonal path, yet the speed of light cannot change. So each tick takes longer. As seen by someone who is not moving with it, a moving clock ticks more slowly. Fast-moving particles called muons, which survive far longer than expected, confirm the effect.

Idea 3 · There is no universal "now"

Because of this, two observers moving differently can disagree about whether two distant events were simultaneous. What they never disagree about is the order of cause and effect. Strictly speaking, relativity does not say that "everything is relative": it identifies what is relative (timing, length) and what is not (the speed of light, causal order).

What you just saw

1 · Same light speed
In both clocks the light moves at exactly the same speed.
2 · A longer path
In the moving clock the pulse must travel diagonally, so it covers more distance on each tick.
3 · So fewer ticks
The same speed over a longer path takes longer, so the moving clock ticks more slowly, as seen from outside.

Both clocks work perfectly. They simply measure time along different paths.

Does the present moment really exist?

If observers disagree about what is simultaneous, then "the present" looks different for each of them. Does that make the present an illusion? Three families of answer are on the table:

PositionSupporters argue…Critics counter…
A · Block universePast, present and future are all equally real, and "now" is only a point of view, just as "here" is.It seems to leave no room for the felt flow of time or for a genuinely open future.
B · A preferred presentOnly what exists now is real, which fits ordinary experience.Relativity offers no way to pick out one "now" for the whole universe, so it needs extra structure that cannot be tested.
C · Growing blockThe past and present are real, and the future is added as time passes.It still needs a universal "now" at the leading edge, which meets the same difficulty.

Precision toolkit: separating what is relative from what is not

Specifying the frame

· As measured from …

· Relative to …
Challenging a slogan
· That overstates it: only … is relative.
· It's not that …; rather, …
· Strictly speaking, …
· That conflates … with …
Rewrite it: rewrite each overstated claim so that it is accurate and carefully hedged. Attempt first, then compare.

1. "Einstein showed that everything is relative."

2. "A moving clock is broken, because it runs slow."

3. "Relativity proves that the future already exists."

1. Relativity shows that some quantities, such as the timing of distant events and the length of fast-moving objects, depend on the observer, while others, such as the speed of light, do not.
2. A moving clock is not faulty: it correctly measures the time along its own path, and it appears slow only as measured from another frame.
3. Some philosophers argue that relativity supports a block universe in which the future exists, but that conclusion is disputed and does not follow directly from the physics.
