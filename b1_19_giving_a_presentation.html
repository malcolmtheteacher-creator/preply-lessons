What Makes a Good Presentation?

Presentations are common in school, work, and business. They help us share ideas, teach others, and communicate confidence about a topic.

What This Lesson Covers

In this lesson, you will:

  • Practice phrases for delaying and managing interruptions
  • Understand how audiences ask questions and clarify understanding

Presentation Dos and Don"ts

What should you DO during a presentation?

  • Make eye contact with your audience
  • Speak clearly and at a good pace
  • Use pauses for emphasis
  • Be prepared for questions
  • Handle interruptions politely

What should you AVOID?

  • Reading directly from notes or slides
  • Speaking too fast or mumbling
  • Ignoring audience interruptions rudely
  • Getting defensive when questioned
  • Losing your place or getting flustered

Presentation Vocabulary

When giving a presentation, you"ll encounter these key concepts. Click on each word to see its meaning:

Interrupt
Meaning: Break in while someone is speaking. When an audience member asks a question during your presentation instead of waiting until the end.
Clarify
Meaning: Make something clearer or easier to understand. When you explain something in a different way or with more detail.
Deal with
Meaning: Handle or manage a situation. For example, "I"ll deal with that question later" means you"ll answer it at a different time.
Get back to
Meaning: Return to a topic later. If you don"t answer a question immediately, you promise to return to it: "I"ll get back to that point in a moment."
Respond
Meaning: Give an answer or reaction to something. How you reply to a question or comment.
Point out
Meaning: Draw attention to something. To show or mention something specifically: "Can I point out that..." means you want to emphasize something important.
Follow up
Meaning: Continue with a related question or topic. If an audience member has another question on the same topic, they might say "I have a follow-up question."
Wrap up
Meaning: Finish or conclude. To end your presentation: "Let me wrap up by saying..." means you"re finishing your main points.

Key Phrases for Presentations

1. Delaying Phrases: How to Manage Interruptions

When someone interrupts you during a presentation, use these phrases to politely ask them to wait:

"Thanks, Jan, but I was planning to deal with this point later."
Use this when someone asks a question about something you"ll explain soon.
"Would that be OK?"
Use this after delaying a question. It"s polite and invites agreement.
"OK, so as I was saying..."
Use this to smoothly return to your point after an interruption.
"Would you mind if I take questions at the end?"
Use this at the beginning if you want to prevent interruptions throughout your presentation.

2. Audience Phrases: How to Understand Questions

How Audiences Check Understanding
"Did you say...?"
Audience member is checking if they heard you correctly. Example: "Did you say the project costs £5,000 or £50,000?"
"Sorry, I"m not clear about what you mean."
Audience member needs clarification. They don"t understand part of your presentation.
How Audiences Signal Questions
"I"d like to ask about..."
Polite way to introduce a question. Example: "I"d like to ask about the timeline for this project."
"My question is this..."
Direct way to ask a question. Shows the question is important. Example: "My question is this: how will you measure success?"

3. Presenter Phrases: How to Respond

When You Agree with the Question
"OK, I see your point."
You understand and accept what they"re saying. Use this when the question or comment is valid.
"That"s a really good question."
Praise the question. This encourages more audience participation and buys you time to think if you need it.
"Yes, I see what you mean."
Express understanding. Acknowledge their concern or point.
When You Disagree with the Question
"I see your point, but..."
Diplomatic way to disagree. You validate their view, then explain your perspective. Example: "I see your point, but our research shows something different."
"That might be true, but..."
Another diplomatic disagreement. You concede part of their argument, then redirect. Example: "That might be true, but it wasn"t relevant to our specific situation."

Practice: Give Your Own Presentation

You"re going to give a short presentation about a product you own and handle live questions and interruptions.

Step 1: Choose Your Product

Think of something you own and use regularly: a phone, a laptop, a watch, a camera, a car, a piece of furniture, headphones, a kitchen gadget, clothing, or sports equipment.

What You Should Prepare

  • What is it? Name and type of product
  • Where did you get it? Shop, online, gift, etc.
  • How long have you had it? Days, months, years
  • What do you use it for? Main functions or purposes
  • Why do you like it? Benefits, features, what makes it special
  • What are its strengths? Positives about the product
  • What are its weaknesses? Anything that could be better

Step 2: 2-Minute Preparation

Make notes about your product using the checklist above. Think about how you"ll explain it clearly.

Preparation Time

2:00

Step 3: 3-Minute Presentation

Give your presentation about the product. Cover the key points: what it is, what you use it for, why you like it, and its strengths and weaknesses.

Speaking Time

3:00

Step 4: Handle Questions

After your presentation, you"ll answer questions. Remember to:

  • Listen carefully to each question
  • If you don"t understand: "Sorry, I"m not clear about what you mean. Can you explain?"
  • If you need time to think: "That"s a really good question..."
  • If you agree: "I see your point" or "Yes, I see what you mean"
  • If you disagree diplomatically: "I see your point, but..." or "That might be true, but..."
  • Stay calm and professional

✓ Remember: Presentations are about sharing knowledge. Audiences ask questions because they"re interested!

Real-World Applications

When would you give a presentation in real life?

School, university, work, community meetings, or personal projects. Being able to handle questions is a valuable skill in all these situations.

Presentations are one of the most common communication tasks in professional and academic life. Handling interruptions and questions gracefully shows confidence, respect for your audience, and strong communication skills.
