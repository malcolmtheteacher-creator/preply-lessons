The Performance Review

Twenty minutes with your manager. Two-thirds of it is genuinely good — and then there's the other third, which is half right, half wrong, and entirely uncomfortable. You also want something from this room before you leave it.

The Scene 🎬

Small meeting room, glass wall, the blind half down. Your annual review. Claire has a page of notes she keeps flat on the table so you can't read it upside down, and twenty minutes before her next call.

Most of it is good. Genuinely good — not the polite kind. And then, about eight minutes in, comes the one criticism, and it lands somewhere just under the ribs. The worst part isn't that it's wrong. The worst part is that it's half right: the shape of it is fair, and you've quietly known it for a year — but the example she's chosen to prove it is the one occasion when you were actually right. So now you have two things to do at once, in a room where you feel about eleven years old: take the true part without falling apart and without going on the defensive, and separate it out loud from the part that isn't true, without turning twenty minutes into a courtroom.

And there's a third thing, which is the real reason your heart is going: you want something this year. A bigger project. A step up. Which means you have to absorb a criticism and then, roughly ninety seconds later, ask the same person to bet on you.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of the last time someone criticised your work to your face. What did you do in the first three seconds — and honestly, was it any use to you?
  2. Which is harder for you: being told something true that you didn't want to hear, or being told something that's nearly true but with the facts slightly wrong? Why does the second one make people so much angrier?

Who's who

You
Good at the Job, New to This Room 💼

You do strong work and you know it. What you've never learned is how to be told you're wrong — you either go quiet and agree with everything, or you defend the detail and lose the room. Today you do neither. And you leave having asked for the thing.

The other voice
Claire, Your Manager 📋

Direct, not cruel, and short of time. She's given the criticism real thought — she's simply picked the wrong evidence for it. She can be moved by facts, but she won't be flattered or argued out of something she believes. Push well and she'll listen; push badly and she'll write you down as someone who can't take it.

First: read the conversation in the next tab — but read it as a detective. Find the exact line where You could easily have got defensive and didn't. Mark it. Then work out what You does instead in that moment: what comes out of the mouth in place of the defence, and what that small substitution buys for the rest of the twenty minutes.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Watch the order things happen in: what gets agreed with, and when, and how much later the disagreement arrives.

ClaireRight — sit down. Twenty minutes. Most of this is good news, so you can stop looking like that.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Claire names the deck, and You says almost nothing for a moment. What comes out of the mouth in that gap — and what would have come out instead if the gap hadn't been there? Why would that have cost the whole rest of the meeting?

2. You agree with Claire before disagreeing with her — but the agreement isn't about the deck at all. So what exactly is being agreed to, and why does putting it first change how the disagreement lands ninety seconds later?

3. You produce a genuinely good fact — the brief changed on the Thursday — and Claire still doesn't fully back down. What does she keep hold of, and what does You do with the bit she keeps?

1. Out comes a request for three seconds, not an answer. What would have come out otherwise is the Thursday — the fact, fired straight back — and fired that early it isn't a fact, it's an excuse. Everything said afterwards, including the honest part, would have sounded like a person defending themselves. Those three seconds are what let the true part come out of your mouth before the objection does.
2. The agreement is about the pattern — holding work back until it's finished. That's the shape of Claire's complaint, and it's true, and it's admitted without being asked twice. Once that's on the table, the disagreement about the deck can only be read as someone getting the facts right — not as someone who can't be told anything. You've already proved you can be told.
3. She keeps the silence — not nine o'clock, but nine o'clock with no warning. And she's right, and the fact about Thursday doesn't touch it. You concedes it instantly ("that's fair"), points out it's the cheaper repair, and then turns the whole thing forward by asking what better would look like by the summer — which converts a complaint into a standard, and a standard is something you can be seen to hit.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the twenty minutes a year that decide how you're described when you're not in the room. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. This lesson is almost entirely voice. Every phrase below can be said two ways, with the same words and completely different results. "That's not how I remember it" — said low, slow, and half a second late — is a grown adult in a room. The same seven words said fast, up at the end, with the pitch climbing, is a teenager at a kitchen table. Nothing in the vocabulary changed. What changed was pace and pitch: slower than feels natural, flatter than feels natural, and always, always a beat of silence first. If you're speaking quickly here, you're defending. Slow it down and the same sentence becomes a statement of fact.

🎬 Setting the tone

Phrase 1

Claire hasn't started yet. You have one sentence to change what she's about to do — because managers soften things by default, and a softened review teaches you nothing. All three of these ask for honesty. Only one of them will actually get you a different meeting. Which — and what's wrong with the other two?

A. "Before you start — feel free to be honest with me."
B. "Before you start — I'd rather have the real version than the kind version."
C. "Before you start — don't worry, I can take it."
B — "Before you start — I'd rather have the real version than the kind version."
It works because it names the two options and chooses. A is the sentence everybody says and nobody means; a manager hears it as noise and softens anyway. C is about you and your feelings, which is exactly what makes her softer, not straighter. B tells her that kindness will be received as a disappointment — and that's the only thing that actually changes what she says next. Note the pairing: real against kind, as if they were rival products and you're picking one off the shelf.

🎯 Taking it properly

Phrase 2

She's said something vague — "you're hard to work with sometimes". You can't accept it and you can't argue with it, because there's nothing there yet. You need the film, not the label. Put it in order (two sentences):

the same thing you are.Can you give meI want to be picturingan example?
"Can you give me an example? I want to be picturing the same thing you are."
The second sentence is doing the real work. "Can you give me an example?" on its own can sound like a challenge — prove it. Add the reason, and it becomes cooperation: you're not asking her to justify herself, you're asking to see what she saw. "Picturing" is deliberate — it asks for a scene with a date and a room in it, and vague criticism usually dies the moment it has to become a scene. And if it doesn't die, you've now got something specific enough to actually fix.
Phrase 3

The criticism lands. You feel the heat go up your neck and there is a sentence already halfway out of your mouth. Do not let it out — take three seconds instead, out loud, so the silence looks deliberate rather than wounded. The verb here is one you'd normally use with a chair. Complete it: "Let me ___ ___ that for a second."

"Let me sit with that for a second."
To sit with something is to stay next to a difficult thought without doing anything about it — not solving it, not arguing with it, just letting it be in the room. That's why it's the perfect verb here: it tells her you're taking it seriously, and it buys you the three seconds in which your first answer — the defensive one — dies quietly of old age. These are the most useful three seconds of your career. Almost every review that goes badly goes badly in the first sentence after the criticism.
Phrase 4

Here's the hinge of the whole thing. Her criticism is two-thirds right. You're going to disagree with a third of it — but not yet. Which opening makes her most likely to actually hear the disagreement when it comes?

A. "I think that's a little unfair, actually."
B. "Okay. Understood. I'll work on it."
C. "The part I recognise is the last-minute thing. That's real, and it's mine."
C — "The part I recognise is the last-minute thing. That's real, and it's mine."
This disarms a manager completely, and here's the mechanism: she came in braced. She has spent the morning expecting you to defend yourself, and the whole second half of her page is written for that fight. You hand her agreement instead — specific, unforced agreement about the true part — and the fight she prepared for has nowhere to go. Now anything you say afterwards is the opinion of someone who has just proved they can be told the truth. The part I recognise is… also does something clever and quiet: by naming which part you recognise, you've marked out the part you don't, without contradicting a single word yet. A is a fight in sentence one. B is surrender, and surrender teaches her you can't be talked to honestly — you just fold — so next year she'll soften everything, and you'll learn nothing.

🤝 Pushing back without a fight

Phrase 5

Now the disagreement. It has to be small, slow and low — a hand on a door, not a shoulder against it. Put it in order:

isgently,Where I'dpush back,
"Where I'd push back, gently, is…"
To push back is to resist something being asked or claimed — a firm, everyday business word, and much less aggressive than "disagree", because it's about the idea, not the person. Three things are doing the diplomacy. "Where" limits it: one specific spot, not the whole review. "I'd" makes it conditional — softer than "I do". And "gently" — an adverb about your own tone, dropped in mid-sentence — is you telling her how to hear the next ten words before you say them. Say it slowly; the comma before "gently" is a real pause, not decoration.
Phrase 6

You have a fact she doesn't have. Fired, it's an excuse; offered, it's information. The trick is one word. Not "saying", not "mentioning", not "adding" — you want the verb that puts the fact quietly into her head instead of into your mouth. Complete it: "It might be worth ___ that…"

"It might be worth knowing that…"
Look at what the grammar has done. "It might be worth mentioning" is about you speaking. "It might be worth knowing" is about her knowing — the sentence is now a small gift, offered across the table, that she is free to pick up. There's no person in it to argue with. Add "might" and "worth" and the whole thing is deniable: you're not correcting her, you're leaving something useful on the desk. Then say the fact flat, with no adjectives and no injured tone, and let it do its own work.

🌱 Turning it into something

Phrase 7

The complaint has been made and half-answered. Now do the thing almost nobody does: turn it forward. Two things have to be in this question — a word in quotation marks, because you're borrowing her standard rather than inventing your own, and an end date, because a complaint with no date just gets repeated next year. Put it in order:

look likeWhat wouldby the summer?'better'
"What would 'better' look like by the summer?"
This one sentence converts a criticism into a task, and a task is something you can be seen to finish. Notice the two moves. The quotation marks around "better" force her to define it in her own words — vague complaints have to become visible behaviour, and if she can't describe it, it wasn't a real complaint. And "by the summer" gives it an end. Without a date, a criticism just sits there forever and gets repeated next year; with a date, it's a thing you can walk in and say you've done.

💼 Asking for what you want

Phrase 8

Ninety seconds after being criticised, you have to ask her for the bigger job. All three of these ask. Which one opens a negotiation between equals — and what exactly is wrong with the other two?

A. "I was wondering if there might possibly be a chance of something a bit bigger this year?"
B. "There's something I'd like to put on the table for this year."
C. "Everyone else has been given the big projects."
B — "There's something I'd like to put on the table for this year."
To put something on the table is to formally offer it up for discussion — the image is a negotiation, two people, one table, an item placed on it that now has to be dealt with. That's the frame you want: not a request being granted from above, but an item in a conversation. It also announces itself half a second before it arrives, which stops the ask from sounding like an afterthought muttered on the way out. A buries the ask under so much hedging that she can miss it entirely and both of you can pretend it didn't happen. C is a complaint dressed as an ambition, and it makes you smaller as it leaves your mouth.
Phrase 9

She hasn't said no — she's said it isn't hers alone to decide, which is where most people say "of course, no problem" and lose the year. Don't accept the softness. Make her name the test. Put it in order:

in the frame for that?to beWhat would I needto show you
"What would I need to show you to be in the frame for that?"
To be in the frame for something is to be one of the people seriously considered for it — the picture is a photograph: you're either in the shot or you're outside it. It's a beautifully modest ask. You're not asking for the job, you're asking to be considered for the job, and it's very hard to refuse someone that. And "show you" is the load-bearing word: not "do", not "prove" — show makes her the audience and turns the whole thing into evidence. Her answer, whatever it is, is a list of things you can go and do. If she can't produce a list, that itself is your answer, and worth knowing in June rather than December.

🚪 Closing

Phrase 10

She's finished. She could have given you twenty comfortable minutes and a good lunch, and instead she gave you the difficult third, which took her more effort and more nerve. Close the meeting in two sentences: thank her for the honesty in a way that makes her more likely to do it again next year, and name what you'd rather have. Say it out loud, then reveal.

"Thank you for being straight with me. I'd rather this than a pat on the back."
To be straight with someone is to tell them the unsoftened truth; a pat on the back is the small, cheap, physical gesture of praise you give someone on the way past — it means approval that costs nothing and tells you nothing. Putting them side by side does two jobs at once. It thanks her for the hard part specifically rather than for the meeting generally. And it books next year: managers give honesty to the people who received the last lot well, and quietly stop giving it to everyone else. This sentence is how you stay on the first list. Say it walking to the door, lightly. Heavy is worse.

🔑 Make them yours

These are no use as general phrases. Pin them to the real conversation that's coming — the real person, the real thing they're going to say — and say each answer out loud until it sounds bored rather than rehearsed.

The criticism you're most afraid of hearing this year — the one that's a bit true. Write it in their words, not your kind ones. Then write the part of it you'd genuinely recognise.

The fact they don't have. Write it flat, in one sentence, with no adjectives and nothing that sounds hurt — then work out how you'd offer it rather than fire it.

The thing you want this year but haven't said out loud to anyone who can give it to you. Name it, and name the date you'll ask.

Rules: no preamble, no apologising for it, and when they land a good fact — and they will — don't collapse and don't pretend it didn't happen. Keep the shape, give up the detail.

The Twist

🎯 It didn't come from her

Back in your own chair, and run it once more — except this time, when you offer your beautiful fact about the Thursday, Claire says: "I should tell you it isn't my observation. It came from someone on your team. And no, I'm not going to say who." Everything you'd prepared is now useless. There's nobody to correct, no meeting to re-describe, no fact that helps — and the person who said it is someone you'll sit next to tomorrow morning and be perfectly pleasant to. The disagreement is dead. So what do you ask instead? Start the timer and find the question that's actually worth asking now — and notice how badly you want to spend the four minutes trying to work out who it was. That instinct is the whole problem. Ask something better than "who".

Before you close the tab…

Go back over what you actually said out loud. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I asked for the honest version before she started — and meant it.
  • When the criticism landed, I took the silence instead of the first sentence in my mouth.
  • I said the true part out loud, specifically, before I touched the untrue part.
  • I disagreed about the facts without my voice speeding up or climbing.
  • I asked for what I wanted anyway — and left knowing exactly what I have to show her.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that'll cost you in the real room. Run the twist again and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Think about how rarely it happens. In a whole year, perhaps two people will tell you something true about yourself that you didn't already know — and one of them will be paid to. Everyone else has decided it isn't worth the twenty seconds of discomfort, and they're mostly right: it usually isn't, because of what people do with it.

We talk about being able to take criticism as if it were a temperament — some people are thick-skinned, some aren't, bad luck. It isn't. It's a skill, and it's made of about three seconds of silence and a habit of saying the true part out loud before you say anything else. Both of those can be learned in an afternoon, which is roughly what you've just spent.

And here's the strange part. The people who improve fastest aren't the ones with the most feedback — they're the ones who make it safe to be criticised. Handle it well once and something changes: people start telling you things. Handle it badly once and the room goes quiet around you, permanently and politely, and nobody ever tells you that's what happened.

Two bigger questions

  1. Almost everyone says they want honest feedback, and almost nobody gives it. If the wanting is real, why does the giving so rarely happen — and whose fault is that: the person who won't say it, or the person who once made it expensive to say?
  2. When a criticism is half true, we usually spend our energy on the wrong half — arguing the detail that's wrong instead of sitting with the part that's right. Why is the false half so much more attractive to fight about? And what would it cost you, honestly, to let a wrong detail go unchallenged in order to be told the truth more often?
