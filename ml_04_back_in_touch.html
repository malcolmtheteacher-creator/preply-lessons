Back in Touch

Two years. No falling out, no reason — it just stopped. You've drafted the message about six times and deleted it. Ringing is worse and better: worse for thirty seconds, better for everything after.

The Scene 🎬

Nobody did anything. That's what makes it hard to undo.

You and Ronan were in each other's lives constantly, and then he moved, and you had a bad year, and the gaps between messages got longer until they became a gap. There was no argument. There is nothing to apologise for, exactly — and that's the problem, because there's no obvious way back in.

So the awkwardness is entirely about the silence itself: two years is long enough to need acknowledging and short enough that pretending it hasn't happened would be strange.

And there's a second failure mode waiting at the end. Both of you will say "we must do this more often", mean it completely, and never do it — unless one of you turns it into a date before you hang up.

  1. Is there someone you've drifted from for no reason? What's actually stopped you ringing?
  2. Why do we say "we must catch up" and then not? What would make it happen?
YouTwo years of meaning to. Slightly embarrassed, and determined not to come off the phone without a date.
RonanAn old friend. Pleased and a bit wrong-footed. Just as guilty about the silence as you are.

1. Whether everything's alright — whether You is ringing with bad news. After a long silence, an unexpected call reads as an announcement, so saying early that it's "genuinely just this" is a kindness.

2. It "closed over" — nothing happened, the gap simply healed shut. Ronan says it first and You agrees, which is what makes it nobody's fault.

3. Names a specific date, tells him to put it in the diary there and then, and offers to travel. Each one removes a reason for it not to happen.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for getting back in touch after too long — and for turning a nice conversation into an actual meeting. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Warm and slightly self-deprecating, and fast at the start — the first ten seconds are the awkward ones and speed carries you through them. Do not be solemn about the gap. Solemn makes it a Thing that needs discussing; light makes it a fact you're both stepping over on the way to the good bit.

☎️ The first ten seconds

Phrase 1

The job: naming the awkwardness and making it funny before it can be uncomfortable. Which opening?

A. "Hi — it's me. Long time no speak."
B. "Please tell me I haven't left it so long that you've deleted my number."
C. "Hi! Sorry, I know it's been ages, I'm so sorry, I've been meaning to ring for months…"
B — "Please tell me I haven't left it so long that you've deleted my number."
A is fine but flat, and leaves the awkwardness sitting there. C opens with an apology spiral, which makes him reassure you in the first breath — you've handed him work. B admits the length of the silence, makes a joke of it, and asks him to reassure you about something trivial instead of something real. Everybody laughs and you're in.

Take it anywhere: any reconnection — a friend, a former colleague, a relative.
Phrase 2

The job: acknowledging the call is unexpected, without apologising for it. Complete it:

"I know I'm ringing a bit ___ ___ ___ ___."

"I know I'm ringing a bit out of the blue."
Out of the blue means unexpectedly, with no warning. This does the acknowledging job without the word sorry anywhere in it — you're naming the situation rather than seeking forgiveness for it. "A bit" keeps it light. Use it and then move straight on; don't leave a pause for him to reassure you.

Take it anywhere: "I know this is a bit out of the blue."

🤝 Dealing with the gap

Phrase 3

The job: taking your share of a two-sided silence without taking all of it. Complete it:

"Which is ridiculous, and that's on me as much as ___."

"That's on me as much as anyone."
Beautifully balanced. To say something is on you is to accept responsibility for it. "As much as anyone" accepts your full share while quietly noting it wasn't only yours — which is true, and which stops him having to either absolve you or accept blame himself. He'll almost always respond by taking his half, as Ronan does.

Take it anywhere: "That's on me as much as anyone." Any mutual lapse — a friendship, a project, a team.
Phrase 4

The job: describing how the friendship lapsed in a way that blames nobody. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Nothing happened — it just closed over."
The image does the work: something closing over is nobody's action. It's the accurate description of most lost friendships, and saying it out loud is a relief to both people, because each has privately wondered whether they did something. Get this said early and the rest of the call is free.

Take it anywhere: "Nothing happened — it just closed over." / "Life sort of closed over the gap."
Phrase 5

The job: saying why you rang, warmly and without a reason attached. Complete it:

"You've genuinely been ___ ___ ___."

"You've genuinely been on my mind."
Simple and rare. Most people supply a pretext — they saw something, heard a song, bumped into a mutual friend — and a pretext makes the call an accident rather than a choice. This says you thought about him and acted on it, which is a much better thing to hear. "Genuinely" stops it sounding like a formula.

Take it anywhere: "You've been on my mind." Works in a call, a message, a card.
Phrase 6

The job: reassuring them there's no bad news — because after a long gap they'll assume there is. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Nothing awful, no. Everyone's fine. It's genuinely just this."
Say it before they ask if you can. An unexpected call after two years reads as an announcement — illness, a death, a divorce — and until that's cleared they aren't really listening. "It's genuinely just this" is also quietly lovely: the call has no purpose beyond itself, which is the nicest reason to ring anyone.

Take it anywhere: "Nothing's wrong — it's genuinely just this."

📅 Turning it into a date

Phrase 7

The job: naming the trap you're both about to fall into, then dodging it. Put the pieces in order:

and then not —Let's not do theare you free on the 20th?"we must catch up" thing
"Let's not do the 'we must catch up' thing and then not — are you free on the 20th?"
The whole lesson in one sentence. Naming the failure mode out loud makes it slightly funny and much harder to enact, and then the specific date lands immediately, before the moment passes. A date is a question that can be answered; "we should do this more" is a sentiment that can only be agreed with.

Take it anywhere: "Let's not do the 'we must catch up' thing and then not — are you free on ___?"
Phrase 8

The job: getting it into the diary while you're both still on the phone. Which version?

A. "I'll text you some dates in the week."
B. "Put it in now. I mean it this time — properly."
C. "Let me know what works for you and we'll sort something."
B — "Put it in now. I mean it this time — properly."
A and C both defer the commitment to a future action that neither of you will take — that's precisely how two years happened. B closes it inside the call. "Put it in now" is a small instruction, but it's the one that converts intention into a calendar entry, and "properly" acknowledges you've both said this before and meant it before.

Take it anywhere: "Put it in now." The four words that make plans real.
Phrase 9

The job: removing the last obstacle by taking on the effort yourself. Complete it:

"I'll ___ ___ you, it's easier."

"I'll come to you, it's easier."
Every plan has a small unresolved obstacle — who travels, who books, who organises — and unresolved obstacles are where plans quietly die. Taking it off him removes the last reason for the date to slide. "It's easier" is the polite fiction that lets him accept without feeling he owes you anything.

Take it anywhere: "I'll come to you." / "I'll sort it — it's easier."
Phrase 10

The job: saying the true thing before you hang up. Complete it:

"It's so good to have you back — I've missed this ___ ___ I realised."

"It's so good to have you back — I've missed this more than I realised."
"More than I realised" is what makes it believable — it's an admission rather than a compliment, and it's usually true: you don't know how much you missed a friendship until you're back inside it for ten minutes. Say it near the end, once, and don't build it up.

Take it anywhere: "I've missed this more than I realised."

The Echo 🕰️

Marco Polo left Venice as a teenager and came back twenty-four years later. The story goes that his own family didn't recognise him at the door — a middle-aged man in foreign clothes, speaking Venetian with something odd in it, claiming to be someone they had stopped expecting.

He had to prove it. He unpicked the seams of his travelling coat and let the rubies and emeralds fall out onto the table, and that was the moment the room understood who had just walked in.

Two years is not twenty-four and you don't need jewels. But the first thirty seconds of that phone call are the same thirty seconds — a person at a door that used to be theirs, hoping to be recognised.
