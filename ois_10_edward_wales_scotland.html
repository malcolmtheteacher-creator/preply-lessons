The Bridge: Edward I's Dream

Canute wanted to rule the waves — but the sea would not obey. Edward I (1272–1307), the son of Henry III, dreamed something different: he wanted to unite all of Britain under one crown. Wales was there. Scotland was there. Both would resist. And from that resistance came stories that Marshall told with such force they still echo.

Edward I became known as the "Hammer of the Scots" — a name that tells you everything about his ambition and the violence required to try to achieve it.

II. The Conquest of Wales

Edward I's first target was Wales. To understand why, remember that Wales had never fully accepted English rule. The Welsh princes ruled their own lands. Edward saw this as a challenge to his authority — and perhaps as an opportunity to prove his power.

Llywelyn ap Gruffudd: The Last Native Prince

The great Welsh leader was Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, Prince of Gwynedd. For a time, Llywelyn and Edward coexisted. But when Llywelyn refused to do homage to Edward (to acknowledge him as overlord), war became inevitable.

Edward was determined to be master not only of England, but of Scotland and Wales as well. And so he determined to conquer Wales and make Llywelyn do homage to him. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story (adapted from historical account)

The war lasted from 1276 to 1282. It was brutal. Marshall describes how Edward pursued Llywelyn relentlessly, cutting off his escape routes, surrounding his stronghold on the island of Snowdon.

At length Llywelyn, hemmed in on the island of Snowdon, with no way to escape, found his position hopeless. Food ran short. His men grew fewer. There was nothing left for him but to submit to Edward or to die. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story (adapted)

Llywelyn tried one final rebellion in 1282, but it ended in his death — either killed in battle or in a subsequent fight. Marshall's account emphasizes his defeat as the end of an era:

With Llywelyn's death, the last of the native Welsh princes fell. There would be Welsh nobles, Welsh lands, and Welsh pride — but never again a Prince of Wales who ruled as Llywelyn had ruled, free from English overlordship. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story (adapted)

Edward's Ring of Iron Castles

To hold Wales, Edward did something extraordinary: he built a ring of massive castles across the Welsh landscape. They were symbols of power, strongholds of English control, and architectural marvels.

The Iron Ring: Five Great Castles

Caernarfon Castle (1283) — built at the site where Edward wanted to establish his seat of power. The castle's distinctive polygonal towers echo the walls of Constantinople. Deliberately magnificent.

Conwy Castle (1283) — built on the coast, dominating the Conwy River estuary.

Harlech Castle (1283) — perched on a rocky crag, seemingly impregnable.

Beaumaris Castle (1295) — the last and most perfect of the concentric designs, on the isle of Anglesey.

These castles were not built for beauty alone. They were military instruments, designed by the brilliant military architect Master James of St George. They controlled roads, harbours, and supply lines. They were a message: you are conquered.

The historian's note: The "ring of castles" is sometimes called the "ring of iron." In 2021, Caernarfon, Conwy, Harlech, and Beaumaris castles were collectively designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognized as outstanding examples of medieval military architecture. What Marshall saw as symbols of domination, we now see as marvels of engineering and strategic brilliance — and as symbols of a deep historical wound that Wales has carried for centuries.

The Legend of Edward II

Here Marshall tells a famous story — possibly apocryphal, but told as if true:

Edward made a promise to the Welsh people: he would give them a prince who was born in Wales and spoke no English. Years later, Edward kept this promise, for he presented his newborn son, Edward of Caernarfon, born at Caernarfon Castle, as Prince of Wales. The child, of course, spoke only Welsh at first, having just been born. It was a clever trick — the kind Edward delighted in. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story (adapted)
The historian's note: This story is almost certainly not true as told. Edward II was born at Caernarfon, yes — but there's no evidence Edward I used this trick. Yet the story persists because it captures something true about Edward's cunning and about how power operates through words and symbols. Whether he did it or not, someone imagined that he might have — and that's interesting in itself.

III. Wallace, Bruce & Bannockburn

Having crushed Wales, Edward turned his ambition northward. Scotland had a king, and that king would not accept Edward as his overlord. What followed was a conflict that would define both nations.

William Wallace: The Blue-Faced Rebel (1297)

Marshall introduces William Wallace as a figure of fierce resistance. He was not a nobleman, not of royal blood. He was a knight, a fighter, a man who embodied Scottish defiance against English domination.

William Wallace rose from the common people, determined to free Scotland from Edward's grip. He gathered an army and marched to Stirling Bridge, where the English forces awaited. There, in a narrow place where numbers meant nothing, Wallace and his Scots crushed the invaders. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, adapted from historical sources

The Battle of Stirling Bridge (1297) was a shock to Edward. A relatively small Scottish force, led by Wallace, defeated a much larger English army. Marshall emphasizes the drama:

The English cavalry charged across the narrow bridge, convinced of easy victory. But the bridge could only hold so many horses at once. As the front ranks pressed forward, the Scots fell upon them with terrible force. Many were trampled by their own comrades as they tried to flee. The bridge became a place of death, and the Scots held the day. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, adapted

Wallace's victory did not last. Edward returned with renewed armies. Wallace was eventually captured, brought to London, and executed in 1305 with the brutal torture that the age reserved for traitors. But his resistance had shown Scotland that victory was possible.

The historian's note: Wallace's story became legend almost immediately. Marshall tells it as a tale of heroic resistance — and so it was. But she writes in 1905, and by then the real Wallace had already been transformed by generations of storytellers into the figure we know. The "Braveheart" of modern imagination owes as much to legend as to history.

Robert the Bruce: The Spider in the Cave

After Wallace's defeat, Scotland seemed lost. But a new leader emerged: Robert the Bruce, Earl of Carrick. Marshall tells the most famous story about him — the story of the spider.

Bruce had been defeated in battle and was hiding in a cave, apparently in despair. Legend says he watched a spider trying to build its web. The spider failed repeatedly, falling each time. But the spider did not give up.

Robert the Bruce lay in a cave, his cause seemingly lost. He was ready to give up, to abandon his dream of Scottish freedom. But as he lay there, he watched a spider. Again and again, the little creature tried to spin its web from one side of the cave to the other. Six times it failed and fell. But on the seventh attempt, the spider succeeded, spinning a perfect web across the gap. Bruce watched, and a new resolve filled him. "If a tiny spider can try seven times and succeed," he said to himself, "then I can try again. If it should be the will of God, I shall free my country." — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, adapted from the Bruce legends

Whether this story is literally true or not matters less than what it reveals: Bruce did not give up. He rallied his forces, defeated Edward II (Edward I's less capable son) at the Battle of Bannockburn in 1314, and secured Scottish independence.

The Battle of Bannockburn (1314)

Bannockburn was the decisive victory. An English army vastly outnumbering the Scots was broken and scattered. Marshall's account captures the drama:

The English cavalry, proud in their iron and their numbers, charged across the field. But the ground had been prepared with hidden pits and ditches. The horses stumbled and fell. The Scottish spearmen, standing in close formation, were unbreakable. And when Edward II's nerve failed and he fled the field, the English army collapsed. What had been a confident invasion became a rout. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, adapted

With Bannockburn, Scotland was free. The English would attempt many more invasions over the centuries, but they would never again control the entire island of Britain. The dream Edward I had harboured — to rule all of Britain as one kingdom — died on the fields of Bannockburn.

The historian's note: The Battle of Bannockburn is a symbol in Scottish history — perhaps more powerful in symbol than in its actual military significance. But symbols matter. The battle became shorthand for: we fought, we won, we are free. That story has sustained a nation through many dark centuries.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these reflection questions.

Did Edward I succeed or fail?

He conquered Wales. He failed to conquer Scotland. Is that success or failure? What does "success" mean in empire?

What makes the spider story powerful?

The spider in Bruce's cave may be legend, not history. Why do we keep telling this story? What does it teach us?

Why did Stirling Bridge matter so much?

One battle doesn't win a war. Yet Wallace's victory changed how Scotland saw itself. Why does one victory carry such symbolic weight?

The castles we build

Edward's castles were military instruments of domination. Now they're UNESCO heritage sites and tourist attractions. What changed?

Marshall's heroes

Marshall writes about Wallace and Bruce as heroes, yet she lived in an age of empire. How did she balance telling imperial and anti-imperial stories?

The cost of conquest

Thousands of people died in these wars. How do we honour them? Do they disappear into the story, or stay visible?
