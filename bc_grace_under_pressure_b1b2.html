Grace Under Pressure

How do the calmest people stay calm when everything is on the line — and what does a lifetime of experience really teach us?

Staying calm when it matters

Some people seem to get calmer when everything around them gets faster and louder. Where does that come from — and can it be learned? Let us start with you.

Let's talk:
  • Think of the calmest person you know. What are they like? What do they do that other people don't?
  • When was the last time you had to stay calm in a difficult moment? What helped you?
  • Do you think people are born calm, or do they learn it over many years?
  • In your work, how important is it to keep a cool head? What happens when someone loses it?

Two ways to react

When pressure hits, people usually do one of two things. Some freeze, panic, or rush. Others slow down, breathe, and think clearly — even though the clock is running. The second group is not braver or luckier. Very often, they have simply done it many times before.

Over to you:
  • Which group were you in when you were young? Has that changed over the years?
  • Is there a job or a moment where staying calm is the most important skill of all?

208 seconds that became famous

Here is a true story about staying calm. Read it, then we will talk about it.

On 15 January 2009, an experienced pilot named Chesley Sullenberger — everyone called him “Sully” — took off from an airport in New York. He had been flying planes for more than forty years.

About ninety seconds after take-off, a large group of birds hit the plane. Both engines stopped. Suddenly, a plane full of 155 people had no power. Below them was one of the biggest cities in the world.

Sully had almost no time — only a few minutes. But he did not shout or panic. He spoke quietly and clearly to the control tower. He quickly understood that he could not reach any airport. So he made an extraordinary decision: he would land the plane on the Hudson River.

Carefully, he brought the plane down onto the water. It worked. Every single person survived. The newspapers called it “the Miracle on the Hudson”.

But Sully did not like the word “miracle”. He said it was not luck at all. For forty-two years, he explained, he had been making small “deposits” into a bank — a bank of knowledge and experience. On that day, he simply made one big withdrawal.

It was not a miracle. It was a whole life of preparation, ready for one impossible moment.

Let's talk about it:
  • Sully said it was experience, not a miracle. Do you agree with him? Why?
  • He stayed calm and quiet. Why is that so important when there is no time?
  • You also have to make fast, important decisions in your work. Does Sully's story remind you of anything you have lived through?
  • What do you think is in your own “bank of experience” — things you know now that you could never have known at the start?

What helps people stay calm?

People who work in high-pressure jobs often say the same few things help them most. Which of these do you believe in?

Agree or disagree?
  • “Slow is smooth, and smooth is fast.” (Going slowly and carefully is often quicker in the end.)
  • “Train hard, so that the real moment feels easy.”
  • “You cannot control the situation, but you can control your own breathing and your own voice.”
  • “The team is calmer when the leader is calm.”

Quick grammar check — telling a true story

When we tell a story, we often use the past simple for the main actions, the past continuous for what was happening around them, and the past perfect for what happened earlier. Put the verb in the best form.

1. The pilot ____ (fly) planes for forty years before that day.

2. While the passengers ____ (relax), the birds hit the engines.

3. He ____ (stay) very calm and ____ (speak) quietly to the tower.

4. By the time help arrived, everyone ____ (leave) the plane safely.

1. had flown (past perfect — before that day)

2. were relaxing (past continuous — happening around the main event)

3. stayed … spoke (past simple — the main actions)

4. had left (past perfect — earlier than “help arrived”)

A · A time you stayed calm (5 minutes)

Tell me about a real moment when you had to stay calm under pressure. It can be from work or from your own life.

Try to tell it like a story: where you were, what was happening, what suddenly went wrong, what you decided, and how it ended. Use phrases like “I remember one time when…” and “all of a sudden…”.

B · Advice to a younger you (4 minutes)

Imagine a young person on their very first day in a difficult, high-pressure job. They are nervous and afraid of making mistakes.

What would you tell them? Give them your three best pieces of advice about staying calm and dealing with pressure. Use “If I were you, I would…” and “The most important thing is to…”.

C · Is experience the secret? (4 minutes)

Sully said his calm came from experience, not from luck. Do you agree?

Talk about something that experience has taught you — something you understand now that you simply could not have understood when you were young. What can only be learned by doing it, again and again, for years?
