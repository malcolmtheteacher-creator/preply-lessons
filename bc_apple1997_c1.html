Ninety Days from Empty

In 1997 Apple was losing a billion dollars a year and making dozens of products nobody could tell apart. The rescue began with a cheque from its biggest rival and a very short list.

Today: you'll work through one of the most famous turnarounds in business — and you'll practise the structures leaders use to put one idea in the spotlight: what we need is…, it was the software that…, all I'm asking for is…

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. The company sold desktop computers, laptops, printers, digital cameras, a handheld organiser and a games console, in dozens of versions with confusing numbers. Nobody in the shops could explain the product line.
a product line (n) — the full range of related products a company sells. Near-synonyms: product range, portfolio. Collocations: to extend / rationalise / cut back the product line.
2. Revenue was falling every quarter while the costs of running the business stayed high. At that rate of cash burn, the money in the bank would last only a few months.
cash burn (n) / burn rate — the speed at which a company uses up its cash when it is spending more than it earns. Common in start-up and turnaround language: to reduce the burn.
3. Warehouses were full of computers that had been built months earlier and were losing value every week they sat unsold. The new operations chief made cutting inventory his first priority.
inventory (n, uncountable) — stock: goods held by a company before they are sold. In technology, old inventory quickly becomes worthless, so days of inventory is a key efficiency measure. British English also uses stock.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: turnaround · licensing · insolvency. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means the situation of a company that cannot pay its debts when they are due?
insolvency (n) — adjective insolvent. Formal and legal; in conversation people say a company went bust or ran out of money. Bankruptcy is the legal process that often follows.
2. Which term means the process of bringing a failing business back to health and profit?
a turnaround (n) — a turnaround plan, a turnaround specialist. Verb: to turn a company around. Compare a comeback, which is less formal and more about reputation.
3. Which term means giving other companies the legal right to use your technology or brand, usually for a fee?
licensing (n) — to license (v, US/UK spelling) / a licence (n, UK) or license (US). In this case, other manufacturers were licensed to build computers that ran Apple's operating system — known as clones.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. After the chief executive left, the returning co-founder agreed to run the company as interim CEO while the board searched for someone permanent. Interim means…
  (a) acting temporarily, until a permanent arrangement is made   (b) sharing the role with another person   (c) working without a salary
(a) — interim (adj): an interim CEO, interim results (published before the full-year figures), in the interim (= meanwhile). Formal register.
2. The rival bought 150 million dollars of non-voting shares, so it could not influence decisions at shareholder meetings. Non-voting shares are…
  (a) shares that cannot be sold for several years   (b) shares owned only by employees   (c) shares that give part-ownership and a financial return but no say in company decisions
(c) — non-voting shares (n): the investor gets a financial stake but no control. Useful when a deal must reassure people that a competitor is not taking over.

D · Collocation & word building

1. Twelve models became four; whole divisions were closed. The company decided to ___line its range. Complete the verb — the first part is a word for a small river — and say what it suggests.
to streamline — to make an organisation or process simpler and more efficient, usually by removing parts. The image is of a shape that moves easily through water. Noun: streamlining. A softer word than cut, which is exactly why managers like it.
2. A new colourful all-in-one computer became the product that represented the whole company. It was the ___ship product. Complete the word, and give another business use of it.
flagship — originally the ship that carries the admiral. A flagship product, a flagship store (the biggest, most important shop, often in a capital city), a flagship programme.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — Too many computers, too little time

In 1985, Apple's co-founder Steve Jobs lost a boardroom battle and left the company he had started. Through the early 1990s the company that remained was profitable for a while, then steadily less so. Computers running Microsoft Windows took more and more of the market. Apple responded by making more products: many versions of the Macintosh with confusing model numbers, printers, early digital cameras, a handheld organiser called the Newton, even a games console. It also began licensing its operating system to other manufacturers, whose cheaper copies mainly took sales from Apple itself.

Underneath, the core problem was software. Apple's operating system was ageing, and a long, expensive project to replace it had failed. Three chief executives came and went in about four years. In late 1996, the company solved the software problem by buying Jobs's later company, NeXT, for roughly four hundred million dollars. The deal brought in a modern operating system — and brought back Jobs, officially as an adviser.

By mid-1997 Apple was losing money fast: roughly a billion dollars that financial year. The share price had collapsed, respected commentators were suggesting it should be sold or closed, and the chief executive was removed by the board in July. Jobs later said that the company had been within about ninety days of running out of money. He agreed to lead it on a temporary basis.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Apple's response to competition in the early 1990s make its problems worse?

2. What did the purchase of NeXT give the company, apart from software?

1. It multiplied products and model numbers, confusing customers and spreading resources thinly, and it licensed its operating system to clone makers whose cheaper machines took sales from Apple rather than from Windows.

2. It brought back the co-founder, Steve Jobs — first as an adviser, and within months as the temporary head of the company.

Discuss:
  • Adding products is often the first response to losing market share. When has your organisation — or one you know — tried to grow its way out of a problem that was really about focus?

Part 2 — A cheque and a grid

In August 1997, at a large Apple conference in Boston, Jobs announced a deal that shocked the audience. Microsoft — the rival many of them saw as the enemy — would invest 150 million dollars in non-voting Apple shares and commit to making its Office software for the Mac for at least five years. The two companies also settled a long patent dispute. When Bill Gates appeared on the giant screen by video link, parts of the audience booed. The share price jumped by around a third that day. The money mattered less than the message: the most important software for business customers was not going to disappear from the Mac.

Then came the cuts. Jobs ended the licensing programme and bought back the clone business of the largest licensed manufacturer. He replaced most of the board. And he reviewed the product line and found dozens of products and variations. He reduced the future to a simple grid of four boxes: computers for consumers and for professionals, each as a desktop and as a portable. Most existing products and projects were stopped, including the Newton and the printers. In 1998 a new operations chief, Tim Cook, joined and cut inventory dramatically, from months of stock to days.

In May 1998 the company presented the iMac: an all-in-one computer in translucent coloured plastic, designed for connecting to the internet at home. It was not technically revolutionary, and it removed the floppy-disk drive that many users still relied on. It sold extremely well. For the 1998 financial year, Apple was profitable again.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the Microsoft deal include, and why was its message more important than the money?

2. Describe the four-box grid. What happened to products that did not fit it?

1. A $150 million investment in non-voting shares, a commitment to produce Office for the Mac for at least five years, and a patent settlement. The message told customers and developers that essential business software would stay on the Mac, which reduced fear that the platform would die.

2. Consumer and professional computers, each in a desktop and a portable version. Products outside the grid — including the Newton handheld, printers and many Mac variants — were discontinued, and the clone licensing programme was ended.

Discuss:
  • Taking money from your main rival looked like humiliation to many loyal customers. When is accepting help from a competitor a sign of strength rather than weakness?

Part 3 — The long line and the size problem

The iMac was the start of a long run: the iPod in 2001, the online music store in 2003, the iPhone in 2007, the iPad in 2010. Jobs died in 2011, and Tim Cook, the operations chief who had cut the inventory, became chief executive. Under Cook, Apple became the first American company to be valued at a trillion dollars, and later at more than three trillion.

The turnaround became a legend with a simple moral: focus. Jobs himself liked to say that deciding what not to do was as important as deciding what to do. Business books reduced the story to the four-box grid. But the full picture has more parts. His predecessor had already started cutting costs and had made the NeXT purchase that rescued the software. Microsoft was not acting out of kindness: commentators have long pointed out that a living Apple was useful to a company already under pressure from competition regulators. And the cuts were made by a returning founder, with a kind of authority that very few hired chief executives ever have.

Today Apple is the opposite of a four-box company. It sells phones, computers, tablets, watches, headphones, a headset, and a large and growing business in services, from music and television to payments and cloud storage. In 2024 it reportedly cancelled a car project after about a decade of work — a very expensive no, but a no. So the question is live: is the lesson of 1997 that great companies must stay small in their ambitions? Or was it only a rule for emergencies — and can a company this large still say no often enough?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Give two reasons why focus alone may not explain the 1997 turnaround.

2. Why is Apple's current size a challenge to the lesson people usually take from 1997?

1. Any two of: the previous CEO had begun cuts and bought NeXT, whose software became the foundation of the new Mac system; Microsoft's investment and Office commitment gave vital time and confidence, and Microsoft had its own reasons for keeping Apple alive; the cuts were possible because a returning founder had unusual authority.

2. Apple now sells a very broad range of hardware and services, so the simple “four boxes” model no longer describes it; the question is whether focus was a permanent principle or an emergency measure, and whether a company of this size can still stop things.

Discuss:
  • In your organisation, who has the authority to stop a project that many people care about? Is that authority used often enough — or too often?

Grammar — cleft sentences for focus

A turnaround speech lives or dies on emphasis: the listener must know, within one sentence, what the real problem is and what matters now. Cleft sentences split one idea into two clauses so that the most important part lands in the spotlight. Used well, they sound decisive. Overused, they sound like a stage performance.

1 · It-cleft: It is/was + focus + that/who…
Puts one element in contrast with others, often implied.
• “It was the supply chain that failed, not the design.”
• “It's our largest customers who are asking for this.” (who for people; that is always possible)
• Focus on time or place: “It was in the third quarter that margins began to fall.”
2 · Wh-cleft: What + clause + be + focus
Builds a little suspense, then delivers the point at the end.
• “What this plan lacks is a deadline.”
• With an action, use do: “What the new team did was (to) close two regional offices.”
• Reversed for a strong ending: “A deadline is what this plan lacks.”
3 · Other spotlight frames
• All = only this and nothing more: “All we're proposing is a pilot.”
• The reason (why)… is that…: “The reason margins fell is that we discounted too early.”
• The thing / The last thing / The one thing…: “The one thing we can't afford is another delay.”

Plural focus: you will hear both “What we need is new products” and “What we need are new products.” In formal writing, is is the safer choice.

1. It-clefts — put one part in the spotlight

Complete the second sentence so that the part in bold is in focus. Write only the missing words.

1. The retailers, not the customers, complained first. → It complained first.

2. The board replaced the chief executive in July. → It that the board replaced the chief executive.

3. The software, not the price, lost us the market. → It lost us the market.

1. was the retailers who (or that)   2. was in July   3. was the software that

Say each one aloud and stress the focus word. The stress is half the meaning.

2. Wh-clefts and spotlight frames

Complete the second sentence so that it means the same as the first. Write only the missing words.

1. We need fewer products, not more. → What we need fewer products.

2. They cancelled every project without a clear customer. → What they cancel every project without a clear customer.

3. Nobody understood why there were twelve versions of the same computer. → What nobody understood why there were twelve versions of the same computer.

4. I definitely don't want a longer list. → The last thing I a longer list.

1. is   2. did was (to)   3. was   4. want is

Sentence 2 is the one most people get wrong: when the focus is an action, the cleft needs did — and the verb that follows is in the base form.

3. The all-hands opening — complete it, then read it aloud

A leader returning to a struggling company opens the first staff meeting. Complete the speech using the structures in brackets, then read it aloud slowly, with stress on the focus.

“Let me be direct with you. (wh-cleft: we have) is not a talent problem; it is a focus problem. (it-cleft: our customers) who are confused, not our engineers. (all-cleft: I am asking for) is permission to stop. (the reason) we are cutting most of the range (be + that) no team can do fifty things well. And (it-cleft: the next twelve months) we will be judged on — not the next quarter.”

What we have · It is our customers · All I am asking for · The reason (why) · is that · it is the next twelve months that

Count the clefts: five in one short speech. Now say it again with only two of them, and notice which version sounds more like a real leader.

Going deeper

A note on the figures: losses, valuations and investment amounts here are rounded and taken from public reporting; how close the company came to running out of money is based on its own leader's later account. Apple's current strategy is, of course, still developing.

What the case actually tests. The first reading is the famous one: subtraction saves companies. A business that had tried to answer every competitor with another product was rescued by a leader willing to say no to almost everything, and the four-box grid made the strategy understandable to engineers, retailers and customers at once. The discipline extended into operations — cutting inventory freed cash as surely as cutting products did. On this reading, focus is not a mood but a set of decisions that someone has to be powerful enough to make.

The second reading adds the parts the legend leaves out. The rescue had a lifeline: Microsoft's money and, more importantly, its five-year software commitment, which bought time and calmed customers — and Microsoft had strategic reasons of its own. The software that powered the next decade came from a purchase made by the previous chief executive. And the cuts were made by a founder whose authority came from history, not from a contract. A hired CEO with the same plan might never have got it past the board, the staff and the loyal users. The lesson may be less “focus” than “focus is only possible for someone who can survive the people it hurts.”

A third question is about time. Focus was the right medicine for a company in crisis. Today Apple is one of the broadest companies in the world, and its critics argue about whether it still stops things early enough. Perhaps focus is not a permanent identity but a phase in a cycle: companies expand, lose clarity, cut back, and grow again. If so, the management skill is not choosing focus once — it is knowing which phase you are in.
Talk it through:
  • Separate the explanations: the grid, the lifeline, the founder's authority, the earlier NeXT purchase. If you could keep only one, which best explains the survival — and why?
  • Who in your organisation could cut two-thirds of what it does, and survive the reaction? If nobody could, what does that tell you?
  • Where is your industry in the cycle of expansion and focus right now? What evidence are you using?
  • Accepting Microsoft's money looked like surrender and worked like strategy. Name a partnership with a competitor that would make sense in your sector — and what it would cost in pride.

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

It is 1997. You are the interim chief executive. An analyst says: “You've just taken money from your biggest competitor and announced you're cancelling most of your products. Isn't this simply a smaller company waiting to die?”

Answer properly: concede what is true about the risk, say clearly what the real problem was, explain why fewer products means more strength, and finish with a commitment the analyst can test in twelve months. Then do it again in ninety seconds — the compression is the exercise.

Task C · Four rooms, one cut (4 minutes)

Take one decision to stop something — real or invented — and announce it four ways, one minute each. Use at least one cleft sentence in each room, and notice where the focus changes.

  • To the board: what you are stopping, what it saves, and what risk remains.
  • To the team whose project is being closed: honest, respectful, and clear about what happens to them.
  • To loyal customers who use the product you are ending: what support continues, and why this is better for them.
  • To a partner or supplier: what changes in the relationship, and what does not.

Prefer not to use your own organisation? Take one of these instead:

  • A restaurant chain cutting its menu from eighty dishes to twenty.
  • A university closing a third of its courses to invest in the rest.
  • A software company ending an old product that many customers still use.
  • A charity deciding to work on one cause instead of five.

  2. Founders can often make decisions that hired leaders cannot. Is that a strength organisations should protect, or a risk they should limit?
  3. Should very large companies deliberately keep trying many new things, even if most fail — or is that the 1990s mistake again?
