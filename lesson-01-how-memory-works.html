How Memory Works

A man who could not forget anything shows us how memory really works, and why most of us forget so much.

The whole course  ·  Pictures That Stick →

In this lesson: you will learn the three stages of memory, why we forget, and how chunking and spaced review work against forgetting. You will also meet the idea that runs through the whole course: pictures hold better than words.
Before we begin
How do you remember a phone number, or a shopping list, at the moment? Say what you actually do.
Most people repeat it to themselves, or write it down. Repeating works for about a minute and then fades. Keep your answer in mind: by the end of this lesson you will have something stronger to compare it with.
Think of something you remember perfectly from years ago. Why do you think that one stayed?
Memories that last usually had something extra: strong feeling, surprise, a picture, a place, a story. Almost never just repetition. That is the pattern this whole course uses on purpose.

The reporter who never wrote anything down

Moscow, in the 1920s. Every morning a newspaper editor gave his reporters their jobs for the day: a long list of addresses, names, and instructions about whom to see and what to ask. The reporters scribbled it all down. All except one.

The young man in the corner sat with his hands folded. He did not write a single word. The editor, annoyed, stopped in the middle of the list and told him to pay attention. The reporter stood up, and repeated the whole list back, in order, word for word, including the editor's own jokes.

What did the reporter do that surprised the editor?
He wrote nothing down, yet he repeated the whole list perfectly, in order.
Talk about it. If you were the editor, what would you ask him first? What would you want to know about how he did it?

The editor sent him to a psychologist, Alexander Luria, who studied him for about thirty years and later wrote a famous book about him. In the laboratory the reporter, known in the book only as S., was given long tables of numbers, lists of words, even strings of nonsense. He read them once or twice, and then repeated them forwards, backwards, and in any order Luria asked for. Years later he could still do it.

Luria could not find the limit. How did S. do it? He did not repeat the numbers over and over. He saw them. Numbers had colours and shapes. A voice could look like a cloud of yellow or smell like smoke. Everything he heard turned into a vivid picture, and he placed those pictures along a street he knew, so he could walk past them again.

What was S.'s secret, according to the story?
He turned everything into vivid pictures (he often saw numbers and sounds as colours and shapes) and placed them along a street he knew.
Talk about it. Do you ever see colours or shapes when you hear a word or a number? What would happen to your memory if every word came with a picture?

A perfect memory sounds like a gift. For S. it was also a burden. His pictures never faded, so old ones piled up and got in the way of new ones. A line of poetry could call up a crowd of images and make it hard to follow the meaning. He even tried to forget numbers by writing them down, hoping that once the paper held them his mind could let go.

He found it hard to recognise faces, because a face changes every second and each change looked like a new picture. And abstract ideas gave him real trouble, because they could not easily be turned into a picture. So S. teaches us two things at once. First: memory is mostly a matter of how you take in what you want to keep. Second: you do not need a gift to do it. You can learn to turn things into pictures, on purpose, and put them in places you know. That is this whole course.

Why was S.'s memory sometimes a problem?
He could not forget. Old pictures piled up and interfered with new ones, and he found abstract ideas and changing faces hard.
Talk about it. Would you want a memory that never forgot anything? What would you like to be able to forget, and what would you never want to lose?

Three stages, one limit, one curve

Memory is not one thing. It is a chain of three jobs, and a memory can fail at any link.

If any link is weak, the memory is lost.
StageWhat happensHow it failsWhat helps
EncodingYou turn what you see or hear into something the brain can hold.You were not really paying attention, or you only repeated words.Attention, meaning, and a vivid picture.
StorageThe memory is kept over time.It fades because you never came back to it.Review at growing intervals.
RetrievalYou bring it back out.It is in there but you cannot find the way to it.A place or a link to start from: a cue.

One limit: working memory

When you hold a number in your head for a moment while you dial it, you are using working memory. It is small. In 1956 the psychologist George Miller famously suggested about seven items, plus or minus two. More recent work puts it nearer four. Either way, it is a small desk, not a warehouse.

The way round it: chunking

The limit is about chunks, not letters. FBICIANASA is ten letters, but FBI, CIA, NASA is three chunks you already know. A chunk is anything you can treat as one unit. You make chunks by grouping, and you make them stronger by giving them meaning.

Chunking in action
Remember: 1 9 6 6 1 0 6 6 2 0 0 0
RawTwelve digits. Far too many for working memory.
Group1966 · 1066 · 2000. Three years you may know: England win the World Cup, the Norman conquest, the year 2000.
ResultThree chunks instead of twelve digits.
The picture: Footballers in a boat, with a Norman soldier, going into the year 2000 as fireworks go off.

Why we forget: the curve

In the 1880s Hermann Ebbinghaus tested himself on lists of nonsense syllables and found a pattern that has been confirmed many times since: forgetting is fastest at the start and then slows down. If you review the memory just as it begins to fade, it fades more slowly the next time.

A picture of the idea, not measured data.
  1. Review soon. Go back to it within a day.
  2. Then wait a little longer each time. A few days, a week, a month.
  3. Test yourself instead of re-reading. Pulling the memory out is what strengthens it.

Make chunks

Here are twelve digits: 4 9 1 7 3 8 2 0 6 5 1 9. Write them in chunks that mean something to you (years, ages, a date, a phone-style grouping). How many chunks did you make?
There is no single answer. For example: 49 · 1738 · 2065 · 19 is four chunks, or 491 · 738 · 206 · 519 is four chunks of three digits. The best chunks are the ones that already mean something to you.

Sort a list into chunks

Here are eight items: hammer, saw, rose, drill, football, tennis ball, sunflower, basketball. Group them into the fewest sensible chunks and give each chunk a name.
Three chunks: tools (hammer, saw, drill), flowers (rose, sunflower) and balls (football, tennis ball, basketball). Eight items become three. Name the chunks and you only have to remember the names.

Your first picture

The word hippocampus names the part of the brain that helps to form new memories. It sounds like hippo + campus. Make one vivid picture: a hippo on a university campus. Add what you see, hear and feel. Write it down.
