The stereotype: "Americans never take holidays — they live to work." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — No legal minimum

The USA is the only major advanced economy where paid vacation is not required by national law. Most full-time workers are offered some paid time off by their employers — often around two weeks a year at first — but it depends on the job, and many low-paid or part-time workers get little or none. Paid public holidays are also not guaranteed by federal law.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Is paid vacation required by US national law?

2. Who often gets little or no paid time off?

1. No.

2. Many low-paid or part-time workers.

Discuss:
  • Should holidays be a legal right?

Part 2 — Not using it

Even when time off is offered, it is often not used. A 2023 Pew Research Center survey found that almost half of US workers with paid time off took less than they were offered, often because they worried about falling behind or that colleagues would have to cover for them. "Hustle culture" — the idea that constant work is admirable — is celebrated by some and criticised by others as a cause of burnout.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Pew find in 2023?

2. Why?

1. Almost half of workers with paid time off took less than offered.

2. Worry about falling behind or burdening colleagues.

Discuss:
  • Why might people not take holidays they are given?

Part 3 — Canada's middle way

In Canada, minimum paid vacation is set by each province, and it is usually two weeks after a year of work, rising with years of service. Statutory holidays such as Canada Day (1 July) are protected too. Canadians are often said to sit between the American and European models: more legal protection than in the USA, but less than the four or five weeks common in much of Europe.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who sets minimum vacation in Canada?

2. When is Canada Day?

1. Each province.

2. 1 July.

Discuss:
  • Which model would you prefer?

Grammar — The Passive & Reporting Passives

When we talk about beliefs and reputations, we often don't know or care who says them. That is the job of the passive.

How it works:
• be + past participle: "The survey is carried out every year."
• Reporting passives: "Finns are said to be shy." / "It is often claimed that…"
• Add by only when the doer matters.

1. Make it passive

Complete each sentence about Live to Work? Vacation and the Hustle. Use the word in brackets.

1. Paid vacation not (require) by US federal law.

2. Most workers (offer) some paid time off.

3. The survey (carry out) by Pew in 2023.

1. is … required

2. are offered

3. was carried out

2. Choose the right passive form

1. Minimum vacation (set) by each province.

2. Hustle culture (criticise) as a cause of burnout.

3. Americans (often / say) to live to work.

1. is set

2. has been criticised / is criticised

3. are often said

Going deeper

The stereotype has a solid base in law and survey data, but it hides variation: many professionals have generous leave, many Americans value family time highly, and long hours are also common in countries such as Japan or South Korea. Ideas of self-reliance and individual success help explain why work is so central to American identity.

Worth noticing: The passive (is required, are offered) focuses on the action or rule, not who does it.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Ask, don't assume. When working with American colleagues, don't be surprised by short holidays or quick replies — but don't assume they want to work all the time either. Agree on response times, and when you're on holiday, set a clear out-of-office message.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Is hard work a virtue or a trap?
  • Should laws limit work emails?
  • How much holiday do you really take?
