Saying How Much, Exactly

For when "a lot" or "most" isn't precise enough — in survey results, negotiations, or just telling someone what really happened.

Spot the pattern

Three things people actually say when they want to be exact:

  • "Virtually all of the team agreed to the new shift pattern — only one person objected."
  • "It was a narrow majority — just over half of the residents voted for the change."
  • "Everyone's on board with the plan, with the exception of the two newest hires."

Before you check: what job is each bold phrase doing?

Check the pattern CLICK
Each phrase tells you exactly how big a group is, without giving a single number. Virtually all = close to 100%. A narrow majority / just over half = barely past 50%, not a comfortable win. With the exception of names exactly who's outside the group. Vaguer words like "most" or "a lot" would lose that precision.
virtually all / almost all → close to 100%
the majority / most → clearly over half
a narrow majority / just over half → barely over half
a minority / a small number → clearly under half
with the exception of / except for / apart from → naming who's outside the group
as a rule / in most cases / on the whole → a general pattern, not an absolute claim

From a guess to the real picture

"Almost everyone liked it."
→
"96 of the 100 people surveyed liked it — virtually all of them."

The number does the convincing; the phrase after it confirms exactly how you're reading that number.

Your turn

1. A satisfaction survey came back: 196 of the 200 replies were positive.

Tell your manager: " of the replies were positive."

2. Same survey, a different question: 106 of the 200 people said they'd recommend the service to a friend; 94 said they wouldn't.

Report it honestly: " of the people said they'd recommend us."

3. Everyone in the office is happy with the new nine o'clock start, except for two people who work the early shift.

Sum it up precisely: "Staff are happy with the new start time, the two people on the early shift."

Watch for words like "everyone", "always" and "never" when they're not literally true. A softer, more accurate version — "as a rule...", "in most cases...", "on the whole..." — protects you from being caught out by the one exception someone always seems to remember.

Making It Clear Who You Mean

For when "he", "she", "it" or "this" could point to more than one thing — and the wrong guess actually matters.

Spot the pattern

Three fixes for the same everyday problem:

  • "Tell Anna about the change before she hears it elsewhere." (not: "tell her before she hears about it")
  • "Looking through the invoices, I found two duplicate payments." (not: "looking through the invoices, two duplicate payments were found")
  • "Sales dropped and two staff resigned — that combination worried the board." (not: "this worried the board")

What do all three fixes have in common? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Each one replaces something that could point to more than one thing — with a specific name or phrase. "She" and "it" only work once "Anna" and "the change" are named first. "I" has to be right there to do the looking, next to "looking through". "That combination" says exactly what worried the board, instead of a bare "this".
Name the person before you rely on he / she / they
Make sure the noun right after a comma is the one actually doing the action
Replace a bare "this / it / that" with a short phrase that says exactly what you mean

From ambiguous to unmistakable

"The client asked the assistant to send him the invoice."
→
"The client said to the assistant, 'Please send me the invoice.'"

A direct quote — or simply repeating the name — removes the guesswork completely.

Comparing Precisely

For saying exactly how much bigger, smaller, faster or slower something is — in negotiations, price comparisons, or comparing two options out loud.

Spot the pattern

  • "We received twice as many applications this year as last year."
  • "The new office is a third smaller than the old one."
  • "Turnout was only marginally higher than last year — nothing like the jump we'd hoped for."

What connects all three? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Each one commits to an exact multiple or fraction (twice, a third) or an exact sense of scale — marginally means barely at all, while considerably means by a lot. None of them just says "more" or "a bit different"; they all commit to how much.
twice / three times / four times + as + many / much / adjective + as
a half / a third / a quarter + more / less / fewer / smaller / higher...
considerably / significantly ≠ marginally / slightly  (a big gap vs a tiny gap)
One common slip: "the double of" isn't standard English — say "twice as many / much (...) as" or "double the number of". Also worth knowing: strict grammar prefers "fewer" with countable nouns ("fewer emails") and "less" with uncountables ("less time") — but in relaxed conversation, plenty of native speakers say "less" for both. For precise writing or a careful report, "fewer" with countables is the safer choice.

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: "fewer" goes with things you can count — "fewer chairs", "fewer typos" — and "less" goes with things you can't — "less noise", "less traffic". In relaxed conversation, many people use "less" for both, but "fewer" is the more precise choice with countable nouns.
