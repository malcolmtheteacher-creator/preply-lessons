Laid-Back or Lazy? Work, Fairness and “She'll Be Right”

Australians and New Zealanders are relaxed. But they also work hard and help other people.

Key words

Read the sentence. What does the bold word mean? Then click Reveal.

1. The manager is relaxed but works hard.
relaxed (adj) — calm and not worried. Example: “The manager is relaxed but works hard.”
2. People work about 35 or 40 hours a week.
work (v) — to do a job. Example: “People work about 35 or 40 hours a week.”
3. A volunteer watches the beach.
volunteer (n) — a person who helps without pay. Example: “A volunteer watches the beach.”
4. Everyone wants a fair chance.
fair (adj) — right and equal for everyone. Example: “Everyone wants a fair chance.”
5. After work, many people walk on the beach.
beach (n) — sand next to the sea. Example: “After work, many people walk on the beach.”
6. A humble person does not boast.
humble (adj) — not proud of yourself. Example: “A humble person does not boast.”

The stereotype: “Australians and New Zealanders are lazy.”

The story

Part 1 — Hard work

People in Australia and New Zealand work about the same hours as people in other rich countries. About 150 years ago, in 1856, workers in Melbourne won the eight-hour day. That was the first time in the world. Many people work part-time and like to spend time outside, but many people also work very long hours in cities, mines and farms.

Quick check:

1. When did workers in Melbourne win the eight-hour day?

2. Do people there work fewer hours than in other rich countries?

1. In 1856.

2. No, about the same.

Discuss:
  • How many hours do you work in a day?

Part 2 — A fair go

Many people believe in “a fair go”. This means everybody has a chance. Bosses and workers use first names. People do not like boasting. If someone wins a medal, they say, “It went pretty well.” This is a way to be humble.

Quick check:

1. What is a “fair go”?

2. What do people not like?

1. A fair chance for everybody.

2. Boasting.

Discuss:
  • Do you like people who boast?

Part 3 — Serious when it matters

Many volunteers watch the beaches and save swimmers. Thousands of volunteers fight fires in the bush. “She'll be right” means “It will be OK”. It shows that people are positive. But it is also good to talk about problems. Mental health is very important, too.

Quick check:

1. Who watches the beaches?

2. What does “she'll be right” mean?

1. Volunteers.

2. It will be OK.

Discuss:
  • Is it good to say “It will be OK” when you have a problem?

Grammar — very, quite, a bit and really

Very / really = strong: “It is very hot.” “She works really hard.”
Quite = medium: “The beach is quite busy.”
A bit = small: “I am a bit tired.”
These words go before the adjective or adverb.

1. Choose the word

1. He works hard. He never stops. (very / a bit)

2. The beach is busy, but there is some space. (quite / very)

3. I am tired after a long day. (a bit / really)

1. very

2. quite

3. a bit

2. Make your own

1. I am very ______.

2. My town is quite ______.

3. I am a bit ______ today.

1. ...busy. / ...happy.

2. ...quiet. / ...small.

3. ...tired. / ...nervous.
