The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — The loneliest island

Far away in the Pacific Ocean, there is a very small island. It is more than 2,000 miles from South America and more than 1,000 miles from the next island. The people who live there call it Rapa Nui. We also call it Easter Island. It is one of the loneliest places on Earth.

About 800 years ago, a small group of people came in boats. They sailed across thousands of miles of open sea, with no map. They used the stars, the wind and the birds to find the way. When they arrived, the island was full of tall trees — a thick forest. They decided to stay.

Over many years, these people made something amazing. They carved giant faces out of stone. We call these statues the moai. There are almost 900 of them! Some are ten metres tall and very, very heavy. The people moved them across the island and stood them up, with their backs to the sea. The stone faces looked over the land, like guards watching the people.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is Easter Island special? Where is it?

2. What are the moai, and how many are there?

1. It is one of the loneliest places on Earth — very far from any other land, in the middle of the Pacific Ocean.

2. They are giant stone faces (statues), carved by the people. There are almost 900 of them.

Talk about it:
  • The people worked for many years to make and move hundreds of giant statues. Why do you think they did this?

Part 2 — The trees are gone

In 1722, ships from Europe found the island. The visitors were very surprised. The big forest was gone! The land was almost empty, with only grass. How did the people make these huge statues and then lose all their trees?

For a long time, many people believed one sad story. In this story, the islanders cut down their own forest. They cut the trees to make fields, to make fires, and to move their big stone statues. But when the trees were gone, the soil washed away. They could not build boats to fish. There was not enough food.

People said that the hungry islanders then started to fight. In their anger, they pushed over each other's statues. So, in this story, the people destroyed their own island. Many writers told this story in books, and it became famous all over the world. They said it was a warning for us today. But some scientists now think that this story is wrong.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What surprised the European visitors in 1722?

2. In the old, sad story, what did the islanders do to their own island?

1. The big forest was gone. The land was almost empty, with only grass — but the people had made huge statues.

2. They cut down all their trees (partly to move the statues). Then there was no food, they started to fight, and they pushed over each other's statues.

Talk about it:
  • This sad story became famous because it is a warning for the world today. Do you think a good "lesson" can make people believe a story, even if it is not true?

Part 3 — A different story

The new story is different. Yes, the trees did disappear — that is true. But maybe the people did not cut down all the trees. When the first people came, they brought rats. The rats had no enemies, so there were soon millions of them. The rats ate the seeds of the trees. So new trees could not grow. Many scientists now think that the rats killed the forest, not only the people.

And losing the trees did not kill the islanders. They were clever. They grew food in new ways, using stones to protect their plants. The number of people stayed about the same. Many scientists now think that there was no big disaster before the Europeans came.

So what really hurt the people of Rapa Nui? The new story says the danger came from outside. European ships brought new diseases. Then, in 1862 and 1863, ships from Peru came and took about 1,500 people away to work far from home. Most of them never came back. The few who returned brought a terrible disease with them. In just a few years, thousands of people became only about one hundred. But the people did not disappear. Their families still live on the island today, and they have stood some of the fallen statues up again.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. In the new story, what may have killed the forest?

2. What happened to the people in 1862 and 1863?

1. Rats. The first people brought rats, and the rats ate the tree seeds, so new trees could not grow. Many scientists think the rats killed the forest, not only the people.

2. Ships from Peru took about 1,500 people away to work far from home. Most never came back, and the few who returned brought a terrible disease. The number of people crashed to about one hundred.

Talk about it:
  • One story says the islanders hurt themselves. The other says people from outside hurt them. Why might it be important which story we believe?

Grammar: say / tell / think + that

This story is full of ideas about what really happened. To talk about these ideas, we use say, tell and think with the word that.

say that + idea — we do not need a person: "Scientists say that the rats killed the trees."
think that + idea — for an opinion: "Some people think that the islanders cut down all the trees."
tell + person + that — tell always needs a person: "The books told readers that the island was a warning."
Two easy rules:
• The little word that is optional. "She thinks that it is true" = "She thinks it is true." Both are correct.
• tell needs a person after it; say does not. Write "He told me that…" or "He said that…" — but not "He said me that…" and not "He told that…".

Write the right word in each gap. Look: is there a person after the gap?

Task C · A story of your own (3 minutes)

Not sure what to talk about? Choose one:

  • A news story you saw, where two sides told a completely different version.
  • A famous place or building, and the different ideas about how it was made or why.
