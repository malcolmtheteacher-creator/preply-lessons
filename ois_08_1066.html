II. The Battle of Hastings

September 25, 1066: Stamford Bridge

But Hastings was not the first battle that year. Harold, the Saxon king, had barely been crowned when terrible news arrived from the North.

When Harold had been king only a few weeks, news was brought to him that a great fleet had sailed up the River Humber, and that King Harold Hardrada of Norway had landed in Yorkshire. Hardrada was a mighty king and a mighty warrior. With him came Tostig, the wicked brother of Harold. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XXV

Harold had no choice. He gathered his army and marched north at incredible speed — nearly 200 miles in four days. On September 25th, at a place called Stamford Bridge, the armies met.

Both Tostig and King Harold Hardrada were killed, and their soldiers fled in all directions. So Harold won a great victory, and he punished the Norwegians terribly, saying that they should have land enough in which to bury their dead, and that no more of them should have the kingdom of England. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XXV
The historian's note: This is actual history — the Battle of Stamford Bridge did happen on 25 September 1066, and Harold Godwinson did defeat Hardrada and Tostig. But it came at a cost. Harold's army was exhausted. And he had no time to rest.
♦ ♦ ♦

October 14, 1066: The Battle of Hastings Itself

Three weeks later, terrible news came from the South. William of Normandy had landed in Sussex with an invasion fleet. Harold and his worn army had to march south again — another 240 miles in less than two weeks.

Harold moved his army from the south to the middle of England in just a few days, and arrived at York by September 24th. But the Normans, as they crossed the Channel, sailed towards the south coast, and landed in Sussex near Hastings. When Harold heard that the Normans had landed, he collected his soldiers together and marched south to meet them. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XXV

On October 14th, the armies met on a hillside near the town of Hastings. Marshall describes the scene with vivid drama:

Harold camped on the hill called Senlac, and there it was that the battle took place. The English army was not nearly so large as the Norman, but Harold chose a very good place on the top of a hill. He also built a strong fence all round his camp.

William did not hesitate. He marched boldly forward with his army, and when they reached the foot of the hill, they began to climb it. The English defended themselves bravely, and the Normans could not get up the hill at all. Many of the Normans were slain, and William began to think he was beaten. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XXV

But William was clever. He ordered a false retreat — his soldiers pretended to flee. The English, thinking victory was theirs, rushed down the hill. And then the Normans turned and cut them down.

The English, thinking that the Normans were indeed beaten, and that the victory was theirs, began to run down the hill to pursue them. But this was just what William wanted. He gave a signal, and the Normans turned, and flew at the English. They fell upon them suddenly and killed great numbers of them. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XXV

And then came the moment that changed everything. The moment every child learns about the Norman Conquest:

It was in this way that Harold, the unhappy king, met his death. An arrow had struck him in the eye, and he fell. With him fell the last of the Saxon kings. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XXV
The historian's note: The arrow in the eye. This image has become legendary — it appears in the Bayeux Tapestry, that extraordinary embroidered chronicle of the battle made within a generation of the events. But did it really happen this way? We don't know for certain. Harold certainly died in the battle, but medieval sources contradict each other about how. What we do know: the battle was hard-fought, the English were exhausted, William was ruthless, and the outcome changed everything.

III. William's England

Christmas Day 1066: The Coronation

William of Normandy had won the battle. But winning the kingdom was another matter. Many English nobles refused to acknowledge him. He was a foreigner, a bastard, an invader.

On Christmas Day 1066, just 71 days after Hastings, William was crowned King of England in Westminster Abbey. Marshall captures the moment:

William had conquered England, and now he meant to rule it. He was crowned King of England at Westminster on Christmas Day. But although he had won the throne by the sword, the English did not love him for it. They called him "the Conqueror," and the name tells us how he was regarded by his new subjects. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XXVI

The Harrying of the North (1069–1070)

The English rebelled. Nowhere more fiercely than in the North. William's response was ruthless — what came to be called "the Harrying of the North," one of the most brutal campaigns in English medieval history.

The people of Northumberland rose in fierce rebellion against William, and he in as fierce anger marched against them with his soldiers. From north to south he laid waste to the country, burning towns, destroying farms, killing cattle, murdering the people, till the whole of Northumberland was one dreary desert. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XXVI
The historian's note: The Harrying was devastating. Archaeological evidence shows widespread destruction. Entire villages were wiped out. Famine followed. Some historians estimate tens of thousands died. It was terrorism — designed to ensure no region would dare rebel again. It worked.

The Feudal System: Remaking the Hierarchy

William didn't just conquer England with armies. He conquered it with a completely new system of power. Every piece of land, every title, every right of rule came directly from the king. English thegns (nobles) became Norman vassals. Monasteries and earldoms were given to Norman knights who had fought at Hastings. The old English aristocracy was swept away.

The Feudal Hierarchy Under William

The King owns all land. He grants it to his Norman lords (barons) in exchange for military service and loyalty. Those lords sublet to lesser nobles (knights) in exchange for the same. Even the Church held land by this system — the Archbishop of Canterbury owed the king military service. Every relationship was one of obligation and obedience flowing upward to the crown.

1086: The Domesday Book

In 1086, twenty years after Hastings, William did something no English king had ever done. He ordered a complete survey of his entire kingdom. Every field. Every animal. Every person. Every asset. It was the first English census, and it was motivated by power and taxes.

William wanted to know exactly how rich his kingdom was, and what taxes he could demand. So he sent his commissioners all over England, and they asked endless questions. What lands did each man own? How many cattle? How many sheep? How many ploughs? And the answers to all these questions were written down in a great book, which was called the Domesday Book, because people said that the Day of Judgment itself, when all secrets would be revealed, could hardly be more thorough and all-revealing. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XXVII (paraphrased and expanded)
The historian's note: The Domesday Book survives, and it is extraordinary. Two volumes, over 400 pages, in a medieval script so tiny it requires magnification to read. It records about 13,000 settlements and over 200,000 people. Domesday — from Old English "Domes daeg," the day of judgment. The name itself reveals how people felt about this unprecedented intrusion into their privacy.

Norman Transformation

Within a generation, England was transformed:

Language: Norman French became the language of the court and nobility. English became the language of the peasants. This split lasted centuries, creating two words for every animal: "cow" (English, for the peasant who tended it) and "beef" (French, for the noble who ate it).

Architecture: Norman castles and cathedrals rose across the land. The Tower of London, begun by William himself, still stands.

Government: The feudal system centralized power in the king's hands more completely than the old Saxon system ever had.

Culture: Norman art, law, and customs became the standard. The English identity itself began to blend Saxon and Norman elements.

VI. Your Turn — Think, Discuss, Remember

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on:

Why Did Harold Lose?

Harold won at Stamford Bridge but lost at Hastings. Was it bad luck (the forced march)? Was William simply better? Was it the Norman cavalry tactics? What is the difference between a good general and a lucky one?

The Arrow in the Eye

Did Harold really die from an arrow to the eye? We don't know for sure. Why has this image become the defining moment of the battle? What is the power of a single vivid detail in how we remember history?

The Cruelty of the Harrying

William destroyed the North to prevent rebellion. It worked. But thousands died. Is brutality ever justified in consolidating power? Did it make William a worse ruler, or a more effective one?

The Domesday Book as Superpower

William knew everything about his kingdom — every field, every animal, every person. In what ways is knowledge itself a form of power? How is this different from earlier kings?

Conquest and Blending

Norman and Saxon don't stay separate. Within a few generations, they blend. By 1200, even Norman nobles call themselves English. Was the conquest an ending or a beginning?

Why 1066 Matters

Is 1066 really the most important date in English history? What about other dates we've seen — 55 BC (Caesar), 410 (Rome's withdrawal), 597 (Augustine's mission)? What makes a date "important"?
