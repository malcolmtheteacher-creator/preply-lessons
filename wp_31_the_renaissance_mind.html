A question to hold

Can a person know themselves without God? Augustine wrote the Confessions five centuries earlier, examining his own soul in conversation with God. The Renaissance produced its own revolution: the person turning inward, asking radical questions about themselves, with or without faith. This lesson charts that turn.

II. The Story — Three Minds, Three Approaches

The Renaissance was a rebirth — of classical learning, of human individuality, of the belief that reason could reform the world. But three thinkers represent three very different visions of what reason could do.

Niccolò Machiavelli (1469–1527)

Machiavelli was a diplomat and military strategist in Florence, watching princes rise and fall. He saw something no medieval philosopher had dared to say aloud: that politics had its own logic, separate from morality.

It is necessary for a prince wishing to hold his own to know how not to be good, and to use this knowledge and not use it, according to the necessity of the case. — Niccolò Machiavelli, The Prince, Chapter XV

This was explosive. Medieval thinkers had assumed that a good ruler followed Christian virtue. Machiavelli said: no. A ruler must do whatever preserves power. Be cruel if it prevents rebellion. Be generous if it buys loyalty. Morality is a tool, not a law. The goal is lo stato — the state. And the state's logic is survival.

Why this mattered: Machiavelli didn't invent cynicism. But he gave it philosophy's first honest voice. He looked at power as it actually worked, not as it should work. This was a kind of realism — and it horrified the medieval mind. But it opened the door to political science. You cannot understand politics if you assume politicians are moral.

Desiderius Erasmus (1466–1536)

If Machiavelli stripped politics of morality, Erasmus tried to build a bridge between reason and faith. Born in Rotterdam, educated in classical texts, he became the greatest humanist of his age. He believed that reason and faith could not contradict each other — they illuminated the same truth from different angles.

The Christian religion was not irrational, as some imagine, but the truest philosophy. And the philosophers of old, if they had rightly understood their own teachings, would have found themselves approaching the spirit of Christian wisdom. — Desiderius Erasmus, Enchiridion Militis Christiani (Handbook of the Christian Knight)

Erasmus devoted himself to studying Scripture in its original Greek and Hebrew. He believed education — not dogma, not fear — was the path to reform. He criticized the Church's corruption without leaving it. He wanted to recover the early Christian texts and the wisdom of the pagan philosophers under one roof.

His contradiction: Erasmus lived through the Reformation. Luther would not be so gentle. Erasmus tried to reform from within; Luther broke the Church in half. Erasmus represents the humanist dream: reason and faith in conversation. Luther represents the revolutionary verdict: they have been at war too long.

Michel de Montaigne (1533–1592)

Montaigne was a French nobleman who withdrew to his tower and asked a question that would change philosophy: "Que sais-je?" What do I know? And behind that, a deeper one: Who am I?

He invented the essay — literally, an "attempt" or "trial" — as a form of self-examination through writing. He would begin with an idea, a quote, an observation. And then he would follow his own mind as it wandered, contradicted itself, discovered itself.

There is no desire more natural than the desire for knowledge. I make this practise of examining myself all the time so much that I am curious even about my own curiosity. I do not fix my mind on any high or distant object; I am accustomed to feed my understanding wherever it is, and to look into every object that offers itself to it. — Michel de Montaigne, Essays, Book III, Chapter 13

Montaigne was radically honest about his own weakness, contradiction, and confusion. He did not pretend to wisdom. He was interested in what it felt like to be himself — a particular man with particular fears, desires, and shames. This was revolutionary. Philosophy before Montaigne had been about universal truths. Montaigne made the particular — himself — the subject of philosophy.

What They Disagreed On (And What It Means)

Three Answers to One World

Machiavelli said: Reason can understand power. Politics is amoral. Accept it.

Erasmus said: Reason confirms faith. Education and wisdom can reform the world.

Montaigne said: Reason can examine the self. But I am full of contradiction. Wisdom is honesty about confusion.

The Renaissance did not produce a single philosophy. It produced three windows into modernity: the amoral analyst (Machiavelli), the reformer (Erasmus), and the self-examiner (Montaigne). All three would echo forward through the centuries.

III. The Ideas — Reason Takes Three Forms

Idea 1: Political Realism

Power operates by its own logic, separate from morality.

This is not cynicism for its own sake. It is the insight that you cannot understand how power actually works if you assume it should be moral. A doctor must understand disease without approving of it. A strategist must understand ambition without sharing it. Machiavelli offered political science based on observation rather than aspiration. Whether this is wisdom or a dangerous game is still debated.

Idea 2: Christian Humanism

Classical learning and Christian faith are not enemies; they are partners.

Erasmus believed you could read Plato and Paul in the same morning and find they were saying the same thing in different languages. The wisdom of the pagans and the revelation of Christ spoke to the same truth. Education — especially in languages, in texts, in critical thinking — was the path to reform both the Church and the world. This was the dream of the humanists. History would test it harshly.

Idea 3: Self-Knowledge Through Writing

The examined self, honestly written, is a proper subject of philosophy.

Before Montaigne, philosophy was about the universal. After Montaigne, it had to account for the particular — the individual person, with their fears, shames, and contradictions. The essay became philosophy's most intimate form. You write not to teach but to discover. You follow your own confusion. And in that honesty, you find something true about being human.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Unresolved Question

Montaigne represents a radical turn inward. He examines himself without appealing to God for meaning. But can self-knowledge alone save us? Montaigne was a skeptic — he doubted everything, including his own certainty. He found comfort in accepting his ignorance. But is acceptance enough? Or does the self, left alone without God, discover only confusion?

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Is Machiavelli right?

Can power ever be moral? Or does seeking power automatically corrupt morality? Is Machiavelli cynical or realistic?

Can reason and faith coexist?

Erasmus believed they could. The Reformation proved they could not. Who was right? Can education bridge the gap?

Is honesty enough?

Montaigne is honest about his confusion and contradiction. Does that honesty amount to wisdom? Or is it just the absence of false certainty?

The turn inward

Montaigne marks a shift: philosophy now looks inside the self, not just at universal truths. Is this progress? What did we lose?

Augustine vs. Montaigne

Augustine looked inward too, but always toward God. Montaigne looks inward and finds only himself. What is the difference?

The essay as philosophy

Montaigne invented the essay. Is a personal, wandering, honest piece of writing philosophy? Or is philosophy something else?

The Unresolved Tension

These three thinkers point in different directions. Machiavelli strips away illusions and leaves us with naked power. Erasmus tries to build bridges and preserve both reason and faith. Montaigne looks inward and finds only himself — contradictory, honest, alone. The Renaissance did not resolve this tension. It sharpened it. And the next generation of philosophers would have to choose.
