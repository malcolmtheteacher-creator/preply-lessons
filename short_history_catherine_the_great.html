The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The foreign bride

She was not born Russian, and she was not born great. In 1729 she was Sophie of Anhalt-Zerbst, the daughter of a minor German prince, one of dozens of small aristocratic families scattered across the German lands. Her family had a title but little money and no real power. What the young Sophie did have was a sharp, restless mind and an ambition far larger than her circumstances.

At fourteen she was chosen as the bride for Peter, the awkward, immature heir to the Russian throne. She travelled to a country whose language she could not speak and whose religion was not her own. But she set about the task of belonging with astonishing discipline: she learned Russian late into the night, embraced the Orthodox faith, took the Russian name Catherine, and made herself, by sheer effort, more Russian than the Russians. Her marriage, however, was cold and unhappy from the start.

For eighteen long years she waited — reading widely, watching the court, making allies. When the old Empress Elizabeth died in 1762, Catherine's husband finally became Emperor Peter III. He was a disaster on the throne. He admired Russia's enemy, Prussia, threw away hard-won victories, insulted the powerful Orthodox Church, and alienated the army and the nobility within months. Catherine, watching quietly, understood that his weakness was her opportunity.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was Catherine before she came to Russia, and what did she have to do to fit into her new country?

2. Why did Peter III lose the support of the army, the church and the nobility so quickly?

1. She was Sophie, the daughter of a minor German prince. She had to learn Russian, convert to the Orthodox faith and take a Russian name to belong at the Russian court.

2. He admired Russia's enemy Prussia, threw away Russian victories, insulted the Orthodox Church and offended the army and nobility within months of taking power.

Discuss:
  • Catherine spent eighteen years quietly preparing while she had almost no power. What does it take to wait that long for a single opportunity?

Part 2 — The coup

By the summer of 1762, Catherine had built a network of allies among the palace guards and the officers who despised her husband. Chief among them were the Orlov brothers, ambitious young soldiers loyal to her. The plan was to strike before Peter could act against her — and in July the moment came, when one of the conspirators was arrested and Catherine realised she could wait no longer.

Before dawn she left the palace and rode to the barracks of the guards regiments. There she stood before the soldiers and asked them to protect her and support her claim. Regiment after regiment came over to her side. Dressed in a military uniform, she rode at the head of her troops toward the capital. Peter, isolated and abandoned, found that no one would fight for him. Within a day the coup was complete: he was forced to abdicate, and Catherine was proclaimed sole ruler — Empress Catherine II of Russia.

Days later, in custody and away from the capital, the deposed Peter died. The official announcement blamed illness; many at the time and since have doubted it, and the shadow of his death would follow Catherine for the rest of her reign. She herself almost certainly did not order it, but she took the throne it left empty. She had arrived in Russia as a powerless foreign girl of fourteen. Now, at thirty-three, she ruled the largest country on earth, with no legal claim to it beyond the loyalty of the men who had raised her up.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Catherine win the support of the guards, and what forced her to act when she did?

2. What happened to Peter III after the coup, and why did it cast a shadow over Catherine's reign?

1. She had built a network of loyal officers (including the Orlov brothers) and rode to the barracks to ask the regiments for support; they came over to her. She acted when a fellow conspirator was arrested and she could wait no longer.

2. He was forced to abdicate and died days later in custody; the official cause was illness, but the doubt around his death followed Catherine for the rest of her reign.

Discuss:
  • Catherine held power only because others chose to give it to her that morning. How stable is power that rests on loyalty rather than law?

Part 3 — The long reign

Catherine ruled for thirty-four years, the longest reign of any woman in Russian history. She had grown up on the ideas of the Enlightenment and corresponded for years with French thinkers such as Voltaire and Diderot. Early on, she summoned a great commission to write a new legal code and produced a famous document of guiding principles that spoke of tolerance, reason and the rule of law. She founded schools, including one of Europe's first state institutes for the education of girls, championed the arts, and built the vast collection that became the Hermitage museum.

Yet the empire she governed rested on serfdom, and here the reformer met her limits. Catherine's power depended on the nobility who had put her on the throne, and she dared not turn against them. Under her, the condition of the serfs grew harsher, not gentler. In the 1770s a huge peasant uprising led by Pugachev swept across the empire before her armies crushed it — and afterwards her reforms grew more cautious and her rule more conservative.

Abroad, she was formidable. Her armies defeated the Ottoman Empire and won Russia the northern coast of the Black Sea; she annexed Crimea, and through a series of agreements with neighbouring powers she absorbed vast territories to the west. By the time she died in 1796, Russia was larger, richer and more respected in Europe than she had found it. Catherine had never had a drop of Russian blood or any right to the throne by birth — yet she left behind an empire transformed, and a reputation, fiercely debated ever since, as one of the great rulers of her age.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Catherine achieve in law, education and the arts, and which ideas inspired her?

2. Why did her hopes of reforming serfdom fail, and what happened in the 1770s?

1. Inspired by the Enlightenment (and thinkers like Voltaire and Diderot), she summoned a commission for a new legal code, wrote guiding principles on tolerance and reason, founded schools including one for girls, and built the Hermitage collection.

2. Her power depended on the nobility, so she could not turn against them; serfdom grew harsher, not gentler. In the 1770s a huge peasant uprising led by Pugachev was crushed, after which her rule became more conservative.

Discuss:
  • Catherine believed in Enlightenment ideals yet ruled over serfdom she could not end. Can a ruler be judged "great" when their ideals and their actions pull in opposite directions?

Grammar — past modals: obligation, permission & ability

Catherine's whole story is a story of rules: what she had to do to survive at court, what she was allowed to do as a foreign bride, and what she could — and finally was able to — achieve once power was hers. English marks each of these differently in the past. Get them right and you can describe any situation of duty, freedom or capability.

Obligation in the past — what was necessary:
• had to + base verb — it was necessary; there was no choice: "She had to learn Russian." "The serfs had to work the noble's land."
• didn't have to + base verb — it was not necessary; there was no obligation (this does not mean it was forbidden): "As empress, she didn't have to ask anyone's permission."
Permission in the past — what was allowed:
• was / were allowed to + base verb — someone had permission: "Nobles were allowed to travel abroad." "She was not allowed to choose her own husband."
• Careful: for permission we normally use was/were allowed to, not "could", in a specific past situation.
Ability in the past — what was possible:
• could + base verb — general ability over time: "She could speak several languages." "He couldn't win the army's respect."
• was / were able to + base verb — ability on one specific occasion, especially a success: "On that July morning she was able to win the guards to her side." (Here we prefer was able to, not "could", for a single achieved action.)

1. Which idea — obligation, permission or ability?

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence with the right past structure.

1. As a fourteen-year-old bride, Catherine learn a whole new language. (it was necessary)

2. Under the new law, the nobility travel abroad freely. (they had permission)

3. After years of study, she speak Russian, French and German. (general ability)

1. had to (obligation — it was necessary)

2. were allowed to (permission)

3. could (general ability over time)

2. could or was able to?

One sentence describes a general ability; the other describes a single success on one occasion. Choose the better form.

1. Even as a child, she read for hours without tiring. (general ability)

2. On the morning of the coup, she win the guards to her side before Peter could react. (one specific success)

3. Peter keep the loyalty of the army — they abandoned him. (a general failure — negative)

1. could (general ability over time)

2. was able to (a single achieved action on one occasion)

3. couldn't (in the negative, "couldn't" works for both general and specific — "wasn't able to" is also correct)

3. Tell the story — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the right past structure: had to, didn't have to, was/were allowed to, could or was able to. Read it aloud once you're done.

When she arrived in Russia, Catherine learn the language and change her religion — she had no choice. As a young wife she make no important decisions on her own; the court decided everything for her. But she read and study as much as she liked, and over the years she quietly built her allies. Then, on one decisive morning, she win the guards to her side. As empress at last, she ask anyone's permission ever again.

had to (obligation) · wasn't allowed to (no permission) · was allowed to / could (freedom to study) · was able to (a single success) · didn't have to (no obligation)

Notice the arc: forced duty (had to) → no permission (wasn't allowed to) → a freedom she did have (was allowed to) → one decisive success (was able to) → total freedom from obligation (didn't have to).

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Catherine arrived with no claim to the throne and took it by a coup. Does how a ruler comes to power change how we should judge what they did with it?
  2. She is called "the Great." Who decides which rulers earn that title — their own age, or the centuries that follow — and whose voices tend to be left out of that judgement?
  3. Under Catherine, Russia grew larger and richer while the serfs grew less free. When we weigh up a reign, how should we balance national glory against the lives of ordinary people?
