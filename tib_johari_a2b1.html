The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — Two psychologists

In 1955, two American psychologists worked at a university in California. Their names were Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham. Everyone called them Joe and Harry.

At that time, many psychologists were interested in groups. What happens when people work together? Why do some groups work well? Joe and Harry taught in a summer course for people who wanted to learn about teams.

They had a problem. It was difficult to explain one idea: when people tell each other the truth, something important happens. They needed a simple picture.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

2. What were psychologists interested in?

3. What did Joe and Harry need?

1. In 1955, at a university in California.

2. Groups — how people work together.

3. A simple picture to explain their idea.

Discuss:

Part 2 — Four parts

They drew a window with four parts. The window asks two questions. Do I know this about me? Do other people know it?

Part one is open: you know it, and everybody knows it. Your job, your family, your favourite team.

Part two is hidden: you know it, but you don't tell people. Maybe you are afraid of something.

Part three is the blind spot: other people see it, but you don't. Maybe you speak too fast, or you look angry when you are only thinking.

Part four is unknown: nobody knows it yet. Maybe you are a great cook, but you have never cooked.

They needed a name for the picture. So they used their own names: Joe + Harry = Johari.

1. What two questions does the window ask?

2. What is a “blind spot”?

3. Where does the name Johari come from?

1. Do I know it about myself? Do other people know it?

2. Something other people can see about you, but you can't.

3. From their names: Joe and Harry.

Discuss:

Part 3 — Opening the window

The idea is simple: the open part can grow. When you tell people something about yourself, the hidden part gets smaller. When you ask for feedback and really listen, the blind spot gets smaller.

Today, teachers, nurses and managers all over the world use this window. In one popular game, you choose six words for yourself, and your friends choose six words for you. The same words go in the open part. Different words go in the blind spot. It can be a surprise.

But be careful. Some things are private, and that is fine. And feedback can hurt. The window works best with people you trust.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Tell people about yourself, and ask for feedback.

2. Words your friends chose but you didn't choose.

How the tool works

Two questions, four parts. Do I know it? Do other people know it? The aim is a bigger open part.

Using it, step by step

  1. Choose people you trust: a class, a team, a few friends.
  2. Each person chooses six words about themselves. Then the others choose six words for that person.
  3. Same words = open. Only from the others = blind spot. Only from you = hidden.
  4. Talk about the surprises. Then say one new thing about yourself.

Try it yourself

Which part of the window is it? Choose, then check.

3. You can play the piano very well, but you have never tried.

5. People say you look angry when you read. You never knew.

1. Blind spot — they see it; you don't.

2. Hidden — you know it; they don't.

3. Unknown — nobody knows it yet — not even you.

4. Open — you know it and other people know it.

5. Blind spot — other people saw it first.

6. Hidden — you know it and you keep it private.

Where the tool breaks

It is not a test. It is a picture to help people talk.

Private is OK. In many countries and families, you don't tell everybody everything.

Feedback can be wrong. One person's opinion is only one opinion.

Talk it through:
  • Is it good to know your blind spot? Why?
  • How can you give feedback kindly?

Grammar — look and seem

This window is about how people look to other people. Here is how to say it in English.

look + adjective — what you see.
“You look tired.”  “The soup looks good.”
look like + noun — it is similar to something.
“He looks like his father.”  (NOT He looks like tired.)
seem + adjective — your impression, from everything you see and hear.
“She seems happy in her new job.”
sound + adjective — your impression from a voice or an idea.
“Your plan sounds great.”
Careful: we don't use very here in the same way. Say “You look really tired”.
