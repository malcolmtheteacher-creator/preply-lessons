The Manhattan Project

The secret race to build a weapon that would change the world forever.

Today: you'll tell the story of the greatest and most terrible scientific project in history — the secret wartime race to build the atomic bomb — and you'll master the passive voice, the structure English reaches for when the action matters more than who did it, and when responsibility is quietly left unsaid.

The words for today's story

Ten words you'll meet in the story, unlocked four different ways. Do the thinking first, then reveal to check — the answer stays hidden until you press the button.

A · Work out the meaning from the situation

Each sentence gives you enough to guess the bold word. Say what you think it means, then reveal.

1. For years the work went on behind fences and armed guards; even the workers' own families had no idea what they were building. It was completely classified.
classified (adj) — kept officially secret by a government, especially for reasons of security.
2. Once the reaction began, there was no stopping it: in an instant it would unleash a power greater than anything the world had ever seen.
to unleash (v) — to suddenly release a great force, energy or violence that then becomes hard to control.
3. One neutron splits one atom, which throws out more neutrons, which split more atoms, and in a fraction of a second the chain reaction has spread through the whole mass.
a chain reaction (n) — a series of events in which each one causes the next, growing very fast; in physics, splitting atoms that go on to split still more atoms.

4. Which word means "a person forced to leave their own country, often to escape war or danger"? First letter: r…
a refugee (n) — a person forced to leave their country to escape war or persecution. Example: many brilliant physicists arrived in America as refugees from Europe.
5. Which word means "weapons and military equipment"? Think of what a country builds when it prepares for war. First letters: arm…
armaments (n, plural) — weapons and military equipment. Example: both sides poured money into armaments.
6. Which word means "the deliberate setting off of a bomb or explosive charge"? Think of the technical term for the exact moment the device goes off. First letters: det…

C · Synonym or opposite?

Decide how the two words relate, then reveal.

7. Is devastating closer in meaning to harmless or to destructive?
devastating (adj) — causing terrible, overwhelming damage. It is close to destructive; the opposite of harmless.
8. Is to capitulate closer in meaning to hold out or to give in?
to capitulate (v) — to stop resisting and accept defeat, usually on the enemy's terms. It is close to give in / concede; the opposite of hold out / keep resisting.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A letter and a fear

The story begins not with an explosion but with a letter. In the 1930s, physicists in Europe discovered that the atom could be split — and that splitting it might unleash a vast, sudden release of energy. Many of the brightest minds working on this were refugees who had fled fascism in Germany and Italy, and they were haunted by one thought: if such a weapon could be built, Nazi Germany might build it first.

In 1939, urged on by fellow scientists, Albert Einstein signed a letter to the American president, Franklin Roosevelt, warning that "extremely powerful bombs of a new type" might now be possible. The warning was taken seriously. Slowly at first, and then with enormous urgency after the United States entered the war in 1941, a secret programme was set up. It would come to be known, from its early offices in New York, as the Manhattan Project.

It was placed under the command of an army engineer, General Leslie Groves, who chose a brilliant, complicated physicist named J. Robert Oppenheimer to lead the scientific work. Their task was almost unimaginable: to turn a few lines in a physics paper into a working weapon, before the enemy could — and to tell almost no one they were doing it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did so many of the scientists fear that Germany might build the bomb first?

2. What was the Einstein letter, and what did it lead to?

1. Splitting the atom had been discovered in Europe, and many of the leading physicists were refugees from fascism who knew how advanced German science was; they feared the Nazis would get there first.

2. A 1939 letter signed by Einstein warning President Roosevelt that powerful new bombs might be possible; it led to the secret programme that became the Manhattan Project.

Discuss:
  • The project was born out of fear that the enemy would build the weapon first. Does that fear justify building it yourself? Where does that logic lead?

Part 2 — A secret city and a flash of light

What followed was one of the largest and most secretive efforts ever attempted. Whole towns were built where none had stood before. At Los Alamos, high in the New Mexico desert, thousands of scientists and their families were gathered behind fences to work on the bomb's design. At Oak Ridge in Tennessee and Hanford in Washington, vast plants were constructed to produce the rare materials — uranium and plutonium — that the weapon needed. More than a hundred thousand people were employed, yet the work was so tightly classified that most of them never knew what they were making.

The physics was as hard as the secrecy. To make the bomb work, a chain reaction had to be started and controlled with terrifying precision. Rival designs were argued over, tested and rebuilt. Money was spent on a scale that only a wartime government could command. And all the while, a quieter unease grew among some of the scientists: what exactly were they about to give the world?

Before dawn on 16 July 1945, in the desert at a site codenamed "Trinity", the first atomic device was detonated. The blast lit the sky brighter than the sun and was felt for miles. Watching it, Oppenheimer later recalled a line from an ancient Hindu text: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds." The weapon worked. By then, Germany had already surrendered — but the war in the Pacific went on.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why were whole new towns like Los Alamos and Oak Ridge built, and why did most workers not know their real purpose?

2. What was the Trinity test, and what had changed in the war by the time it happened?

1. They were secret sites to design the bomb and to produce uranium and plutonium; the work was so classified that most of the workforce were told only their small part, not the whole purpose.

2. Trinity was the first atomic test, in the New Mexico desert on 16 July 1945; by then Germany had surrendered, but the war against Japan continued.

Discuss:
  • Germany had already surrendered before the bomb was ready. Some scientists began to ask whether it should still be used. Why might that question have been so hard to raise?

Part 3 — Hiroshima, Nagasaki, and after

In early August 1945, the decision was taken to use the new weapon against Japan. On 6 August, an atomic bomb was dropped on the city of Hiroshima; three days later, a second was dropped on Nagasaki. Tens of thousands of people were killed at once, and many thousands more died afterwards from their injuries and from the effects of radiation. Most of the dead were civilians. On 15 August, Japan surrendered, and the Second World War came to an end.

The debate over that decision has never truly ended. To some, the bombings brought a swift close to a terrible war and spared the still greater loss of life that a full invasion of Japan might have caused. To others, the deliberate destruction of two cities full of civilians can never be justified. Both arguments are still made, seriously and in good faith, to this day.

For the scientists, the reckoning was personal. Oppenheimer, who had led the work, later spoke of the "sin" that physicists had come to know, and pressed for the new weapons to be controlled rather than multiplied. He was largely ignored. Within a few years the Soviet Union had built its own bomb, and the world entered the long, tense standoff of the nuclear age — a shadow under which, in a sense, we all still live. The Manhattan Project had answered the question of whether such a weapon could be built. It left humanity with the far harder question of what to do now that it existed.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened in August 1945, and how did the war end?

2. Why does the story say the project left "a far harder question"?

1. Atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima (6 August) and Nagasaki (9 August), killing tens of thousands, most of them civilians; Japan surrendered on 15 August, ending the Second World War.

2. Because building the bomb proved it was possible, but did not answer how humanity should live with such a weapon; other countries soon built their own, and the nuclear age began.

Discuss:
  • Oppenheimer helped create the weapon and then spent years arguing it should be controlled. Can the person who builds something also be its conscience? Should they be?

Grammar — the passive voice

Read the story again and you'll notice how often it says what was done without saying who did it: the bomb was dropped, the towns were built, the work was kept secret. That is the passive voice — and it is no accident that history, science and news lean on it so heavily. It lets you put the action centre-stage and leave the doer in the background.

How it's formed: be (in the right tense) + the past participle of the main verb.
• Present: "Weapons are made here."
• Past: "The bomb was dropped on 6 August." · "Whole towns were built in secret."
• Present perfect: "The debate has never been settled."
• With a modal: "The weapon could be controlled." · "It had to be tested first."
Why choose the passive?
• The doer is unknown or obvious: "Uranium was produced at Oak Ridge."
• The doer is deliberately left out — very common with responsibility: "The decision was taken to use the bomb." (by whom, exactly?)
• You want the action or the object to be the topic: "Two cities were destroyed."
To name the doer, add by: "The letter was signed by Einstein."

1. Make it passive

Rewrite the active verb as a passive one. Keep the same tense; you don't need to name the doer.

1. Scientists split the atom in the 1930s. → The atom in the 1930s.

2. The army built a secret city at Los Alamos. → A secret city at Los Alamos.

3. They have never settled the debate. → The debate settled.

1. was split (past simple passive: was/were + past participle)

2. was built

3. has never been (present perfect passive: has been + past participle)

2. Complete with the correct passive form

Put the verb in brackets into the passive, in the tense the sentence needs.

1. More than a hundred thousand people (employ) on the project.

2. The material (must / produce) before the bomb could be assembled.

3. Today these weapons (still / hold) by several countries.

1. were employed (past simple passive)

2. had to be produced (modal passive: had to + be + past participle)

3. are still held (present simple passive)

3. Active or passive — which serves the sentence better?

Fill each gap with the given verb in whichever voice fits the note in brackets. Read the paragraph aloud when you're done.

The first device (detonate) before dawn in the desert (the action is the focus; the doer doesn't matter). Watching it, Oppenheimer (feel) that the world had changed (we want him as the subject). Later, the decision (take) to use the weapon (responsibility left unnamed on purpose).

was detonated (passive — the action matters, not who pressed the switch) · felt (active — Oppenheimer is the subject) · was taken (passive — the doer is deliberately left unsaid)

Notice how switching to the passive quietly removes the person responsible — which is exactly why it's so common when the subject is a difficult decision.
