Nature of God & Creation

Omnipotence, Omnibenevolence, Justice

Omnipotent: All-powerful. God can do anything consistent with His nature. Examples: Creation, miracles, resurrection.

Omnibenevolent: Perfectly good and loving. God always acts in humanity's best interest. Shows through grace, forgiveness, care.

Just: Fair and righteous. God judges sin but also offers redemption. Revealed through law, prophets, Jesus.

Key question: How do these attributes coexist when evil exists? This is the Problem of Evil.

Trinity: One God, Three Persons

Definition: God exists eternally as Father, Son (Jesus), and Holy Spirit—three distinct persons in one divine substance.

Biblical basis: Matthew 3:16-17 (Baptism of Jesus—all three present); 2 Corinthians 13:14 (Trinitarian blessing).

Why it matters: Shows God's relationship with humanity through incarnation and presence of Holy Spirit in believers.

Creation (Genesis 1-2)

God created universe in six days. Humans made "in God's image"—bearing moral and relational capacity. Creation is good.

The Word Made Flesh (John 1:1-3)

Jesus is the divine Word through whom all creation was made. Incarnation unites divine and human natures.

Incarnation, Crucifixion & Resurrection

The Incarnation

What: God became human in Jesus Christ. The divine entered history and human experience.

Significance: Bridges heaven and earth. Allows God to experience suffering, temptation, death. Reveals God's love by joining us in our condition.

Theological impact: Makes salvation possible through Jesus' perfect humanity and divinity.

Crucifixion: The Death That Changed Everything

Historical context: Jesus crucified by Romans around 30 CE under Pontius Pilate.

Theological meaning: Jesus bore human sin; His death was a sacrifice for redemption.

Different atonement theories:

  • Substitutionary: Jesus died in place of sinners (forensic/legal model).
  • Christus Victor: Jesus defeated evil and death through resurrection.
  • Moral influence: Jesus' sacrifice inspires transformation in believers.

Resurrection & Ascension

Resurrection (Easter): Jesus rose from death three days after crucifixion. First-hand accounts by disciples. Proves Jesus' divinity and power over death.

Ascension (40 days later): Jesus returned to heaven in bodily form. Marks transition from earthly to spiritual presence via Holy Spirit.

Hope for believers: Resurrection guarantees eternal life for those who believe; Ascension promises Christ's continued intercession.

Afterlife & Salvation

Heaven

Eternal presence with God; reward for believers. State of perfect communion, joy, absence of suffering.

Hell

Eternal separation from God; consequence of rejecting salvation. Some see as conscious torment, others as complete separation.

Purgatory (Roman Catholic)

State of cleansing for those with venial sin before entering heaven. Not universal in Protestant theology. Reflects belief in God's mercy and final justice.

Three Salvation Paths in Christianity

By Grace: Salvation is God's free gift (Ephesians 2:8-9). Accepted through faith. Most common Protestant view.

By Works: Salvation includes active living out of faith through obedience and good deeds. Catholic perspective includes sacraments.

By Spirit: Salvation involves regeneration—being "born again" through Holy Spirit (John 3:3). Pentecostal/evangelical emphasis.

Key Concepts Summary

The Four Pillars

1. God's Nature: Omnipotent, omnibenevolent, just. Creator of all.

2. The Trinity: One God in three persons—Father, Son, Holy Spirit.

3. Jesus' Work: Incarnation, crucifixion, resurrection, ascension—cosmic salvation event.

4. Afterlife Hope: Resurrection promises eternal life; judgment promises justice.
