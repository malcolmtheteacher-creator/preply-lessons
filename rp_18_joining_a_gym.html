Joining a Gym

New year, new you — or so the salesperson hopes. Time to ask the right questions and hold your ground.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

You've walked past the new gym on the corner for three weeks now, telling yourself "next Monday". Today, on a whim, you actually go in. Glossy floors, a wall of running machines, someone lifting something enormous in the corner — and a bright, friendly face heading straight for you with a clipboard.

You want a few honest answers: how much it really costs, what you're tying yourself into, and whether it suits what you actually want — not what looks good on the poster. The trouble is, the person greeting you is paid to sign you up today, and they're very, very good at it.

So this isn't about being talked into anything. It's about staying friendly, getting the facts, and walking out with either a fair deal or your dignity — ideally both.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Have you ever joined a gym — or signed up for a class, an app, a course — full of good intentions? Be honest: how long did it last, and what killed it?
  2. Some people are brilliant at saying "no thanks" to a pushy salesperson; others cave every time. Which are you — and what's the last thing you got talked into that you didn't really want?

Who's who

You
The Newcomer 👟

Curious but cautious. You'd genuinely like to get fitter — but you've been burned before, and you're not signing anything until you understand exactly what you're getting.

The other voice
The Membership Consultant 📋

Warm, quick, and quietly relentless. Every objection has a smooth answer ready. There's a "today only" deal — there's always a "today only" deal.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how the newcomer keeps smiling while saying, in effect, "not so fast".

ConsultantHi there, welcome in! First time? You've picked a great day — we've got a bit of an offer on. Are you looking to join?
YouMaybe — I'm just looking into my options at the moment, to be honest. I thought I'd come in and get a feel for the place before I commit to anything.
ConsultantAbsolutely, no pressure at all. Well, let me show you around. What are you hoping to get out of it — weight loss, strength, general fitness?

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The newcomer walks in wanting "a bit fitter, a bit stronger" — nothing more. Why do they make a point of saying they're "not training for a marathon", and how does that shape which plan makes sense for them?

2. Three times the consultant nudges the newcomer towards the premium, twelve-month plan. Pick ONE of those nudges and explain the pressure tactic hidden inside it.

3. "Is that offer really only available today?" The newcomer asks it politely, but it isn't an innocent question. What are they actually testing — and what does the consultant's answer reveal?

1. By naming a modest goal out loud, the newcomer quietly resists being sold a bigger, pricier package than they need — it's a defence, not just small talk. It points them towards the cheaper or off-peak plan, since they don't need every premium extra.
2. Any of: "for a few pounds more, premium's the one everyone ends up wishing they'd taken" (social pressure + fear of regret); "that's the one that locks in your saving" (framing a year-long tie-in as a smart move); "signed up today it's thirty-two… but it has to be today" (artificial urgency). Each dresses up the consultant's goal as the customer's benefit.
3. They're testing whether the "today only" deadline is real or just a pressure tactic. The consultant's honest answer — the promotions "do tend to come round again" — confirms the urgency was manufactured, which frees the newcomer to walk away and think.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for getting the facts, naming your goals, and refusing to be rushed — at this gym and anywhere someone's trying to sell you something. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚪 Coming in without committing

Phrase 1

The moment you walk in, you're asked "Are you looking to join?" You are interested — but you don't want to sound like an easy sale, or a firm no. Which reply keeps the door open while making it clear you haven't decided?

A. "Yes, sign me up."
B. "No, just browsing, thanks."
C. "I'm just looking into my options at the moment."
C — "I'm just looking into my options at the moment."
It signals genuine interest without a shred of commitment. "Looking into my options" says you're comparing, thinking, in no hurry — the opposite of a done deal. Handy anywhere: "I'm just looking into my options" works for cars, jobs, flats, phone contracts.
Phrase 2

You want to make it clear you're here to look, not to sign — that today is about getting a feel for the place, not deciding. Complete the chunk: "I thought I'd come in and ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ before I decide."

"…get a feel for the place before I decide."
"Get a feel for" = form an impression by experiencing something first-hand. It buys you time and permission to wander without buying. Cousins: "get a feel for how it works", "get a feel for the area".

💷 Getting the facts on money

Phrase 3

You want the price — but you don't want an exact quote that pins you down, just a ballpark so you can compare. Put the pieces in order:

a rough ideacould youof the monthly cost?give me
"Could you give me a rough idea of the monthly cost?"
"A rough idea" lowers the stakes of the question — you're not committing, just gathering numbers. It also lets them answer honestly without a hard sell. Swap in anything: "a rough idea of the waiting time / the total / how long it takes".
Phrase 4

They've quoted a discounted rate, but you want to know what you're really signing up to over time — a rolling month, or a year locked in? What single question gets straight to the length of the tie-in, without sounding suspicious?

"What kind of commitment am I looking at?"
"What am I looking at?" is a lovely, casual way to ask "what are the real terms here?" — money, time, or obligation. "What kind of commitment" pulls the contract length into the open before you talk price. Also: "What sort of timescale / cost / waiting list am I looking at?"
Phrase 5

There's a joining fee on top, and you'd like to test whether it's actually fixed. You want a friendly way to ask "can this come down?" — not a demand, just a nudge. What's the phrase?

"Is there any wiggle room on that?"
"Wiggle room" = flexibility, space to negotiate. It's warm and slightly playful, so it opens a negotiation without turning it into a battle. Use it on prices, dates, deadlines: "Is there any wiggle room on the start date?"

🎯 Naming what you actually want

Phrase 6

You're asked what you want out of the gym. You've no grand ambitions — you just want to get moving again. Which answer sets a modest, honest goal AND quietly stops them upselling you a champion's package?

A. "I want to completely transform my body."
B. "I don't know, whatever you recommend."
C. "I'm mainly looking to get back into some kind of routine."
C — "I'm mainly looking to get back into some kind of routine."
"Mainly looking to…" names one realistic priority and rules out the rest. A alerts the salesperson to a big spender; B hands them the wheel. C keeps you modest, honest, and in control of what you'll pay for.
Phrase 7

They're pushing the top-tier plan, "everything included". You suspect you'd never touch half of it. You want to flag that — gently, without insult. Complete the chunk: "I'm not sure I'd ___ ___ a premium membership throws in."

"I'm not sure I'd use everything a premium membership throws in."
"I'm not sure I'd…" is soft doubt, not flat refusal — easy to say, hard to argue with. "Throws in" = includes as an extra. Together they question the value of the upsell without calling it a rip-off.

🛡️ Holding your ground on the hard sell

Phrase 8

Here comes the push: sign today, save today. You're not ready — but you don't want a row. In three or four words, say you won't be hurried, kindly. What's your line?

"I'd rather not rush into anything."
"I'd rather not…" is a soft, self-focused refusal — it's about your comfort, not their failure, so no one loses face. "Rush into" quietly labels the pressure for what it is. Variants: "I'd rather not decide on the spot", "I'd rather take my time with this".
Phrase 9

They insist the deal is "today only". You suspect that's a tactic. Which version calls the bluff politely, leaving them room to be honest — rather than accusing them of lying?

A. "That's obviously just a sales trick."
B. "Is that offer really only available today?"
C. "Whatever, I don't believe you."
B — "Is that offer really only available today?"
The little word "really" does all the work — it signals mild doubt while handing them a graceful way to climb down. A and C win the argument and lose the goodwill; B tests the deadline and keeps you both smiling.

🚶 Leaving the door open

Phrase 10

You're ready to leave — undecided, but on good terms, and you might well come back. You want to end warmly without promising anything. Which exit line does that best?

A. "No."
B. "I'll definitely join, I promise."
C. "Let me take the details away and have a think, and I'll come back to you."
C — "Let me take the details away and have a think, and I'll come back to you."
"Take the details away and have a think" claims your right to decide in your own time; "I'll come back to you" keeps it friendly and leaves the door ajar. A slams it; B over-promises. C is the graceful non-decision.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole visit out loud using all ten, in order — walk in without committing → get the facts on money → name your goal → survive the hard sell → leave on good terms. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

🏋️ The friend who won't take no

One more round — no script, no safety net. This time the person selling to you isn't a stranger: it's a friend who's become a personal trainer, and they want you as their first paying client. It's a "special rate, just for you", three sessions a week, starting Monday — and they keep bringing up how good it would be for the friendship, too. You'd like to help them, but three sessions a week is far more than you want, and the price is more than you can spend. Start the timer and talk your way through it: be kind, be honest, protect both your wallet and the friendship. Can you say no to the offer without saying no to the person? One rule: no white lies — no pretending you're "too busy" or "already have a trainer". Refuse honestly.

The Echo 🕰️

You're worrying about thirty-two pounds a month and whether you'll actually turn up. Spare a thought for the Spartans, who took fitness rather more seriously. In ancient Sparta, physical training wasn't a New Year's resolution — it was the whole point of being a citizen, drilled from childhood in a brutal state programme called the agogē.

No "today only" offers, no off-peak discount, no quietly skipping leg day. You trained, or you weren't really a Spartan. Twenty-five centuries later, we've turned the same instinct into a business — glossy floors, monthly direct debits, and a friendly face with a clipboard.

The Spartans never had to be talked into it. You, on the other hand, just have to survive the sales pitch.
