Story one: The Track to Nowhere

Britain, 1840s. A brilliant new technology. A country that couldn’t stop investing. Before each part, guess. Then reveal.

1A new technology makes travel faster than anyone imagined. Investors rush in. What happens to share prices?
Prices climbed steeply. Between about 1843 and 1845, railway shares roughly doubled, and ordinary people, not only the rich, wanted a piece.
2Anyone can propose a railway if Parliament approves it. What would you expect to happen?
The peak came in 1846: Parliament passed more than 250 acts for new lines, covering roughly 9,500 miles of proposed track in a single year. Many buyers paid only a small deposit, planning to sell at a profit before the rest was due.
3What breaks the spell?
The remaining payments came due just as confidence slipped. People who couldn’t pay were forced to sell, and prices fell steeply from 1847 and kept falling for years. Many small investors lost their savings.
4Was it all wasted?
Not at all. Many planned lines were never built, but the country was left with a huge network that served it for generations. The technology was real. The price people paid for it wasn’t.

Story two: The Company Everyone Believed

A giant energy-trading company was named “America’s Most Innovative Company” by a leading business magazine for six years running. Then, in autumn 2001, hidden debts came to light, and by 2 December it had filed for bankruptcy. Its name was Enron.

What outsiders could see

Fast growth. Awards. A rising share price. Clever people and a confident story.

What they couldn’t see

Large debts and losses moved into separate partnerships the company controlled, so they didn’t appear on its main accounts.

Your Phrase Kit

Eight phrases for eight difficult moments. Look at the situation, say what you’d say, then flip the card. Steal the ones you like.

Ten words worth owning

overbuild (v) — build more than the market will ever use. Example: "Investors overbuilt the network, and half of it sat empty."
stranded asset (n) — something costly that has lost its value before it has paid for itself. Example: "The empty station became a stranded asset."
exposure (n) — the amount you stand to lose if things go wrong. Example: "Our exposure to a single supplier is too high."
hedge (v) — protect yourself against a possible loss by also taking a balancing position. Example: "They hedged their bets by signing two suppliers."
due diligence (n) — careful checking of facts and risks before you commit to a deal. Example: "The buyer spent months on due diligence."
red flag (n) — a warning sign that something may be wrong. Example: "Debts hidden in separate partnerships were a red flag."
scrutiny (n) — close and critical examination. Example: "The accounts came under heavy scrutiny."
contingency (n) — a plan or reserve for something that might go wrong. Example: "We keep a ten per cent contingency in the budget."
substantiate (v) — support a claim with evidence. Example: "She could not substantiate the rumour."
reportedly (adv) — according to what has been said, though not proven. Example: "The director has reportedly resigned."

The Verdict

Remember this?

Answer aloud first. Then check.

Name three ways to soften a strong claim.
For example: “It seems that…”, “arguably…”, “to some extent…”, “on the whole…”, “tends to…”.
Give two polite ways to disagree.
“I see it a little differently.” · “I take your point, but…” · “I’m not sure that follows.”
How do “might”, “could” and “is likely to” differ?
They climb a ladder of confidence: might is a real but small chance, could is open possibility, is likely to means you expect it.
How do you turn a story into a point in one sentence?
“What this shows is that…” · “The lesson here is…” · “In other words…”
