Cleopatra

The last pharaoh of Egypt — a shrewd ruler whose enemies wrote the story we inherited.

Today: you'll tell the story of a queen's rise and fall, and discuss how history remembers powerful women — using the second conditional to imagine the present differently, and the language of politics and power.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. For five generations the same dynasty had crowned its sons and daughters, each new ruler the child or grandchild of the last.
a dynasty (n) — a line of rulers from the same family who hold power across many generations.
2. When the old king died, three of his children began plotting in secret, each desperate to be the one who finally sat on the throne.
the throne (n) — the position or power of a king or queen (literally, the royal chair).
3. The young prince was crowned co-ruler alongside his sister, so every royal order now had to carry both their names.
a co-ruler (n) — one of two or more people who govern a country together at the same time.
4. The old merchant was too shrewd to be fooled; one glance at the buyer's nervous hands and he doubled his price on the spot.
shrewd (adj) — clever and sharp in practical matters, especially in judging people and situations.
5. Surrounded by stronger neighbours, the two small kingdoms signed an alliance, each promising to send soldiers if the other was ever attacked.
an alliance (n) — a formal agreement between people or states to cooperate, often for protection or power.
6. Two runners had trained for this race their whole lives, and each crossed the line glaring at the rival in the next lane.
a rival (n) — a person competing with you for the same thing; an opponent. (adj: rival)
7. By morning the city walls were covered in posters and pamphlets, all of them propaganda painting the general as a hero and his enemy as a monster.
propaganda (n) — information, often biased or misleading, spread to shape how people think and feel.
8. In the old film the villain is a seductress, smiling and whispering until the powerful man forgets his duty and does exactly what she wants.
a seductress (n) — a woman portrayed as using her attractiveness to tempt and manipulate men. (Here, an unfair label.)
9. The flood annihilated the village: when the water finally fell, not a single house, fence, or tree was left standing.
to annihilate (v) — to destroy something utterly and completely. (n: annihilation)
10. After the war the victorious empire simply annexed the little republic, redrawing the maps so it became just another province under their flag.
to annex (v) — to take control of a territory and add it to your own state, usually by force.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The queen who fought for her throne

Cleopatra VII was born around 69 BC into the Ptolemies, a Greek-Macedonian dynasty that had ruled Egypt since the death of Alexander the Great. For nearly three hundred years her family had governed from Alexandria while remaining culturally Greek — and, remarkably, Cleopatra was the first of them who is known to have actually learned the Egyptian language. She was famously gifted with languages and could reportedly speak with envoys from many nations without an interpreter. Far from the simple beauty of legend, she was an educated, multilingual and politically shrewd ruler.

When her father died in 51 BC, the throne passed jointly to Cleopatra and her younger brother, Ptolemy XIII — for the Ptolemies often married within the family and ruled as co-rulers. But sharing power with an ambitious brother proved impossible. His advisers pushed her out, and she was forced to flee. Her chance to return came from Rome, in the person of Julius Caesar, who arrived in Egypt pursuing his own civil war.

Cleopatra understood that Egypt's survival now depended on Rome, and that her own survival depended on Caesar. She formed an alliance with him; their relationship was both political and personal, and she bore a son, Caesarion, whom she presented as Caesar's. With Caesar's backing she defeated her brother's faction and was restored to the throne. For a few years, the most powerful man in the world was her ally.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was unusual about Cleopatra compared with the rest of her dynasty?

2. Why did she form an alliance with Julius Caesar, and what did she gain from it?

1. Her family, the Ptolemies, were Greek-Macedonian and had stayed culturally Greek for centuries; Cleopatra was the first known to have learned the Egyptian language, and she was strikingly multilingual and politically shrewd.

2. She had been pushed off the throne by her brother's faction; allying with Caesar (Rome's most powerful man) let her defeat them and be restored to power, while protecting Egypt's position with Rome.

Discuss:
  • Cleopatra's only realistic path back to power ran through a foreign general. When a small state depends on a great power, how much genuine independence can its ruler ever really have?

Part 2 — Antony, Octavian and Actium

In 44 BC Caesar was assassinated in Rome, and Cleopatra's careful arrangement collapsed overnight. Power in Rome was now contested between Caesar's heir, the young Octavian, and his most experienced general, Mark Antony. When Antony summoned Cleopatra to meet him, she arrived in spectacular style — and the two formed an alliance that was, once again, both political and personal. They had three children together, and Antony increasingly governed Rome's eastern provinces alongside her, granting lands and titles to her and to her children.

To Octavian, this was a gift. He could not openly attack a fellow Roman without starting another civil war, so instead he turned his propaganda machine on Cleopatra. He painted her as a foreign seductress who had bewitched a great Roman soldier and meant to rule Rome from Egypt — and Antony, in his telling, as a man who had thrown away his Roman virtue for her. By shifting the blame onto a foreign queen, Octavian made war on Antony look like a war to defend Rome itself.

The decisive clash came at sea, off the coast of Greece, at the Battle of Actium in 31 BC. Octavian's smaller, faster ships, commanded by his able admiral Agrippa, outmanoeuvred Antony's larger fleet. In the confusion Cleopatra's squadron broke away and sailed for Egypt, and Antony followed her. Their combined naval power was effectively annihilated. The war was not quite over, but after Actium their cause was lost.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Octavian aim his propaganda at Cleopatra rather than openly at Antony?

2. What happened at the Battle of Actium, and why did it matter?

1. Attacking Antony directly would have looked like another Roman civil war; by branding Cleopatra a dangerous foreign seductress, Octavian could frame the conflict as Rome defending itself against an outside threat.

2. At Actium (31 BC) Octavian's faster ships under Agrippa outmanoeuvred Antony's fleet; Cleopatra's squadron broke away and Antony followed, and their naval power was effectively annihilated — after which their cause was lost.

Discuss:
  • Octavian won the war of words long before he won at sea. Why is controlling the story about an enemy sometimes more powerful than defeating them in battle?

Part 3 — Two deaths and a legend

The following year, 30 BC, Octavian's forces reached Egypt itself. As Alexandria fell, Antony, believing Cleopatra was already dead, took his own life with his sword. Cleopatra outlived him only briefly. Rather than be paraded as a captive through the streets of Rome in Octavian's victory celebration, she chose to die on her own terms.

How exactly she died is still debated. The famous image is of an asp — an Egyptian cobra, a symbol of royalty — brought to her in a basket. But several ancient writers suggest she more likely used a concentrated poison, perhaps with a sharp implement, and some historians wonder how freely the choice was even made. What is certain is that with her death the Ptolemaic dynasty ended. Egypt, the last great independent kingdom of the Mediterranean world, was annexed and became a Roman province; Octavian became Augustus, Rome's first emperor.

The victor also wrote the history. For centuries afterwards, Cleopatra survived mainly as the seductress of Roman propaganda — a beautiful, dangerous woman who ruined good men. Modern historians tell a very different story: of a capable ruler who inherited a struggling kingdom, kept it wealthy and independent for two decades, commanded fleets, negotiated with the masters of the world, and very nearly changed the course of Rome itself. The "femme fatale" was, in large part, her enemy's invention.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is the manner of Cleopatra's death uncertain?

2. How does the modern view of Cleopatra differ from the image Roman propaganda left behind?

1. The famous snakebite (an asp) is only one version; several ancient writers suggest poison instead, perhaps applied with a sharp implement, and some historians even question how free her choice was — so the details are genuinely disputed.

2. Propaganda left her as a beautiful, dangerous seductress who ruined men; modern historians see a shrewd, capable ruler who kept Egypt wealthy and independent for two decades and nearly altered Rome's history — the "femme fatale" being largely her enemy's invention.

Discuss:
  • Cleopatra has been remembered for two thousand years mainly through the words of the people who defeated her. How much of any famous figure's reputation is really written by their enemies?

The grammar this story runs on: the second conditional

Cleopatra is irresistible to "what ifs" — and the second conditional is the tool for them. It lets you imagine the present or future differently: situations that aren't real now, or that are unlikely. "If Cleopatra were alive today, she would command any room she entered." She isn't alive — so this is an imagined present, not a memory of the past.

The shape:
• If + past simple, … would + base verb. "If Egypt had a stronger navy, Rome would think twice."
• would → could / might when the result is an ability or a possibility, not a certainty: "she could outmanoeuvre them", "Rome might hesitate".
• If I were… — in careful, formal English we use were for every subject in the if-clause: "If I were in her place…", "If he were a weaker ruler…".
The past tense here is not about the past. It signals distance from reality — an imagined now.

1. Building the second conditional

Use if + past simple for the imagined situation, and would / could / might + base verb for the result.

1. If we (have) Cleopatra's own diaries, we (understand) her far better.

2. If a ruler today (speak) nine languages, the press (call) it extraordinary.

3. If Egypt (not control) the grain Rome depended on, its queen (have) far less leverage.

1. had / would understand

2. spoke / would call

3. did not control / would have

2. were · could · might — adding nuance

Use were for every subject in the if-clause, and reach for could (ability) or might (possibility) instead of would when you're not certain.

1. If Cleopatra (be) a man, historians (might / judge) her ambition very differently.

2. If I (be) in her place at Actium, I'm not sure I (could / do) anything wiser.

3. If her charisma (be) the only thing she had, Rome (not fear) her so much.

1. were / might judge

2. were / could do

3. were / would not fear

3. Inversion — "Were she…" (dropping if)

In formal or literary English you can drop if and put were first: If she were… → Were she…. The meaning is identical; the tone is more elevated. Rewrite each sentence with inversion, then read it aloud.

1. If Cleopatra were alive today, she would dominate the news. → alive today, she would dominate the news.

2. If Rome were less dependent on Egypt's wealth, it would treat her with less caution. → less dependent on Egypt's wealth, it would treat her with less caution.

3. If I were advising her, I would tell her to trust no one in the Senate. → advising her, I would tell her to trust no one in the Senate.

1. Were she

2. Were Rome

3. Were I

Notice: the past form (were) signals an imagined present, not the past — Cleopatra is not actually alive now.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Cleopatra survived by allying with the powerful men of her age. Was she using them, were they using her, or is that the wrong way to look at it?
