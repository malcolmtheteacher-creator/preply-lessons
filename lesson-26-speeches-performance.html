A speech laid out like a poem

London, 1940. A prime minister is about to give a speech that the whole country is waiting to hear. He does not read it from a page of dense paragraphs. His secretaries have typed it in short lines, with extra space between phrases, in a layout they called psalm form because it looked like a psalm in a prayer book.

Winston Churchill, famous for his speeches, wrote them out in full and went over them again and again. But on the day he did not trust them to a block of text. The layout told his eye where to breathe, where to pause, and where the next idea began.

What was 'psalm form'?
A way of typing a speech in short lines with spaces, so the speaker can see where to pause and where each idea starts.
Talk about it. How do you prepare when you must speak for a long time? Do you write it out?

There is a lesson in it for memory. A long speech is not one thing. It is a series of small pieces, each with its own beginning, middle and end. If you know where each piece starts, you can always find your way.

A twenty-minute talk is too long for a single list of five pictures. But it is easy for a speaker with a main palace, in which each room holds one section, and in which each section has its own small chain of pictures, like drawers in a cabinet.

How can a long speech be kept in mind?
As a series of small pieces: a main palace with one room per section, and a small chain of pictures in each.
Talk about it. Think of a speech or lecture you remember. Can you say what its sections were?

The best speakers also use patterns that listeners can follow without effort. The oldest is the rule of three: ideas in threes feel complete, and are easy to remember for both the speaker and the audience. Another is the return: an image or phrase that comes back at the end.

In this lesson you will plan a longer speech, add small anchors between the sections, and rehearse it aloud the way a performer does: for timing, for breath, and for the moment when everything goes wrong and you must carry on.

Nested palaces for a longer talk

For a talk of about twenty minutes, use a main palace for the sections and a small chain in each room for the details.

  1. Break the talk into five or six sections. Opening, three main parts (the rule of three), a return, a close.
  2. Give each section a room. The main palace: door, hall, living room, kitchen, stairs, bedroom.
  3. Put one big picture in each room. This is the section's title: what the room is about.
  4. Add a short chain of three or four details in the room. On furniture or walls: statistics, examples, the story.
  5. Add an anchor at every turn. A fixed gesture or picture: a door opening at each new section.
A twenty-minute talk on a town's new library
Six sections.
Door: openingA giant book is knocking on the door and a crowd cheers: 'Welcome!'
Hall: why it was neededA cracked old roof leaks rain on the books; three buckets in a row: leaks, cold, crowding.
Living room: what it offersThree armchairs: children, students, retired people, each reading a different glowing book.
Kitchen: what it costCoins pouring from a pot, counted in three bowls: council, donors, volunteers.
Stairs: the returnThe giant book from the door walks up the stairs, now wearing a crown.
Bedroom: the closeA lamp is switched on over a pillow made of pages: 'Come back tomorrow'.
The picture: One main palace, six rooms, and three little chains: the whole twenty minutes is a walk.

Rehearsing like a performer

  • Time each section. Say the speech aloud with a clock. Mark where you are at the halfway point.
  • Practise the pauses. A pause before an important point lets the audience catch up.
  • Practise recovery. Stop in the middle on purpose, and find the next picture. You will be glad you did.
  • Use the body. Your ten body pegs (lesson 3) can hold the ten most important phrases, if you need them on stage.
  • Calm the start. Breathe out slowly, and look at the first place on your route.

Plan a long talk

Choose a subject you could talk about for ten to twenty minutes. Write five or six sections, with one big picture for each.
Subject: why people should walk more. 1 Opening: giant shoes at the door. 2 Health: a heart jogging. 3 Money: a piggy bank on legs. 4 Mood: a smiling sun. 5 Return: the giant shoes again, now with wings. 6 Close: a path stretching into the horizon.

Add details

Pick two sections. For each, write a chain of three details (facts or examples) as pictures on the furniture of the room.
Health room: on the sofa, a cushion shaped like a heart; on the table, a pill bottle dancing; on the window, a weighing scale with wings. Money room: on the fridge, coins; on the stove, a smiling wallet; on the shelf, a ticket to the cinema.

Anchors

Write one fixed anchor you will use at every change of section (a gesture, a step, a picture of a door opening).
Every time I move to a new section I take one step forward and picture a door swinging open.
