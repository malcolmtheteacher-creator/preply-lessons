Understanding Arguments

B1 Intermediate Academic English

⏱️ Time: 5:00

The Three Parts of an Argument

All academic arguments have the same basic structure: claim + reasons + evidence.

1. Claim (Main Idea)

What the author believes or argues. Often stated in the introduction.

2. Reasons (Why)

Why the author believes the claim. Logical explanations that support the main idea.

3. Evidence (Proof)

Facts, statistics, examples, or research that support the reasons. Shows the claim is true.

Simple Example

Claim: "Universities should require all first-year students to live on campus."

Reason: "Living on campus helps students adapt to university life and build community."

Evidence: "A 2023 study found that residential students report 23% higher satisfaction and are 15% more likely to graduate on time."

Spotting Weak Arguments

Not all arguments are strong. Good critical readers identify weaknesses in reasoning.

Common Logical Fallacies (Weak Arguments)

  • Hasty Generalisation: "One person did this, so everyone does" — Not enough evidence
  • Ad Hominem: Attacking the person instead of their argument — Irrelevant
  • False Cause: "This happened, then that happened, so one caused the other" — May be coincidence
  • Circular Reasoning: Using the claim to prove the claim — No real evidence
  • Appeal to Authority: "Famous person X believes this, so it's true" — Authority isn't always right
  • Straw Man: Attacking a weak version of the argument — Misrepresenting the opposing view

Asking Critical Questions

  • Is the claim clearly stated?
  • Are the reasons logical and relevant?
  • Is the evidence sufficient and credible?
  • Are there counterarguments the author hasn't addressed?
  • Is the argument biased or one-sided?

Identifying Argument Components

For each passage, identify the claim, reasons, and evidence.

Example 1

"Smartphones are harming young people's mental health. Research shows that teenagers who spend more than four hours daily on social media experience higher rates of anxiety and depression. A 2022 study of 5,000 adolescents found that heavy phone users had 30% more depressive symptoms than light users."

Claim: "Smartphones are harming young people's mental health." Reason: Heavy phone use correlates with anxiety and depression. Evidence: 2022 study of 5,000 adolescents showing 30% more symptoms in heavy users. Strong argument with specific evidence.

Example 2

"Exercise is good for you. Many people exercise regularly. Therefore, society should require daily exercise."

Claim: "Society should require daily exercise" Weak reason: "Many people do it" (that doesn't mean it should be required). Missing evidence: No data or research. The logic is weak—popularity doesn't justify mandatory requirements.

Example 3

"Remote work reduces productivity. When companies switched to remote work during 2020, some experienced difficulties coordinating teams. Therefore, all employees should work in offices."

Claim: "All employees should work in offices" Weak reason: Some companies had difficulties (cherry-picked examples) Weak evidence: No actual data. Logical fallacy: hasty generalisation from selected examples. Ignores successful remote work models.

Example 4

"Online education is effective. A meta-analysis of 355 studies comparing online and in-person learning found no significant difference in learning outcomes. Furthermore, online students reported greater flexibility and lower costs. However, online learning requires strong self-discipline, which not all students possess."

Claim: "Online education is effective" Strong reasons: Equivalent learning outcomes, greater flexibility, lower costs. Strong evidence: Meta-analysis of 355 studies. Additional note: acknowledges counterargument (self-discipline requirement). Balanced, well-supported argument.
