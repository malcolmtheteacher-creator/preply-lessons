The Mould in the Dirty Dish

The most famous accident in medicine — and the forgotten decade of work that turned one dirty dish into the drug that saved a hundred million lives.

Today: you'll take apart medicine's favourite lucky accident — and you'll master the grammar of what almost didn't happen: if he had washed that dish, millions would have died.

The vocabulary of this story

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. He came back from holiday to a bench of unwashed plates. On one, something furry and green had grown — a spot of mould, blown in from somewhere.
mould (n) — the furry fungus that grows on old bread, damp walls — and one legendary dish. (US spelling: mold.) Adjective: mouldy.
2. Around the mould was a clear ring where the bacteria had died — as if the fungus were producing something that killed them. That something was the first true antibiotic.
an antibiotic (n) — a medicine that kills bacteria (and does nothing against viruses — the distinction every doctor spends a career explaining). A course of antibiotics; antibiotic resistance.
3. The mould juice was powerful but hopelessly fragile: nobody could purify it — separate the active substance from the soup around it — in useful amounts.
to purify (v) — to remove everything except the substance you want. Noun: purification. The unglamorous verb on which this entire story actually turns.

B · Which word means…?

Three words: to scale up · a compound · a trial. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which word means a chemical substance made of combined elements?
a compound (n) — an active compound, a chemical compound. The compound here: penicillin, named after the mould Penicillium.
2. Which word means a formal test of a medicine on patients?
a trial (n) — a clinical trial; to run a trial; trial results. The first penicillin "trial" was one dying policeman — a story you are about to meet.
3. Which phrase means to go from laboratory amounts to industrial production?
to scale up (v) — from grams to tonnes. Noun: scale-up. The heroes of this verb grew mould in bedpans, milk churns and eventually 100,000-litre tanks.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Fleming published his finding in 1929, and the world's reaction was a collective shrug — the paper was largely overlooked. Overlooked means…
  (a) carefully studied   (b) not noticed or not given attention   (c) banned
(b) — to overlook (v) — to fail to notice or value. "An overlooked discovery / detail / person." Ten years of penicillin's history fit inside this one word.
2. By 1944 factories were producing enough for every wounded soldier — an industrial quantity. Industrial means…
  (a) experimental   (b) on the scale of factories, not laboratories   (c) military
(b) — industrial (adj) — industrial production / scale / quantities. The gap between a discovery and an industry is where this story's forgotten heroes live.

D · Word building & collocation

1. You know resist. Build the medical family: bacteria develop ___ (noun) · a ___ infection (adjective: the drug no longer works) · superbugs are ___ to almost everything.
Resistance · a resistant infection · resistant to almost everything. Fleming himself warned about resistance in his Nobel speech in 1945 — the story's dark sequel, arriving exactly as he predicted.
2. One phrase completes all three: to happen ___ chance · to leave nothing ___ chance · a game ___ chance. And what is the adjective for a lucky accident of science?
By chance — happen by chance, leave nothing to chance (careful: that one takes to!), a game of chance — trick spotted? The set is by / to / of. The adjective: serendipitous; the noun serendipity — finding what you weren't looking for.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — September 1928: the accident

Alexander Fleming was, by the cheerful admission of everyone who knew him, not a tidy man. In September 1928 he returned to his cramped laboratory at St Mary's Hospital, London, from a two-week family holiday, and began sorting through the stacks of culture plates he had left unwashed on the bench. On one — a plate of staphylococcus bacteria — a blob of mould had grown, blown in from who knows where. Around it, something remarkable: a clear ring where the bacterial colonies had dissolved. Whatever the mould was making, it was killing bacteria. Fleming's response has entered legend: not "Eureka" but a mild "That's funny."

The luck involved was almost absurd, and scientists have enjoyed listing it ever since. The mould was a rare strain of Penicillium, possibly drifting up from an allergy researcher's lab a floor below. The plate had escaped the disinfectant bath. And that fortnight's London weather had been unusual — cool days that favoured the mould, warmer days that grew the bacteria — in the exact sequence that produced a visible killing ring. A tidier man, a different fortnight, one floor's difference in the building: no ring, no story.

Fleming did real work on his discovery — he grew the mould, showed its "juice" killed many dangerous bacteria while sparing white blood cells, and named the active substance penicillin. But he could not purify it: the compound was unstable and vanished during every attempt at concentration. He concluded it would probably be useful as a laboratory tool, perhaps as a local antiseptic, published a paper in 1929 that attracted almost no attention — and, by the mid-1930s, had largely moved on. The most important medicine of the twentieth century sat in a journal, overlooked, for a decade.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. List three pieces of luck that had to combine for the discovery to happen.

2. What did Fleming do with it — and what could he not do?

1. An untidy bench and unwashed plates; a rare Penicillium strain drifting in (possibly from the lab below); and a freak sequence of cool-then-warm weather that let mould and bacteria grow in exactly the order that produced a visible ring.

2. He identified and named penicillin, showed it killed dangerous bacteria without harming white blood cells — but he couldn't purify or stabilise it, judged it a laboratory curiosity, published to silence in 1929 and moved on.

Discuss:
  • "That's funny" — the ability to notice that a ruined experiment is interesting. What kills that ability in most workplaces, and how would you protect it?

Part 2 — Oxford, 1940: the real heroes

The story restarts in 1938, in Oxford, when a team led by the Australian pathologist Howard Florey and the German-Jewish refugee chemist Ernst Chain went hunting through old journals for natural antibacterial substances — and found Fleming's forgotten paper. What followed was everything the legend leaves out: two years of brutal chemistry to purify the unstable compound, with the endlessly inventive Norman Heatley improvising equipment from bedpans, biscuit tins, milk churns and a bathtub, because wartime Britain could spare nothing. In May 1940 came the pivotal experiment: eight mice infected with lethal streptococci; the four given penicillin lived, the four without died overnight. Florey, not a dramatic man, called it a miracle.

Then the first human. In February 1941, Albert Alexander, a 43-year-old Oxford policeman, was dying of a raging infection that had begun — so the famous telling goes — with a scratch from a rose thorn; it had cost him an eye and was consuming his face and lungs. Given penicillin, he began to recover within days — visibly, astonishingly. But the team's entire stock was so small that they collected his urine each day to re-extract and re-inject the drug. After five days the penicillin ran out. The infection returned, and Alexander died. The lesson was written in one man's fate: the medicine worked, and a dose too small to finish the job was a death sentence. They resolved to treat children next — smaller bodies, smaller doses — until there was more.

"More" meant industry, and Britain at war had no capacity to build it. In the summer of 1941 Florey and Heatley flew to the United States — carrying the mould, with spores famously smeared into their coat linings in case the samples were lost — and lit the fuse of one of history's great scale-ups. American laboratories found better production methods (a mouldy melon from a market in Peoria, Illinois supplied a far more productive strain), corn-processing waste proved a perfect growth medium, and pharmaceutical companies, pushed by the war effort, turned mould juice into an industry. Output rose from enough for one patient in 1941 to millions of doses by D-Day in June 1944, when penicillin landed in Normandy alongside the troops. Infected wounds, which had killed soldiers by the hundred thousand in the previous war, had met their answer.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the Oxford team contribute that Fleming could not?

2. What happened to Albert Alexander, and what did his case prove?

1. Everything between curiosity and medicine: purification of the unstable compound (Chain, Heatley's improvised equipment), the decisive mouse experiment, the first human trials, and the transatlantic scale-up to industrial production.

2. The dying policeman recovered dramatically on penicillin — until the tiny supply ran out (even recycling his urine), the infection returned, and he died. Proof that the drug worked, and that supply was now the whole problem.

Discuss:
  • Albert Alexander recovered and then died for lack of supply. What does his story say about the distance between "it works" and "it exists" — and who usually pays for that distance?

Part 3 — The credit, and the warning

In 1945 the Nobel Prize went, justly, to three people: Fleming, Florey and Chain. But the public story had already been written otherwise. Fleming — modest, quotable, attached to a hospital with an energetic press operation — became the lone genius of the dirty dish; Florey, who distrusted journalists and refused most interviews, watched the decade of Oxford labour compress into a footnote. Historians have been redistributing the credit ever since, and the pattern has a name in the sociology of science: the world prefers one discoverer and one lightning bolt to a team and a decade. It is worth asking, every time you hear any discovery story, whose years of unphotogenic work the lightning bolt is hiding.

Fleming used his fame for one thing above all: a warning. In his Nobel lecture he described exactly how penicillin would fail — a careless public under-dosing itself, bacteria surviving exposure and breeding resistance — and events have followed his script with grim fidelity. Resistant strains appeared within years; today, drug-resistant infections kill on the order of a million people annually, and the World Health Organization lists antibiotic resistance among the gravest threats to global health. The accident gave us the antibiotic era; nothing guarantees the era is permanent.

Which returns the story to its favourite question: was it luck? The mould was luck. Everything else was selection: Fleming was the one man in the building who would look twice at a ruined plate; Florey's team was the one group in Europe systematically mining old journals for exactly such curiosities; Heatley's bedpans and the Peoria melon were improvisation, not fortune. Chance dealt one card, and a series of prepared minds played it perfectly across sixteen years. Pasteur's line remains the best summary the story has: chance favours the prepared mind — to which this story adds a correction, because the mind that was prepared was never just one man's.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Fleming become the story's lone hero, and what does the pattern hide in general?

2. What did Fleming's Nobel warning predict, and how has it aged?

1. He was modest, quotable and well-publicised, while Florey avoided the press — and the public prefers one genius and one lightning bolt to a team and a decade. The pattern hides the years of purification, trials and scale-up behind every "eureka".

2. That careless under-dosing would breed resistant bacteria. Precisely so: resistance appeared within years, and drug-resistant infections now kill around a million people a year — the era the accident opened is not guaranteed to last.

Discuss:
  • "Chance dealt one card; prepared minds played it for sixteen years." Where should a country or company invest if it wants more stories like this — in lightning, or in the people who catch it?

Grammar — the third conditional

This story exists because a dozen accidents fell the right way — which makes it the perfect story for the grammar of alternative history: if X had happened, Y would have happened.

The form: If + past perfect, would have + past participle.
• "If Fleming had washed his plates, he would never have seen the ring."
• "If the war hadn't created the demand, the scale-up would have taken decades."
Both halves are imaginary: he didn't wash them, and he did see it.

Softer results: swap would for might (possible) or could (ability): "Someone else might have found it eventually."
Two upgrades:
• The passive result — this story's signature sentence: "Millions of lives would have been lost." (would have + been + participle)
• Dropping "if" with inversion (formal): "Had the supply lasted five more days, Albert Alexander might have lived."

The classic errors to kill: ✗ "If he would have washed…" — never would in the if-clause. ✗ "If he had washed, millions died." — the result needs would/might/could have.

2. If Florey's team (not / search) old journals, the 1929 paper (stay) forgotten.

3. Someone else (might / discover) an antibiotic later — but how many people (die) in the meantime?

1. The war created the demand; factories were built. Imagine the opposite, with a passive result: If the war hadn't created the demand,

3. The chain of luck — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the correct forms, then read the whole paragraph aloud — it is the entire story in one conditional chain.

If Fleming (be) a tidier man, the plate (be washed) before the holiday. If the mould (drift) in from a different floor, nothing (grow). If Chain and Florey (not / reopen) the forgotten paper, penicillin (remain) a curiosity — and if America's factories (not / scale) it up, the wounded of 1944 (die) as the wounded of 1917 had died. Nine conditionals, one chain — and the last one is the passive-result sentence this grammar exists for.

Going deeper

A note on the record: the rose-thorn detail of Alexander's infection is beloved and disputed (a later account suggests a bombing injury); the coat-lining spores and the Peoria melon are documented; and the luck-list has been polished by ninety years of retelling. The mice, the urine and the dates are solid.

What the story actually teaches. First, that "discovery" is the wrong unit. Between the dish (1928) and the medicine (1944) lie sixteen years, two teams, two countries and an industrial revolution in fermentation — and the world remembers thirty seconds of it. The practical rule hiding here: when you fund, hire or admire "innovation", check whether you are funding lightning or funding the catchers — because the lightning is free and useless, and the catching is expensive and everything.

Second, the anatomy of productive luck. The accident was random; the noticing was not (Fleming had already discovered one antibacterial substance, lysozyme, from an earlier accident — a sneeze onto a plate); the reviving was not (Oxford was systematically mining literature); the scaling was not. Serendipity, examined closely, is a system property: it happens to organisations that keep curious people, tolerate mess at the edges, read old work, and can move when something turns up. That is buildable — and most efficiency programmes dismantle it without noticing.

Third, the warning that came with the gift. Fleming's resistance prediction makes this the rare triumph story that ships with its own expiry mechanism, and the economics are uncomfortable: new antibiotics are the drugs the market least wants to make — used briefly, held in reserve, priced low. The penicillin story, read to its end, is an argument that some medicines are infrastructure rather than products — the same argument, from the Bet the Company series, that markets alone could not have built the sewers either.

Talk it through:
  • Lightning versus catchers: audit a "genius story" you know from your own field. Whose sixteen years does it compress?
  • Design a workplace that manufactures serendipity — three concrete policies, and the efficiency metric each one would damage.
  • Fleming warned; the world under-dosed anyway. Why do clear warnings from credible sources fail — and which of today's warnings will look like his?
  • Series thread: Semmelweis's truth accused its audience, Snow's was cheap to act on, Fleming's arrived with the demand already burning. Rank the three drivers — guilt, cost, urgency — by their power over acceptance.
