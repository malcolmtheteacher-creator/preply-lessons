The Repair Counter

A customer walks in with a machine that's 'making a sort of noise, sometimes'. You're the expert — but the expert in your second language, in a country where people talk fast. Ten minutes to diagnose, quote, advise honestly, and send her out trusting you with her wheels.

The Scene 🎬

Every repair trade is two jobs: fixing the thing, and the conversation at the counter. The second one decides whether you get to do the first.

You run the repair side of a bike shop — though everything in this lesson works word-for-word if your counter takes cars, boilers, phones or shoes instead. It's Tuesday morning, and in comes Deb: friendly, fast-talking, holding her commuter bike with the expression of someone describing a mystery illness.

The customer's version of a fault is never the fault. It's a story — "a sort of clicking, but only sometimes, and my brother-in-law reckons it's the gears." Your first job is to get the real symptoms out of the story without making her feel silly. Your second is the quote — honest about what you don't know yet, with no surprises later. Your third, and it's the one that builds a business: telling the truth even when it costs you money. "Not worth fixing" loses you thirty dollars today and wins you a customer for ten years.

And underneath it all, the immigrant tradesman's daily test: staying in charge of the conversation when the customer talks at full local speed. Asking someone to slow down feels like weakness. Done right, it's the opposite — it's what the expert in the room sounds like.

  1. Think of a tradesperson you trust completely. What did they SAY — not do — that won you?
  2. Why does "it's not worth fixing" make a customer trust you more, when it's an admission you can't help?

The Conversation 💬

Tuesday, 8:40am. The bell over the door. Deb wheels it in, already talking.

DebMorning! Right, so this thing's been driving me mental — there's a clicky sort of noise, my brother-in-law reckons it's the gears but he also reckoned the last one was the gears and it was the pedal thingy, and I've got work at nine so whatever it is, is it quick?
YouMorning. Let's find out — talk me through what it's doing. Just the noise, not the brother-in-law.
DebHa! Okay — clicking. Sort of tick-tick-tick. Started maybe two weeks back.
YouGood. When does it do it — all the time, or only when you're pedalling hard?
DebOoh. Only pedalling, now you say it. Uphill mostly. And it stops when I stop.
YouThat's useful — that rules out half the bike already. One more: once per turn of the pedals, or faster than that?
DebOnce per… you know what, I reckon once per turn. Like a clock. So it IS the pedal thingy? Craig'll be unbearable.
YouMight be. The short version is: it's the crank or the pedal bearing, not the gears — I'll know for certain in two minutes on the stand. Give me a moment. …There. Pedal bearing's dry and the crank bolt was half loose.
DebIs that bad? That sounds bad. What's the damage — and be gentle, it's nearly rent week.
YouNot bad, caught early. You're looking at somewhere between forty and sixty — I'll know exactly once it's open. Sixty only if the bearing's worn through and needs replacing rather than a service.
DebBetween forty and sixty I can live with. But if you get in there and it's suddenly a two-hundred-dollar situation—
YouThen it stops being my decision. If it turns out to be more than sixty, I'll call you before I touch anything. No surprises at pickup — that's the rule of this counter.
DebSee, Craig's guy doesn't do that, the invoice just GROWS. When can I grab it — tomorrow arvo? I can shoot through after smoko if you knock off late?
YouWhoa — run that past me again, slower. You lost me at "arvo".
DebHa! Sorry, love. Tomorrow afternoon. Can I pick it up tomorrow afternoon?
YouYou can. I'll say Thursday morning to be safe — if it's ready tomorrow, I'll ring you. I'd rather surprise you early than apologise late.
DebDeal. Oh — one more thing, since I'm here. My daughter's old bike, it's been in the shed three years, tyres are perished, gears rusted solid. Worth doing up? She's after a bike for uni.
YouHonest answer? By the time I've replaced tyres, cables, chain and gears on a bike that age, you're past two hundred — and it's still an old frame. I'd be taking your money. For that price you'll find her something better second-hand, and I'll happily check over whatever she finds. Free.
Deb…You just talked yourself out of two hundred bucks.
YouAnd talked you into coming back for the next twenty years. One thing while yours is on the stand — your brake pads are down to the metal. The clicking could wait; the brakes can't. The pads need doing now, the rest can wait. Fifteen extra, while it's open.
DebDo the brakes, absolutely. Right — I'm off. You're better than Craig's guy, don't tell Craig.
YouOne last thing so there's no confusion: crank and bearing, brake pads, forty to sixty plus fifteen, a call if it's more, ready Thursday or sooner. And if anything I've touched plays up after — if it's my work, it's fixed for nothing. Bring it in.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for anyone whose job is fixing things for the public — bikes, cars, boilers, phones, teeth. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. At a repair counter, calm and specific IS the expertise — customers can't judge your work, so they judge your sentences. Questions in plain order, prices as honest ranges, promises you can keep. And when the local speed of talk beats you, slow the room down like someone in charge of it.

🔍 Getting the truth out of the story

Phrase 1

The job: opening the diagnosis — the symptoms, not the theories. Complete it:

"___ me ___ what it's doing."

"Talk me through what it's doing."
Not "what's wrong with it?" — the customer doesn't know, that's why they're here, and the question invites the brother-in-law's theories. "What it's DOING" asks for symptoms: noises, feelings, when and where. You sort the evidence; they just have to describe it. Doctors open the same way, for the same reason.

Take it anywhere: "Talk me through what it's doing." Machines, software, bodies, businesses — the diagnostician's opener.
Phrase 2

The job: narrowing it down — the either/or question. Complete it:

"When does it do it — ___ the time, or only when ___?"

"When does it do it — all the time, or only when you're pedalling hard?"
The expert's move: offer two doors instead of an open field. "When does it happen?" gets a shrug; "all the time, or only uphill?" gets "ooh — only uphill, now you say it." Every either/or rules out half the machine, and — watch Deb — the customer starts feeling clever instead of interrogated. Two or three of these and the mystery is a part with a name.

Take it anywhere: "All the time, or only when ___?" The fault-finding question for any trade — and any IT helpdesk on earth.
Phrase 3

The job: the local's talking at full speed and you've lost the thread. Stay in charge. Which one?

A. "Sorry, sorry — my English is not so good."
B. "Whoa — run that past me again, slower."
C. *nod and hope*
B — "Whoa — run that past me again, slower."
A apologises for existing and hands over the room. C is how a Tuesday-afternoon pickup becomes a Thursday-morning argument. B is what confident locals say to fast talkers — it treats the speed as the problem, not your ears, and it keeps you the expert who happens to need a sentence repeated. Deb's reaction is the proof: a laugh, an apology, and the slow version. Nobody thought less of anyone.

Take it anywhere: "Run that past me again, slower." Slang, mumblers, phone calls — the authority-keeping repeat.
Phrase 4

The job: delivering the diagnosis without the lecture. Complete it:

"The ___ version is: it's the crank, not the gears."

"The short version is: it's the crank, not the gears."
The customer doesn't want the engineering — they want to know what it is, what it isn't, and what happens next. "The short version is" promises exactly that, and naming what it ISN'T ("not the gears") answers the fear they walked in with. Keep the long version for the customers who ask for it; they'll tell you.

Take it anywhere: "The short version is: ___." Reports, test results, bad news, good news — lead with the short version, always.

💰 Money without surprises

Phrase 5

The job: quoting when you don't know everything yet. Complete it:

"You're looking at somewhere between ___ and ___ — I'll know exactly once it's ___."

"You're looking at somewhere between forty and sixty — I'll know exactly once it's open."
The honest range: a floor, a ceiling, and the reason for the gap. A single confident number now is a lie you'll pay for at pickup; "I can't say" sounds like you don't know your trade. The range says both true things at once — I know this job, and I haven't seen inside yet. Saying what the ceiling depends on ("sixty only if the bearing's worn through") makes it airtight.

Take it anywhere: "You're looking at somewhere between ___ and ___." Repairs, projects, timelines — quote in ranges, explain the ceiling.
Phrase 6

The job: the promise that separates you from the invoice that GROWS. Put the pieces in order:

I'll call youIf it turns out to be more,before I touch anything.
"If it turns out to be more, I'll call you before I touch anything."
Every customer at every repair counter carries the same scar: the invoice that grew in the dark. This sentence removes the fear at its root — past the agreed ceiling, the decision goes back to the person paying. It costs you nothing (the call takes a minute) and it's the single strongest trust-builder in the trade. Then keep it, every time, even for ten dollars.

Take it anywhere: "If it's more than ___, I'll call you before I touch anything." Any work billed to someone who can't watch you do it.
Phrase 7

The job: promising time you can actually keep. Complete it:

"I'll say Thursday to be ___ — if it's ready sooner, I'll ___ you."

"I'll say Thursday to be safe — if it's ready sooner, I'll ring you."
The trade runs on two clocks: the hopeful one and the honest one. Quote the hopeful one and every delay is a broken promise; quote the honest one and every early finish is a gift. "To be safe" tells the customer you're deliberately padding — which, strangely, they love, because it means the date is real. As You says: better to surprise them early than apologise late.

Take it anywhere: "I'll say ___ to be safe." Deadlines, deliveries, dinner guests.

⚖️ The honesty that builds the business

Phrase 8

The job: advising against a job you'd profit from. Complete it:

"Honest answer? It's not worth fixing — I'd be ___ your ___."

"Honest answer? It's not worth fixing — I'd be taking your money."
The thirty-second sentence that builds ten-year businesses. You turned down two hundred dollars and Deb said the truest thing in the whole scene: "you just talked yourself out of two hundred bucks" — and she'll now bring you everything she owns with wheels on it, forever. The follow-up matters too: don't leave them with nothing. "You'll find something better second-hand, and I'll check it over free" turns the no into service.

Take it anywhere: "Honest answer? It's not worth it — I'd be taking your money." The phrase that costs a sale and buys a customer.
Phrase 9

The job: the honest upsell — urgent separated from optional. Complete it:

"The ___ need doing now; the ___ can wait."

"The pads need doing now; the rest can wait."
The upsell customers hate is the one where everything is urgent. This is its honest cousin: a hard line between must and may. Telling her the clicking "could wait" while the brakes can't proves you're sorting by HER safety, not your till — which is exactly why she said yes without blinking. Recommend everything you see; rank it truthfully.

Take it anywhere: "___ needs doing now; ___ can wait." Repairs, dentistry, house maintenance, work priorities — the triage sentence.
Phrase 10

The job: standing behind your work, out loud. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "If it's my work, it's fixed for nothing — bring it in."
Notice the honest boundary inside the generosity: "if it's MY work" — not everything that ever goes wrong with the bike, but everything you touched, guaranteed without argument. It's the counter's closing handshake: it says you expect your work to hold, and it turns the scariest moment for a customer — something's wrong AGAIN — into a reason to come back rather than a reason to go elsewhere.

Take it anywhere: "If it's my work, it's fixed for nothing." Every trade, every freelance job, every deliverable with your name on it.

⚡ The twist

Thursday. Deb is back, and the smile is gone: "The clicking's fixed, I'll give you that. But the gears are jumping now, and they were FINE before you had it. Craig reckons you've knocked something. I'm not paying to fix what you broke."

You checked those gears on the stand — worn, but working; you touched the crank and the brakes, nothing else. Maybe the fresh crank has changed how the wear feels. Maybe something did shift. You don't actually know yet — and she's already decided. Handle it: what do you say at the counter, what do you do with the bike, and where does your "if it's my work" promise start and stop?

The Echo 🕰️

In 1901, sponge divers sheltering off the Greek island of Antikythera found an ancient shipwreck — statues, coins, and one corroded lump of bronze the size of a shoebox that nobody thought much of. It sat in a museum drawer while the treasure got the attention. Then someone noticed it had gears.

It took a hundred years of patient diagnosis — X-rays, then CT scans, generation after generation of experts asking better and better questions of the same broken machine — to understand what it was: a two-thousand-year-old mechanical computer that predicted eclipses and tracked the planets, more sophisticated than anything built for the next fourteen centuries. Nobody could ask its maker. The machine had to be talked through what it was doing, one gear at a time.

Every broken thing that lands on a counter is a small Antikythera: it can't tell you what's wrong, so the skill is in the questions — what does it do, when does it do it, what does that rule out. The divers found the lump. The askers found the computer.
