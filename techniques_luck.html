Luck

Mark Twain (1886) — a celebrated military hero, and the one man at the banquet who knows the truth about him

Easier version available: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Read in three parts

Twain claims, at the very start, that this is a true story told to him by a clergyman who insisted on confidence. Keep that frame in mind: everything you read is one man reporting what another man told him. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The verdict on the hero arrives at the very end — do not skip ahead.

Part 1 — The banquet, and a whisper

It was at a banquet in London in honour of one of the two or three conspicuously illustrious English military names of this generation. For reasons which will presently appear, I will withhold his real name and titles, and call him Lieutenant-General Lord Arthur Scoresby.

I sat at the table, fascinated. Here was this great and famous man, the idol of the nation, and I was drinking in the sight of him. I was worshipping, and feasting my eyes on this demi-god; the people about me were doing the same.

At my side sat a clergyman, who was an old acquaintance of mine — a person of refinement and culture, a man who had been Scoresby's instructor at the military academy half a century before. Just at this moment a veiled and singular light glimmered in his eyes, and he leaned down and muttered to me — indicating the hero of the banquet with a gesture:

"Privately — he is a fool."

This verdict was so startling that I sat dazed. If he had said the rivers ran uphill I could not have been more astonished. He saw my amazement, but went tranquilly on with his coffee. Presently he said:

"If you will keep the matter to yourself, I will tell you the whole truth about this man. I happen to know it, for I had charge of the early part of his education."

Pause and discuss:
  • The narrator opens by saying he will hide the hero's "real name and titles." Why might a storyteller insist on this before saying a single word against the man?
  • "He is a fool" is delivered in a whisper, in the middle of a banquet held in the man's honour. What does the setting do to the line?
  • We are told the clergyman is "a person of refinement and culture." Why does the story need us to trust the source before it tells us anything?

Part 2 — The pity, and the crammings

"This was a long time ago. I was an instructor in the military academy. I observed, in a sentimental way, the boy Scoresby. He was the most monumentally good-natured creature in the world, and the most hopelessly stupid. Everybody liked him; nobody could be impatient with him. But oh, he was incurably stupid, and bound to fail."

The clergyman lowered his voice further, and went on:

"I pitied him. I said to myself, this poor blockhead will be flung out into the world to be shot at, and the disgrace will break his mother's heart. So I took him under my wing. I privately coached him, and crammed him, and stuffed him for the examinations. He got through — by the merest miracle, and by my own dishonest help — and was rewarded with praise where I had expected to see him expelled in shame."

"I was aghast at what I had done. I had meant to ruin him, and here I had floated him into a commission. I comforted myself, however, with the reflection that war was a long way off, and that surely, when the real test came, he would be found out and quietly retired. Whereas I had only stuffed his head with sham learning, the day of reckoning would come, and the examiners of life would expose him."

"Then the Crimean war broke out. Of course there had to be a war, I said to myself. We couldn't have peace and let this man die without being found out. I waited for the crash. It never came. He went steadily up, from grade to grade, over the dead bodies of his betters, leaving a long track of worshippers behind him."

Pause and discuss:
  • The clergyman's first impulse was pity. His method was dishonest. Was he wrong to cram a hopeless boy through his exams? Where exactly was the mistake?
  • He expected the cramming to be "found out." He treats real war as "the examiners of life." What is Twain suggesting about exams, credentials, and the difference between them and reality?
  • Notice "over the dead bodies of his betters." The joke is funny, but what is its dark edge?

Part 3 — Every blunder, a triumph

"In his first battle he managed to get an order twisted, and led his men in exactly the wrong direction — straight, by pure accident, into a hidden flank of the enemy who were preparing a surprise. He routed them utterly. The commander-in-chief was beside himself. Such inspiration! Such daring! Such a grasp of the field! Scoresby was decorated on the spot, and his blunder was written up in every dispatch as a masterpiece of tactics."

"And so it went on, year after year. Every time he did an idiotic thing, the freakish goddess of luck turned it, at the last instant, into a stroke of genius. Whenever there were two ways of doing a thing, he chose the imbecile way — and the imbecile way happened, that day, to be the right one. He was loaded down with medals he had never earned, and worshipped by a nation that had never once seen him do a wise thing on purpose."

The clergyman set down his cup. The applause for the after-dinner speech rolled down the long table toward us, and the great man rose to bow. My companion leaned close once more, and said, with the quiet finality of a man laying down a verdict he has carried for fifty years:

"He is a fool. There is no other word for it. He is a marvellous fool. He is now what you see him — the idol of the nation, adored by a whole country. But the truth is, just as I have told you: he is the supremest ass in the universe; and there isn't a man living who knows it but me. He has been pursued, day by day and year by year, by the most astonishing and unbelievable luck."

The final discussion — the verdict lands:
  • Read the last line again. "Pursued... by the most astonishing and unbelievable luck." What has that single sentence done to the figure we admired in Part 1?
  • The whole nation worships Scoresby. One man in the room knows the truth. Does knowing the truth give the clergyman any power at all?
  • Twain never lets the hero speak in his own defence — we never hear from Scoresby once. Why does the story keep him silent?
  • Your turn: Tell me about a "Scoresby" you have known — a person whose reputation, in your honest private opinion, ran far ahead of what they had actually earned. What kept the truth hidden?

Register and tone — how Twain controls our reaction

The whole story is a quiet little machine for irony. Twain frames it as a true confidence, hands the telling to a refined clergyman, and keeps the narrator's voice perfectly straight. Notice how each layer steers what we feel.

The grammar this story teaches itself

The whole tale is one man reporting what another man told him — so reported speech is not a side-topic here, it is the very engine of the narrative. We will convert the clergyman's direct lines into reported speech, watch the tenses back-shift, and sort out say vs tell.

1. The framed narrative — why everything back-shifts

Because the narrator is recounting the clergyman's words after the fact, his report sits one tense further back than the original. Direct present becomes past; direct past becomes past perfect. This is the same machinery you use every time you tell a colleague what someone said in a meeting.

Pattern: present → past · past simple → past perfect · will → would · can → could · "this" → "that" · "now" → "then".

1. was (present "is" → past "was")

2. had crammed (past simple → past perfect)

3. pitied … would (present → past; will → would)

4. could … turned (can → could; present → past)

5. was not … knew (present → past; "but me" → "but him")

3. Reporting verbs — say vs tell, and the others

Twain reaches for a whole range of reporting verbs — muttered, said, explained, insisted, added — to colour exactly how each line is delivered. Choose the natural verb form for each gap.

1. The clergyman me that Scoresby was a fool. (tell)

2. He that he had crammed the boy through his exams. (say)

3. He me the whole truth about the general. (tell)

4. He to me that the man had never earned a single medal. (admit)

5. He that the matter should be kept confidential. (insist)

1. told (tell + person object: tell me / him / us)

2. said (say + that, no person object)

3. told (tell me the truth)

4. admitted (admit + that, or admit TO me)

5. insisted (insist + that)

Say vs tell note: We tell a person something — tell needs a person object: "He told me the truth." We say something (to a person) — say takes no direct person object: "He said the truth" / "He said to me…". So: tell someone, but say something.

What Twain does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are why this little 1886 sketch still bites in 2026.

1. The frame narrative — a story inside a story

Twain does not tell us Scoresby is a fool. He builds a frame: a narrator at a banquet, an old clergyman beside him, a confidence whispered under the applause. The verdict reaches us second-hand, through a trusted witness. This distances the author from the judgement and elevates it into testimony — we believe it because we trust the man who carried it for fifty years.

2. Dramatic and situational irony — the room worships; we know better

The whole nation adores Scoresby; we, with the clergyman, hold the secret. Every cheer at the banquet now sounds hollow. The irony is also situational: the very blunders that should have destroyed him are what raised him. Twain ironises the gap between what the world sees and what is true.

3. Satire of reputation and merit

Strip away the comedy and the target is plain: a culture that mistakes confidence and decoration for competence, that reads a lucky accident as "a grasp of the field." Twain satirises our hunger to worship heroes, and recasts the very idea of earned reputation as something far more fragile and accidental than we like to admit.

4. The withheld verdict, revealed last

"He is a fool" arrives early — but the full verdict, the closing hammer-blow about "the most astonishing and unbelievable luck," is held back to the final sentence. Twain withholds the complete judgement and lets it land last, so the story reframes itself the instant we finish it. We re-read the banquet in a new light.

Three speaking tasks

Task A · Succeeded on luck, not merit (4 minutes)

Describe a person — from your own world or from public life — who, in your honest view, succeeded far more through luck than through merit. Tell it in three stages, in order:

  1. the reputation they hold, and how the world sees them;
  2. the lucky breaks or accidents that actually carried them up;
  3. your own private verdict — the thing you believe but rarely say aloud.

Use at least three effect verbs from the Technique tab (ironises, recasts, satirises, reframes…), and at least once make your verdict in the clergyman's confidential register: "Privately, I think…"

Task B · Report a story a friend once told you (3 minutes)

Think of a striking story that a friend, colleague or relative once told you in confidence — a secret about a workplace, a hidden truth about a person everyone admired, a thing they swore you not to repeat (anonymise it).

Retell it entirely in reported speech, the way Twain's narrator retells the clergyman: "She told me that… He said that he had… She insisted that…" Keep the tenses back-shifted throughout, and use at least three different reporting verbs (told, said, admitted, claimed, insisted).

Task C · Is reputation ever truly earned? (4 minutes)

Twain suggests a reputation can be built almost entirely on luck, while the world reads it as merit. Argue your own position: is reputation ever truly earned, or is every great reputation part luck, part timing, part the story people choose to tell?

Speak for two minutes for the idea that reputation can be earned, then two minutes against it. Use cleft sentences ("What the world never sees is…" / "It's the luck, not the skill, that…") and at least one modal of speculation ("he must have been lucky" / "she can't have done it alone").

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The clergyman is the only man alive who knows the truth, and he can do nothing with it but whisper it once, at a banquet. Is private knowledge of a fraud a kind of power, or a kind of prison?
  2. The clergyman cheated to push a hopeless boy through his exams. Years later that boy commands armies. Where, exactly, does the moral responsibility for Scoresby's career lie?
  3. We never hear Scoresby speak. If he could answer the clergyman across fifty years, what do you think he would say?
  4. Think of your own field. Is there a reputation everyone accepts that you privately suspect is built more on luck than on merit? What keeps the suspicion unspoken?
