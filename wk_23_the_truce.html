The Truce

You've just been paired with the one colleague you can't stand. For six months. Nobody has done anything wrong, nobody is in charge — and it still has to work.

The Scene 🎬

The email came round at half four on Friday. New project, two leads, six months, and there it is in the second line: you and Stefan Bauer. You read it twice, in case it was somebody else called Stefan.

Nothing has ever actually happened between you. He hasn't wronged you and you haven't wronged him, which is rather the problem — there's no incident to point at, nothing anyone could put in writing. He talks over you slightly. He fixes things in the corridor before the meeting and then looks surprised when you've got a view. You go quiet when you're annoyed, and he has almost certainly noticed. Two years of very small things, and the two of you have settled into being cool with each other and never saying why.

So on Monday you're going to ask him for two minutes by the window at the end of the corridor. Not to resolve anything. Not to become friends — that's not on the table and you both know it. Just to say the thing out loud, once, so that it stops sitting under every meeting for the next half a year.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Have you ever worked closely with someone you didn't like? What did you actually do about it — say something, or quietly get on with it? Looking back, was that the right call?
  2. In your culture and your language, is it normal to name a bad atmosphere out loud at work, or is that considered a bit much? Would you do it in English more easily, or less?

Who's who

You
The One Who Says It 🪟

You're not after an apology and you're not offering one. You want six workable months, and you've decided the only way to get them is to be the person who mentions the thing nobody mentions.

The other voice
Stefan Bauer 🧊

Your new co-lead, and the colleague you cannot stand. Impatient, decides things early, not remotely stupid — and just as aware of the atmosphere as you are. He'll meet you halfway if you make it small enough to be worth his while.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's Monday, it's the window at the end of the corridor, and neither of them especially wants to be there.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The very first thing said is an offer with a limit on it. What was being limited — and why does putting a limit on an awkward conversation make it so much easier for the other person to agree to have it?

2. One complaint gets made, about a meeting. What did the other person do with it — and what did that do to the rest of the conversation?

3. Every time one of them says something critical, something else follows almost immediately. What's the pattern — and what would the conversation have turned into without it?

1. Two minutes, and then never mentioning it again. That's the whole offer, and it's small on purpose: Stefan isn't being asked to open a long conversation about how the two of them feel, which he would obviously refuse, but to stand there for a short, fixed length of time with a guaranteed end. Almost anyone will agree to something they can see the end of.
2. He accepted it — he agreed that he probably does do that, and then explained himself without using the explanation as an excuse. Once a complaint has been accepted there's nothing left to argue about, so the conversation moves on to what the two of them will actually do rather than whether the complaint was true.
3. Each criticism is followed by an admission about themselves — going quiet when annoyed, having talked about him behind his back to Marta. It keeps the thing level. Without it, one person is presenting a list of the other person's faults, which is a different conversation entirely and not one that ends in a truce.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for clearing the air with someone you don't like. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. This whole conversation only works if it's underplayed, so keep your voice flat, ordinary and slightly bored — the tone you'd use for moving a meeting or asking about a delivery. The words themselves are doing quite enough work, and if you deliver them warmly or seriously on top of that, you turn two awkward minutes into a scene, which is exactly what the other person is dreading. Quick, dry, a bit embarrassed, and then out.

🚪 Getting the two minutes

Phrase 1

You've cornered him by the window. He doesn't want to be there. Which opener is he most likely to say yes to?

A. "I think we need to talk about our working relationship."
B. "Can we have an awkward two minutes and then never mention it again?"
C. "Have you got a moment? There's some feedback I'd like to give you."
B — "Can we have an awkward two minutes and then never mention it again?"
It works because it promises three things at once: it will be short, it will be uncomfortable and you both already know it, and it will be over for good. Admitting the awkwardness up front takes the danger out of it, because the thing people dread isn't two awkward minutes — it's not knowing how long the awkwardness will last. A sounds like the beginning of a very long conversation, and C sounds like you've appointed yourself his manager.
Phrase 2

Now say the actual thing — that the two of you don't get on — but lightly, and as something you're both already agreeing on rather than an accusation. Put it in order:

are we?each other'sWe're notfavourite,
"We're not each other's favourite, are we?"
"Each other's" makes it mutual, so nobody is being accused of anything one-sidedly, and the tag question "are we?" invites him to agree rather than telling him how things are. It's also an understatement, which is why it's easy to say out loud: "not each other's favourite" is a mild, almost funny way of describing two people who cannot stand one another, and English does an enormous amount of difficult work this way.

🎯 Setting the bar low enough to clear

Phrase 3

He's now waiting to hear what you actually want from him — and if it sounds like a lot, he'll say no. Which line makes the ask small and believable?

A. "I'm sure we'll get on brilliantly once we know each other better."
B. "I'm not asking us to be friends."
C. "I don't really want anything from you, to be honest."
B — "I'm not asking us to be friends."
Saying what you're not after is the fastest way to lower someone's guard, because the thing he's braced for is being asked to like you, and you've just taken that off the table before he had to refuse it. A is the opposite move and neither of you would believe it. C sounds like nothing needs to change, which leaves you standing by a window for no reason.
Phrase 4

So having said what you're not after, name what you are — the length of time, and a target set deliberately low. Put it in order:

wasn't horrible.I'd ratherWe've got six months of this —it
"We've got six months of this — I'd rather it wasn't horrible."
Notice how modest the target is: not good, not friendly, just not horrible, which is low enough that he can agree without pretending to feel anything he doesn't. Naming the six months matters too, because it turns the conversation from a complaint about the past into a practical problem you're both stuck inside. The grammar is worth stealing: after "I'd rather" you use a past form — "I'd rather it wasn't", "I'd rather we didn't" — even though you're talking about now.

💬 One complaint, and taking one

Phrase 5

Time for the single thing that actually bothers you. It has to be one event, not a verdict on him as a person, and it has to sound smaller than it feels. Say it out loud — your version, about a colleague who fixed a decision before a meeting you were meant to be part of.

"I'll be honest, I found the thing in the meeting a bit annoying."
Three quiet decisions are doing the work here. "I'll be honest" warns him that something real is coming, so it doesn't land as an ambush. "The thing in the meeting" is deliberately vague, and that vagueness is a kindness — it points at an event rather than building a case against his character. And "a bit annoying" undersells it on purpose, because if you name the feeling at its full size he has to defend himself, whereas if you name it small he can simply agree.
Phrase 6

Swap sides for a second. He's just told you that you do something irritating, and he's right. Which reply actually takes it?

A. "Well, you do exactly the same thing to me."
B. "That's fair. I probably do do that."
C. "I'm not sure that's really true, honestly."
B — "That's fair. I probably do do that."
"That's fair" concedes the point in two words, and once it's conceded there's nothing left to fight about, which is why the conversation can move forward. The doubled "do do" isn't a mistake — the first "do" is there for emphasis, the way you'd say "I do like it", so "I probably do do that" means "yes, you're right, I really do". And "probably" lets him agree without having to make a full confession, which is often the difference between someone agreeing and someone digging in. A turns it into a competition; C spends your goodwill defending something you know is true.

📋 The rules for the next six months

Phrase 7

You both talk about each other to other people. You want one rule that stops it — and the whole phrase turns on two very small words. Complete it: "Can we agree to say it ___ each other rather than ___ each other?"

"Can we agree to say it to each other rather than about each other?"
Two prepositions carry the entire idea: saying it to someone is a complaint they can answer, while saying it about someone is a complaint that goes round the office and comes back to them anyway. "Can we agree to…" makes it a rule you're both signing up to rather than an instruction you're issuing, which matters when neither of you is in charge of the other.
Phrase 8

You've said your bit. If you stop here, you've delivered a list of demands — so hand the conversation over and let him name his price. Say it out loud: one short question that gives him the floor.

"What do you need from me to make this work?"
This is the question that turns your two minutes into an agreement instead of a telling-off, because up to this point everything has flowed one way. "Need from me" quietly admits that you're part of the problem too, and "make this work" keeps the whole thing on the project, where it's a practical matter, rather than on the two of you, where it isn't. Ask it and then be quiet, however long the silence gets.
Phrase 9

Last rule: you'd rather hear about the next irritation early than have it collect for six months. There's a fixed expression for the low-level irritation one person causes another — complete it: "If I'm getting on your ___, just tell me."

"If I'm getting on your nerves, just tell me."
To get on someone's nerves is to irritate them steadily by being yourself — not by doing anything wrong, just by existing near them in a particular way, which is exactly the kind of dislike this conversation is about. Offering it about yourself first is generous and also strategic, because you can't reasonably ask him to hear complaints unless you've invited your own.

🚶 Getting out

Phrase 10

You promised him two minutes. Keeping that promise is most of why he'll trust the next one, so close it before either of you is tempted to say more. Put it in order:

Shall weRight —get on with it?that's that.
"Right — that's that. Shall we get on with it?"
"Right" is the standard British signal that a piece of business is finished and something else is starting now. "That's that" means it's settled and closed and we are not returning to it — which is you keeping the promise you made at the start. Then "shall we get on with it?" points you both at the work, which is the friendliest thing you can offer someone who never wanted this conversation: an ordinary afternoon.

The Twist

🎯 "Who did you say it to?"

Back in your own seat. It's gone better than you'd dared hope — he's agreed to all of it, he's almost warm, and you're four words from being out of there. Then he doesn't move. "One condition. You said you'd said it about me. I want to know what you said, and who to." He's not angry. He's just waiting, and he's using the rule you invented thirty seconds ago. Start the timer and handle it live. You can tell him — and watch the truce you've just built take the weight of what you actually said about him in the pub in March. Or you can decline — and be the person who made the rule and then wouldn't keep it, in front of the one colleague who'll notice. There's no third option and there's no time to think. Choose out loud, in English, and live with it.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that'll cost you in the real corridor. Run the twist again and get it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Most bad working relationships aren't built out of anything. No row, no incident, nothing you could take to anyone — just two years of small things that nobody said, hardening quietly into a fact about the two of you.

What the two minutes at the window do is not repair. Nothing is repaired. You still don't like him, and on Thursday he'll do the corridor thing again and you'll notice. But it's been said now, once, in daylight, and a thing that's been said out loud takes up much less room than a thing that hasn't.

That's the whole return on it: not a friendship, just six months that are merely a bit annoying rather than horrible. Which, over half a year of your actual life, is worth two minutes of being embarrassed by a window.

Two bigger questions

  1. We tend to treat "we don't get on" as something to be fixed or else endured in silence. Is there a third thing — simply agreeing to dislike each other and work well anyway — and why do so few workplaces seem able to say that out loud?
  2. Almost everyone talks about colleagues behind their backs, and almost everyone knows it's happening to them too. Is the rule in this lesson realistic, or is a certain amount of complaining about people the thing that stops us complaining to them? What would your workplace be like if everyone actually kept that rule?
