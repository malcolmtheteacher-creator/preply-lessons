The Jesuit and the palace

China, the late sixteenth century. An Italian priest of the Jesuit order, Matteo Ricci, has lived in the country for years. He has learned to speak, read and write Chinese, and dresses as a scholar. He is there to talk about his religion, but he has found that the best door into Chinese society is a surprising one: his memory.

In China, ambitious young men prepared for years for the imperial examinations, which tested their knowledge of an enormous body of classical texts. Memory was a serious matter. When Ricci reportedly showed scholars that he could learn a long list of Chinese characters, read once, and say it back in order, and then backwards, they wanted to know how.

Why were people in China interested in Ricci's memory?
Exam candidates needed to memorise a huge number of texts, so a method that worked on long lists of characters was valuable.
Talk about it. What is the largest body of material you have ever had to memorise for an exam?

In 1596 he wrote a short book in Chinese to explain the art. In it he taught the memory palace. He told his readers to choose a building, to fix places in it, and to put a picture in each place. He explained that the scholar should fix places in order and walk through them. He did not present it as his own invention; it came from the West.

The scholars who read it faced the same problem as every student with a lot to learn. A single building is not enough. One house with fifty places holds fifty things, and the examination was thousands of lines long. What they needed was more buildings, and a way to move between them.

What did Ricci teach in his book?
The memory palace: choosing a building, fixing places in it and putting a picture in each.
Talk about it. How many places do you think you could find in the buildings you know well?

Which is exactly where this lesson begins. A network of palaces is like a town. You do not memorise one house; you memorise streets, and the streets connect houses, and each house is about one subject. A bank of buildings for history, another for languages, another for your job.

The part of the network that matters most is not the houses but the connections. A good connection is a short, vivid step between the last place of one palace and the first of the next. With those, a hundred buildings can be walked in one long journey, and you can enter and leave wherever you want.

What is the most important part of a network?
The connections between palaces: a short, vivid link from the last place of one to the first of the next.
Talk about it. Which five places that you know well could be the start of a network?

Many palaces, one journey

One palace holds as many items as it has places. A network of palaces holds a subject, a course, or a career.

A hub connected to three subject palaces, with a bridge between two of them.
  1. Choose one building per subject. History in your old school, languages in your grandmother's house, work in your office.
  2. Make each building distinct. Different styles, colours, sizes: so you never mix a room of one with a room of another.
  3. Pick a hub. A place you know very well, with doors to the others. Your own home works.
  4. Build the bridges. A vivid, short link, for example: you open the front door of your home and walk straight into the school playground.
  5. Keep a plan. A list of palaces, what is in each, and how many places are free.

Practical rules

  • Fifty places per palace is a lot. Twenty to thirty is a good size to start with.
  • Keep one palace per subject. A subject that overflows gets a second building and a bridge.
  • Keep the order fixed in each, and the order of buildings fixed along the journey.
  • Reuse after clearing. When a palace is no longer needed, clear it by imagining it empty, clean and bright.
  • Review the bridges. The weak point of a network is a connection that has faded.

Plan a small network

Choose three subjects you want to keep. For each, name a building you know well, and say how it differs from the others. Add a hub.
Hub: my flat. History: my old school (a red brick building, long corridors). Languages: my grandmother's house (small, warm, full of plants). Work: the office (glass and steel). All three look very different from each other.

A bridge

Write a bridge from the last place of your first palace to the first place of your second palace. Make it vivid and short.
The last place of the school is the front gate. A banner from the gate flies across the street and wraps itself round the front door of my grandmother's house like a ribbon.
