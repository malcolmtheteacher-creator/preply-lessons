Key Word Transformations

CAE · Reading & Use of English, Part 4 · Past paper practice

The format in one minute

You are given a sentence, a key word in capitals, and a second sentence with a gap. Your job is to complete the second sentence so that it means the same as the first, using the key word. You must write between three and six words, including the key word. You cannot change the key word in any way.

There are six questions. Each one is worth up to two marks: one for the grammar and one for the lexis. So a half-right answer can still earn you a mark — never leave a gap blank.

Worked example

James insisted that he should pay for the meal.

ON

James for the meal.

Step 1 — What changed? "insisted that he should pay" becomes a structure with ON.
Step 2 — What pattern uses ON? insist on + -ing. This is a reporting verb with a fixed preposition.
Step 3 — Build it. insisted on paying — three words including the key word.
Step 4 — Count. Three words — inside 3–6. Done.
Show full answer
James INSISTED ON PAYING for the meal.

Five rules to keep in your head

  • Three to six words, including the key word. Contractions count as two (don't = do not).
  • Never change the key word. No plurals, no tenses, no derivations.
  • Meaning must match exactly. Same time, same modality, same emphasis.
  • Two marks per question — grammar and lexis are scored separately, so half-credit is real.
  • Never leave it blank. A best guess can still score one mark.

The patterns that come up again and again

Most past paper questions test one of about ten grammar/lexis patterns. Tap a card to see the pattern.

Reporting verb + preposition insist ON, accuse OF, blame FOR, apologise FOR, congratulate ON, succeed IN
Passive transformations "They are repairing the road" → "The road is being repaired"
Causative have / get "The mechanic is fixing my car" → "I am having my car fixed"
Modal perfects must have done, can't have done, needn't have done, should have done
Conditional inversion "If I had known" → "Had I known"; "If you should need" → "Should you need"
Negative adverb inversion "He had no sooner..." / "Not until... did he" / "Never have I..."
Wish / regret wish + past perfect (regret about past); regrets + V-ing
Comparatives not nearly as... as / nowhere near as... as / the more... the more
Cleft sentences "It was John who..." / "What I need is..." / "The reason... is that..."
Phrasal verb ↔ formal verb put up with = tolerate; bring about = cause; turn down = reject

Practice Set 1 — six past paper questions

Work through these one at a time. Say your answer out loud before tapping the reveal button. Remember: three to six words, including the key word, no changes to the key word.

Q1

"I really regret not buying that flat when prices were low."

WISH

I that flat when prices were low.

Show answer

I WISH I HAD BOUGHT that flat when prices were low.

Pattern: wish + past perfect for regret about the past. Five words including the key word.

Trap: students write "wish I bought" — past simple is for present regrets, not past ones.

Q2

"It is the first time Tom has visited London."

BEFORE

Tom London before.

Show answer

Tom HAS NEVER VISITED London before.

Pattern: "first time + present perfect" → "has never + past participle + before". Four words.

Trap: shifting the tense to past simple (never visited) loses the grammar mark.

Q3

"James insisted that he should pay for the meal."

ON

James for the meal.

Show answer

James INSISTED ON PAYING for the meal.

Pattern: reporting verb with dependent preposition. insist on + -ing form.

Trap: writing "insisted on to pay" — after a preposition, English always uses -ing.

Q4

"The view from the hotel was much worse than we had expected."

NEARLY

The view from the hotel was we had expected.

Show answer

The view from the hotel was NOT NEARLY AS GOOD AS we had expected.

Pattern: "much worse than" → "not nearly as good as" (a downgrade comparative). Five words.

Trap: keeping worse — the structure flips polarity to good.

Q5

"Helen is sorry now that she didn't accept the job offer."

REGRETS

Helen the job offer.

Show answer

Helen REGRETS NOT ACCEPTING the job offer.

Pattern: regret + -ing (for past actions). The negative goes before the gerund.

Trap: "regrets she didn't accept" works in conversation but exceeds the word count and shifts the structure away from REGRETS as a transformation target.

Q6

"The film was so boring that I fell asleep."

SUCH

It that I fell asleep.

Show answer

It WAS SUCH A BORING FILM that I fell asleep.

Pattern: so + adjective ↔ such a + adjective + noun. Five words.

Trap: dropping the article ("was such boring film") — such needs a/an with a singular countable noun.

Timed Set 2 — six more questions

In the real exam you have roughly two minutes per question. Twelve minutes on the clock for the whole set. Click Start Timer when you are ready. Try not to reveal an answer until you have finished all six.

Time remaining: 12:00
Q7

"If you had told me earlier, I could have helped you."

HAD

told me earlier, I could have helped you.

Show answer

HAD YOU ONLY told me earlier, I could have helped you.

Also accepted: HAD YOU told me. Three or four words.

Pattern: third conditional inversion. Drop if, move the auxiliary to the front.

Trap: writing "Had you have told" — the auxiliary already carries the past perfect; no second have.

Q8

"We didn't need to buy any food because Jim had brought lots."

NEED

We any food because Jim had brought lots.

Show answer

We NEEDN'T HAVE BOUGHT any food because Jim had brought lots.

Pattern: the original means we DID buy — unnecessarily. didn't need to + V is ambiguous; needn't have + past participle specifies that the action happened and was unnecessary.

Trap: "didn't need to buy" only carries one mark because it doesn't tell us whether we bought any.

Q9

"Why don't we go for a walk after dinner?"

ABOUT

for a walk after dinner?

Show answer

HOW ABOUT GOING for a walk after dinner?

Also accepted: WHAT ABOUT GOING.

Pattern: suggestion frame — How/What about + -ing. Three words.

Trap: "How about to go" — after a preposition you always need the -ing form.

Q10

"I will only call you if there is an emergency."

UNLESS

I there is an emergency.

Show answer

I WILL NOT CALL YOU UNLESS there is an emergency.

Also accepted: WON'T CALL YOU UNLESS (four words — contractions count as two).

Pattern: "only if X" → "not... unless X". Polarity flips.

Trap: using if not — possible but doesn't use the key word.

Q11

"The mechanic is fixing my car at the moment."

HAVING

I at the moment.

Show answer

I AM HAVING MY CAR FIXED at the moment.

Pattern: causative have — subject arranges for someone else to do the action. Structure: have + object + past participle.

Trap: word order — the past participle (fixed) goes after the object (my car), not before.

Q12

"You should have told me about the change of plan earlier."

OUGHT

You me about the change of plan earlier.

Show answer

You OUGHT TO HAVE TOLD me about the change of plan earlier.

Pattern: modal perfect of past obligation. ought to + have + past participle. Four words.

Trap: dropping to — unlike should, ought always takes to.

The five mistakes that cost most marks

Read each one, then check the example. These are the patterns that come back lesson after lesson.

1. Changing the key word

The key word is fixed. No plurals, no tenses, no derivations. WISH stays as wish, not wished or wishes.

Show example

Wrong: "I wished I had bought that flat..." — the past tense of WISH changes the key word.

Right: "I WISH I HAD BOUGHT that flat..."

2. Word count outside 3–6

Contractions count as two words. won't = will not = two words. I'd = I would/had = two words.

Show example

Risky: "I won't call you unless" (four words). Fine.

Wrong: "I'll not be calling you unless" (six words but heavy and awkward).

Best: "I WILL NOT CALL YOU UNLESS" — clean five words.

3. Tense drift

The transformation must match the original sentence's time frame. Past stays past, perfect stays perfect.

Show example

Original: "It is the first time Tom has visited London."

Wrong: "Tom never visited London before." — past simple loses the present-perfect grammar mark.

Right: "Tom HAS NEVER VISITED London before."

4. Missing the dependent preposition

Reporting and emotion verbs each have a fixed preposition. Learn them in pairs: insist on, accuse of, blame for, congratulate on, succeed in, apologise for, complain about.

Show example

Wrong: "James insisted to pay" — missing ON and wrong verb form.

Right: "James INSISTED ON PAYING for the meal."

5. Half-translating from L1

If the answer "feels" like a literal translation of your first language, slow down. CAE transformations almost always test idiomatic English structures, not word-for-word swaps.

Show example

Original: "Why don't we go for a walk?"

Wrong: "About to go for a walk?" — this is L1-to-L2 substitution.

Right: "HOW ABOUT GOING for a walk?" — the actual fixed English frame.
