YEAR 8 · UNIT 4: PEACE & CONFLICT

Religion and Violence — The Big Question

Is religion a force for peace or conflict?

Starting Point: What Do You Already Know?

Before we explore this question together, let's think about what you already know. You've probably heard about religious conflict in the news, or learned about it in history lessons. You might also know about religious people and organisations working for peace.

Question 1: Can you name one historical example of conflict where religion played a role?

🔒Try to recall an example first!

Examples include: The Crusades, the partition of India (Hindu-Muslim violence), the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, sectarian violence between Sunni and Shia Muslims, or the Troubles in Northern Ireland (Catholic-Protestant).

Question 2: What is the difference between a religion and an ideology?

🔒Think about the definitions first!

Answer: A religion is a belief system centred on faith in a higher power or spiritual truths. An ideology is a set of political or social beliefs and values. While related, they're distinct—though people often mix them.

Our Big Question

Throughout this unit, we're going to ask: Is religion a force for peace or conflict? This isn't a question with a simple yes-or-no answer. The evidence shows that religion has been used both to wage war and to build peace. Our job is to understand why, and to look at what different religions teach about these issues.

Key idea: When we study religion and conflict, we need to distinguish between what a religion teaches and how people use those teachings. Sometimes conflict happens because of religion's core message. Sometimes conflict uses religion as a label or justification for conflicts that are actually about power, land, or politics.

Meeting the Evidence: Historical Examples

Let's look at some real examples where religion and violence have intersected. These are complex situations, and we'll look at both how religion contributed to the conflict and how it also worked for peace.

The Crusades (1096–1291)

Christian crusaders from Europe fought to reclaim the Holy Land from Muslim control. For centuries, this was presented as purely a religious war. But historians now understand it was also about power, wealth, and trade routes. Christian leaders used religious motivation to persuade people to fight.

Was this a religious conflict? Partly. But it was also deeply political and economic. The Pope gained power by launching the Crusades. European nobles gained land and wealth. Merchants wanted control of trade routes. So religion was certainly involved—but it wasn't the only cause.

The Partition of India (1947)

When India gained independence from Britain, the subcontinent was divided into India (primarily Hindu) and Pakistan (primarily Muslim). Over 1 million people died in communal violence. Religious identity became a marker for who was considered "Indian" and who wasn't.

What this shows: Religion became the boundary for nationality. But beneath the religious labels were fights over land, power, and resources. People on both sides were also divided along caste, class, and regional lines.

The Troubles in Northern Ireland (1968–1998)

Catholics and Protestants fought for decades. The conflict was labelled a "religious war," but it was rooted in 800 years of British colonisation, land disputes, and economic inequality. Religion was the identity marker, but the causes were political and economic.

Religious Peace Movements (Same Period)

During many of these conflicts, religious leaders and communities also worked actively for peace:

  • Indian Independence: Gandhi, a Hindu leader, preached non-violence and worked alongside Muslims and Christians.
  • Northern Ireland: Religious leaders from both traditions helped broker the 1998 Good Friday Agreement.
  • South Africa: Desmond Tutu and other faith leaders fought apartheid and led reconciliation efforts.
The Pattern: Religion shows up on both sides of these conflicts. The same religious traditions that were used to justify violence were also used to justify peace-making.

Understanding the Distinction

One of the most important ideas in this unit is the difference between:

1. Religion Causing Conflict

Sometimes the core beliefs of a religion actually lead to conflict. For example:

  • Beliefs about who has the "true" faith and who doesn't
  • Rules about sacred land or sacred objects that different groups contest
  • Beliefs about who should hold power or make laws
  • Teachings about how to respond to those who disagree

2. Religion Being Used to Justify Conflict

More often, conflicts happen because of politics, economics, or history—and religion becomes the language used to describe them. For example:

  • Political leaders use religious identity to unite followers
  • Economic competitions get framed as religious differences
  • Historical injustices are described in religious terms
  • Religion provides a powerful way to distinguish "us" from "them"

Question 3: Which type of conflict is more common?

🔒Think about the examples!

Answer: Religion is more often used as justification for conflicts rooted in politics, economics, or power struggles. Pure religious conflict is less common than we might think.

A Helpful Framework: REASOM

When we analyse a religious conflict, we can ask about multiple causes:

  • Religious: What religious beliefs or teachings are involved?
  • Economic: What resources, trade, or wealth are being contested?
  • Ancestral/Historical: What past injustices or grievances are at play?
  • Social: What group identities or social divisions exist?
  • Organisational: What institutions (governments, militaries, religious hierarchies) are involved?
  • Military: What weapons, armies, or military strategies are being used?
Try it: Take the partition of India. It involved religious identity, British colonial rule, land disputes, centuries of resentment, caste divisions, and armies fighting. Religion alone doesn't explain it.

The Paradox of Religion

Here's something important: the same religious traditions can produce both peacekeepers and warriors. Why?

  • Religious texts are complex. Sacred texts contain passages about both peace and war.
  • People interpret differently. Different groups within the same religion read the same texts and reach different conclusions.
  • Context matters. What a text meant in its original time and place might be interpreted completely differently today.
  • Power shapes religion. Religious leaders and institutions have their own interests. They can encourage either conflict or peace depending on their goals.

Applying the Framework

Let's practise using this framework. We'll look at a specific case and break it down.

Case Study: The Israeli-Palestinian Conflict

This is one of the world's longest ongoing disputes. People often describe it as a "religious conflict" between Jews and Muslims. But let's apply our framework:

Religious dimension: For Jews, the land of Israel holds religious significance as a promised homeland. For Muslims, the land is sacred in Islam, and Palestinians have lived there for centuries. Both groups see religious claims to the same land.
Economic dimension: Control of limited resources (water, fertile land), trade routes, and wealth is contested between the two peoples.
Historical dimension: Jewish persecution across centuries, culminating in the Holocaust, made many Jews feel they needed a homeland. Palestinians had been living in the region for generations and saw themselves being displaced.
Political dimension: The creation of Israel in 1948 was a political decision by the international community. It divided the region and displaced Palestinian communities.
Military dimension: Multiple wars between Israel and Arab nations. Military occupation and security measures shape daily life.

The question: Is this a religious conflict? It involves religion, but it's rooted deeply in history, politics, economics, and power. Some Jewish and Muslim leaders work together for peace—showing that religion doesn't have to divide them.

Question 4: If we removed religion from this situation, would the conflict disappear?

🔒Think about the other causes!

Answer: Probably not entirely. The historical grievances, disputes over land and resources, and questions of political power would likely still cause conflict—even if religion weren't involved.

Practice: Analysing Another Conflict

Think about a religious conflict you know about (the Troubles, the Crusades, sectarian violence in the Middle East, or another). Can you break it down using the REASOM framework? What aspects are religious, economic, historical, social, organisational, and military?

Evaluating the Question

Now that we've explored the evidence, let's think critically about our big question: Is religion a force for peace or conflict?

The Case for "Religion Causes Conflict"

  • Religious exclusivity (claiming one true faith) can create division
  • Religious texts are used to justify violence
  • Religious institutions often have power and use it for political ends
  • Religious difference can be used to dehumanise opponents
  • Many historical and contemporary conflicts have religious dimensions

The Case for "Religion Enables Peace"

  • Most religions have core teachings about peace, compassion, and justice
  • Religious communities often lead peace-building and reconciliation efforts
  • Faith can motivate people to sacrifice for others' wellbeing
  • Religious leaders have unique moral authority in their communities
  • Shared religious identity can bind communities together peacefully

The More Nuanced Answer

Religion is a tool. Like any powerful force, it can be used for good or harm. The same religious text can be interpreted as a call to violence or a call to peace. The same community can be a force for war or reconciliation. The question isn't really "Does religion cause conflict?" but rather "How do people use religion—and how can we encourage its peaceful uses?"

Key insight: Research shows that religious conflicts are rare in the pure sense. Most "religious conflicts" have deep roots in politics, economics, history, and identity. Religion often gets blamed because it's the most visible marker of difference—but it's usually not the only cause or even the main cause.

Question 5: Is there a single correct answer to our big question?

🔒Think about the evidence we've explored!

Answer: No. The evidence shows that religion can be both. Different people interpret the same religious traditions differently. The same religion has produced both warriors and peacemakers. So the real question is: what conditions lead religion to be used for peace versus conflict?

Consolidating Your Understanding

Let's recap the key ideas from this lesson before moving forward.

What You Should Understand

  • Religion and conflict are connected but complex. Religion appears in many conflicts, but it's rarely the only cause.
  • We need to distinguish causes. Using a framework like REASOM helps us see the religious, economic, historical, social, organisational, and military dimensions of conflicts.
  • Religion can justify both peace and violence. The same traditions have produced both peacekeepers and warriors throughout history.
  • Labels matter but are misleading. We often call something a "religious conflict," but this can hide the real causes (politics, economics, power).

Vocabulary to Remember

  • Conflict: A disagreement or clash between groups, often involving violence
  • Ideology: A system of beliefs and values, usually political or social
  • Justification: A reason or excuse given to explain an action
  • Sectarian: Relating to division within a religion
  • Reconciliation: The process of restoring peaceful relations after conflict

Questions to Keep Thinking About

As we move through this unit, keep these questions in mind:

  • What do different religions actually teach about peace and war?
  • When religious leaders teach peace, why do some followers choose violence instead?
  • Can religion be "reformed" to be more peaceful, or is the problem with how it's used?
  • What role should religious communities play in building peace?
