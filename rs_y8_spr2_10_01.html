Encounter: Religion, Violence, and Peace

The Central Question

News reports often describe conflicts as "religious violence"—the Israeli-Palestinian conflict, communal violence in Myanmar, sectarian warfare in the Middle East. These stories can create an impression that religion causes violence. But is that true? Or is the reality more complex? This lesson explores that critical question.

Types of Religious Violence: A Spectrum

Holy War (Sacred Combat)

Wars fought explicitly in the name of religion, where the conflict itself is considered religious. Examples include the Crusades (Christian wars against Islamic forces), early Islamic conquest, and some interpretations of jihad. Participants believe they are fighting for religious principles and divine approval.

Sectarian Violence

Conflict between different groups within the same religion. Examples: Sunni-Shia violence in Iraq and Syria, Protestant-Catholic violence in Northern Ireland, Buddhist-Hindu tensions in Sri Lanka. The conflict is framed in religious terms, but often roots lie in political power, resources, and historical grievances.

Communal Violence

Large-scale violence between religious communities, often triggered by religious incidents but driven by deeper political and social tensions. Example: Partition of India and Pakistan (1947), where communal violence killed 1-2 million people framed as Hindu-Muslim conflict but rooted in colonial politics and land disputes.

Violence by Religious Extremists

Terrorist attacks or murders carried out by fringe groups claiming to act in religion's name. Examples: Christian extremists bombing abortion clinics, Islamic extremists like Al-Qaeda or ISIS, Buddhist extremists attacking Rohingya Muslims. These groups represent tiny minorities of their religions.

The Religion-Politics Nexus: A Critical Complexity

Key insight: Religion and politics are deeply intertwined. When scholars analyse "religious violence," they often find that political factors, economic interests, and territorial control are equally or more important than theology itself. This doesn't mean religion plays no role—it can be a powerful motivator and identity marker—but it's rarely the sole cause.

Case Studies: The Complex Picture

The Crusades (11th-13th centuries)

Christian kingdoms launched wars to recapture "holy land" from Islamic control. Presented as religious wars, but they were equally driven by desire for land, trade routes, political power, and resources. Crusaders plundered cities, enslaved populations, and engaged in economic exploitation. Religion provided justification and motivation, but wasn't the only—or even primary—cause.

Partition of India (1947)

British colonial rule divided India into Hindu-majority India and Muslim-majority Pakistan, based on religious boundaries. This triggered massive communal violence: 1-2 million deaths, 15 million displaced. While framed as Hindu-Muslim religious conflict, the roots lay in British colonialism, political leadership decisions, and resource competition. Religion was the identity marker, not the root cause.

Northern Ireland (1968-1998)

Protestant-Catholic conflict killed 3,500 people. While religious identity was central, the conflict was fundamentally about political power, national identity (British vs. Irish), and control of land and resources. Economic inequality between communities intensified the conflict. Religion was the dividing line, but not the underlying cause of inequality and political tension.

Myanmar (2016-present)

Buddhist-majority Myanmar has committed genocide against the Rohingya Muslim minority, killing tens of thousands. While framed as Buddhist-Muslim religious conflict, researchers identify political factors: the military's consolidation of power, resource competition, and xenophobia toward a stateless minority. Buddhist nationalism is weaponised by the state, but religious theology itself doesn't demand genocide.

The Counter-Narrative: Religion as a Force for Peace

But religion isn't inherently violent. Religious communities have been powerful forces for peace, justice, and coexistence. History also shows us:

The Society of Friends (Quakers)

Christian pacifists who refuse to take up arms under any circumstances. Many Quakers were conscientious objectors in World Wars I and II. They pioneered alternative dispute resolution and restorative justice approaches, rooted in their religious belief in the inner light within every person.

Buddhist Monks in Vietnam

During the Vietnam War, Buddhist monks engaged in peaceful resistance, including self-immolation, to protest violence and demand peace. Their activism was rooted in Buddhist principles of non-violence (ahimsa) and compassion. They became symbols of resistance to violence itself.

Islamic Golden Age (8th-14th centuries)

The Islamic world experienced centuries of remarkable coexistence between Muslims, Christians, and Jews in places like Al-Andalus (Spain), Baghdad, and Cairo. Scholars of all faiths collaborated on mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and philosophy. This period shows religion can foster knowledge-sharing and peaceful coexistence.

Buddhist principle of Ahimsa: "Harm no living creature." This foundational Buddhist teaching emphasises non-violence and compassion as core to spiritual practice. Yet Buddhists have committed violence—showing that religious teachings don't automatically prevent violence.
Islamic teaching on peace: "There is no compulsion in religion" (Quran 2:256) and "The best jihad is a jihad of the soul"—emphasising internal spiritual struggle over violent holy war. Yet some use "jihad" to justify terrorism, twisting the teaching's meaning.

The Critical Question: Does Religion CAUSE Violence, or Do Humans USE Religion?

Hypothesis 1: Religion is a Root Cause of Violence

Some religious teachings promote violence (holy war, divine punishment). Religious fundamentalism breeds intolerance. Religious identity is so powerful it overrides moral reasoning. Therefore, religion causes violence and conflict would decrease if people abandoned religion.

Hypothesis 2: Religion is Used to Justify Violence, Not Its Cause

Humans are motivated by power, resources, territory, and tribal identity. Religion is one tool among many (others: nationalism, ethnicity, ideology) that justifies these motivations to ourselves and others. People use religious texts selectively to support violence they want to commit for other reasons. Remove religion, and the same conflicts would continue under different labels (nationalist, ethnic, ideological).

Hypothesis 3: Religion Can Be Either, Depending on Context

Religious texts contain both peaceful and violent passages. Religious communities can emphasise either. In contexts of political instability, economic inequality, and historical grievance, religious identity becomes a marker for political division and can be weaponised for violence. In stable, pluralistic contexts, religion can foster peace. The cause isn't religion itself but how it interacts with political and social conditions.

Statistical Evidence: A Nuanced Picture

The Numbers Debate

How many conflicts are truly "religious"? Scholars disagree. Some studies claim only 5-15% of major conflicts have religion as a primary cause. Others argue religion is present in 30-40% of conflicts but rarely as the sole cause. Counting depends on how you define "religious conflict."

Secular Violence

The 20th century's deadliest conflicts—World Wars, Stalinism, Maoism, Cambodian genocide—were driven by secular ideologies (nationalism, communism, fascism), not religion. This suggests that human capacity for violence isn't uniquely religious. The causes of violence are deeper than belief systems.

Central complexity: Religious violence exists and causes real harm. But religious peace-building is equally real. Religion is a powerful force that humans use for both violence and peace, depending on interpretation, context, and intent.

Evaluate: Weighing the Arguments

Two Serious Arguments About Religion and Violence

Scholars and activists genuinely disagree about whether religion is fundamentally violent or peaceful. Both sides present evidence. Your task is to evaluate both arguments critically.

Argument 1: Religion Is a Primary Cause of Violence

Evidence: Many conflicts are explicitly framed as religious—Crusaders fought for "God," Islamic State operates as a theocracy, Northern Ireland was divided along Protestant-Catholic lines. Religious texts contain passages endorsing war and violence (Joshua's conquest of Canaan, some Quranic verses on jihad). Religious fundamentalism promotes intolerance and absolute truth claims that make coexistence difficult. Religious identity is so powerful that it overrides moral reasoning—believers will commit acts they'd otherwise find abhorrent if commanded by religious authority.

Argument 2: Religion Is Used to Justify Violence, Not Its Root Cause

Evidence: The deadliest conflicts of the 20th century (World Wars, Stalinism, Maoism) were driven by secular ideologies, not religion. When we examine "religious conflicts" closely, we find political power struggles, resource competition, territorial disputes, and colonial legacies underneath. Religion serves as an identity marker and motivator, but the underlying drivers are political and economic. Religious texts also contain profound teachings on peace, compassion, and coexistence—the violence emerges from selective interpretation driven by political agendas. Remove religion, and the same human motivations for power and survival would express themselves through nationalism, ethnicity, or ideology instead.

Key Terms Summary

Holy War
War fought explicitly in the name of religion, where participants believe they fight for religious principles and divine approval. Examples: the Crusades, early Islamic conquests.
Just War
Ethical framework arguing that some wars can be morally justified if fought for just causes, with just intent, as last resort, and with proportionate means. Used to evaluate whether violence is ethically defensible.
Jihad
Arabic term meaning "struggle" or "striving." In Islam, it refers to both spiritual struggle against one's own base desires and, in some interpretations, armed struggle. Often misused in Western media to mean only violent holy war.
Sectarian Violence
Conflict between different groups within the same religion, such as Sunni-Shia in Islam, or Protestant-Catholic in Christianity. Framed in religious terms but often rooted in political power and resources.
Crusade
Series of Christian military campaigns (11th-13th centuries) to recapture the "Holy Land" from Islamic control. Presented as religious wars but equally driven by desire for land, trade, and political power.
Ahimsa
Hindu, Jain, and Buddhist principle of non-violence toward all living creatures. Central to Buddhist and Jain ethics, meaning no harm should be done to any being, not even insects.
Pacifism
Ethical or religious stance opposing war and violence. Pacifists refuse to participate in war or armed conflict, believing violence is morally wrong. Examples: Quakers, Mennonites, some Buddhist and Hindu traditions.
Religious Fundamentalism
Approach to religion emphasising strict adherence to core traditional beliefs and literal interpretation of sacred texts, often with resistance to modernisation and secular influence. Not synonymous with violence, though fundamentalism can sometimes be weaponised.
Pluralism
Belief in coexistence and acceptance of multiple religious traditions within a society. Pluralistic societies allow diverse religions to practice openly and equally, often resulting in interfaith dialogue and cooperation.
Interfaith Dialogue
Communication and collaboration between representatives of different religious traditions, aimed at understanding, reducing prejudice, and promoting cooperation and peace.
Terrorism
Use of violence and intimidation, often against civilians, to create fear and advance political, ideological, or religious goals. Religious terrorism uses religious justification, but terrorism itself is a tactic, not a belief system.
Golden Age
Period of flourishing in culture, knowledge, and achievement. The Islamic Golden Age (8th-14th centuries) saw remarkable scientific, mathematical, and philosophical advances across Muslim, Christian, and Jewish scholars working collaboratively.
The Big Takeaway from This Lesson: Religion and violence have a complex relationship. Some conflicts are explicitly driven by religious doctrine and identity. But most "religious conflicts" reveal deeper political, economic, and social roots when examined closely. Religion can be a force for both violence and peace, depending on how it's interpreted, who controls its message, and what political and social contexts it exists within. The question "Does religion cause violence?" is too simple. A better question is: "In what contexts does religion become weaponised for violence, and in what contexts does it inspire peace-building?" The answer reveals that human motivations for power, resources, and group identity are universal—religion is one vehicle among many that expresses these motivations.
