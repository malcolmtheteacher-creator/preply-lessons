Why We Procrastinate

At some point, almost everyone has promised themselves that today will be different — today they will finally tackle that long-delayed task. And for countless readers of this sentence, the promise will have been broken before the day is out. Procrastination is remarkably stubborn, and remarkably democratic; virtually no profession, personality type or age group is immune to it.

For years, the conventional wisdom held that procrastination was fundamentally a problem of time management. If people simply learned to plan better, the argument went, the problem would disappear. Research in recent decades has complicated this picture significantly. Psychologists now increasingly view procrastination as a problem of emotional regulation: we delay tasks not because we can't estimate how long they will take, but because we cannot tolerate the negative feelings those tasks produce.

This reframing explains some of procrastination's puzzling features. Why, for instance, do we so often delay tasks that are not even difficult? Because ease alone is not what determines our willingness to begin. A boring but easy task — sorting the tax receipts, say — may be delayed precisely because its predicted tedium is unpleasant to anticipate. We choose, briefly, the more enjoyable activity of cleaning the kitchen instead, even though the original task remains undone.

Worse, procrastination tends to be self-reinforcing. Each delay produces a brief surge of relief — we don't have to face the unpleasant feeling right now — which the brain registers as reward. We have, in effect, trained ourselves to avoid certain tasks. Over time, the avoided task acquires an outsized emotional weight, becoming harder to face than it ever needed to be.

Modern life, with its infinite distractions, has made matters considerably worse. In previous eras, a frustrated worker might stare out of the window for ten minutes before returning to the task. Today, the same worker may drift into forty-five minutes of social media, news headlines and video clips — a far more efficient escape mechanism. The digital environment is, in some sense, a procrastinator's paradise.

Solutions exist, but they require honesty about what procrastination really is. Simply exhorting ourselves to be more disciplined rarely works. More effective strategies acknowledge the emotional dimension directly: breaking tasks into tiny, manageable chunks to reduce anticipated dread; committing publicly to deadlines so that social pressure offsets internal resistance; and, perhaps most importantly, forgiving ourselves when we do procrastinate, since harsh self-criticism has been repeatedly shown to worsen rather than reduce future delay.

Part 6: Cross-text Multiple Matching

Four views on free speech in the digital age.

A - Anna Kowalski, Legal Scholar

Debates about online speech too often frame the issue as a clash between freedom and safety, as if we must choose one. This is a false choice. The right to speak has always coexisted with legal limits on defamation, incitement and fraud; extending these established principles into digital spaces is not censorship but continuity. What is genuinely new is the scale and speed at which harmful content now spreads, which makes thoughtful regulation more urgent, not less. We need humility about how tricky this will be.

B - Ben Patel, Tech Entrepreneur

Running an online platform has given me unwelcome insight into how difficult content moderation really is. Every rule we create is immediately exploited by someone. Every nuanced judgement gets portrayed as censorship by one faction and complicity by another. Governments now talk about regulating us, but most proposals I've seen would make things worse, forcing platforms to over-remove lawful speech to avoid legal liability. The solution isn't political intervention but better user tools — more control, more transparency, less automated decision-making.

C - Clara Mendoza, Journalist

As someone who's been on the receiving end of online harassment, I'm impatient with purist arguments about free speech. The "marketplace of ideas" works only when participants are roughly equal; online, that assumption collapses. Anonymous pile-ons, coordinated harassment and manipulated content have silenced countless voices, particularly those of women and minorities. Platforms must take more responsibility. I don't believe this makes me an enemy of free speech — quite the opposite. Protecting expression requires protecting people who want to express.

D - David Fisher, Civil Liberties Advocate

I'm frequently labelled a "free speech absolutist", which is unfair. I accept, as most do, that speech provoking imminent violence falls outside protection. What troubles me is how loosely "harm" is now defined online. Offence, disagreement, discomfort are all treated as harms requiring removal. This creates a dangerous precedent. History shows that speech restrictions invariably fall hardest on marginalised voices, whatever the original intention. I'd rather tolerate some genuinely offensive speech than build machinery that can be turned against anyone.

The Revival of Night Trains in Europe

For much of the 2000s, Europe's iconic sleeper trains seemed destined for extinction. Cheap flights had hollowed out long-distance rail demand, and operators steadily retired the specially designed carriages required for overnight travel. By the middle of the following decade, only a handful of routes remained.

[41]

Since then, an improbable turnaround has occurred. New night services have been launched between cities as varied as Vienna and Paris, Amsterdam and Zurich, Stockholm and Hamburg. Some routes that had been abandoned entirely have been reinstated, and new ones are planned.

[42]

One clear driver is the growing backlash against short-haul flights. Younger travellers, in particular, have embraced the concept of "flygskam" — the Swedish term for flight shame — and actively seek low-carbon alternatives for journeys of under 1,500 kilometres. Night trains, which largely replace a hotel stay with travel time, are an elegant solution.

[43]

Operators have also invested in modernising the experience. Where old sleeper trains were often creaky, cramped and poorly maintained, new rolling stock features private compartments with en-suite bathrooms, better lighting and more generous sleeping space. A journey that was once endured is increasingly marketed as an experience worth paying for in its own right.

[44]

Economics, however, remain stubbornly challenging. Sleeper carriages carry far fewer passengers than daytime trains, and the staff costs of overnight service are considerable. Without public subsidy or supportive policy, many routes would still not be viable, which partly explains why the revival has been led by state-owned operators rather than private firms.

[45]

These policy levers are crucial if the trend is to be sustained. Industry observers note that, while the cultural appetite for slow travel is clearly growing, rail's structural disadvantages against aviation — tax exemptions on jet fuel, for one — still need to be addressed through regulation.

[46]

Whether night trains ever return to their mid-twentieth-century prominence is doubtful. What seems increasingly clear, however, is that they have secured a meaningful place in Europe's future transport mix, offering something that neither budget flights nor daytime trains can genuinely match.

Paragraphs:

A. Governments in several countries have begun adjusting policy accordingly. France has banned short-haul flights where reasonable rail alternatives exist; Austria has invested heavily in its night-train fleet through publicly owned ÖBB.

B. Observers at the time assumed, reasonably enough, that this decline was terminal. Commentators wrote nostalgic farewells to the age of sleeper carriages, and industry publications treated the question as settled.

C. The rebirth is not purely a matter of nostalgia. Multiple factors have combined, some economic, some environmental, and some reflecting genuine changes in what travellers consider worth paying for.

D. High-speed rail, which now connects many major European cities, has complemented rather than replaced this trend. While high-speed daytime services suit short business trips, overnight travel has found its niche for leisure passengers and those wanting to arrive fresh without losing a day.

E. Critics argue that these modernisations have raised prices too sharply, pricing out budget travellers and making night trains a luxury product. Operators counter that premium positioning is essential to financial viability, given their cost structures.

F. The environmental case, it must be said, is not straightforward. While per-passenger emissions on trains are far lower than on aircraft, the gap narrows considerably when sleeper trains run only partially full, as is often the case.

G. There is also, for many travellers, a genuine romance to rail travel that aircraft cannot provide. The quiet rhythm of the wheels, the sight of landscapes passing beyond the window, and the possibility of arriving refreshed in a foreign capital have a cultural appeal that statistics alone cannot explain.

Part 1: Essay (220-260 words)

Your class has discussed how people consume news today. You have the following notes:

How has modern news consumption affected society?
  • Greater access to information than ever before
  • The spread of misinformation and sensational content
  • Decline of local and specialist journalism

Some opinions:

"We're drowning in headlines but starving for depth."

"Social media lets voices be heard that traditional outlets ignored."

"When local papers die, accountability dies with them."

Write an essay discussing two of the three issues and giving your own view.

Model (246 words):

Few changes have been as profound as the transformation of the way we consume news. Where once the evening newspaper and six-o'clock bulletin set the daily agenda, we now carry a limitless stream of updates in our pockets. Whether this represents progress is, in my view, a genuinely open question.

On the positive side, access to information has never been greater. Stories that might once have been ignored by editors can now travel around the world within minutes, and marginalised perspectives can reach audiences that would have been inconceivable a generation ago. In countries with tightly controlled media, digital platforms have given ordinary citizens a voice they could not otherwise find.

These gains, however, come with serious costs. The sheer speed and volume of modern news rewards sensation over substance, and misleading content often outperforms careful reporting in the competition for attention. Worse still, the collapse of local newspapers in many regions has gutted the kind of patient, accountability-focused journalism that no Twitter thread can replicate. Town councils, local courts and regional institutions now operate with far less scrutiny than they once did.

On balance, then, the picture is mixed. The democratisation of news has real value, but the hollowing-out of serious local reporting is a loss we have yet to reckon with fully. A healthy media ecosystem needs both the energy of digital and the discipline of the traditional — and at present, we have far more of the former than the latter.

Model Proposal (248 words):

Proposal: Introducing a Four-Day Working Week

Introduction
This proposal examines the potential introduction of a four-day working week at our company, weighing its likely benefits against the practical risks and recommending a course of action.

Potential benefits
Several companies that have adopted this model report increased productivity, lower absenteeism and improved staff retention. Employees in those organisations describe feeling better rested and more engaged during working hours. Recruitment also becomes easier in a competitive labour market, as candidates increasingly value flexibility.

Likely drawbacks
The most significant concern is client coverage. Our service levels currently depend on five-day availability, and a reduction would require either cross-training or restructured schedules. Additionally, staff in client-facing roles may find the concentrated workload stressful, particularly during busy periods. Not all teams or functions would benefit equally, which could create internal tensions.

Recommendations
Rather than committing to a company-wide change, I propose a six-month pilot in one or two departments where the model is most likely to succeed. During this period, productivity, client satisfaction and staff wellbeing should be monitored against baseline data. A comprehensive review would then inform any further expansion.

Conclusion
The evidence suggests that a four-day working week is worth exploring, but the risks are too significant to justify immediate adoption across the whole company. A carefully structured pilot would allow us to capture the benefits while limiting exposure if the model proves unsuitable.

Model Letter (240 words):

Dear Editor,

I am writing in response to last Sunday's article, which suggested that today's young people are less resilient than their predecessors. As someone who works closely with teenagers and young adults, I find the argument too sweeping to be helpful.

It is true, of course, that younger generations express stress and anxiety more openly than their grandparents did. But to interpret this as evidence of fragility is to mistake communication for substance. When older generations suffered silently, we did not call them resilient; we assumed they were fine because they said so. Young people today talk more honestly about mental health, which reflects self-awareness rather than weakness.

The article also overlooks the genuinely new pressures young people face. Constant comparison on social media, uncertain job markets, climate anxiety and rising housing costs were not issues for previous generations in anything like the same form. Any fair assessment of resilience has to take account of what is actually being endured.

That said, I do not wish to dismiss every concern raised. There are genuine questions about how we prepare young people to manage discomfort, set long-term goals and recover from setbacks. These questions deserve serious discussion, but they are poorly served by the sort of generational caricature your article presented.

A more sympathetic, evidence-based approach would be welcome in future coverage.

Yours faithfully,
J. Morris
