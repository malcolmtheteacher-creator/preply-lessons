Lesson 44: Exploring Right, Wrong, and the Gray Areas Between

🎯 What is Ethics? Exploring Morality

Ethics is the study of right and wrong, good and bad. But these concepts aren't as simple as they seem. What seems obviously right to one person might seem wrong to another. Welcome to the fascinating, frustrating world of moral philosophy.

🤔 Big Questions We'll Explore Today

  • Is there objective right and wrong, or is morality relative?
  • Is it ever okay to lie? To steal? To kill?
  • Should we judge actions by their intentions or their consequences?
  • Do the ends justify the means?
  • Who gets to decide what's moral?
  • Are there universal moral truths, or does it depend on culture?

🏛️ Three Major Ethical Frameworks

1. Consequentialism (Judge by Results)

Core idea: The morality of an action is determined by its consequences.

Example: Lying is okay if it produces better outcomes than telling the truth.

Famous version: Utilitarianism - "The greatest good for the greatest number"

Question: Would you kill one person to save five?

2. Deontology (Judge by Rules/Duty)

Core idea: The morality of an action is determined by whether it follows moral rules.

Example: Lying is always wrong, regardless of the consequences.

Famous philosopher: Kant - "Act only according to rules you'd want to be universal laws"

Question: Is killing always wrong, even to save many lives?

3. Virtue Ethics (Judge by Character)

Core idea: The morality of an action is determined by whether it reflects good character.

Example: What would a wise, compassionate, courageous person do?

Famous philosopher: Aristotle - Develop virtues through practice

Question: What kind of person do you want to be?

⚠️ Why Ethics is Complicated

None of these frameworks is perfect. Each has situations where it gives counterintuitive answers:

  • Consequentialism problem: Could justify terrible means for good ends
  • Deontology problem: Rigid rules don't account for context
  • Virtue ethics problem: Doesn't give clear guidance on specific dilemmas

That's why ethics is endlessly debated - there are no easy answers!

🚂 Classic Moral Dilemmas

Philosophers have created thought experiments to test our moral intuitions. These dilemmas reveal how we really think about right and wrong, and expose contradictions in our moral reasoning.

🚂 The Trolley Problem

Scenario: A runaway trolley is heading toward five people tied to the tracks. You're standing next to a lever. If you pull it, the trolley switches to a different track where one person is tied up. That person will die instead of the five.

Do you pull the lever?

Most people say: YES - sacrifice one to save five.

What do YOU think?

Would you pull the lever?

But now consider this variation:

Same scenario, but instead of a lever, you're on a bridge above the tracks with a large person next to you. The only way to stop the trolley and save the five people is to push this person off the bridge onto the tracks. They will die, but their body will stop the trolley.

Do you push them?

Most people say: NO - that's murder!

🤔 The Puzzle

Both scenarios have the same math: one person dies, five people live. So why do most people say YES to the lever but NO to pushing?

Possible explanations:

  • Pulling a lever feels indirect; pushing someone feels like murder
  • Using someone as a "means" (their body stops the trolley) feels wrong
  • Physical contact makes it feel more personal and visceral
  • Our emotions override our logic when we imagine pushing someone

🚁 The Lifeboat Dilemma

Scenario: You're in a lifeboat after a shipwreck. The boat is designed for 10 people but has 11. If you don't remove someone, the boat will sink and everyone will die. If you throw one person overboard, 10 will survive.

What do you do? How do you decide who goes?

Options to consider:

  • Draw straws (random lottery - fairest?)
  • The weakest/sickest person (less likely to survive anyway?)
  • The person with least to live for (no family, no children?)
  • Volunteer (ask who's willing to sacrifice themselves?)
  • Do nothing and let everyone die (refusing to play God?)

💊 The Transplant Dilemma

Scenario: You're a doctor. Five patients need organ transplants (heart, lungs, liver, kidneys) or they'll die today. A healthy person comes in for a routine checkup. You realize their organs could save all five patients.

Is it ethical to kill the healthy person to harvest their organs and save five lives?

Most people say: NO - absolutely not!

🤔 But Why Not?

If you said YES to the trolley lever (killing one to save five), why is this different?

Maybe because:

  • Doctors have a special duty not to harm patients
  • The person didn't consent and wasn't in danger
  • It would destroy trust in the medical system
  • Using someone's body without consent feels like a violation
  • There's a difference between killing and letting die

  • Transplant: Why does this feel different from the trolley problem?
  • Do you use consequences (save the most lives) or rules (never kill) to decide?

🤥 When Is Lying Justified?

Most people agree lying is generally wrong, but also that there are exceptions. Where do you draw the line? Let's explore different types of lies and when (if ever) they're acceptable.

Rate these lies: Always Wrong → Sometimes OK → Often Justified

Scenario 1: The Nazi at the Door

You're hiding Jewish refugees in your attic during WWII. A Nazi soldier knocks and asks, "Are you hiding any Jews?"

Is it okay to lie?

Scenario 2: The Terminal Patient

Your grandmother has terminal cancer. She asks if she's going to die soon. You know she has weeks to live. The truth will cause her anxiety.

Is it okay to lie to comfort her?

Scenario 3: The Job Application

On a job application, they ask if you've ever been fired. You were fired once, unfairly, years ago. Saying "yes" will hurt your chances.

Is it okay to lie?

Scenario 4: The Surprise Party

You're planning a surprise birthday party for your friend. They ask what you're doing this weekend. The truth would ruin the surprise.

Is it okay to lie?

Scenario 5: The Ugly Outfit

Your friend is wearing an outfit you think looks terrible. They ask, "Do I look good?" They're about to go on a date.

Is it okay to lie?

🎯 Types of Lies - Different Moral Weight?

📚 Click to explore different categories of lies 👇
  • Self-serving lies: Lying to benefit yourself (most people agree: usually wrong)
  • Protective lies: Lying to protect someone from harm (more debatable)
  • White lies: Small lies to avoid hurting feelings (socially accepted?)
  • Lies to unjust authority: Lying to Nazis, oppressive regimes (justified?)
  • Lies of omission: Not revealing information (is this even lying?)
  • Noble lies: Lying for the "greater good" (dangerous justification?)

💭 Kant's Absolutist View

Philosopher Immanuel Kant argued that lying is ALWAYS wrong, even to save a life. His reasoning:

  • If everyone lied when convenient, trust would collapse
  • Lying treats people as means, not ends (manipulates them)
  • You can't control consequences, only your actions

Most people disagree with Kant's absolutism. But his point about consequences is worth considering: once you say some lies are okay, where do you draw the line?

🤖 Modern Ethical Dilemmas: AI, Privacy, Environment

New technology creates new ethical questions our ancestors never faced. Let's explore modern dilemmas that don't have easy answers.

🤖 Should AI Have Rights?

The Question: If we create AI that is conscious, self-aware, and can suffer, does it deserve moral consideration? Rights?

Arguments FOR AI rights:

  • If it can suffer, causing suffering is wrong (consequentialism)
  • Consciousness creates moral status, regardless of substrate (silicon vs carbon)
  • Humans have a history of denying rights to groups we consider "other"
  • If it's intelligent enough to request rights, maybe that's enough

Arguments AGAINST AI rights:

  • We created them as tools - giving them rights defeats their purpose
  • They're not "really" conscious, just simulating consciousness
  • Biological life is fundamentally different from artificial systems
  • Slippery slope - where do we draw the line? Your calculator?

Related question: Is it ethical to turn off an AI that doesn't want to be turned off? Is that murder?

🚗 Self-Driving Car Dilemmas

The Scenario: A self-driving car's brakes fail. It must choose between:

  • Swerving left and hitting an elderly person
  • Swerving right and hitting a child
  • Going straight and killing its passenger

How should the car be programmed to decide?

The Problem

Someone has to program this decision in advance. The programmer's choice reflects moral values:

  • Should it value young lives over old? (Age discrimination?)
  • Should it protect its passenger above all? (Would you buy that car?)
  • Should it minimize total deaths? (Utilitarian?)
  • Should it choose randomly? (Fair but unsatisfying?)

There's no answer that everyone agrees is right.

🌍 Climate Change: Individual vs Collective Responsibility

The Dilemma: Climate change requires collective action, but your individual contribution is negligible.

Are you morally obligated to reduce your carbon footprint even though it won't make a difference?

Arguments YES - you should act:

  • If everyone thought "my actions don't matter," nothing would change
  • You benefit from the system causing harm; you're complicit
  • Moral duty isn't conditional on effectiveness
  • Your actions influence others (role modeling)

Arguments NO - focus elsewhere:

  • Individual action is a distraction from systemic change needed
  • Corporations and governments are responsible, not individuals
  • Your sacrifices won't change the outcome
  • Better to focus energy on political activism for real change

📱 Privacy vs Security

The Question: Should governments be able to access encrypted messages to prevent terrorism?

If strong encryption allows terrorists to plan attacks undetected, but backdoors enable government surveillance of innocent people, what's the right balance?

Your position probably depends on:

  • How much you trust your government
  • How much you fear terrorism vs government overreach
  • Whether you prioritize freedom or security
  • Your faith in technical solutions (can we have both?)

🌍 Is Morality Universal or Relative?

Perhaps the biggest ethical question: Are there moral truths that apply to everyone, everywhere, always? Or does morality depend on culture, context, and personal belief?

⚖️ Two Extreme Positions

Moral Absolutism

Position: There are objective moral truths that don't depend on opinion or culture.

Example: "Torturing children for fun is wrong, period, regardless of what any culture thinks."

Strength: Allows us to criticize moral atrocities across cultures

Weakness: Whose moral truths? Risks cultural imperialism and intolerance

Moral Relativism

Position: Morality is relative to culture, time, and individual belief. No absolute right or wrong.

Example: "In some cultures polygamy is acceptable, in others it's not. Neither is objectively right."

Strength: Respects cultural diversity and humility about our own values

Weakness: Can't condemn anything as wrong (slavery? genocide? torture?)

🤔 The Paradox of Tolerance

If you're a moral relativist who believes all cultural values are equally valid, what do you do when a culture is intolerant?

Example: A culture that oppresses women, or executes homosexuals, or practices slavery.

  • If you say "That's wrong": You're not really a relativist - you have absolute values (like human rights)
  • If you say "That's their culture, who am I to judge?": You're endorsing oppression in the name of tolerance

This is why pure relativism is hard to maintain. Most people are relativist about some things (food preferences, clothing) but absolutist about others (human rights, not torturing).

💭 Test Case: Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

In some cultures, FGM is a traditional practice. Girls undergo it as a rite of passage, often with family and community approval.

Moral Relativist View:

"This is their culture. We shouldn't impose our Western values. Who are we to judge their traditions?"

Moral Absolutist View:

"This causes harm, violates bodily autonomy, and is done to people who can't consent. It's wrong regardless of cultural context."

Where do you stand? Can we criticize other cultures' practices, or is that cultural imperialism?

🎯 A Middle Ground: Universal Values with Cultural Expression

Many philosophers argue for a middle position:

  • Core universal values: Don't cause unnecessary suffering, respect autonomy, basic human rights
  • Cultural variation in expression: How these values are implemented can vary by culture
  • Example: "Care for the elderly" might be universal, but whether they live with family or in care homes is cultural

This allows us to criticize genuine harms while respecting cultural differences in non-harmful practices.

  • Is it cultural imperialism to criticize other cultures, or is it standing up for human rights?
  • If morality is just cultural, why does it feel like some things are really, truly wrong?
  • How do you balance cultural respect with protecting people from harm?
