The Sibling Who Never Comes

You do every weekend. Your brother rings on Sundays and thinks that's the same thing. Tonight you're going to say so — and you'd like to still have a brother afterwards.

The Scene 🎬

It's Sunday night. You've just got in from your mum's — the shopping put away, the bins out, the fence saga from next door heard for the fourth time, the eye clinic letter that came on Thursday and that somebody will now have to ring about at half eight on Monday morning. That somebody has been you for about two years.

Your brother Dan lives two hours away. He rings her every Sunday, without fail, and they talk for forty minutes and she's delighted, and she tells you all week what Dan said. He is, in her eyes, a lovely son. He is not, in yours, doing any of it — and the difference between those two facts has started sitting in your chest like something you swallowed wrong.

There's no manager here to escalate it to. Nobody's HR department is going to look into the fairness of it. You can't hand in your notice on being someone's daughter. There's just you, your phone, and a brother who is going to hear the first four words and immediately get defensive.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. In your family, who is the one who actually does things — and how did that person end up being that person? Was it ever decided out loud, or did it just happen?
  2. Is it possible to ask a family member for something fair without it turning into an argument about the last twenty years? What makes the difference, do you think?

Who's who

You
The One Who Goes 🚗

Tired, fond of your brother, and quietly furious. You don't want an apology and you don't want him to feel terrible. You want a different Tuesday.

The other voice
Dan, Your Younger Brother 📞

Two hours away, kids, shifts, a real life. He rings your mum every Sunday and genuinely believes he's involved. He isn't lazy — he simply hasn't looked at it properly, and he'd rather not.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how the caller asks, rather than what they get. Notice how quickly they say what this call isn't; how they use one plain fact instead of a list of complaints; how they admit their own worst bit before Dan can use it against them; and how, at the end, they refuse the easy yes and go looking for a harder one.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how the caller keeps the anger in the facts and out of their voice.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. In the middle of pressing her brother, the caller admits something about herself that isn't flattering. What is it — and why would you ever say that out loud when you're the one in the right?

2. The caller makes two asks, one big and one smaller. Which one does Dan agree to first, and what does that tell you about how to pitch a request?

3. Dan's last turn doesn't just say yes. What extra details does he add, and why do they matter more than the word "yes" does?

1. She admits she's been counting — keeping a private tally of every weekend she's done and he hasn't. She says it because it takes the weapon out of his hands before he can pick it up, and because it changes what the call is about: it stops being a case against Dan and becomes something going wrong in her, which she'd like to stop.
2. He agrees to the smaller one first — the phone calls and the appointments — because it's the one he can see himself doing. A big ask gives someone a chance to explain why they can't; a small, specific ask gives them somewhere to say yes, and a yes he keeps is worth more than a yes he doesn't.
3. He names the mechanism — the work calendar — and a starting date, and he says he'll text her when it's done. That matters because she had just told him not to say yes only to end the conversation, so a bare yes would have proved nothing. Details are what a real commitment looks like from the outside.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for asking a family member for something fair — without the twenty-year argument. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. The whole thing turns on tone. These lines are hard lines, and if you say them hard, you get a fight; if you say them lightly and evenly, as though you're describing the weather, the other person can hear the facts instead of defending themselves against you. Keep your voice low and unhurried, especially on the sentences that would be easiest to shout.

🚪 Getting in the door without a fight

Phrase 1

He's going to be defensive within four words — he can hear it in your voice already. Which opener tells him what this call isn't, before he arms himself?

A. "We need to have a serious talk about Mum."
B. "I'm not ringing for a row, before you start."
C. "I suppose you're far too busy to talk to me."
B — "I'm not ringing for a row, before you start."
A row is an argument, the shouting kind, and "before you start" means "before you begin defending yourself" — you're naming his reaction a second before he has it, which tends to make it look silly to him. A sounds like the beginning of bad news, so he'll brace; C is a complaint pretending to be a question. B is the only one that lets him relax enough to listen.
Phrase 2

You want him to discover the gap himself rather than be told about it, so you ask the one question he can't answer vaguely. Put the pieces in order:

actuallygo round?When did youlast
"When did you actually last go round?"
To go round is to visit someone at their house, and the question needs a date as an answer, which is why it works — he can't say "quite recently" and move on. Actually is doing the quiet work: it pushes past the impression he has of himself and asks for the real thing. Best said mildly, as if you genuinely can't remember either.

⚖️ Naming the imbalance

Phrase 3

Now he needs to feel the size of it. You could list everything you do, or you could say one fact twice. Which one is harder to argue with?

A. "I do quite a lot for Mum, you know."
B. "You never do anything for her."
C. "It's every weekend, Dan. Every single one."
C — "It's every weekend, Dan. Every single one."
A is so vague that he can agree with it and change nothing. B contains never, and the moment you say never he only has to remember one time he helped for your whole point to fall apart. C states a fact and then repeats it in smaller words, which makes him hear the scale rather than dispute it — and there is nothing in it for him to correct.
Phrase 4

You've been counting, privately, for months, and you're ashamed of it. Rather than hide that, you say it and then immediately take it back — and the taking-back is the honest half. Put the pieces in order:

well, I am, a bit,and that's the problem.I'm not keeping score —
"I'm not keeping score — well, I am, a bit, and that's the problem."
To keep score is to count who has done what, the way you'd count points in a game, and nobody wants to admit to it because it sounds petty. Correcting yourself mid-sentence is what makes this land: you deny it, then you're honest, then you say the honesty is the thing you want to fix. He can't accuse you of keeping score once you've beaten him to it.

🤲 Giving him his side — then asking small

Phrase 5

He starts explaining the drive, the kids, the shifts. You already know all of it, and you need him to hear that you know it — so you say it before he finishes, with the stress on the auxiliary. Complete the chunk: "I know you're two hours away, I ___ know that."

"I know you're two hours away, I do know that."
The little do is only there to be stressed, and stressing it is how English insists on something someone doubts — you're not informing him, you're pushing back on his assumption that you've forgotten his life exists. Saying his excuse for him takes it off the table without you ever having to call it an excuse.
Phrase 6

Here's the ask. He'll agree to almost anything vague and do none of it. Which version gives him something he can genuinely say yes to?

A. "You'll have to start doing your share, Dan."
B. "Could you try and come a bit more often?"
C. "Could you do one weekend in three? That's all I'm after."
C — "Could you do one weekend in three? That's all I'm after."
B has no number in it, so there's nothing to agree to and nothing to fail at; A tells him what he must do, which starts an argument about whether he must. C names a small, countable amount — one weekend in three means one out of every three — and then caps it. "That's all I'm after" promises the price won't go up next month, and that promise is often the thing people are really frightened of when they say yes.
Phrase 7

If the driving is genuinely impossible, offer him a job he can do from a chair — the letters, the clinics, the ringing-up. Complete the chunk (two words): "Even taking the appointments ___ ___ would help."

"Even taking the appointments off me would help."
To take something off someone is to lift a job from them so it's yours now and not theirs any more, and it's a warmer way of asking than "you should do the appointments." Even is the useful word: it means this is the smaller version, the least you'd accept, which makes refusing it look like refusing everything.

🔒 Refusing the easy yes

Phrase 8

He's warming up. You can feel a lovely, generous, entirely imaginary promise coming. In your own words, ask him for the thing he'll genuinely do rather than the thing he wishes he were like. What would you say?

e.g. "Tell me what you can actually commit to — not what you'd like to do."
To commit to something is to promise it in a way that costs you if you break it, which is exactly the difference from wanting to. Notice the shape: you ask for one thing and refuse another in the same breath, so he can hear which of the two he was about to hand you. It's kinder than it looks, because it lets him offer less and be believed.
Phrase 9

His yes came fast, and you know what fast yeses are for — he wants this conversation over. Name what he actually wants. Complete the chunk: "Don't say yes now just to get me off the ___."

"Don't say yes now just to get me off the phone."
To get someone off the phone is to end a call you didn't want, and saying it out loud is a small risk — it's nearly rude — which is why it works. It tells him you'd rather have a smaller true answer than a big false one, and it means that if he does say yes now, he knows you'll remember it.

🕰️ Why you're doing this at all

Phrase 10

He's hurt, and you're about to explain that this call isn't an attack on him — it's an attempt to save him from what you can feel yourself turning into. Put the pieces in order:

than resent youI'd rather sort this out nowin ten years.
"I'd rather sort this out now than resent you in ten years."
To sort something out is to fix it while it's still fixable, and to resent someone is to carry a quiet, permanent anger at them for something they did or didn't do. Putting the two side by side turns the whole call from a complaint into a warning about the future, which is much harder to be offended by — you're not telling him he's a bad brother, you're telling him you'd like to keep having one.

🔑 Make them yours

These lines aren't only for brothers. Anywhere you've quietly become the person who does it — a flat, a team, a friendship — the same three moves apply. Pin them to something real in your own life, or they'll stay on the page. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"When did you actually last go round?" — think of somewhere the vague impression and the actual facts have drifted apart. What's the one question that would need a real answer?

"Could you do one weekend in three? That's all I'm after." — turn something you're carrying into a small, countable ask with a cap on it.

"I'd rather sort this out now than resent you in ten years." — where is a resentment quietly beginning? Who would you have to say this to?

The Twist

💷 He offers money instead

One more round — no script. Dan doesn't argue and he doesn't refuse. He says: "Look, I'll be honest with you. One in three isn't going to happen, and I'd rather tell you that than promise it. But I can pay. I'll put four hundred a month in, we get someone in twice a week — a proper carer, someone who knows what they're doing. That's more use to Mum than me sitting in her kitchen once a month, isn't it?" And the maddening thing is he might be right: the money is real, it's more than you can afford, and it would take the bins and the shopping off you tomorrow. It just leaves him exactly where he is. Start the timer. Do you take it? If you take it, what do you still need from him that money can't do — and can you name that out loud without it sounding like you're being difficult about a generous offer? If you don't take it, you'd better be able to say why, to a man who has just offered you four hundred pounds a month.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I told him what the call wasn't before he had time to get defensive.
  • I used one plain fact instead of a list, and I never said "never" or "always".
  • I admitted my own worst bit before he could use it against me.
  • My ask had a number in it, and I capped it so he knew the price wouldn't rise.
  • I refused the easy yes and asked for something he could actually keep.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Almost nobody chooses to be the one who goes. It's decided by geography, or by who answered the phone that first bad Tuesday, and then it hardens, and by the time anyone notices there's a rota that nobody ever wrote down.

What makes it hard to raise isn't the work. It's that there's no structure to appeal to — no manager, no contract, no notice period. There's only the two of you, and a childhood you both remember slightly differently, and the fear that saying it out loud will cost you the person you're saying it to.

Which is why the ask has to be small and the tone has to be flat. Not because the feeling is small — it isn't — but because a small ask is the only kind anyone can say yes to on a Sunday night, and a yes on a Sunday night is worth more than being right for ten years.

Two bigger questions

  1. Is a son who rings every Sunday doing less than a daughter who takes the bins out, or just something different? Who decides which counts — and does the person being cared for get a vote?
  2. Where care is concerned, is money help? If your brother can pay and you can't, and you can drive and he can't, have you both done your share — or is there something in showing up that can't be transferred?
