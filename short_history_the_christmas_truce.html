1. The men lived for months in a trench — a long, narrow ditch cut into the ground, its walls held up with timber and its floor thick with mud and cold water.
a trench (n) — a long, deep ditch dug into the earth for soldiers to shelter in and fight from.
2. Between the two armies lay no man's land — a stretch of torn, empty ground that belonged to neither side and that no one dared to cross.
no man's land (n) — the dangerous open ground between two enemy front lines, controlled by neither.
3. Fields of sharp coiled barbed wire were strung across the ground to slow any soldier who tried to advance.
barbed wire (n) — wire with sharp points twisted along it, used as a barrier to stop people passing.
4. From the enemy trench came the sound of a carol — a familiar Christmas song, carried faintly across the frozen ground.
a carol (n) — a traditional Christian song sung especially at Christmas.
5. Both sides agreed to a short lull in the fighting so that they could bury their dead in peace.
a lull (n) — a quiet pause in activity or noise, a temporary calm.
6. The gesture was entirely spontaneous — no general had ordered it, and no plan lay behind it; it simply happened, all along the line at once.
spontaneous (adj) — happening naturally and suddenly, without being planned or ordered.
7. Cautiously, one soldier climbed up onto the parapet, the raised edge at the top of the trench, and stood in full view of the other side.
a parapet (n) — the protective bank or wall along the top edge of a trench.
8. The generals were determined to fraternise with no one; mixing in a friendly way with the enemy was strictly forbidden.
to fraternise (v) — to mix and behave in a friendly way with people, especially with the enemy.
9. High command feared that such friendliness would ruin the men's willingness to fight, so they gave harsh orders to prevent any repeat of the armistice.
an armistice (n) — a formal agreement to stop fighting a war; a laying down of weapons.
10. What survives is not the strategy but the humanity of it — the simple recognition that the man opposite was, after all, a person like oneself.
humanity (n) — kindness and compassion towards others; the quality of behaving like a caring human being.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A war that would not be over by Christmas

When the First World War broke out in the summer of 1914, many believed it would be short. "Home by Christmas," the soldiers of Britain, France and Germany told one another as they marched away. They were wrong. By December the armies had dug themselves into a line of trenches that stretched roughly 700 kilometres across Belgium and France, from the North Sea to the Swiss border — the Western Front.

Life on the front line was miserable. The men lived in narrow trenches, ankle-deep in freezing mud, often only a few dozen metres from the enemy across a shattered strip of no man's land laced with barbed wire. They were cold, wet, exhausted and far from home. And now the first Christmas of the war was approaching — the season, for many of them, most bound up with family, warmth and peace.

In the days before Christmas, small parcels began to reach the trenches. The British received tins of tobacco and a brass box of gifts sent in the name of Princess Mary; the Germans were sent small Christmas trees, candles and cigars. On both sides, men who had expected a quick war found themselves marking the holiest night of their year in a ditch, within earshot of the people they had been ordered to kill.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What had many soldiers expected the war to be like, and what was the reality by December 1914?

2. What were conditions like on the Western Front that winter?

1. They expected a short war, "home by Christmas"; instead the armies were dug into some 700 km of trenches across Belgium and France, with no end in sight.

2. Cold, wet and miserable — men lived in muddy trenches only metres from the enemy, across barbed wire and no man's land, far from home.

Discuss:
  • Why might Christmas, of all times, have been the hardest moment of that first winter for the men in the trenches?

Part 2 — The night the guns fell silent

It began, in many places, with singing. On Christmas Eve, British soldiers heard voices drifting across no man's land from the German trenches — carols, sung in German. "Stille Nacht" answered by "Silent Night." Then, along the parapet of the enemy line, small lights appeared: candles, and little Christmas trees set out openly on the edge of the trench. Cautious shouts crossed the gap. "You no shoot, we no shoot." Slowly, in place after place, the shooting stopped.

On Christmas morning, something almost unthinkable happened. Unarmed men began to climb out of their trenches and walk into no man's land — the very ground that meant death on any ordinary day — to meet their foe face to face. There was no order from above; the truce was entirely spontaneous, springing up separately along long stretches of the line. Soldiers shook hands. They exchanged small gifts: tobacco, chocolate, tins of food, buttons and badges cut from their own uniforms. They showed each other photographs of wives and children. In several places they held joint burial services, laying to rest the dead who had lain out in no man's land, and stood together, bareheaded, to mourn.

And in a handful of spots, between the shell holes, men kicked a ball about. The football of the Christmas Truce has grown half into legend, and we cannot always separate the real kickabouts from the stories told afterwards — but soldiers' own letters do describe informal games in the frozen mud. For a few hours, in the middle of the deadliest war the world had yet seen, thousands of men chose, on their own, simply to stop.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the truce begin, and what did the soldiers do when they met in no man's land?

2. Who ordered the truce?

1. It began with carols sung across no man's land and candles on the parapets; then men climbed out unarmed, shook hands, swapped gifts, shared photographs, buried the dead together, and in places played football.

2. No one — it was spontaneous, arising by itself in many separate places along the line, with no order from the generals.

Discuss:
  • The men who walked into no man's land were disobeying the whole logic of the war. What do you think gave ordinary soldiers the courage to do it?

Part 3 — Why it never happened again, and its long echo

The truce could not last. In some sectors it held for only Christmas Day; in others the quiet stretched on into the new year until officers ordered their men back to war. When news reached the generals, they were alarmed. An army that had shaken hands with the enemy and mourned beside him might lose its will to fight. Commanders on both sides issued strict orders forbidding any fraternisation, and warned that men who repeated it would face severe punishment.

The war, meanwhile, grew crueller. Over the next four years it hardened into a grinding slaughter of artillery, gas and machine guns that would eventually cost millions of lives. Later Christmases saw nothing on the same scale: high command deliberately arranged bombardments over the holidays, rotated units, and made certain the men on opposite sides never again grew familiar enough to climb out and meet. The spontaneous truce of 1914 remained a single, unrepeated moment.

And yet it never faded from memory. More than a century on, the Christmas Truce is one of the most retold stories of the entire war — kept alive in letters, songs, films and quiet acts of remembrance. It endures not because it changed the course of the war, which it did not, but because of what it revealed: that even in the machinery of mass killing, ordinary men on both sides could recognise one another's humanity, and, for one night, choose peace. That is its long echo — the reminder that the enemy, close up, is only ever another human being.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the generals move to stop the truce, and why did nothing like it happen again?

2. Why is the Christmas Truce still remembered today, according to the story?

1. They feared an army that had befriended the enemy would lose the will to fight, so they banned fraternisation and, in later years, deliberately arranged bombardments and rotated troops to prevent any repeat.

2. Not because it changed the war (it didn't) but because it showed that even in mass killing, ordinary men could recognise each other's humanity and, for a moment, choose peace.

Discuss:
  • The generals were probably right that the truce threatened the war effort. Does that make their decision to stamp it out understandable — or does it make the truce all the more remarkable?

Grammar — used to / would (past habits)

To tell this story well, you need to paint the ordinary, repeated life of the trenches — what the men always did, night after night — and then set the one extraordinary night against it. English has two neat tools for repeated past action, and one crucial rule about when only one of them works.

The two structures:
• used to + base verb — a past state or a repeated past action that is no longer true: "The men used to live in the trenches." "They used to be enemies."
• would + base verb — a repeated past action or habit (but not a state): "Every evening the shelling would start again." "They would sing to keep their spirits up."
The key difference:
• For repeated actions, both work: "Each night they used to sing / would sing."
• For states (be, have, know, believe, live-as-a-condition), use used to, not would: "They used to be afraid." ✓ "They would be afraid" ✗ (in this sense).
• For a single, finished event, use neither — use the past simple: "On Christmas Eve they heard carols." (not "used to hear")

1. used to, would, or past simple?

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence.

1. Before the war, the two nations trade freely with one another. (a past state, no longer true)

2. Night after night, the guns open fire across no man's land. (a repeated action)

3. On Christmas Eve, the men suddenly voices singing carols. (one finished event)

1. used to (a past state; "would" doesn't work for states)

2. would (or used to) (a repeated habitual action)

3. heard (a single completed event — past simple, not "used to")

2. Fix the form

Each sentence uses the right idea but the wrong form. Write the corrected verb or phrase.

1. The soldiers used to (sang) carols in their own languages.

2. Every dawn the shelling would (started) again.

3. Before 1914, the men would (be) strangers who never expected to meet. (careful — this is a state!)

1. sing (used to + base verb)

2. start (would + base verb)

3. used to be ("would be" doesn't work for a state — switch to "used to")

3. Set the scene, then break it — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with used to, would, or the past simple. Read it aloud once you're done.

Every night the two sides exchange fire across the mud, and the men afraid to lift their heads above the parapet. Then, on Christmas Eve, everything changed: they carols drifting from the enemy trench, and one by one they climbed out to meet.

would exchange (repeated habit) · used to be (a state — not "would be") · heard (a single event — past simple)

Notice the shape of the story: the repeated, habitual past (used to / would) sets the scene, and the past simple marks the one night that broke it.
