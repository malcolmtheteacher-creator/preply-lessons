Six Moves for Influence Without Authority

Click each move. These are the tactics that work when you can't just order someone to do something.

1. Know Their Currency +

Why it matters: Different people care about different things. Your job is to figure out what matters to the specific person you're pitching.

Manager A cares about cost control. Manager B cares about speed to market. Peer A cares about personal impact. Peer B cares about team morale.

How to find it: Listen to what they talk about. What problems do they mention? What frustrates them? That's their currency. Lead there.

2. The Reframe +

Why it matters: Connect your idea to something they already want. That's not manipulation — that's understanding what actually drives them.

You want to adopt a new tool. Manager is resistant because it's another thing to learn. You say: "This will cut the time you spend in meetings on status updates by 30%. That means more focus time on what matters."

The shift: You went from "We need this tool" to "This solves a problem you already have."

3. The Coalition +

Why it matters: A solo pitch is easy to dismiss. A coalition is hard to dismiss.

Before you pitch to your resistant manager, get two other team leads on board. Then when you pitch, you say: "We've talked to X and Y. They're aligned. What would make this work for you?"

The benefit: Now they're not saying no to you — they're saying no to a majority view.

4. The Pilot +

Why it matters: Don't ask for full commitment. Ask for a test. Low risk. Easy to kill if it doesn't work. Easy to expand if it does.

Instead of "We need to adopt this tool company-wide," you say: "Let's run this with one team for two weeks. We measure impact. If it works, we expand. If it doesn't, we drop it."

The benefit: You've removed the risk from their perspective. They can say yes easily.

5. The Data Point +

Why it matters: One specific number beats ten vague claims.

Vague: "This will save us time."
Specific: "In similar companies, this has cut manual data entry by 15 hours per week per person."

Why it works: It's no longer an opinion. It's a fact. And it's something they can share with their boss if they need to justify it.

6. The Long Game +

Why it matters: If they say no, you need a way to stay in the conversation without damaging the relationship.

"I understand the timing isn't right. Can we revisit this in Q2 when project X is done? I'll keep gathering data. The case will be even stronger by then."

The benefit: You've acknowledged their no. You've given them a path to yes. And you've signalled that you're not pushy — you're committed.

Pitch Four Stakeholders

Pick one scenario. You're pitching the same idea to a different type of resistant stakeholder. They have different fears, different currencies. Tailor your pitch. 3 minutes to prepare, 2 minutes to pitch each.

A. The Risk-Averse Senior

Their fear: Failure. What could go wrong?

Their currency: Precedent. How has this worked elsewhere? What's the downside protection?

Your pitch approach: Lead with case studies. Emphasize the pilot. Show downside protection.

B. The Territorial Peer

Their fear: Your idea threatens their domain. It looks like you're taking credit, poaching their space, or making them look bad.

Their currency: Protecting their turf. How does this make them stronger, not weaker?

Your pitch approach: Position them as the leader. Make it their idea. Show how it makes their team better.

C. The Busy Sponsor

Their fear: Wasted time. They have 30 seconds before their next meeting.

Their currency: The headline. Give them the headline, not the detail. They'll ask questions if interested.

Your pitch approach: Lead with the ask and the benefit. One sentence. Wait for them to ask for more.

D. The Sceptic

Their fear: Hype. They've heard pitches before. They're allergic to enthusiasm.

Their currency: Evidence. Hard data. What's the unbiased proof?

Your pitch approach: Lead with data. Be understated. Anticipate their questions.

Reflection & Language Bank

Influence Without Title

Your power comes from them needing what you're offering. Not needing your approval or authority — needing the outcome you're proposing.

That's why currency matters. When you understand what they actually need, you can show them how your idea gets them there. Now they're not doing you a favor — they're solving their own problem.
