Learning intention: Today we're working on explaining why time seems to run in one direction, so you can argue a precise, carefully hedged position on a big open question in science.

The hook: a film played in reverse

Imagine a film of a glass falling off a table and smashing. Now imagine it running backwards: the fragments leap off the floor, fit together and the glass jumps back onto the table. Everyone laughs. Nobody needs a physics degree to know the reversed film is fake.

Here is the strange part. Almost every fundamental law physicists have written down works just as well in either direction. Run the equations backwards and they are still satisfied. So if the laws have no preferred direction, where does the direction come from?

SPEAK: Before you read on, offer a theory in 30 seconds. Where does the arrow come from?

Roughly: the direction isn't in the laws. It comes from statistics (there are vastly more ways for things to be messy than tidy) plus a remarkable fact about how the universe began. The next tabs unpack both halves of that sentence.

DISCUSS (co-construct the pattern):

Put the six scenes into two groups. What do the "wrong-looking" scenes have in common that the "fine" ones lack? Try to state the pattern in one sentence, then test it against the borderline case: a single gas molecule bouncing around a box. Would a reversed film of it look wrong? Why or why not?

The "wrong" scenes involve enormous numbers of particles and energy spreading out irreversibly (friction, heat flow, mixing, breakage). The "fine" scenes involve few objects and negligible dissipation. A single molecule's film looks perfectly plausible in reverse. The arrow only becomes visible when there are many parts. That is the clue.

Three ideas, in small steps

Idea 1 · The laws don't pick a direction

Newton's laws, Maxwell's electromagnetism, the Schrödinger equation and general relativity are all (to an excellent approximation) time-symmetric. A handful of obscure particle processes show a tiny asymmetry, but it is far too small to explain a broken glass.

Visual: a ball's trajectory ▶ and the same trajectory ◀ are both legitimate solutions.

Idea 2 · Entropy counts arrangements

In the 1870s Boltzmann proposed that entropy measures how many microscopic arrangements correspond to what you can see. His formula, S = k log W, links entropy S to W, the number of such arrangements.

A brand-new deck is in one particular order. A shuffled deck is in some order, and there are about 8 × 1067 possible orders (52 factorial). That is far more than the number of atoms in the whole Earth, which is roughly 1050.

◀ few arrangements (low entropy: "tidy")        many arrangements (high entropy: "messy") ▶

Idea 3 · "Never" really means "overwhelmingly unlikely"

The second law says that in an isolated system entropy almost always increases. It doesn't forbid a decrease. It says a decrease is so improbable that, to all intents and purposes, you will never see one.

Demonstrate · Production

The puzzle that remains

Statistics explains why entropy goes up from a low starting point. But follow the logic backwards. If entropy is rising now, it must have been lower yesterday, lower still last year, and extraordinarily low at the very beginning. The universe, roughly 13.8 billion years ago, must have started in a state of astonishingly low entropy. Many physicists call this the past hypothesis.

Why was the beginning so special? Nobody has a settled answer. Three families of response are on the table:

ResponseSupporters argue…Critics counter…
A · A brute factIt is simply an initial condition, and every theory needs starting assumptions.It explains nothing. A fluke this extreme invites an explanation.
B · A bigger pictureOur universe may be one part of a larger structure in which low-entropy beginnings arise naturally.Such proposals are very hard to test, so they may not count as science yet.
C · A selection effectObservers like us could only exist in a universe that began this way, so we shouldn't be surprised.Noting that we exist doesn't say why the conditions were possible.

Precision toolkit: language for claims you can't fully prove

Calibrating certainty
· It is overwhelmingly likely that…
· To all intents and purposes, …
· Strictly speaking, … ; in practice, …
· There is no fundamental law against…, but…
Handling disagreement
· That hinges on whether…
· This is at odds with…
· A case in point is…
· Even granting that, it doesn't follow that…
Transformation task: rewrite each overstated claim so that it is accurate and carefully hedged. Attempt first, then compare.

1. "Entropy always increases."

2. "The reversed film of the smashing glass is impossible."

3. "Scientists know why the universe began in such an ordered state."

1. In an isolated system, entropy almost always increases.
2. No fundamental law forbids the reversed film, but it is, to all intents and purposes, impossible.
3. Scientists have yet to agree on why the universe began in such a low-entropy state.
