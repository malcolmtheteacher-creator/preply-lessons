Use It or Lose It

In 1974 Sweden gave fathers the right to paid time with a newborn. Almost none of them took it. Twenty years later, one small change in the rules made all the difference.

Today: you'll follow a true story about work, family and fairness, and use a full range of future forms — be about to, be due, will be doing, will have done — to talk about what is coming.

How Sweden taught fathers to take their leave

Read each part. Answer the quick check from memory, then discuss.

Part 1 — A law nobody used

In 1974, Sweden did something no country had done before. It replaced mothers' maternity leave with a paid parental leave that either parent could take. On paper, it was equality: a mother or a father could stay at home with a new baby, and the state would pay.

The law was generous, and the politicians were proud of it. But when researchers looked at the numbers, they found a strange result. Fathers hardly used it. At the start, only about half of one percent of all the leave days were taken by men, and for years afterwards the figure stayed very small.

The reasons were not complicated. In most families the father earned more, so the household lost less money if the mother stayed home. Employers expected men to be at work. And many fathers worried about the stigma: would colleagues think they were not serious? The right existed. The take-up did not.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

2. What was the problem with the results?

3. Give two reasons why fathers did not use the leave.

1. It was the first paid parental leave that either parent could take, instead of leave for mothers only.

2. Almost no fathers used it — only about half of one percent of leave days at the start.

3. Any two: fathers usually earned more, employers expected men to work, and men feared how colleagues would see them.

Discuss:
  • Why can a right exist on paper but not in real life? Can you think of other examples?

Part 2 — The month that disappeared

For twenty years, Sweden tried persuasion: public campaigns encouraging fathers to share the leave. It helped a little. It did not change much.

Then, in 1995, the government tried something different. It reserved one month of the leave for each parent. If the mother wanted to use the father's month, she could not. If the father did not take it, the family simply lost it. The newspapers called it the "daddy month". The principle was brutally simple: use it or lose it.

The effect was fast. Once the month belonged to fathers, the incentive was clear — and the worry about what colleagues thought grew weaker, because every father was in the same position. Sweden added a second reserved month in 2002 and a third in 2016. Today, each parent has a quota of 90 days that cannot be transferred.

Iceland copied the idea in 2000 with a "3+3+3" model: three months for the mother, three for the father, and three to share. Before the change, men used only a fraction of one percent of the leave. Afterwards, around nine in ten fathers took some.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Sweden try before 1995, and did it work?

2. What was the rule of the "daddy month"?

3. What was Iceland's 3+3+3 model?

1. It tried campaigns and posters; they helped a little but did not change much.

2. One month of leave was reserved for the father. If he did not take it, the family lost it — it could not be transferred.

3. Three months for the mother, three for the father, and three the parents could share.

Discuss:

Part 3 — Did it work?

Researchers who study families have found that the reserved months did more than put fathers at home. In some studies, the longer a father took leave, the more involved he stayed in daily care later on. A man who had spent weeks as the main caregiver, they argue, understands the work in a way that a visitor never does. It also helps him bond with the child early.

But the story is not simply a triumph. Fathers in Sweden still take fewer leave days than mothers — in recent figures, roughly a third of the total — and equality campaigners say progress has slowed. Some couples also feel the state is telling them how to live: why should a family that wants one parent to stay home be punished for it?

Supporters reply that the rule only works because it is firm: a quota that can be swapped is a quota that mothers end up using. Critics reply that fairness cannot be forced. Both agree on one thing. The shift in what people think of as normal for a father has been dramatic. In 1974, a father pushing a pram on a weekday morning would have been an unusual sight. Today it is ordinary.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What do the studies say about fathers who took longer leave?

2. Give one criticism of the reserved-month rule.

3. What do both sides agree on?

1. They tend to stay more involved in daily care of the child later on.

2. Any one: it limits families' freedom to choose; it can be seen as the state telling people how to live; fathers still take fewer days than mothers.

3. That the shift in what is seen as normal for fathers has been dramatic.

Discuss:
  • Which is the stronger argument — "fairness needs firm rules" or "families should choose for themselves"? Why?

Grammar — Talking About the Future

A story about a policy is full of futures — what is coming, what will be happening, what will already be finished. English has several ways to look ahead, and each says something different.

1 · be about to + verb — it is starting right now. “Pack your bag — the taxi is about to arrive.” “The new law is about to come into force.” It means very soon, almost immediately.
2 · be due (to) — it is scheduled or expected. “The baby is due in March.” “The report is due to be published on Friday.” Use it for births, deadlines and timetables.
3 · will be + -ing (future continuous) — in progress at a future time. “This time next month, I will be changing nappies at three in the morning.” It pictures the middle of an action.
4 · will have + past participle (future perfect) — finished by a future time. “By June, I will have used all my leave.” It looks back from a point in the future.
5 · will or be going to? Going to is for plans and evidence: “I am going to take the full month.” Will is for offers and quick decisions: “I 'll hold the baby while you eat.”
6 · The traps. about to arriving → “about to arrive”.  due arrive → “due to arrive”.  will be have finished → “will have finished”.  By Friday I will be finishing it → if it is complete by then, use “I will have finished”.

Going deeper — fairness, freedom and the rules that change behaviour

The Swedish story is really an experiment about human behaviour: when does advice work, when do rules work, and who decides what is fair?

Why "use it or lose it" works

The reserved month changed three things at once. It gave families a financial reason to act. It gave fathers a reason to say to an employer, "This is the rule, not my choice." And it removed a silent conflict inside the couple: neither person had to ask the other to give something up.

Notice what the rule did not do. It did not tell fathers how to feel, how to behave at home, or what to believe. It simply changed the cost of doing nothing. Many policies, from pension schemes to savings plans, use a similar idea: they change what happens if you do nothing.

1. Which word means "something that gives you a reason to act"?

2. What is the rule's main principle, in four words?

3. Say in the future perfect: “The family will lose the month” (if the father does not take it by his child's first birthday).

1. An incentive.

2. Use it or lose it.

3. By the child's first birthday, the family will have lost the month.

Two sides of the argument

People who think carefully about this still disagree.

Firm rules are fairWithout a reserved quota, the leave tends to go to the mother, because of income and expectations. A firm rule gives fathers a safe reason to take it and keeps the system equal.
Families should chooseEvery family is different. A couple might prefer one parent at home for a year. A rule that punishes that choice treats all families as if they were the same.
