The Second Arrow

The Buddha on why small things hurt so much, and how to let go

The cracked phone

Have a go, out loud. There are no wrong answers.
  • What comes into your head when you hear the words the Buddha or Buddhism? Say any words, places or pictures.
  • Think of the last small thing that went wrong for you: a missed bus, a broken cup, a rude message. How long did it bother you?
You run for a bus. Your phone falls out of your pocket. The screen cracks from corner to corner.

The same thing happens to two people. Read what each one thinks.

Person A
"I'm such an idiot. I should have put it in my bag. This always happens to me. My whole day is ruined."
Angry all day. Still talking about it at the weekend.
Person B
"Oh no. That's annoying. Well, it still works. I'll get it fixed on Saturday."
Forgot about it by lunchtime.

Same phone, same crack. Why does Person A suffer so much more than Person B?

Nothing worse happened to Person A. The difference is what A said to themselves afterwards: blaming, exaggerating ("always", "whole day ruined") and replaying it. That extra pain was added by A's own thoughts.

When something goes wrong, how much of the pain comes from what happened, and how much from what we tell ourselves about it?

Two arrows

Read one part at a time. After each part, answer the question from memory before you check.

I

The prince who left the palace

About 2,500 years ago, in what is now Nepal and northern India, a prince called Siddhartha Gautama grew up in a palace. His father kept every sad sight away from him. When Siddhartha was 29, he went outside and saw, for the first time, an old man, a sick man and a dead body. He understood that nobody, not even a prince, can escape getting old, getting ill and dying.

He left the palace to find out how people could live with this. After years of searching, he sat down under a tree and, his followers say, finally understood. They called him the Buddha, which means "the one who woke up".

What three sights changed Siddhartha's life, and what does "Buddha" mean?

An old man, a sick man and a dead body. "Buddha" means "the one who woke up".
II

The arrow story

The Buddha once gave his students a picture. Imagine a man is hit by an arrow. It hurts. Then, straight away, a second arrow hits him in the same place. Now he feels two pains instead of one.

The first arrow is the pain that life brings: illness, loss, a cracked phone. Nobody can avoid it. The second arrow is the pain we add ourselves: the worrying, the blaming, the "why me?". The Buddha said that ordinary people are hit by both arrows, but a wise person feels only the first.

This teaching comes from an old Buddhist text called the Sallatha Sutta, "the discourse on the arrow".

Which arrow can we avoid, and which can't we?

We can't avoid the first arrow: things will go wrong in every life. We can learn to stop the second arrow, the extra pain our own thoughts add.

First arrow or second arrow?

Worked example"I failed my driving test." → First arrow. It happened; it's a fact.
"I'm useless. I'll never pass anything." → Second arrow. It's a story my head adds, and it makes the pain bigger.

Arrow English

The second arrow is usually made of words: the sentences we say to ourselves after something goes wrong. In English they come in a few patterns. Each card shows the pattern, an example, and what it means in plain words.

The words of the second arrow

should have + 3rd form of the verb
"I should have left earlier."
I'm telling myself I made the wrong choice in the past. (3rd form: leave – left – left, take – took – taken, know – knew – known.)
If only + had / hadn't + 3rd form
"If only I hadn't taken it out of my bag."
I'm wishing the past had been different, and it hurts because I can't change it.
I wish + had / hadn't + 3rd form
"I wish I'd been more careful." ('d = had)
The same feeling as "if only", a little softer.
If + had (past) → would + verb (now)
"If I hadn't dropped it, I wouldn't be so stressed now."
One thing in the past → a result I'm feeling now. It joins yesterday's mistake to today's mood.
always · never · everything · ruined
"This always happens to me. Everything is ruined."
Making the problem bigger than it really is.

None of these sentences is wrong. "I should have left earlier" can be useful the first time you say it, because it helps you plan. It becomes the second arrow when you say it again and again and it only hurts.

The words of a loose grip

Here is English for answering the second arrow calmly:

These things happen.It is what it is.At least…It's not the end of the world.What can I do about it now?Next time, I'll…Let it go.
Worked example: the same rainy day, two waysSecond arrow: "I should have checked the weather. If only I hadn't come today. The whole trip is ruined."
Loose grip: "It's raining. That's a pity. At least the museum is open. Next time, I'll check the forecast."

The mustard seed

This is one of the best-known stories in Buddhism. It is about the biggest first arrow of all: losing someone you love.

I

Kisa Gotami

In the Buddha's time there was a young mother called Kisa Gotami. Her little son, her only child, became ill and died. She could not accept it. She carried his body from house to house, begging: "Please, give me medicine for my son." People thought her grief had driven her mad.

At last someone sent her to the Buddha. He said: "I can help. Bring me a handful of mustard seeds." Mustard seeds are tiny seeds used in cooking; almost every kitchen had them. "But," he added, "they must come from a house where nobody has ever died."

Why did Kisa Gotami think this would be easy?

Because mustard seeds were in almost every kitchen. She only needed one house to give her some.

Knock on the doors with her

Click each door. Read what the family says.

II

Evening

By evening, Kisa Gotami had knocked on door after door and had not one seed. And slowly she understood. Death had come to every family in the town. She was not being punished, and she was not alone. She buried her son, went back to the Buddha, and became one of his followers. Later she became a nun.

Why did the Buddha send her for mustard seeds instead of simply telling her "Everyone dies"?

If he had just told her…By knocking on the doors, she…She needed to see for herself that…
If he had just told her, she probably wouldn't have listened: her pain was too strong. By knocking on the doors, she discovered it herself, from real families. She needed to see with her own eyes that every family loses someone, so her grief became something shared, not a punishment.

The big idea: everything changes

The same candle, a few hours apart.

Nothing stays the same: phones, cities, bodies, friendships, the people we love. The Buddha taught that this is simply how the world is. We suffer most when we demand that things stay exactly as they are, because they never do.

4. Sandcastles are beautiful partly because they are impermanent: by evening the sea has taken them.
impermanent (adj) — not lasting for ever; sure to change or end. Example: "Fame is impermanent: most stars are forgotten within a few years."
5. After her husband died, she needed time to grieve before she could think about the future.
grieve (v) — to feel deep sadness, especially because someone has died. Example: "The whole town grieved when the old doctor died."
6. In the end, Kisa Gotami accepted that her son was gone.
accept (v) — to agree that something is true or real, even if you don't like it. Example: "It took him years to accept that the business had failed."

The chariot

Around 150 BC, a Greek king called Milinda (the Greeks called him Menander) ruled lands in what is now Afghanistan, Pakistan and north-west India. He loved arguing with wise men. One day he met a Buddhist monk called Nagasena. Their conversation was written down in a famous book, The Questions of King Milinda. Here is the beginning, told simply.

King: What is your name?
Nagasena: People call me Nagasena. But "Nagasena" is only a name. There is no fixed, unchanging person behind it.
King: Then who is talking to me? This is nonsense!
Nagasena: How did you come here today, Your Majesty?
King: In my chariot.
Nagasena: Then show me the chariot. Is the pole the chariot? Are the wheels the chariot? The axle? The box you stand in?
Which of these parts is "the chariot"?

The king said no to every part. So where is the chariot? Say what you think the king answered, then check.

King: "Chariot" is just the name we use for all these parts put together.
Nagasena: Exactly, Your Majesty. And "Nagasena" is just the name for a body, feelings, thoughts and memories put together, and every one of them is changing.

What it means

In everyday words: there is no little, fixed "me" hidden inside you that never changes. "Me" is a name for many parts (your body, your feelings, your thoughts, your memories), all changing all the time, like the water in a river.

Why this matters for the arrowsWhen the second arrow says "I'm useless" or "I always fail", it treats you as a fixed thing that will never change. But the person who failed yesterday is already changing. The label doesn't stick for ever.
7. A bicycle has dozens of components: wheels, chain, brakes, pedals and many small screws.
component (n) — one of the parts that something is made of. Example: "The factory makes small parts and other components for car engines."
8. She hated the label "shy"; she just preferred quiet evenings to big parties.
label (n) — a name or word used to describe someone or something, often too simply. Example: "'Chariot' is just a label for a pile of wheels, wood and metal."

The grip

Drinking sea water: the more you drink, the thirstier you get.

So why do we shoot the second arrow? The Buddha's answer was one word, often translated as thirst. We want things, we want more of them, and we hold on tight to the things we have. Buddhist teachers often compare it to drinking sea water: every sip makes the thirst worse.

Worked examples from everyday life"Just one more episode": at 2 a.m., you're still watching.
You post a photo, then check the likes every five minutes.
A new phone feels exciting for a week; then you start wanting the next one.
A relationship ends, and you read the old messages again and again.

Notice what the Buddha did not say. He did not say "never enjoy anything". Buddhist teachers explain that the problem isn't enjoying a good meal or loving a person. It's clinging: needing them to stay, or to be more, and suffering when they don't. One picture often used: hold life in an open hand, not a closed fist.

9. After a week without her phone, she craved it so much she couldn't sleep.
crave (v) — to want something very strongly, often too strongly. Example: "When I'm tired, I crave sugar."
10. Some people cling to a job they hate because change feels frightening.
cling (v) — to hold on to someone or something very tightly and not want to stop. Example: "The child clung to his mother on the first day of school."
11. Buddhists say our attachment to things, needing them to stay, is what makes losing them hurt so much.
attachment (n) — a strong feeling of needing or loving someone or something. Example: "Her attachment to her old car was surprising; it barely worked."
12. When you finally let go of an old anger, you often feel lighter.
let go (phr v) — to stop holding something, or to stop thinking and worrying about it. Example: "It's time to let go of that argument; it happened years ago."

The doctor

Imagine you go to a doctor with a pain. A good doctor does four things, always in this order. Buddhists often describe the Buddha as a doctor, and his first teaching follows exactly the same four steps.

PrescriptionFour steps
1The doctor names the illness.
"You have a bad back."Life brings pain, and we add more to it: the first and second arrows.
2The doctor finds the cause.
"It's from sitting all day."The cause is the grip: craving and clinging.
3The doctor says it can get better.
"This can be cured."The grip can loosen, and the second arrow can stop.
4The doctor gives the medicine.
"Walk every day, and do these exercises."The medicine is a way of living: eight habits (below).

The medicine: eight habits in three groups

Seeing clearly1. Understanding the four steps
2. Wanting good things for yourself and others

4. Kind actions: no harming, no stealing
5. Earning a living without harming others
Training the mind6. Steady effort  ·  7. Noticing what is happening, moment by moment  ·  8. Calm, focused attention (meditation)

Two last pictures

The raftYou build a raft to cross a river. On the other side, do you carry the raft on your head for the rest of your journey? Of course not. The Buddha said his own teaching is like that raft: use it to cross, then don't cling even to it.
The fireWhen a fire has no more wood, it simply goes out. The Buddha's word for the end of the grip, nirvana, literally means "blowing out" or "going out", like a flame. When the craving runs out, the fire of the second arrow goes out too.

What do you think the Buddha meant by the raft? Why shouldn't you cling even to good ideas?

I think he meant that…A good idea can become a problem when…For example…
I think he meant that ideas and rules are tools, not treasures. A good idea can become a problem when you hold it so tightly that you stop thinking, or start fighting people about it. For example, someone can be so attached to "being healthy" that they become anxious about every meal. Use the raft, then put it down.

Connect: two thinkers from Greece and Rome

Compare. The Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius wrote, about 600 years after the Buddha (in an old translation): "If you are pained by any external thing, it is not this thing that disturbs you, but your own judgement about it." How is this like the second arrow? Is there any difference?
Both say that much of our pain comes not from the event but from what our mind adds to it. Marcus's answer is to judge more wisely, by reason. The Buddha goes further: he looks at the craving underneath, and offers a whole way of living and meditation to loosen it.

From Heraclitus

Why can't you step into the same river twice? Which Buddhist idea from today is like this?

Because new water is always flowing, so the river is never exactly the same. It's like the Buddha's idea that everything changes, and like the chariot: "me" is a name for parts that are always changing.
From Marcus Aurelius

According to Marcus Aurelius, what really disturbs us when something bad happens?

Not the thing itself, but our own judgement about it. In his own lesson, he compares the mind to a citadel, a fortress that events can't enter unless our judgement lets them in.
