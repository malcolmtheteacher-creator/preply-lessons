The Cleverest Engineers in the Room

Volkswagen's diesel scandal cost more than thirty billion euros. The engineering worked perfectly. That was the problem.

Today: you'll work through the defining corporate scandal of the decade — and you'll master the grammar that scandals are conducted in: the passive voice, and the difference between "mistakes were made" and "we made mistakes."
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • When a target is impossible and saying so is career-ending, what do people actually do? Walk through the options honestly.
  • Is there a meaningful difference between breaking a rule and defeating the test that checks the rule?
  • Who is accountable when "everybody knew" but nobody decided — and what does your answer imply for your own organisation?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. The engine software recognised the laboratory test — the steering angle, the wheel speeds — and switched into a clean mode that never ran on the road. Regulators called it a defeat device.
a defeat device (n) — the legal term for technology designed to beat a regulatory test rather than meet the standard. The phrase to keep: designed to pass the test, not to meet the rule.
2. On the road, the cars emitted oxides of nitrogen at many times the permitted level — the company later accepted figures of up to forty times in some conditions. The emissions were the substance of the fraud.
emissions (n, plural) — what an engine releases: NOx, CO2; emissions standards, to cut / understate emissions. Note it stayed plural in every headline for a decade.
3. The company's first instinct was to attribute everything to a small group of engineers acting alone — the classic rogue-employee defence.
a rogue employee (n) — someone acting without authority. The defence fails in proportion to how many people, over how many years, the "rogue" turns out to include.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: compliance · a recall · a settlement. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means the function and state of obeying laws and regulations?
compliance (n) — compliance department, compliance culture, non-compliance. The case's bitter joke: the test was passed in perfect compliance with its letter.
2. Which term means calling products back from customers to fix a defect?
a recall (n/v) — to issue a recall; a recall campaign. Here: roughly eleven million vehicles worldwide.
3. Which term means a negotiated resolution of legal claims, usually for money, without a full trial?
a settlement (n) — to reach / pay a settlement. VW's ran across criminal pleas, civil claims and buy-backs — totalling beyond thirty billion euros.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Insiders described a culture in which delivering bad news upward was dangerous and targets were not negotiable. A culture of fear matters to this case because…
  (a) it excuses the fraud   (b) it explains how an impossible demand becomes a hidden workaround instead of a refusal   (c) it proves the board ordered the device
(b) — the mechanism, not an excuse: fear converts "this cannot be done" into "this will be made to appear done." Readers of the Nokia case will recognise the machinery with a darker output.
2. The chief executive resigned saying he was not aware of any wrongdoing on his part. As a piece of language, that sentence is…
  (a) a full confession   (b) a denial engineered to leave every question open   (c) legally meaningless
(b) — parse it: not "nothing wrong happened" but "I am not aware" (knowledge, not fact) "on my part" (scope limited to himself). Today's grammar tab dissects exactly this species of sentence.

D · Collocation & word building

1. You know account. Build the accountability family: to be held ___ for something · to ___ for a decision (explain it) · unable to give a proper ___ of events.
To be held accountable · to account for a decision · a proper account of events. Distinguish responsibility (it was your job) from accountability (you answer for the outcome) — the case turns on who accepted which.
2. One verb completes all three: to ___ up a scandal (hide it) · to ___ for someone (protect them by lying) · the ___-up was worse than the crime.
cover — to cover up · to cover for someone · the cover-up. The proverb in the third slot is Washington's oldest, and this case tested it at industrial scale: the device ran for years after questions were first asked.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — Clean diesel

In the late 2000s Volkswagen set itself the goal of becoming the world's largest carmaker, and the sharpest front in that campaign was the United States, where the group was chronically weak. Its chosen weapon was "clean diesel": the promise that German engineering had squared the circle — diesel's fuel economy and driving feel, with emissions clean enough for America's NOx standards, which were far stricter than Europe's, and without the expensive urea-injection after-treatment its competitors needed on smaller cars. The campaign was loud, confident and, for several years, spectacularly successful with critics and customers alike.

Inside the engine programme, the promise had a problem: it could not be kept. Meeting the American NOx limits with the chosen engine, at the chosen cost, with the required performance, was — as engineers later testified — not achievable in the time given. In the company that then existed, reporting that upward was not a live option. Volkswagen under Ferdinand Piëch and then Martin Winterkorn was famous for brilliance and for fear in roughly equal measure: targets were not discussed, failure was not presented, and executives were known to dismantle engineers in meetings. Somewhere in that atmosphere — the criminal investigations later placed its origins around 2006 — a software path was taken instead. The engine-management code would recognise the official test cycle from the steering, throttle and wheel patterns, and run a clean mode for the duration; on the road, the after-treatment was turned down and the cars emitted NOx at many multiples of the limit, figures up to forty times being cited in the US findings.

It is worth pausing on what this was not. It was not an error, a tolerance stretched, or an ambiguous reading of a rule. It was purpose-built recognition software, refined across years and model generations, installed in roughly eleven million vehicles worldwide, whose entire function was to make a regulatory measurement measure something that did not exist.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What promise did "clean diesel" make, and why couldn't it be kept?

2. What exactly did the software do, and why does the lesson insist it was not an "error"?

1. Diesel economy and performance meeting America's strict NOx limits without costly after-treatment. Engineers concluded it was not achievable at the demanded cost and timing — and reporting that upward was culturally impossible.

2. It recognised the official test cycle and ran a clean mode only then; on the road emissions ran many times over the limit. Purpose-built, refined over years, installed in ~11 million cars — deliberate by construction.

Discuss:
  • "Reporting it upward was not a live option." What makes truth undeliverable in an organisation — and what would have had to be true at VW for one engineer to say "this cannot be done"?

Part 2 — Three researchers and a hire car

The unravelling began with a study so small it is now industry legend: a nonprofit commissioned researchers at West Virginia University — a budget in the tens of thousands of dollars — to measure diesel emissions on real roads, expecting to prove how clean modern diesels were. Driving instrumented VWs between cities, they found NOx far above laboratory results and published in 2014. The California and federal regulators began asking questions; Volkswagen spent more than a year answering them with technical explanations, recalibrations and a limited recall — none of which closed the gap, because the gap was the design. Only when regulators threatened to withhold certification of the 2016 models did the company, in September 2015, formally admit the existence of the defeat device.

The public detonation followed within days. The US Environmental Protection Agency issued its notice of violation; the share price lost roughly a third of its value in two trading days; and Martin Winterkorn resigned with the sentence that belongs to the history of corporate language: he was, he said, not aware of any wrongdoing on my part — accepting, in the same statement, responsibility for the crisis "as CEO" while denying knowledge of its cause. The company's early framing attributed the device to a small circle of engineers; the investigations that followed — internal, criminal, congressional — steadily widened that circle across years, functions and management layers, without ever producing a single signed order from the top. In the United States the consequences became personal in a way European executives had not expected: engineers and managers pleaded guilty or were convicted; one executive was arrested returning from holiday and served years in federal prison; Winterkorn himself was criminally charged in two countries, with proceedings in Germany delayed for years by his health.

The corporate bill kept compounding: criminal guilty pleas in the US, vehicle buy-backs, civil settlements, dealer compensation, investor litigation in Germany — a total conventionally given as more than thirty billion euros, alongside the unpriced items: the "Made in Germany" premium, the credibility of diesel itself, and the regulatory presumption of good faith for an entire industry, since parallel investigations pulled several other carmakers into emissions controversies of varying severity.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How was the fraud actually discovered, and why does the scale of the study matter?

2. Parse Winterkorn's resignation sentence: what does it accept and what does it refuse?

1. A tiny university road-test study — tens of thousands of dollars — found the lab-versus-road gap in 2014; regulators pressed for a year against technical explanations until the 2016-model certification threat forced the admission. The asymmetry is the point: a $70k study versus a multi-billion deception.

2. It accepts responsibility "as CEO" (institutional role) while denying awareness ("not aware") of wrongdoing "on my part" (personal scope) — accountability formally taken and personally declined in a single sentence.

Discuss:
  • The regulators were answered with recalibrations for over a year before the admission. At what moment did managing the problem become the cover-up — and who inside should have called it?

Part 3 — What "nobody decided" costs

The strangest fact in the record is the one every investigation circled: no memo, no meeting minute, no signed instruction from the top has ever surfaced ordering the device to exist. The picture assembled instead was of a machine for producing outcomes without decisions — impossible targets issued downward, unwelcome truths filtered out on the way up (the Nokia mechanism, with fraud as its output instead of delay), engineering pride that preferred a brilliant secret workaround to a public admission of limits, and a governance structure — controlling families, one German state, labour representatives, all inside the supervisory board — with every incentive to steer and none to interrogate. German corporate law's two-tier board, designed precisely to supervise management, watched the largest fraud in the country's industrial history happen underneath it.

What followed inside the company is, depending on the teller, redemption or reinvention under duress. Volkswagen pledged cultural reform, installed compliance machinery of American scale, and made a strategic swerve with consequences far beyond the scandal: partly under its US court settlement — which obliged it to fund electrification infrastructure — and partly as penance-turned-strategy, it threw itself into electric vehicles earlier and harder than any incumbent of its size. A reasonable argument, made seriously by analysts, is that Dieselgate accelerated the entire industry's electric transition by years. It is an odd monument: the scandal's most durable product may be the strategy that buries the engine that caused it.

The case's afterlife is the debate it refuses to close. One reading: a compliance failure — fix it with controls, hotlines, and personal criminal liability, as the US did. Another: a management-system failure — impossible targets plus punished honesty equals fraud, everywhere, always; the controls merely determine what shape it takes. The German post-mortem added a third: a governance failure — a board built for consensus among insiders cannot supervise anything the insiders all benefit from. The question the case hands every executive is the one no settlement priced: in your own organisation, today, what happens to the person who says "the target cannot be met" — and is your honest answer closer to Volkswagen's, or merely untested?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did investigators never find, and what picture emerged instead?

2. What is the scandal's paradoxical legacy?

1. Any top-down order. Instead: impossible targets, filtered reporting, a fear culture, and a supervisory board of aligned insiders — a system that produced the outcome without a traceable decision.

2. Partly compelled by its US settlement, VW pivoted to electric vehicles earlier and harder than peers — arguably accelerating the whole industry's transition. The fraud's most durable product may be the strategy that retires the diesel engine itself.

Discuss:
  • Compliance failure, management-system failure, or governance failure — rank the three readings by explanatory power, and say which one your own organisation is least protected against.

Grammar — passives & the language of accountability

Scandals are conducted in the passive voice. "Mistakes were made" is the most studied sentence in crisis communication because it is grammatically flawless and morally empty. Today you learn to build such sentences, to detect them, and — the real C1 skill — to reverse them.

How the passive deletes the agent:
Active: "Engineers installed the software." → Passive: "The software was installed." The doer is grammatically optional — and what is optional can be omitted.

The full evasion toolkit:
• Agentless passive: "Emissions were understated." By whom? The sentence doesn't say — and doesn't have to.
• Nominalisation — turning the deed into a thing: "the installation of the software", "a failure of communication". Things have no doers.
• Abstract subjects: "The company fell short." · "The culture allowed it." Institutions act; people vanish.
• Knowledge hedges: "I was not aware of…" — shifts the claim from the deed to the speaker's mind, which no investigation can search.
The accountability register — reversing every trick:
• Restore the agent: "Our engineers wrote it; our managers accepted it; I ran the company in which both were possible."
• De-nominalise: "a failure of communication" → "we did not tell the regulator the truth."
• Replace awareness with responsibility: "whether or not I knew, it happened on my watch, and I answer for it."

Legitimate passives exist — when the agent is unknown, obvious or irrelevant: "The study was published in 2014." The test is always: does the omission serve the reader, or the writer?

1. Detect the deletion

Each sentence hides its agent. Rewrite it in the active voice, supplying the most plausible agent. Write the full sentence.

1. "Regrettably, the test cycle was recognised by the engine software." →

2. "There was a failure to escalate concerns." →

3. "It has been acknowledged that targets may have been experienced as demanding." →

1. Our engineers wrote software that recognised the test cycle.

2. Managers who knew did not escalate their concerns — and their superiors made escalation dangerous.

3. The board set targets the engineers knew were impossible.

Notice how much information the passive versions were withholding — that information is exactly what accountability consists of.

2. Build both registers

Write each idea twice: once in full evasion, once in full accountability. Write both sentences.

1. IDEA: The company misled regulators for a year. Evasive: Accountable:

2. IDEA: The CEO's culture made honesty dangerous. Evasive: Accountable:

1. Evasive: "Questions from regulators were addressed over an extended period." Accountable: "For over a year, we gave regulators explanations we knew did not explain the gap."

2. Evasive: "The tone from the top may at times have been experienced as exacting." Accountable: "I led in a way that made it unsafe to tell me the truth."

Model answers. The evasive versions should feel eerily natural — that fluency is what this lesson is inoculating you against.

3. The two statements — complete the paragraph

Below is the same resignation, drafted twice. Fill the gaps: the first paragraph with evasion machinery, the second with accountability language. Read both aloud and feel the difference in your mouth.

DRAFT A: "Irregularities (find, passive) in the emissions software of certain vehicles. I am (aware, negative) of any wrongdoing my part. Nevertheless, responsibility (accept, passive) by me in my capacity as chief executive."

DRAFT B: "Our engineers (build) software to deceive regulators, and managers (let) it ship for nine years. I (run) the company in which that was possible, so the failure is , and I am resigning."

DRAFT A: have been found · not aware · on · is accepted

DRAFT B: built · let · ran · mine

Draft A is grammatically impeccable and was, in substance, what the world heard in September 2015. Draft B is what crisis-communication texts now teach — and almost nobody says.

Take both registers into the Speak tab — you will need to perform each, and to say which one you could actually deliver.

Going deeper

A note on the record: figures are rounded; criminal proceedings continued for years after the events and some remain unresolved; and statements about individuals reflect the public record of charges, pleas and convictions, not judgements this lesson is entitled to add.

What the case actually tests. First, the production of fraud without a decision. The investigations found no order because, plausibly, none was needed: a system that punishes "it cannot be done" and rewards "it is handled" will manufacture deception as reliably as any instruction, while leaving every individual a fragment small enough to live with. This is the Nokia filtering machine with the moral polarity reversed — there, truth was softened; here, it was replaced. The management lesson is uncomfortable precisely because it is systemic: audit not your people's honesty but your targets' possibility, and the career consequences of refusing one.

Second, the asymmetry of detection. Thirty billion euros of deception was undone by a road test costing less than a mid-range company car. In a world of cheap sensors, open data and motivated outsiders, the lab-versus-reality gap of any claim — emissions, ESG, uptime, safety — will eventually be measured by someone you don't control. The operating assumption "we control the test conditions" is now, everywhere, false.

Third, the language record. This case supplied crisis communication with its modern anti-textbook: the awareness hedge, the agentless admission, the responsibility-without-knowledge formula. The grammar tab's claim deserves stating plainly: the passive voice did not cause the fraud, but it is the instrument by which organisations narrate fraud to themselves while it is happening — "the target is being met" — and the discipline of forcing agents back into sentences is, at scale, a compliance control. Language audits are cheaper than emissions tests.

Talk it through:
  • "Audit the targets, not the people." Design the mechanism: how would an organisation systematically detect which of its targets are impossible — before its engineers privately decide?
  • The West Virginia study cost tens of thousands. What is the equivalent cheap external measurement of your organisation's central claim — and would you pass it today?
  • Compare the sentences: Winterkorn's "not aware of any wrongdoing on my part" and Snabe's "we were average" (Maersk). What did each cost, and what did each buy?
  • VW's punishment partly built its electric future. Is penance-as-strategy a redemption story or a moral hazard — and what would deterrence that actually deters look like?

Speak

All of it out loud, against the clock. Today's discipline: know at every moment whether you are speaking in the evasive register or the accountable one — and be doing it on purpose.

Useful language: the software was designed to… — and our engineers designed it · there was a failure to…, which is to say, we failed to… · whether or not anyone knew, it happened on our watch · the question isn't who ordered it; it's what made an order unnecessary

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

You are the incoming chief executive, months after your company's own falsification scandal, before a parliamentary committee. A member reads your predecessor's statement aloud and asks: "'Not aware of any wrongdoing on my part.' Your company has used the passive voice for eight months. Name one person, including yourself, who is accountable — with an active verb."

Answer in the accountable register throughout — agents named, nominalisations unpacked, no awareness hedges. Then compress it to ninety seconds without letting a single passive back in.

Task C · The impossible target (4 minutes)

The scene the whole case turns on, played twice. A leader has just assigned your team a target you know cannot be met honestly. Round one: push back in the meeting — state the impossibility with evidence, propose what is achievable, and hold the line under pressure. Round two: you are the leader receiving that pushback — respond in a way that keeps both the ambition and the honesty alive.

Prefer a listed scenario? Take one of these:

  • A sales team told to double revenue in a flat market without discounting.
  • A construction firm told to cut the schedule by a third with no safety trade-off.
  • A hospital told to halve waiting lists with the same staff.
  • A software team told to ship in six weeks what they estimate at six months.
