Yet evil exists. This seems to contradict Christian beliefs about God's nature. Theologians must explain how these three attributes can coexist with the reality of suffering.

Encounter: Christian Theodicies and Responses to Suffering

The Theodicy Problem: Why We Need Explanations

Since the earliest days of Christianity, believers have struggled with a painful question: If God is all-powerful, all-knowing, and wholly good, why does God allow suffering—especially innocent suffering? Throughout history, Christian theologians have developed theodicies: carefully reasoned arguments attempting to reconcile God's power and goodness with the reality of evil. These aren't just abstract ideas; they're responses to real human pain and loss. They emerge from the deepest human questions about God's justice.

Augustine's Theodicy: Evil as Privation of Good

Augustine (354-430)

Augustine, an early Church father and theologian, developed the most influential classical theodicy in Christianity. Born in North Africa, he struggled with the existence of evil and eventually concluded that evil is not a "thing" created by God, but rather the absence of good—a privation.

The Privatio Boni Explanation

Augustine argued that "evil" is not a substance created by God. Instead, evil is the absence of good, just as darkness is the absence of light or cold is the absence of heat. When a good creation (like human will) is deprived of good, evil results. This means God did not create evil; God created only what is good. Evil emerges when creation falls away from God's goodness.

Privatio Boni: Latin for "privation of good." Augustine's theory that evil is not a created thing but the absence, loss, or corruption of good. A rotten apple is good wood that has been deprived of its life-giving properties. A liar's words are true words deprived of truthfulness.

Augustine's Explanation: The Fall and Original Sin

For Augustine, the origin of moral evil lies in the Fall of Adam and Eve. In the Garden of Eden, humans were created with free will and in a state of grace (perfect relationship with God). When Adam and Eve sinned—choosing their own will over God's—they introduced disobedience into creation. This Original Sin corrupted human nature itself, creating a legacy of sinfulness that affects all humanity. Natural evil (suffering from disease, disaster) results from the corruption of creation caused by Original Sin, not from God's direct action.

"When Adam sinned, sin entered the world. Adam's sin brought death, so death spread to everyone, for everyone sinned." (Romans 5:12) Romans 5:12

Augustine's solution is logically elegant: God created a world of perfect goodness and human free will. Humans freely chose sin, which corrupted creation and introduced suffering. God did not create evil; humans caused it. This protects God's omnipotence, omniscience, and goodness while explaining why evil exists.

Consider: If all suffering ultimately traces back to Original Sin, how does this answer the problem of innocent suffering? Why do children suffer from disease? Why do animals suffer? Did they commit sin?

Irenaeus' Theodicy: Suffering as Soul-Making

Irenaeus (c. 130-200 AD)

Irenaeus, an early Christian theologian, offered a strikingly different explanation. He suggested that God created humans not as finished products but as developing creatures. Humans begin in childhood—immature in spirit and understanding—and must grow toward spiritual perfection through challenge and experience.

The Soul-Making Theodicy

For Irenaeus, suffering is not primarily a punishment for sin, but a necessary condition for spiritual development. Just as muscles develop through resistance and exercise, and character develops through facing challenges, human souls develop morally and spiritually through struggle. Without hardship, virtues like courage, compassion, and perseverance could never emerge.

In Irenaeus' view, a world without suffering would be ethically barren. Consider: Can you show compassion if no one suffers? Can you show courage without risk or danger? Can you develop patience without frustration? Can you learn forgiveness without wrongdoing? A painless world would be a world of moral stunting, where humans never grow or develop virtue.

Soul-Making Theodicy: The theological explanation that suffering is necessary for moral and spiritual development. God permits suffering not as punishment, but as the condition for humans to develop virtues, character, and a deeper relationship with God.

Irenaeus taught that God's plan spans all eternity. In this life, humans face suffering that refines and deepens their souls. Suffering is God's tool for shaping human character, not evidence of God's absence or powerlessness. The eschatological hope—belief in ultimate redemption and perfection in the next life—makes sense of suffering now as part of a larger divine purpose.

Critique: Does this explanation work for all suffering? Is a child's terminal illness "soul-making"? Is genocide a tool for spiritual development? Can this theodicy seem callous when applied to extreme suffering?

The Free Will Defence: Humans, Not God, Cause Moral Evil

A third Christian response focuses on human free will. God, the argument goes, created humans with genuine freedom—the real ability to choose good or evil. A world where humans could only do good would be a world without moral choice at all; we would be puppets, not free agents. God values human freedom so highly that God permits us to cause suffering through our choices.

This explains moral evil (suffering caused by human cruelty, violence, negligence) but not natural evil. If a hurricane kills thousands, free will doesn't explain why God permits it. However, some theologians argue that natural evil results from living in a universe with consistent physical laws. A universe governed by reliable laws (gravity, weather patterns) allows for human science and planning, but those same laws can cause harm. A world with no consistent laws would be chaotic and unknowable.

The Logic of the Free Will Defence:

  • God values human moral freedom genuinely
  • Genuine freedom means the real possibility of wrong choice
  • If God made it impossible to do wrong, freedom would be illusory
  • Therefore, God permits moral evil as the cost of real human freedom
  • This preserves God's goodness: limiting freedom would be less good

Job: God's Answer from the Whirlwind

The Book of Job offers perhaps the most profound biblical response to suffering. Job is a righteous man who loses everything—his wealth, his children, his health. His friends insist his suffering must be punishment for sin, following the logic of cause-and-effect morality. But Job knows he hasn't sinned and refuses to accept easy explanations.

After pages of argument, God appears to Job "from the whirlwind" not with explanation but with a response: God points to the vastness, complexity, and beauty of creation. God asks Job: "Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? Have you ever commanded the morning?" The implicit answer is: You cannot understand the full picture. God's ways are beyond human comprehension. We see suffering as meaningless; God sees the full tapestry of creation and history.

"Then the Lord answered Job from the whirlwind: 'Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? Have you entered the springs of the sea, or walked in the recesses of the deep?'" (Job 38:1-4) Job 38:1-4

The Book of Job doesn't solve the logical problem of evil with a neat argument. Instead, it shifts perspective: rather than demanding that God answer for suffering, Job comes to trust God despite not understanding. This foreshadows a Christian response that emphasises faith and relationship over intellectual certainty.

Jesus's Suffering on the Cross: God Understands

The most distinctly Christian response to suffering is the Incarnation and the cross. Christians believe Jesus Christ is God in human form, and that Jesus experienced genuine suffering—physical torture, emotional anguish, abandonment, and death. This is crucial: the God of Christianity is not distant from human suffering. God does not ask humans to endure what God will not endure.

The cross declares: "God understands suffering from the inside." Jesus knew hunger, betrayal, pain, and death. In Christian theology, the Incarnation answers the problem of evil not with an argument but with presence. God didn't send an explanation from heaven; God came into the world and suffered alongside us. As the Apostle Paul wrote in 1 Peter: "For to this you have been called, because Christ also suffered for you, leaving you an example, so that you should follow in his steps."

"For to this you have been called, because Christ also suffered for you, leaving you an example, so that you should follow in his steps." (1 Peter 2:21) 1 Peter 2:21

Furthermore, many Christians understand Christ's resurrection as God's ultimate triumph over suffering and death. Jesus rose from the dead, transforming the deepest human tragedy (death) into the foundation of hope. For Christians, the cross and resurrection declare that suffering is not the final word in human history.

Modern Christian Responses: The Holocaust and Beyond

The classical theodicies of Augustine and Irenaeus face a crisis in the 20th century, particularly in response to the Holocaust—the Nazi genocide of six million Jews, along with millions of others. How can soul-making explain the systematic murder of children? How can free will defence explain the scale of industrial-scale horror? How can we say God allowed this for moral development?

Modern theologians have developed new responses:

Process Theology

Some theologians argue that God is powerful but not omnipotent—God persuades creation toward good but cannot unilaterally control events. This redefines divine power but struggles with Christian tradition that emphasises God's ultimate sovereignty.

Lament and Trust

Others emphasise that Christianity offers not a logical solution but a relationship. Even if we cannot understand why God permits suffering, we can trust God through faith. The Psalms themselves cry out in pain and confusion, showing that doubt and lament are part of Christian tradition.

Protest Theodicy

Some theologians argue we should protest suffering while maintaining faith in God. To accept suffering passively is wrong; we should rage against injustice even as we trust God. Faith doesn't mean passive acceptance.

Eschatological Hope

Others emphasise that God's full answer to suffering is not found in this world but in the future resurrection and new creation. We live in the "already-but-not-yet"—Christ has already overcome death, but final redemption is still to come.

Key Insight: Modern Christian theology recognises that no single explanation satisfies all suffering. Instead, Christians often hold multiple responses together: trusting God's goodness, recognising human freedom, acknowledging suffering's role in spiritual growth, taking comfort in Christ's presence, and maintaining hope in ultimate resurrection and redemption.

Comparing the Three Theodicies

How do these three Christian responses differ in their understanding of suffering, God's role, and human responsibility?

What is Suffering?

Augustine: A consequence of sin; corruption of good creation by human disobedience.

Irenaeus: A necessary condition for moral development; God's tool for spiritual growth.

Free Will: The result of either human choice (moral evil) or consistent natural laws (natural evil).

God's Role

Augustine: God created goodness; human sin introduced evil. God permits suffering as consequence of fallen nature.

Irenaeus: God permits suffering intentionally as part of divine plan for human development and character formation.

Free Will: God permits suffering to protect human moral freedom. God doesn't intervene to prevent human choices.

Human Responsibility

Augustine: Humans bear responsibility for Original Sin; all suffer as inheritors of Adam's disobedience.

Irenaeus: Humans have responsibility to grow and develop; suffering is the opportunity for moral maturity.

Free Will: Humans are fully responsible for moral evil; we have genuine choice and agency.

Problem Areas

Augustine: Struggles to explain innocent suffering; natural evil seems unrelated to human sin.

Irenaeus: Struggles with extreme suffering (Holocaust); seems insensitive to claim systematic murder develops souls.

Free Will: Struggles to explain natural evil and innocent suffering from uncontrollable causes.

Evaluate: Can Christian Theodicies Really Answer the Problem of Evil?

The Debate: Do These Explanations Work?

Christian theologians and philosophers have debated for nearly 2,000 years whether theodicies genuinely solve the problem of evil. Some argue that Augustine, Irenaeus, and others have provided compelling responses that protect God's goodness. Others argue that no theodicy can justify extreme suffering, especially innocent suffering, and that theodicies sometimes fail to take human pain seriously enough.

Consider both perspectives carefully:

Argument 1: Christian Theodicies Successfully Explain God's Goodness

Christian responses to suffering offer genuinely helpful explanations. Augustine's insight that evil is the absence of good (not something God created) is logically elegant and shows that God bears no responsibility for evil's existence. Irenaeus' soul-making theodicy explains why a world with some suffering is better than a world of moral stagnation. The free will defence shows why genuine moral freedom requires the real possibility of evil. Together, these responses show that God's goodness is consistent with the existence of suffering. Moreover, Christ's suffering on the cross reveals that God is not distant from pain but enters into it. For many believers, these explanations provide intellectual coherence and spiritual comfort.

Argument 2: Christian Theodicies Fail to Address Extreme Suffering

The classical theodicies seem hollow when confronted with real atrocity. Can we truly say the Holocaust developed human souls? That children dying of leukaemia are achieving moral growth? That the enslavement of millions serves divine purpose? Critics argue that theodicies can sound callous and evasive. They prioritise protecting God's reputation over acknowledging genuine human pain. Furthermore, some theodicies seem to blame victims: if all suffering comes from Original Sin, does this mean the severely disabled person somehow "deserves" their condition? Modern atheist philosophers argue that theodicies are ultimately attempts to rationalise the irrational, and that honest inquiry should abandon belief in an all-good, all-powerful God rather than twist logic to defend that belief.

Key Terms Summary

Theodicy
An attempt to justify God's goodness and power in the face of evil. A reasoned argument explaining how an all-powerful, all-knowing, all-good God can permit suffering.
Privatio Boni
"Privation of good." Augustine's theory that evil is not a created substance but the absence, loss, or corruption of good—like darkness is the absence of light.
Original Sin
The biblical concept that Adam and Eve's disobedience to God in the Garden of Eden introduced sin into human nature, affecting all descendants. Augustine traced all human suffering to this event.
The Fall
The narrative in Genesis describing how Adam and Eve, given free will, chose to disobey God, resulting in their expulsion from Paradise and the introduction of suffering into creation.
Soul-Making Theodicy
Irenaeus' explanation that suffering is necessary for moral and spiritual development. God permits suffering so that humans can develop virtue, character, and a deeper relationship with God.
Free Will Defence
The theological argument that God permits suffering to protect genuine human moral freedom. True freedom requires the real possibility of wrong choice, which means the possibility of evil and suffering.
Natural Evil
Suffering caused by natural forces not directly involving human choice: earthquakes, disease, hurricanes, death from accident or illness.
Moral Evil
Suffering caused by human choices and wrongdoing: war, cruelty, theft, violence, injustice, betrayal.
Incarnation
Christian doctrine that God took human form in Jesus Christ. God became fully human while remaining fully divine, entering into human experience including suffering and death.
The Problem of Evil
The fundamental theological challenge: If God is omnipotent (all-powerful), omniscient (all-knowing), and omnibenevolent (all-good), why does evil and suffering exist? How can these attributes coexist with evil?
Omnipotent
All-powerful; capable of doing anything. A traditional attribute of God in Christian theology.
Omniscient
All-knowing; aware of everything including past, present, and future. A traditional attribute of God in Christian theology.
Central Insight from This Lesson: Christianity has developed multiple, sometimes conflicting responses to suffering. Augustine emphasised that evil is a corruption of creation caused by human sin, not something God created. Irenaeus argued that suffering is necessary for spiritual development—God uses hardship to shape human character. The free will defence shows that genuine freedom requires the real possibility of evil. The Book of Job teaches that God's ways exceed human understanding, and faith matters more than intellectual certainty. Most distinctly Christian is the response that God became human in Jesus and suffered alongside us—not solving evil with an argument, but entering into human pain. No single explanation satisfies all suffering, but together these responses show Christianity's sustained attempt to maintain faith in God's goodness while taking human suffering seriously.
