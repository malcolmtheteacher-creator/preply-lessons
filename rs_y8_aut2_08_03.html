Question 1: Define "theodicy" and explain the difference between natural evil and moral evil.

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: A theodicy is an attempt to justify God's goodness and power in the face of evil. Natural evil includes suffering from natural causes (earthquakes, disease, storms) not directly caused by human choice. Moral evil is suffering caused by human choice and wrongdoing (war, theft, cruelty). Understanding this distinction is key because it affects whether we blame God, nature, or human free will for suffering.

Question 2: What is the logical problem of evil, and why does it challenge Christian belief?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: The logical problem of evil argues that if God is omnipotent (all-powerful), omniscient (all-knowing), and omnibenevolent (all-good), then God would be able to prevent evil, would know about all suffering, and would want to stop it. Yet evil exists. This seems to contradict Christian beliefs about God's nature. Theologians must explain how these three attributes can coexist with the reality of suffering.

Question 3: Why is the problem of evil especially difficult in Christianity compared to other religions?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Christianity emphasises God as a personal, loving creator who cares about each individual. Christians pray to God for help and believe God listens. This makes the existence of undeserved suffering especially troubling—if God genuinely loves us and has the power to help, why doesn't God intervene? Other religions may see the divine as less personal or more distant, making the problem feel less acute.

Encounter: Christian Theodicies and Responses to Suffering

The Theodicy Problem: Why We Need Explanations

Since the earliest days of Christianity, believers have struggled with a painful question: If God is all-powerful, all-knowing, and wholly good, why does God allow suffering—especially innocent suffering? Throughout history, Christian theologians have developed theodicies: carefully reasoned arguments attempting to reconcile God's power and goodness with the reality of evil. These aren't just abstract ideas; they're responses to real human pain and loss. They emerge from the deepest human questions about God's justice.

Augustine's Theodicy: Evil as Privation of Good

Augustine (354-430)

Augustine was one of Christianity's most influential theologians. His theodicy emerged partly from his own spiritual struggles and his attempt to reconcile Christian faith with Platonist philosophy.

Core Idea: Evil as Absence, Not Creation

Augustine argued that God didn't create evil. Instead, evil is a privation (absence or lack) of good. Just as darkness isn't a thing itself but the absence of light, evil isn't a created substance but a turning away from God's goodness. God created a good world; evil arises when creatures misuse their free will.

Key Quote

"Evil has no positive nature; but the loss of good has received the name 'evil.'"

— Augustine

Irenaeus's Theodicy: Soul-Making and Spiritual Development

Irenaeus (c. 130-202) offers a different approach. Rather than seeing suffering as a consequence of sin alone, he viewed it as essential for human moral and spiritual growth. The world isn't perfect because humans need to grow into perfection through struggle.

The Soul-Making Defence: Just as an athlete grows strong through training or an artist develops skill through practice, humans develop virtue, compassion, courage, and faith through facing challenges and suffering. Without suffering, these character traits couldn't develop. A world without danger, loss, or pain would be a world where moral and spiritual growth is impossible.

Augustine's View

Focus: Explaining the origin of evil

Key Point: Evil comes from misused freedom, not from God

Problem: Doesn't fully explain innocent suffering or natural disasters

Irenaeus's View

Focus: Understanding the purpose of suffering

Key Point: Suffering develops human character and faith

Problem: Seems to justify unnecessary pain; doesn't address extreme suffering

The Free Will Defence

One of the most common responses to the problem of evil is the free will defence. This argument states:

  1. God created humans with genuine free will—the ability to choose right or wrong
  2. Genuine freedom means the real possibility of choosing evil
  3. A world where everyone always chooses good is a world without real freedom
  4. A world with free creatures who sometimes choose evil is better than a world of puppets who always do good
  5. Therefore, God allows moral evil to preserve human freedom

Challenge: This explains moral evil (caused by human choice) but struggles to explain natural evil (earthquakes, diseases, genetic disorders) that have nothing to do with human freedom.

The Book of Job: Mystery and Faith Beyond Explanation

"Then the LORD spoke to Job out of the storm... 'Where were you when I laid the earth's foundation?... Have you journeyed to the springs of the sea or walked in the recesses of the deep?... Do you know when the mountain goats give birth?'" Job 38:1-4, 38

The Book of Job approaches suffering differently from theological argument. Job is a righteous man who suffers terribly and demands an explanation from God. His friends offer various theodicies—punishment for sin, the need for testing, God's hidden purposes. Job rejects all these explanations.

When God finally responds, God doesn't offer an explanation or theodicy. Instead, God points to the vastness and mystery of creation, essentially saying: "You don't have the wisdom to understand why things happen as they do. Trust me anyway." Job's response is acceptance not because he understands, but because he encounters God personally. The lesson: faith may matter more than intellectual certainty about suffering.

Analysing the Strengths and Limitations

Augustine's Strengths

  • Explains moral evil clearly (humans choose wrongly)
  • Protects God's goodness by denying God creates evil
  • Influences much Christian thought on sin

Augustine's Limitations

  • Struggles with natural evil (disease, disaster)
  • Relies on the concept of privation, which is philosophically complex
  • Doesn't address innocent suffering well

Irenaeus's Strengths

  • Explains why a loving God might allow suffering
  • Values human growth and development
  • Accounts for both moral and natural evil

Irenaeus's Limitations

  • Seems to justify extreme and unnecessary suffering
  • Some suffering appears pointless (childhood cancer)
  • Children who die young cannot develop spiritually

The Problem with All Theodicies

Philosophers distinguish between two aspects of the problem of evil:

1. The Logical Problem: Is it logically possible for an all-powerful, all-knowing, all-good God to coexist with evil? Most philosophers now agree this is possible (theodicies provide ways to reconcile them).

2. The Evidential Problem: Given the amount and severity of suffering in the world, is it reasonable to believe in such a God? This is harder to answer. Even if some suffering is justified, the sheer scale of unnecessary pain in the world challenges faith.

No single theodicy fully satisfies all doubts. This is why Christianity has developed multiple responses—each capturing part of the truth.

Apply: Jesus and the Cross as God's Response to Suffering

Beyond Explanation: God's Presence in Suffering

Christianity offers something more than a logical explanation of suffering—it offers a story of God entering into human pain. Jesus's incarnation and crucifixion present a unique response to suffering: not a solution that makes sense of it, but a divine presence within it.

Jesus's Suffering on the Cross

The Gospel Account

All four Gospels narrate Jesus's arrest, trial, and execution in detail. Jesus is betrayed, beaten, mocked, and crucified—an agonising death reserved for criminals. Even on the cross, Jesus experiences doubt and pain: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34)

Theological Significance

Jesus doesn't remain distant from suffering. God becomes human, experiences injustice, torture, and death. Through the cross, suffering is placed at the very centre of Christian faith. God doesn't explain suffering from a distance—God suffers alongside us.

"For we do not have a high priest who is unable to empathise with our weaknesses, but we have one who has been tempted in every way, just as we are—yet he did not sin." Hebrews 4:15

The Atonement: Redemptive Suffering

Christian theology interprets Jesus's death as redemptive. Multiple models explain how:

  • Substitutionary Atonement: Jesus dies in our place, bearing the punishment for sin
  • Moral Influence: Jesus's sacrificial love moves us to repentance and transformation
  • Ransom Theory: Jesus's death buys humanity's freedom from sin and death
  • Victory Theory: The cross and resurrection defeat evil and death

In each model, suffering becomes redemptive—meaningful not because it's explained away, but because it effects salvation and reconciliation.

Evaluate: Which Response Is Most Convincing?

Evaluating the Theodicies

No theodicy is universally accepted. Each has strengths and weaknesses. Your task is to think critically about which response to suffering you find most persuasive—and why.

Evaluation Criteria

Logical Coherence

Does the theodicy avoid logical contradictions? Can an all-powerful, all-knowing, all-good God coexist with this kind of evil?

Empirical Adequacy

Does it account for the suffering we actually observe? Does it explain childhood disease, natural disasters, and extreme injustice?

Existential Comfort

Does it help people cope with real suffering? Does it provide meaning or hope in times of crisis?

Theological Faithfulness

Does it remain true to Christian scripture and tradition? Does it reflect how Christians throughout history have understood faith?

Case Studies: Testing the Theodicies

Case 1: A Child with Cancer

A five-year-old dies of leukaemia after 18 months of painful treatment. She never did anything to deserve this. How would each theodicy respond?

  • Augustine: This is natural evil, not created by God, but it's hard to explain why God permits it
  • Irenaeus: She may not develop spiritually, so this doesn't fit the soul-making model well
  • Job's Response: We may never understand, but God is still trustworthy
  • Jesus's Suffering: God is present in her pain and suffering alongside her

Case 2: War and Genocide

Millions die in violent conflict. Some is caused by human choice (moral evil), some by circumstance (natural evil). How do the theodicies address this?

  • Augustine: Human free will explains moral evil, but not why God allows such large-scale suffering
  • Irenaeus: Suggests survivors and witnesses develop compassion, but doesn't justify the victims' suffering
  • Job's Response: Humility about human understanding, trust in God's justice
  • Jesus's Suffering: God's solidarity with victims, hope for resurrection and restoration

Review: Key Terms and Insights

Key Vocabulary

Theodicy
A systematic attempt to reconcile God's power and goodness with the existence of evil and suffering. From Greek "theos" (God) and "dike" (justice).
Problem of Evil
The theological and philosophical challenge: How can an all-powerful, all-knowing, all-good God permit evil and suffering?
Natural Evil
Suffering caused by natural processes (earthquakes, diseases, accidents) rather than human action.
Moral Evil
Suffering caused by human choice and wrongdoing (crime, war, cruelty).
Privation of Good
Augustine's concept: evil is not a created substance but an absence or lack of good, like darkness is the absence of light.
Soul-Making Theodicy
Irenaeus's theory that suffering and challenges are necessary for human spiritual and moral development.
Free Will Defence
The argument that God permits evil because genuine human freedom requires the real possibility of choosing wrongly.
Omnipotent
All-powerful; having unlimited power. A traditional attribute of God in Christian theology.
Omniscient
All-knowing; aware of everything including past, present, and future. A traditional attribute of God in Christian theology.
Central Insight from This Lesson: Christianity has developed multiple, sometimes conflicting responses to suffering. Augustine emphasised that evil is a corruption of creation caused by human sin, not something God created. Irenaeus argued that suffering is necessary for spiritual development—God uses hardship to shape human character. The free will defence shows that genuine freedom requires the real possibility of evil. The Book of Job teaches that God's ways exceed human understanding, and faith matters more than intellectual certainty. Most distinctly Christian is the response that God became human in Jesus and suffered alongside us—not solving evil with an argument, but entering into human pain. No single explanation satisfies all suffering, but together these responses show Christianity's sustained attempt to maintain faith in God's goodness while taking human suffering seriously.
