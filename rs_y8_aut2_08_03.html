Christian Responses to Suffering

YEAR 8 · UNIT 2: THE PROBLEM OF EVIL

What You'll Learn

You will explore three major Christian responses to suffering: the free will defence, soul-making theodicy, and the redemptive meaning of Christ's suffering.

Think: Review the Inconsistent Triad

From Lesson 2:

We learned about the logical problem of evil. Let's remind ourselves of the three statements Christians must reconcile:

Question: State the inconsistent triad.

🔒Try to answer from memory!

Answer: (1) God is all-powerful, (2) God is all-good, (3) Evil exists. These seem logically impossible to hold together.

Now let's see how Christianity answers this challenge. Christian theodicies don't deny that God is all-powerful or all-good. Instead, they offer reasons why an all-powerful, all-good God allows suffering.

Encounter: Three Christian Responses

1. The Free Will Defence

This is perhaps the most common Christian response. The argument goes like this:

God created humans with genuine free will—the real ability to choose good or evil. Real freedom means the possibility of choosing wrongly. God could have created robots who always choose good, but that wouldn't be true freedom or genuine love. A world with free beings, some of whom choose evil, is better than a world of programmed obedience.

Therefore: Moral evil (suffering caused by human choice) is a necessary consequence of genuine human freedom. God is not responsible for moral evil; humans are.

2. Soul-Making Theodicy (Irenaeus)

This theodicy, developed by the ancient theologian Irenaeus, suggests a different purpose for suffering:

God created humans not as perfect beings, but as souls in development. Suffering, struggle, and difficulty are necessary for spiritual growth. Just as muscles develop through resistance, souls develop through facing challenges. Virtues like courage, compassion, patience, and faith cannot be developed in a world without suffering and difficulty.

Therefore: Natural evil and difficulty serve a purpose: they allow us to develop into the people God wishes us to become. Suffering can be redemptive and transformative.

3. The Redemptive Meaning of Christ's Suffering

Christians find a unique answer in Jesus's own suffering and death:

God did not remain distant from human suffering. In Jesus Christ, God entered into suffering, experiencing pain, injustice, and death. Jesus's suffering was redemptive—it provided salvation for humanity. Moreover, Christians believe that through Christ's resurrection, suffering and death were ultimately defeated. This gives Christian suffering a meaning it would not otherwise have.

Therefore: Christians can view their suffering as participating in Christ's redemptive work, and can trust in God's ultimate triumph over suffering and death through resurrection.

Explore: The Book of Job

The Bible's most profound exploration of suffering is the Book of Job. It's a story many Christians turn to when struggling with the problem of evil.

Job is a righteous man who suffers terribly: his children die, his property is destroyed, his body is covered in painful sores. Job's friends insist he must have sinned to deserve this punishment. But Job protests his innocence. He demands to know why God allows this injustice.

God's response is remarkable. God does not explain or justify the suffering. Instead, God speaks of the grandeur, complexity, and mystery of creation. God essentially says: "You are too small to understand. Trust me."

The book's message: Sometimes there are no rational answers to suffering. Sometimes we must accept that God's wisdom is beyond our understanding, and trust in God's goodness even when we cannot see the reason for our pain.

Strengths and Challenges of Christian Responses

Strengths

  • Free will preserves human dignity and responsibility
  • Soul-making explains purpose in suffering
  • Christ's suffering resonates with real human experience
  • Offers comfort and meaning

Challenges

  • Does free will really justify children's suffering?
  • Why must God use suffering for soul-making?
  • Does Christ's suffering really solve the problem?
  • Many suffer innocently without "choosing" evil

Apply: Evaluate Christian Arguments

Scenario 1: An innocent child with cancer

A 6-year-old has terminal cancer. She has done nothing to cause this suffering. Which Christian theodicy best explains this? Does free will defence work? Does soul-making fit? What would a Christian say?

Scenario 2: A natural disaster

An earthquake kills thousands of people, many of them children. The free will defence cannot explain this natural evil. How would a Christian respond? Is soul-making theodicy convincing?

Scenario 3: Personal growth through suffering

Can you think of times when difficulty or hardship taught you something important? Does this experience support the soul-making theodicy? Does it fully justify the existence of suffering?

Challenge: Is there a type of suffering that none of the three Christian theodicies can adequately explain?

Evaluate: Is the Christian Answer Convincing?

Why Some Find These Theodicies Compelling

Many Christians find these responses meaningful because they preserve God's omnipotence and omnibenevolence while respecting human freedom. They also offer ways to find meaning and growth in suffering, rather than viewing it as purely destructive.

Why Some Remain Unconvinced

Critics argue that even free will doesn't justify innocent suffering, especially of children or animals who haven't made moral choices. They ask: couldn't God create a world where people are free but rarely choose evil? Or why didn't God create a world with less natural evil? The Job approach of saying "God's wisdom is beyond understanding" feels unsatisfying to those seeking rational answers.

Your reflection:

Does one of these Christian theodicies convince you? Why or why not? What would a more satisfying answer to the problem of evil look like to you?

Review: Key Concepts

The Free Will Defence

God gave humans genuine freedom. Moral evil is the consequence of free creatures choosing wrongly. God is not responsible for moral evil; humans are.

Soul-Making Theodicy (Irenaeus)

Humans are souls in development. Suffering and difficulty are necessary for spiritual growth, allowing us to develop virtues and become the people God intends.

Redemptive Meaning Through Christ

God entered into human suffering through Jesus Christ. Christ's suffering was redemptive, and his resurrection defeated suffering and death. Christians can find meaning in their suffering by participating in Christ's redemptive work.

The Book of Job: When faced with inexplicable suffering, Job learns that God's wisdom is beyond human understanding. Sometimes faith means trusting in God despite not understanding why suffering exists.
