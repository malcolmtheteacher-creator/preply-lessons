Person, Action, Object

A film director with a cast of a hundred and a million possible scenes. Six digits become one picture.

The film set

Imagine you are the assistant director on a film set, and the director has a strange rule. Every scene must contain exactly three things: a person, something that person does, and something they do it with. A woman paints a wall with a feather. A knight kisses a goat with a trumpet. A soldier cooks a pie in a bathtub.

The director shouts a number at the start of each scene, and your job is to remember all the scenes in order. The first number is 341526. Without thinking, the cast takes its places.

What three things must every scene contain?
A person, an action, and an object.
Talk about it. Invent a scene with a person, an action and an object that has nothing to do with each other. Describe it.

The reason the film works is that a scene has just three ingredients, but there are so many possible combinations. If there are a hundred people on the cast list, a hundred actions and a hundred objects, then the director can make a million different scenes, and no two are alike.

In this story every scene corresponds to six digits. The first two digits choose the person, the next two choose the action, and the last two choose the object. 34 is Charles Darwin. 15 is a certain action, 26 is a certain object. And so the scene for 341526 is Darwin, doing the action that belongs to 15, with the object that belongs to 26.

How many different scenes can the cast of a hundred people, a hundred actions and a hundred objects make?
A million: 100 × 100 × 100.
Talk about it. Why would a million possible scenes be useful to a memory athlete?

That is the idea of the PAO system: Person, Action, Object. It grew out of the Dominic System of the last lesson, which used person and action only, and it is used by many memory champions in competitions. One scene holds six digits, so a twelve-digit number needs just two pictures.

You will need to build the cast list yourself: a hundred people, each with one action and one object. It takes some work. But once it is built, you have a piece of equipment you can use all your life.

How is PAO related to the Dominic System?
Dominic uses a person and an action; PAO adds a third element, the object, so each picture holds six digits.
Talk about it. How long do you think it would take to build a list of a hundred people, actions and objects? What would help?

Three entries for every pair

Every two-digit pair, 00 to 99, has three entries: a person, an action and an object. You learn them once. Then any six digits become one scene.

  1. Split the number into pairs of digits. 341526 → 34 | 15 | 26.
  2. Take the PERSON of the first pair. 34 → Charles Darwin.
  3. Take the ACTION of the second pair. 15 → the action of Albert Einstein: writing on a blackboard.
  4. Take the OBJECT of the third pair. 26 → the object of Bruce Springsteen: a guitar.
  5. Put them in one scene. Darwin is writing on a blackboard, with a guitar for a piece of chalk.

Building your own list

  • Person: use the Dominic initials (digits to letters). Choose people who are easy to see: film stars, sports people, family, friends.
  • Action: choose the thing they are best known for. Make it big and physical.
  • Object: choose something you can see them holding. Try not to use the same object twice.
  • Keep entries different. If two people use a guitar, one of them will leak into the other.
  • Start with a few. Build ten pairs a day and test yourself.

Decoding works the other way. You see Darwin writing on a blackboard with a guitar: Darwin is 34, the action is Einstein's (15), the object is Springsteen's (26). So 341526.

Odd lengths. If the number does not divide by six, finish with a smaller scene: two digits = a person, four digits = person + action.
