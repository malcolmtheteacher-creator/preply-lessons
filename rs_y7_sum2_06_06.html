Suggested Answers

Sources of moral authority: Sacred texts, religious leaders, conscience, reason, law, family/culture, empathy, personal experience

Absolute vs Relative: Absolute = right is ALWAYS right, wrong is ALWAYS wrong. Relative = morality depends on circumstances and context.

Golden Rule examples: Christian: "Love your neighbour as yourself." Jewish: "Don't do to others what you wouldn't want done to you." Islamic: "Love for your brother what you love for yourself." Buddhist: "Don't treat others in ways you wouldn't want to be treated."

Key concepts: Jewish = pikuach nefesh (life overrides law). Christian = Greatest Commandment (love God, love neighbour). Islamic = Shariah (Islamic law from Qur'an, Hadith, Ijma, Qiyas). Sikh = sewa (selfless service).

Pikuach nefesh: Saving a life overrides almost every other Jewish law. Shows ethics is not blind rule-following but wisdom about competing values.

Recall from Earlier Lessons

From Lesson 33 (Afterlife Assessment): What do Christians believe happens after death, and how might that belief affect their ethical choices?

Resurrection and eternal life (heaven or hell). Belief in judgment encourages ethical living—you'll answer to God for your actions.

From Lesson 29 (Buddhist Rebirth and Humanist Views): What do Buddhists believe about karma and ethics?

Actions have consequences; good actions lead to better rebirths, bad actions to worse. Karma connects ethics to cosmic consequences.

Comparison Grid: Ethics Across Traditions

Click "Reveal Comparison" to see how traditions approach morality differently.

Tradition Primary Authority Core Principle How Ethics Shape Life
Judaism Torah + Halakhah (law) Pikuach nefesh (life overrides rules); Tikkun olam (repair world); Tzedakah (obligation to give) Comprehensive: covers food, business, family, justice, rest
Christianity Jesus's teaching + love Greatest Commandment: love God, love neighbour (including enemies) Transformation: love and grace reshape character and society
Islam Qur'an + Hadith + Ijma + Qiyas Shariah (Islamic law): 5 categories (fard, mustahabb, mubah, makruh, haram); Zakah (obligatory charity) Comprehensive: law governs all daily actions, speech, behaviour
Sikhism Guru Granth Sahib + Nama Japna, Kirat, Vand Sewa (selfless service); equality; standing against oppression Action: serving others, challenging injustice, sharing wealth
Buddhism Buddha's teaching + karma Noble Eightfold Path; reducing suffering; karma connects ethics to consequences Personal discipline: ethical behaviour leads to better rebirth and eventual Nirvana
Humanism Reason + empathy + human wellbeing No God needed; ethics based on shared humanity and reducing harm Community: building just, compassionate society through reason and action

Key Patterns to Notice

  • All traditions teach something like the Golden Rule. Respect for others is universal.
  • All see ethics as connected to something bigger: God, karma, reason, human dignity, spiritual growth.
  • Approaches differ: Law-based (Judaism, Islam) vs Love-based (Christianity) vs Action-based (Sikhism) vs Consequence-based (Buddhism) vs Reason-based (Humanism).
  • All require action, not just belief. You must live ethically, not just think it.

Quick-Fire Knowledge Test: 10 Questions

Test your knowledge. Click to reveal answers after each question.

Q1: What is morality?

Principles about right and wrong; beliefs about how people should behave.

Q2: Name three sources of moral authority.

Sacred texts, religious leaders, conscience, reason, law, family/culture, empathy (any three)

Q3: What is the Golden Rule?

Treat others as you'd want to be treated. It appears in nearly every worldview.

Q4: What are the Ten Commandments?

God's core laws: 1-4 about God (monotheism, no idols, respect God's name, Sabbath); 5-10 about people (honour parents, no murder, no adultery, no theft, no false witness, no coveting)

Q5: What is pikuach nefesh?

Jewish principle: saving a life overrides almost every other law.

Q6: What is the Greatest Commandment in Christianity?

Love God with all your heart, soul, mind. Love your neighbour as yourself.

Q7: What does "Shariah" mean?

Islamic law; literally "the path to water" (path to life); comprehensive guidance for Muslim living.

Q8: What are the five categories of action in Islam?

Fard (obligatory), Mustahabb (recommended), Mubah (permissible), Makruh (discouraged), Haram (forbidden)

Q9: What are the three core duties of Sikh life?

Naam Japna (remember God), Kirat Karni (earn honestly), Vand Chakna (share with others)

Q10: What is sewa?

Selfless service to others without expectation of reward; foundation of Sikh ethics.

Self-mark your test: 8-10 correct = Excellent. 6-7 = Good. 4-5 = Needs review. 0-3 = Go back to earlier lessons.

Big Question Evaluation

"You don't need religion to know right from wrong."

Decide: FOR, AGAINST, or BOTH? Then explain your position in 120+ words.

Arguments FOR (You don't need religion):

  • Humanists build ethical systems on reason and empathy, not faith.
  • Many atheists and agnostics live ethically without God.
  • Conscience and human wellbeing can guide morality without religion.
  • Some religious people use faith to justify harm; religion isn't necessary or sufficient for ethics.

Arguments AGAINST (Religion is important):

  • Religious traditions have preserved ethical wisdom for thousands of years.
  • Belief in God or karma gives deeper meaning and motivation to ethics.
  • Community and ritual in religion reinforce ethical commitment.
  • Religious traditions teach humility, transcendence, and respect that pure reason might miss.

Both are true:

  • You can be moral without religion, but religious traditions offer valuable resources and community for ethical living.
  • Religion alone isn't enough; it requires wisdom, reason, and genuine commitment—not blind obedience.
  • The question isn't whether religion is necessary, but how we combine faith, reason, empathy, and community for ethical lives.
Suggested length: 120+ words

Final Words from This Course

Religious Studies isn't about proving one tradition right and others wrong. It's about understanding how human beings across cultures and centuries have asked the deepest questions: What is real? How should we live? What happens when we die? What is justice? What is love?

Each tradition offers wisdom. Your job is to learn, to think critically, to respect difference, and to build your own moral compass.
