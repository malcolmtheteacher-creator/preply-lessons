The Problem of Too Much Demand

Novo Nordisk made a drug the whole world wanted at once — and discovered that a blessing at that scale behaves exactly like a crisis.

Today: you'll work through the strangest corporate problem of the decade — success arriving faster than anyone can build for it — and you'll master the language of the forecast: demand is set to outstrip supply well into the decade; by 2030 the market will have tripled.
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • Has demand ever genuinely exceeded your organisation's ability to supply? What broke first — operations, quality, or relationships?
  • When one product becomes most of the profit, what happens to every other conversation in the company?
  • Can a company be too important to its home country? What would that even look like?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Orders ran years ahead of what the factories could make; patients queued, pharmacies rationed, and the gap refused to close. Demand had comprehensively outstripped supply.
to outstrip (v) — to grow faster than something can match. Demand outstrips supply / capacity / infrastructure. The forecaster's verb for a gap that is widening, not just existing.
2. Factories for injectable medicines take years to build and certify; you cannot simply rent more. The constraint was capacity, and money alone could not remove it quickly.
capacity (n) — the maximum an operation can produce. At full capacity, spare capacity, to expand capacity, a capacity constraint. This case is a capacity story wearing a miracle-drug costume.
3. One medicine family came to dominate revenue, profit and the share price so completely that the company's fate rode on a single franchise. Analysts began warning about concentration risk.
concentration risk (n) — dependence on one product, customer or market. The mirror image of Lego's focus lesson: focus wins, until the one thing you focused on stumbles.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: blockbuster · patent cliff · pipeline. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means a product with enormous sales — in pharma, over a billion dollars a year?
a blockbuster (n) — borrowed from cinema. A blockbuster drug. This case concerns arguably the biggest one ever launched.
2. Which term means the moment patent protection expires and cheap copies crash the price?
the patent cliff (n) — the scheduled end of exclusivity; generic or biosimilar competitors typically erase most of the revenue within a couple of years. Every pharma forecast is a race against a dated cliff.
3. Which term means the set of future products currently in research and trials?
the pipeline (n) — a rich / thin pipeline; late-stage pipeline; pipeline readout (trial results). The pipeline is what a pharma company actually sells to investors; today's drugs merely fund it.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. For a period the company's stock-market value exceeded the annual output of the entire Danish economy, and economists argued about the country becoming a one-company economy. The worry is that…
  (a) the company pays no tax   (b) national statistics, currency and policy start moving with one firm's fortunes   (c) other companies must merge with it
(b) — when one firm dominates exports, growth and investment, its results become the nation's macroeconomics. Finland had this conversation about Nokia; Denmark inherited it.
2. When a promising successor drug produced trial results below expectations, the shares fell hard in a day. Markets were repricing the readout. A readout is…
  (a) the published result of a clinical trial   (b) an analyst's report   (c) a production statistic
(a) — a readout (n) — the data moment when a trial reports. A positive / disappointing readout. Binary events around which billions reprice in minutes.

D · Collocation & word building

1. Complete the forecasting collocations — three different verbs: to ___ demand (estimate it ahead) · demand is ___ to double (predicted by outsiders) · the company ___ guidance (formally tells the market what to expect).
To forecast / project demand · demand is expected / set / on course to double · the company issues guidance (and later raises, lowers, or painfully withdraws it). Guidance = the official forecast a listed company must then live with.
2. You know to ration. Give the noun, and complete the harder pair the company faced: to ration supply between existing ___ (people already on the drug) and new ___ (those waiting to begin).
rationing (n) — allocating scarcity. Between existing patients and new starts. Note how instantly a business-school "allocation problem" becomes an ethical one when the unit is patients.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — A century of insulin, then lightning

Novo Nordisk spent a hundred years being admirably dull. Founded in Denmark in the 1920s to manufacture the newly discovered insulin, it grew into one of the world's two or three dominant diabetes-care companies — foundation-controlled, long-horizon, deeply unglamorous. Its scientists had spent decades on a class of molecules called GLP-1s, which mimic a gut hormone that regulates blood sugar and, as it happens, appetite. Out of that patient work came semaglutide: approved for diabetes in 2017 under the name Ozempic, and for weight management in 2021 as Wegovy.

What happened next had no precedent in pharmaceutical history. Clinical results showing weight loss in the range of fifteen per cent collided with social media, celebrity culture and an obesity epidemic affecting a billion people, and demand went vertical. "Ozempic" stopped being a brand and became a word — in monologues, in headlines, eventually in the dictionary conversations. Prescriptions ran to millions; surveys suggested enormous fractions of the adult population wanted the drug; and Novo's factories — built for the sober arithmetic of diabetes care — were suddenly being asked to supply a global consumer phenomenon.

The market did the rest. Novo's valuation multiplied until, in 2023, it passed LVMH to become Europe's most valuable company — a Danish insulin maker worth more than the entire annual output of Denmark. Its results began visibly moving national statistics: economists noted that much of Danish growth was attributable to one company, the central bank held interest rates lower partly against the currency effects of pharmaceutical export earnings, and commentators reached for an uncomfortable precedent — Finland, Nokia, and what happens to a small country wired to one corporate engine.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did semaglutide come from, and why is "overnight success" the wrong description?

2. What made the company a macroeconomic fact for Denmark?

1. From decades of foundation-funded GLP-1 research inside a century-old insulin specialist — approved as Ozempic (2017) and Wegovy (2021). The lightning was sudden; the conductor took forty years to build.

2. Its valuation passed LVMH's to lead Europe and exceeded Denmark's annual GDP; its exports and earnings visibly moved national growth figures and even monetary policy — the Nokia-Finland comparison became standard.

Discuss:
  • Forty years of patient research, then one viral moment. Which part of that could your organisation actually replicate — and which part is luck wearing a lab coat?

Part 2 — Managing a miracle

Then the blessing began behaving like a crisis. Demand outstripped manufacturing capacity almost immediately and stayed ahead of it for years; injectable biologics cannot be scaled like software, and a new sterile filling line takes years to build and validate. The company found itself doing things no marketer dreams of: deliberately restraining launches in new countries, holding back starter doses so existing patients could stay supplied, and asking doctors, in effect, to stop creating new customers. Diabetes patients complained of shortages caused by demand for weight loss; regulators opened questions; compounding pharmacies in America began legally producing unauthorised copies during the official shortage — competition conjured into existence by the company's own inability to supply.

Money answered part of it. Novo committed sums in the tens of billions to new plants across Denmark, France and the United States, and its foundation moved to buy one of the world's biggest contract manufacturers to lock up filling capacity — a deal large enough to draw regulatory scrutiny of its own. But capacity was only the first front. American politics opened a second: list prices around a thousand dollars a month for a drug most of the population arguably could use made Novo a standing exhibit in Senate hearings on drug pricing. And competition opened a third: Eli Lilly's rival tirzepatide arrived with trial results showing greater average weight loss, and the two companies settled into the classic duopoly race — next-generation molecules, oral versions, trial readouts traded like artillery.

Through it all ran the strangest strategic question in the case: how big is this market actually? Forecasts for the obesity-drug market by 2030 ranged from fifty billion dollars to a hundred and thirty — a spread so wide it amounted to a confession. Every capacity decision, every plant, every acquisition was a bet on a number nobody could know, taken under conditions where building too little meant handing the market to Lilly, and building too much meant owning the world's most expensive empty clean-rooms.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Name three unusual actions the shortage forced on the company.

2. What were the three fronts beyond manufacturing, and why was the market-size question a confession?

1. Restraining launches in new markets; withholding starter doses to protect existing patients; effectively asking prescribers to slow new demand — plus watching legal copy-making emerge under US shortage rules.

2. US pricing politics, the Lilly duopoly race, and the forecasting problem itself — 2030 market estimates spanning $50–130bn, a spread wide enough to show that every capacity bet was made against an unknowable number.

Discuss:
  • Building too little hands the market to your rival; building too much buries you in idle capacity. How should a board size a bet when the demand forecast spans a factor of three?

Part 3 — Gravity resumes

The descent, when it came, needed no scandal — only arithmetic and expectation. Through 2024 and 2025 the extraordinary premium deflated piece by piece: a next-generation readout that was merely good rather than miraculous knocked a fifth off the shares in a day; Lilly's data kept pressing; American pricing pressure hardened; supply normalised just as investors noticed that normal supply also meant normal growth. The share price roughly halved from its peak; the chief executive who had presided over the miracle years departed in 2025 amid board pressure; thousands of job cuts followed. Nothing about the underlying business had failed — revenues and profits remained enormous — but the company had been priced for a story, and the story had become a business again. Readers of the WeWork case will recognise the mechanism operating here without a single governance sin.

Ahead sits the industry's oldest cliff with the industry's newest numbers on it. Semaglutide's key patents expire in major markets in the early 2030s — in some large countries sooner — at which point biosimilar copies will do to Wegovy's price what generics always do. The defence is the eternal one, run at unprecedented scale: pour today's extraordinary earnings into the pipeline — oral versions, next-generation molecules, trials in heart disease, kidney disease, liver disease, even dementia — so that when the cliff arrives, something new is standing on the far side. Whether the pipeline delivers is a question currently being answered in trial data, one readout at a time.

The case remains genuinely open, which is why it closes this series. A hundred-year-old company built the decade's defining product, was crowned Europe's champion, distorted its home country's economics, met a demand it could not supply, and was then repriced by success becoming ordinary — all without a villain anywhere in the story. The question it leaves for any leader is the one the forecasting grammar exists to answer honestly: when your own plan assumes a number — for demand, for growth, for how long the miracle lasts — what is the widest range you would defend out loud, and what would you build differently if you said the range instead of the number?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What drove the 2024–25 repricing, given that nothing operationally failed?

2. What is the patent-cliff defence, and why does the case close the series?

1. Expectation arithmetic: a good-but-not-miraculous readout, Lilly's stronger data, pricing pressure and normalising supply converted a story valuation back into a business valuation — shares roughly halved, the CEO left, jobs were cut, while revenues stayed enormous.

2. Reinvest the boom into the pipeline — oral versions, new molecules, new disease areas — so something replaces semaglutide when patents expire in the early 2030s. It closes the series because it is entirely live: a case with no ending yet and no villain at all.

Discuss:
  • "Repriced by success becoming ordinary." Where in your own world is a valuation — of a project, a team, a company — resting on growth staying miraculous?

Grammar — forecasting & projection

Every plan is a sentence about the future, and English offers a full gearbox — from hard commitment to honest range. The skill this case demands is matching the grammar to your actual confidence.

The confidence gearbox, high to low:
• will — commitment or certainty: "The patents will expire in 2031."
• be set to / be on course to / be due to — trajectory established: "Capacity is set to double by 2027." Due to = scheduled.
• be expected / projected / forecast to — someone's estimate, agent discreetly hidden: "The market is projected to reach $100bn."
• be likely to / may well / could — graded probability: "Competition may well halve the price."
• risk + -ing / be at risk of — downside framing: "The company risks over-building."
The two power tools:
• Future perfect — by + date: "By 2030 the market will have tripled." · "By the time the cliff arrives, the pipeline will have reported." The projection that looks back from a future vantage point — the forecaster's signature tense.
• Future continuous: "By then, biosimilars will be eroding the price." — a process in motion at the future moment.

Honest-range language: somewhere in the region of · anywhere between X and Y · on current trends · barring a surprise readout · in a base / bear / bull case — and the discipline sentence: "Our central case is X; we would defend anything between Y and Z."

1. Choose the gear

Complete each sentence with the form in brackets.

1. The key patents (certainty) in the early 2030s — that date is not a forecast.

2. Analysts' estimates vary, but the obesity market (projected) anywhere between fifty and a hundred and thirty billion by 2030.

3. Without the new plants, the company (downside framing) handing the market to its rival.

1. will expire   2. is projected to reach   3. risks / would risk

2. The future perfect vantage point

Rewrite each pair as one sentence looking back from the future date. Write the full sentence.

1. 2030 arrives. Before that, the company completes three new plants. → By 2030,

2. The cliff arrives in 2032. At that moment, biosimilar erosion is an ongoing process. → By the time the cliff arrives,

3. The decade ends. Before then, the pipeline reports its major trials. → By the end of the decade,

1. By 2030, the company will have completed three new plants.

2. By the time the cliff arrives, biosimilars will be eroding the price.

3. By the end of the decade, the pipeline will have reported its major trials.

1 and 3: completed before the vantage point → future perfect. 2: in motion at the vantage point → future continuous.

3. The guidance statement — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the form the confidence level requires, then deliver the whole statement aloud as if to investors.

Let me give you our honest picture. Demand (trajectory) to exceed supply into next year; by mid-year, two of the new lines (future perfect: come) online, and by year-end we (future continuous: ship) at roughly double current volumes. The wider market (someone's estimate) to reach somewhere in the region of a hundred billion by 2030, though we would defend anything between sixty and a hundred and twenty. Competition (graded probability: may well) intensify following our rival's readouts, and pricing (likely) to remain under political pressure in the United States. Barring a surprise readout, our central case (certainty) unchanged.

is set / is on course · will have come · will be shipping · is projected / expected · may well · is likely · remains / will remain

Count the gears used: trajectory, future perfect, future continuous, hidden-agent projection, graded probability, and one plain "will" saved for the only thing actually certain. That distribution is what credible guidance sounds like.

Going deeper

A note on the record: this is the most live case in the series — figures are rounded, the competitive and political picture moves quarterly, and some of what this lesson states in the present tense will have become past tense by the time you read it. That decay is part of the lesson.

What the case actually tests. First, that scarcity is a strategy surface, not just an operations failure. Who gets a constrained product — existing patients or new markets, home country or biggest market, diabetes or weight loss — is a sequence of value judgements wearing logistics clothing. Companies drill for shortage of demand; almost none has a playbook for the opposite, which is why the decisions land on the CEO's desk one crisis at a time.

Second, the concentration paradox that runs through this whole series. Lego was saved by focus; Kodak was killed by it; Novo was crowned and then repriced by it. The resolving variable is not focus itself but what the focused asset is standing on — an unpatentable brick that cannot expire versus a patented molecule with a printed expiry date. Focus on a fortress compounds; focus on a lease — and a patent is a lease from the public — must fund its own succession before the term runs out.

Third, the forecast as governance instrument. The 50-to-130-billion spread was not a failure of analysis; it was the honest shape of the unknown. The organisational failure mode is what usually happens next: the range collapses into a single number for the board pack, the number hardens into a target, and the target quietly becomes a fact that capacity, hiring and guidance are built on. The grammar of this lesson is the antidote — an executive who says "will" only for the patent date and "somewhere between" for the market is not hedging; they are transmitting the truth's actual resolution.

Talk it through:
  • Design the allocation rule: you have half the supply the world wants. Write the priority order you would defend on television, and name what it sacrifices.
  • Apply the fortress-versus-lease test across the series: brick, film, molecule, network, desks. Which is your own core asset — and when does its lease expire?
  • Trace the life of a number in your organisation: where did the current plan's key forecast come from, and does anyone still remember its original range?
  • Denmark now has the Finland question. Should a small country deliberately diversify away from its champion — and who exactly would do the diversifying?
