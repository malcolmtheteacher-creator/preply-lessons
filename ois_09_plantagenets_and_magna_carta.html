Now the bridge: Between Alfred (878 AD) and the Plantagenets (1154 AD), nearly 300 years of Saxon, Danish, and Norman invasion shaped Britain. Henry II of Anjou, great-grandson of the Conqueror, will be the most powerful ruler in all of Europe. A new dynasty rises — the Plantagenets will rule for 331 years, and among them will be kings of such passion and cruelty that their stories echo still.

II. Henry II, Becket & Richard

Henry II: The Angevin Empire (1154–1189)

H.E. Marshall opens her account of Henry II with vivid admiration:

Henry II was a man of great ability and strength of character. He ruled over a vast empire. For his dominions stretched not only over England, but over much of France as well. He had red hair and a terrible temper. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

Henry's empire was extraordinary. His lands in France were vast: Normandy, Anjou, Aquitaine, and more. He controlled more territory than the King of France himself. Yet his greatest asset was also his doom — his temperament.

Thomas Becket: The Murder in the Cathedral (1170)

Henry's friend and Chancellor was Thomas Becket. When the Archbishop of Canterbury died, Henry saw a chance to control the Church and made Becket Archbishop.

Becket changed as soon as he became Archbishop. He became very stern and austere. He wore a hair shirt next to his skin and spent hours in prayer. He opposed the King in everything. The quarrel between the King and Archbishop grew fiercer and fiercer. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

Their friendship turned to hatred. Becket sided with the Church against the King. Henry raged in fury.

Henry was angry, and did not really mean what he said. But there were four rough knights who thought they would please the King. Without waiting to ask his permission, they armed themselves and rode off to Canterbury. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

Marshall tells the dramatic account:

They found Becket in the cathedral. They called upon him to come with them. But Becket refused to leave. He was killed in the very cathedral itself. All Christendom was shocked at the murder of the Archbishop in the House of God. The Pope placed England under an interdict. No church services were allowed to be held in the kingdom. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story
The historian's note: The four knights were Reginald FitzUrse, Hugh de Morville, William de Tracy, and Richard le Breton. They killed Becket on 29 December 1170 at the High Altar of Canterbury Cathedral. Henry did public penance for the murder, walking barefoot and being whipped. But the damage to his reputation was immense. Becket became a saint and martyr. The famous (and likely apocryphal) quote about "turbulent priest" has become the symbol of Henry's careless outburst, though historians debate the exact words.

Richard the Lionheart: The Third Crusade (1189–1199)

Henry II had three surviving sons: Richard, Geoffrey, and John. The eldest was Richard Coeur de Lion (Lionheart), a warrior prince of great courage and military genius.

Richard was a brave and daring prince. He won the love of all Christendom by his deeds on the Crusade. He fought the Saracens with great valor. But he was taken prisoner on his way home and held for ransom. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

Richard spent ten years away from England — first warring against his father, then crushing rebellions in his own lands, and finally leading the Third Crusade to the Holy Land (1191–1192). His ransom cost England an enormous sum, which the people had to pay in heavy taxes.

The historian's note: Richard was ransomed for 150,000 marks (a fortune equivalent to three years of England's royal income). He was captured by Leopold of Austria and held by Holy Roman Emperor Henry VI. He spent less than a year actually in England during his ten-year reign. When he died in 1199 from an infected wound received at Chalûs-Chabrol, he was fighting in France.

III. King John & Magna Carta

King John: The Worst King (1199–1216)

When Richard died, his youngest brother John inherited the throne. Marshall's portrait of John is scathing:

John was a cruel, treacherous, and evil king. He was hated by his barons, the Church, and the common people. He had lost most of his lands in France through his own weakness and wickedness. And he was always plotting and scheming to get more money from his subjects. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

John's reign was a cascade of disasters. He quarreled with the Pope and the Church. He taxed his barons mercilessly. He squandered England's wealth. He lost his Norman and Angevin lands to the King of France. The barons grew restless, angry, and finally — desperate.

The Barons Rebel: Runnymede, 1215

Marshall captures the moment of uprising:

The barons could endure him no longer. They rose in rebellion against their King. They marched on London and took the city. Now the King, afraid for his life, agreed to meet the barons at a place called Runnymede, not far from Windsor. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story

On 15 June 1215, on a meadow by the Thames, King John placed his seal upon a document that would change the course of history. It was written in Latin and called Magna Carta Libertatum — the Great Charter of Liberties.

What Was Magna Carta?

Marshall explains:

In the Great Charter, the King promised that he would not take money from his subjects without their consent. He promised not to throw men into prison without a fair trial. He promised to be bound by the law, just like ordinary people. It was a wonderful charter, for it meant that the King was no longer above the law, and that the people had rights that the King could not take away. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story
The historian's note: Magna Carta had 63 clauses. Some dealt with feudal rights, Church independence, and trade. The most famous promised that no free man shall be imprisoned or punished except by judgment of his peers and the law of the land. Though written in the context of feudal disputes, Magna Carta became the foundation of the rule of law. Later lawyers and reformers would interpret it as protecting individual liberty. John tried to repudiate it almost immediately, but his death in 1216 preserved it, and it was reissued (with revisions) by his successors. By the 17th century, Magna Carta was seen as a charter of English liberties and became a touchstone for parliaments and revolutionaries everywhere.

The Great Charter's Meaning

Why Magna Carta Mattered

Magna Carta said something revolutionary: the King is not above the law. He cannot simply seize money, land, or freedom. He must obey the same rules as his subjects. This principle became the foundation of constitutional government. When English colonists went to America, they brought this idea with them. When they rebelled against King George III, they appealed to Magna Carta. It shaped democracies worldwide.

John died in 1216, just one year after sealing Magna Carta. His nine-year-old son Henry III inherited the throne. The barons, sensing an opportunity, continued to push for the principles of the Great Charter. It was reissued and confirmed many times over the coming centuries, gathering strength and meaning with each reissue.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these reflection cards. Click them to select, then write your thoughts in the textarea below.

Henry II's Anger

His rage at Becket led (possibly) to "Will no one rid me of this turbulent priest?" and four knights killed him. Can words alone cause murder? Is a king responsible for violence inspired by his words?

Who Was the Traitor?

Henry made his friend Becket Archbishop to control the Church. Becket transformed and opposed the King. Was Becket a traitor to his friend, or was he true to a higher calling?

Richard's Absence

Richard spent ten years away from England — crusading, fighting his father, in captivity. Yet he remains a legend (Lionheart). Is a king absent from his kingdom still a good king?

John's Weakness

Marshall calls John weak and wicked. He lost his lands, angered the barons, and was forced to sign Magna Carta. Can bad governance create good law? Can oppression lead to justice?

Magna Carta's Real Power

John sealed Magna Carta under duress. Did he mean it? He died a year later. Yet it became the foundation of liberty. How can a document forced out of a reluctant king reshape the world?

The Rule of Law

Before Magna Carta, a king answered to God alone. After, he answered to law. What's the difference between a lawful tyrant and a tyrant answerable to no one?
