Numbers Become Pictures

A child's rhyme, a trick with knuckles, and two simple systems that turn the digits 0 to 9 into things you can see.

← Links and Stories  ·  The whole course  ·  The Major System →

In this lesson: you will learn why numbers are hard to remember, use a shape system and a rhyme system to give every digit a picture, and encode short numbers as one vivid scene.
Where we left off
What is the difference between a palace and a story chain?
A palace uses fixed places for order; a chain uses links between items. A palace is better for long lists, a chain for short quick ones.
Why should you never link item 1 straight to item 5 in a chain?
Each link should hold only two pictures. Each item leads to the next, so the chain stays simple.
What did Parry and Lord find about epic singers?
They remembered the shape of the story and its familiar scenes, and rebuilt the words each time.

Thirty days hath September

It is a wet afternoon and a child is trying to remember how many days there are in each month. Her grandmother does not give her a table. She says a rhyme that her own grandmother said, and that has been said in English for hundreds of years:

Thirty days hath September, April, June and November. All the rest have thirty-one, except for February alone.

The child says it three times, and by the end of the afternoon she has twelve numbers in her head that she will keep for the rest of her life.

How does the rhyme help the child?
The rhythm and the rhyme carry the numbers, so she remembers the sound of the verse, and the verse brings back the numbers.
Talk about it. Do you know any rhyme or song that helps you remember a fact? Say it.

Then the grandmother shows her another trick. She makes a fist and says: 'Count the knuckles and the dips between them.' Every knuckle is a long month with thirty-one days; every dip is a short month. January is the first knuckle, February the first dip, March the next knuckle. When she gets to the last knuckle, July, she jumps back to the first one for August, and the pattern carries on.

The child can now check any month in a few seconds, anywhere, with no rhyme and no paper. The numbers have been put in a place you can see and touch.

How does the knuckle trick work?
Knuckles are the thirty-one-day months and dips are the shorter months; you count along the hand.
Talk about it. Which would you use, the rhyme or the knuckles? Why?

A rhyme and a hand can hold twelve numbers. But what about a phone number, a PIN, a year, a passport number, a bank code? Numbers like these are hard for a simple reason: a number is not a picture. The digits 7, 3, 9 do not look like anything and mean nothing. The mind has nothing to hold on to, and it lets go.

The answer is the oldest trick in this course: give them pictures. In this lesson you will meet two simple systems for doing it. One gives each digit a picture by its shape; the other gives each digit a picture by a rhyme. In the next lesson you will learn a stronger system, but these two are quick to learn and are a good start.

Why are numbers so hard to remember?
A number is not a picture. The digits mean nothing by themselves, so there is nothing for the mind to hold on to.
Talk about it. Which numbers in your life do you remember? Why do you think you remember those?

Two ways to picture a digit

Learn ten pictures once, and every number you meet can be told as a scene. Choose the system that comes easier to you, or learn both.

  1. Split the number into pairs. 1066 → 10 and 66. Two pictures per pair is plenty for one scene.
  2. Look up the picture for each digit. Say them in order.
  3. Put the pictures in one scene. They must act on each other. Make it vivid (lesson 2).
  4. Place the scene. On a body peg, in a palace place, or at the end of a chain.

Encode 25 (shape)

Shape system: 2 is a swan and 5 is a hook. Make one vivid scene for the number 25.
A huge black swan has a rusty hook caught on its neck and is dragging a fisherman across the lake, roaring like a motor-boat.

Encode 67 and 84 (shape)

6 is a cherry, 7 a cliff, 8 a snowman, 4 a sailboat. Make a scene for 67 and another for 84.
67: A cherry the size of a car rolls off the edge of a cliff, bouncing and splashing. 84: A snowman is sailing a little sailboat across a sea of hot chocolate, melting as he goes.

Encode 39 and 51 (rhyme)

Rhyme system: 3 tree, 9 vine; 5 hive, 1 bun. Make one scene for 39 and one for 51.
39: A tree with a vine wrapped around its trunk, which is pulling it over like a rope. 51: A beehive in the middle of a hot bun, and the bees are eating the crumbs.

A four-digit number

Use either system to encode 1776. Split it into 17 and 76 and make two scenes. Write them.
Shape: 17 = candle + cliff: a candle burns on top of a high cliff and gives off blue smoke. 76 = cliff + cherry: another cliff throws a huge cherry into the sea. Two scenes, four digits.
