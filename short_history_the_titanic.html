The Titanic

The unsinkable ship, and the night that proved otherwise.

Today: you'll tell the story of the most famous shipwreck in history, and discuss confidence, risk and disaster — using narrative tenses and the language of the sea.

Key words — meet them, then sort them

Before we read, let's unlock the ten words you'll need. First, a question to wake up what you already know: what do you already know about the Titanic? Say three things aloud.

Now read each word and its example. Try to say what it means before you reveal the gloss — then check.

1. The Titanic set out on her maiden voyage — the very first time the brand-new ship had ever carried passengers across the sea.
maiden voyage (n) — the first official journey made by a new ship.
2. She was an ocean liner — an enormous passenger ship built to cross the open ocean on a fixed, scheduled route.
an ocean liner (n) — a large ship that carries passengers on long sea routes.
3. Newspapers called her practically "unsinkable" — so well built, they claimed, that nothing could ever send her to the bottom.
"unsinkable" (adj) — impossible to sink. (Said of the Titanic — wrongly, as it turned out.)
4. In the dark she struck an iceberg — a vast mass of floating ice, most of it hidden beneath the surface of the water.
an iceberg (n) — a huge piece of floating ice, most of which lies underwater.
5. High up in the crow's nest, the lookout strained his eyes into the blackness, watching for any danger ahead.
a lookout (n) — a crew member whose job is to watch for danger ahead.
6. There was room in every lifeboat for only a fraction of the people aboard — small open boats meant to save lives in an emergency.
a lifeboat (n) — a small boat used to rescue people from a sinking ship.
7. Within hours, the great ship began to founder — to fill with water and sink slowly beneath the waves.
to founder (v) — (of a ship) to fill with water and sink. (Also used figuratively: a plan can founder.)
8. The wireless operators tapped out a desperate distress signal, calling any ship near enough to come to their aid.
a distress signal (n) — an urgent message sent to ask for rescue (e.g. SOS).
9. In the freezing water, hundreds would perish before help could reach them — they would die in the cold and the dark.
to perish (v) — to die, especially in a sudden or violent way. (Formal.)
10. For over seventy years the wreck lay undiscovered on the seabed — the broken remains of the sunken ship.
the wreck (n) — the remains of a ship that has been destroyed or sunk.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The unsinkable ship

In the spring of 1912, the RMS Titanic was the largest ocean liner the world had ever seen — a floating palace built for the White Star Line, longer than any ship before her and fitted out with a luxury that astonished the age. To the public, and to the press, she was something close to a miracle of engineering. Newspapers reported that she was practically "unsinkable," and a great many people believed it.

On 10 April 1912, around noon, she left Southampton on her maiden voyage, bound for New York. She called at Cherbourg in France and then at Queenstown in Ireland, taking on more passengers at each stop, until over 2,200 people were aboard — among them some of the richest in the world, travelling in first class, and hundreds of emigrants in third class, crossing the Atlantic to begin new lives in America. Her captain was Edward Smith, the most senior of all the line's captains, a man widely trusted and admired.

Yet beneath the confidence lay a quiet flaw. The Titanic carried only 20 lifeboats — enough for roughly 1,178 people, far fewer than were on board. By the rules of the time this was perfectly legal; the lifeboats were thought of less as a means of saving everyone than as a way of ferrying passengers to a rescuing ship. After all, why would a ship like this ever need them?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What made the Titanic so celebrated before she even sailed — and what did people believe about her?

2. Where did her maiden voyage begin, where was it bound, and roughly how many people were aboard?

1. She was the largest ocean liner ever built, a luxurious "floating palace" for the White Star Line, and the press claimed she was practically "unsinkable."

2. She left Southampton (calling at Cherbourg and Queenstown), bound for New York, with over 2,200 people aboard — from the very rich in first class to emigrants in third.

Discuss:
  • The lifeboats were legal, but there were nowhere near enough. Why do you think a ship believed to be "unsinkable" ended up with too few? What does that tell us about how people thought?

Part 2 — Thirty-seven seconds

For four days the crossing was calm and the voyage went well. But the North Atlantic that April was full of ice, and the Titanic was sailing straight towards it. Over the course of 14 April, her wireless operators received no fewer than seven separate ice warnings from other ships. And yet she pressed on through the night at roughly 22 knots — close to her full speed.

At 23:40, the lookout high in the crow's nest saw it: a dark shape rising out of the blackness directly ahead. He rang the warning bell and telephoned the bridge. The officers threw the engines and the wheel into a desperate turn — but there was almost no time. From the moment the iceberg was sighted to the moment it struck, only about thirty-seven seconds passed. The ship turned just far enough to avoid hitting it head-on, and instead scraped along its side, the ice tearing open the hull below the waterline.

At first, to many on board, almost nothing seemed to have happened — a faint shudder, a distant grinding sound, and then quiet. But the captain and his engineers soon understood the truth. The sea was already pouring in through the wound in her side, faster than any pump could fight it. The "unsinkable" ship had perhaps two hours left to live, and there were lifeboats for barely half the souls aboard.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many ice warnings had the ship received that day, and roughly how fast was she travelling?

2. How long was it between the lookout's sighting and the collision — and what exactly did the iceberg do to the ship?

1. She had received seven ice warnings that day, yet was still travelling at roughly 22 knots, close to full speed.

2. Only about 37 seconds passed between the sighting and the impact; she scraped along the iceberg's side and the ice tore open her hull below the waterline.

Discuss:
  • Seven warnings, full speed, thirty-seven seconds. Where would you put the blame — and is "blame" even the right way to think about an accident like this?

Part 3 — The long night, and after

As the ship began to founder, the crew sent up distress signals and the order went out: women and children first. But the evacuation was slow, frightened and confused. Many of the early lifeboats were lowered only half full, and survival split sharply along the lines of class — first-class passengers, closer to the boats, escaped in far greater numbers than those crowded in third class far below. There was simply no room for everyone, and everyone, by now, knew it.

At 02:20 on 15 April 1912 — two hours and forty minutes after the collision — the Titanic broke apart and sank beneath the freezing Atlantic. Around 1,500 people died that night, most of them in the water, in a cold that no one could survive for long. It remains one of the deadliest peacetime disasters at sea in history.

Help, when it came, came too late for most. The RMS Carpathia, racing through the same dangerous ice, reached the scene and by morning had rescued the roughly 710 survivors from the lifeboats. The wreck itself vanished into legend: it lay lost on the seabed, more than three kilometres down, until it was finally discovered in 1985 — and only then could the world begin to see, with its own eyes, what had really become of the ship that could not sink.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How long after the collision did the ship sink, and roughly how many people died?

2. Which ship rescued the survivors, how many were saved, and when was the wreck finally found?

1. She sank at 02:20 on 15 April, two hours and forty minutes after the collision; around 1,500 people died, most of them in the freezing water.

2. The RMS Carpathia reached the scene and rescued the roughly 710 survivors by morning; the wreck was finally found on the seabed in 1985.

Discuss:
  • "Women and children first" — but survival also depended heavily on class. What does the way people behaved that night reveal about a society in a crisis?

The grammar this story runs on: narrative tenses

A story like this needs three past tenses working together. Get them right and your storytelling sounds effortless; get them wrong and the timeline collapses.

The three tools:
• Past simple — the main events, in sequence: "she struck the iceberg," "the ship sank."
• Past continuous — the background, the longer action in progress: "while the lookout was watching the sea…"
• Past perfect — a step further back, something that happened before another past event: "the ship had received seven warnings before she struck the ice."

1. Past simple vs past continuous — event against background

Use the past continuous for the longer action already in progress, and the past simple for the shorter event that interrupts or sits inside it.

1. While the ship (sail) calmly through the night, the lookout suddenly (see) the iceberg.

2. The passengers (sleep) peacefully when the ship (strike) the ice.

3. As the sea (pour) in, the crew (lower) the first lifeboats.

1. was sailing / saw (background in progress / the event)

2. were sleeping / struck (longer action interrupted by a sudden event)

3. was pouring / lowered

2. Past perfect — the step further back

When you are already telling the past, and you reach back to something earlier still, use had + past participle.

1. By the time she struck the iceberg, the Titanic (already / receive) seven ice warnings.

2. The ship that everyone (call) "unsinkable" went to the bottom in under three hours.

3. Before the Carpathia arrived, around 1,500 people (already / perish) in the water.

4. The wreck lay hidden because no one (ever / find) it until 1985.

1. had already received

2. had called

3. had already perished

4. had ever found

3. Put them together — narrate a paragraph

Fill each gap with the tense the meaning needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

The lookout (stare) into the darkness when the iceberg suddenly (appear) ahead. The ship (travel) at almost full speed, and her officers (ignore) the warnings that other ships (send) earlier that day. Within seconds the hull (tear) open, and the sea (begin) to pour in.

was staring (background) · appeared (event) · was travelling (background) · had ignored (earlier event) · had sent (earlier still) · tore (event) · began (next event)

Notice how the three tenses layer the time: the long background, the sudden events, and the things that had already happened before.

Task B · Confidence and recklessness (4 minutes)

The disaster is often blamed on overconfidence and too few lifeboats. When does confidence become recklessness? Build an argument: where is the line between being bold and being careless — for a ship, a company, or a person — and how do we usually only see it after something has gone wrong?

Task C · Your own disaster or failure (4 minutes)

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. More than a century on, the Titanic still grips us — films, books, museums, the wreck itself. Why this disaster, and not others? What makes a story like this last?
  2. Survival that night depended partly on luck, partly on class, partly on where you happened to be standing. How fair, in the end, was who lived and who died?
  3. After the Titanic, the rules of the sea were changed forever — enough lifeboats for everyone, constant radio watch. Why do we so often only fix things after a catastrophe?
