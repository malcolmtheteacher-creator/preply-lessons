The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — From the harbour to the helm

Almost nothing is known of her early life — not even the name she was born with. She appears in the record around 1801 as a young woman working in a floating brothel in the port city of Canton, in southern China. History remembers her mainly by titles: Ching Shih, meaning "widow of Cheng," and Zheng Yi Sao, "wife of Zheng Yi." Zheng Yi was one of the most feared pirates on the South China coast, and in 1801 he married her. According to the stories, she agreed only on remarkable terms: an equal share of his power and his plunder.

Zheng Yi did something few pirates had managed. Rather than let rival captains fight one another, he bound the scattered pirate bands of the coast into a single confederation — a vast alliance of ships flying coloured flags. By the middle of the decade it may have numbered many hundreds of vessels. Then, in 1807, Zheng Yi was killed, swept overboard in a storm. His widow was left with a choice: fade into the background, or take his place.

She took his place. Moving quickly, she secured the loyalty of the fleet's most important commanders — above all a young captain named Chang Pao, whom Zheng Yi had adopted and who now became her right hand and, later, her husband. Where a lesser figure might have watched the confederation break apart, she held it together and made it hers.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Ching Shih come to be part of the pirate world, and on what terms did she marry Zheng Yi?

2. What happened in 1807, and how did she respond?

1. She was working in a brothel in Canton when the pirate leader Zheng Yi married her in 1801; she reportedly insisted on an equal share of his power and wealth.

2. Zheng Yi was killed in a storm; rather than step aside, she seized command of the confederation, backed by the loyal captain Chang Pao.

Discuss:
  • She began with almost no power of her own and married into someone else's. What does it take to turn an inherited position into a genuinely personal one?

Part 2 — The largest pirate fleet in history

What Ching Shih built has no rival in the history of piracy. At its height, her confederation is estimated to have controlled somewhere between 40,000 and 80,000 pirates and many hundreds — by some accounts, over a thousand — of ships. No European pirate of the so-called Golden Age ever came close. She commanded not a gang but a floating state, with its own supply lines, its own taxes and its own laws.

That order was held in place by a famously strict code, said to have been written down and enforced without exception. Plunder had to be registered and shared out by fixed rules; a pirate who stole from the common fund, or who gave an order and was disobeyed, could be put to death. Villagers who paid their "protection" dues were not to be harmed. Most striking of all were the rules protecting female captives: a pirate who mistreated a woman taken prisoner faced severe punishment, and a pirate who took a captive as a wife was bound to remain faithful to her. Harsh as the world she ruled was, it ran by rules — and the rules were hers.

With this machine, she throttled the coast. Merchant ships were seized, coastal towns were raided, and ransoms were demanded up and down the Pearl River delta. The Qing empire sent its navy against her and was beaten again and again; ships sent to destroy her were captured and added to her own fleet. Portuguese and British vessels were drawn into the fight, and still she could not be broken. For a few years, one of the most powerful naval forces on the planet answered to a former brothel worker from Canton.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Roughly how large was her fleet, and why is it historically remarkable?

2. Give two rules from her code, including one that protected captives.

1. Tens of thousands of pirates (estimates of 40,000–80,000) and many hundreds of ships — far larger than any Western pirate operation; effectively a floating state.

2. Any two, e.g.: stolen goods had to be registered and shared by fixed rules; stealing from the common fund could mean death; villagers who paid were not to be harmed; female captives could not be mistreated, and a pirate who took one as a wife had to stay faithful.

Discuss:
  • Her fleet was built on robbery, yet it was governed by one of the strictest legal codes of its time. Can an organisation be both lawless and highly disciplined at once?

Part 3 — The retirement no one else got

Most feared outlaws in history end on a scaffold or a battlefield. Ching Shih did not. By 1810, the confederation was under growing pressure — worn down by the empire, by foreign navies, and by a bitter split between her own coloured fleets. Rather than wait to be crushed, she did something almost unheard of: she opened negotiations with the very government that had spent years trying to destroy her.

The terms she won were extraordinary. In the spring of 1810 an amnesty was agreed. The great majority of her pirates were pardoned; many kept their plunder, and a number were even taken into the imperial military. Ching Shih herself walked free — no execution, no prison. She surrendered her fleet on her own terms and kept the fortune she had made. Chang Pao was given an official naval rank and went on to hold a government post.

She lived out the rest of her life quietly, reportedly running a gambling house in Canton, and died around 1844, an old woman surrounded by her family. The most successful pirate who ever lived was never caught, never hanged, and never truly defeated — she simply decided when the story would end. Her long echo runs through every retelling since: in histories of the South China Sea, in fiction and film, she stands as the outlaw who out-thought an empire and, almost alone among pirates, was allowed to keep everything she had taken.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Instead of fighting to the end, what did Ching Shih do in 1810, and what did she gain by it?

2. How did the rest of her life, and Chang Pao's, turn out?

1. She negotiated a surrender and amnesty with the Qing government; most of her pirates were pardoned, many kept their loot, and she walked free with her fortune intact.

2. She lived quietly, reportedly running a gambling house, and died around 1844 among her family; Chang Pao was given a naval rank and an official position.

Discuss:
  • She is remembered less for how she fought than for how she quit — walking away rich and free. Is knowing when and how to stop a rarer skill than knowing how to win?

Grammar — the passive voice

Read the story again and you'll notice how often it says what was done rather than who did it: "ships were captured," "an amnesty was agreed," "the code was enforced." That's the passive voice — and history, news and formal narrative lean on it constantly, because the interesting thing is often the deed, not the doer.

How it's built: a form of be + the past participle.
• Present: "Merchant ships are seized."
• Past: "The navy was beaten." "Ransoms were demanded."
• With a modal: "A traitor could be put to death."
• Perfect: "The code is said to have been written down."
Why choose the passive?
• The doer is unknown, obvious, or unimportant: "Nothing is known of her early life."
• You want to put the result or victim first: "Coastal towns were raided."
• To sound formal, official or impersonal — the voice of records and law: "An amnesty was granted."
• If you do need the doer, add by: "Her fleet was held together by a strict code."

1. Make it passive

Rewrite the underlined active verb in the passive. Keep the same tense. (You don't need to name the doer.)

1. The Qing navy could not defeat her. → She could not by the Qing navy.

2. Her pirates enforced the code without mercy. → The code without mercy.

3. The government pardoned most of her crew. → Most of her crew .

1. be defeated (modal + be + past participle)

2. was enforced (past passive)

3. were pardoned (past passive, plural subject)

2. Correct form of the participle

Each sentence is passive but the verb in brackets is in the wrong form. Write the correct past participle.

1. Ships that were sent to destroy her were often (take) and added to her fleet.

2. An amnesty was (agree) in the spring of 1810.

3. A pirate who stole from the common fund could be (put) to death.

1. taken (irregular past participle of "take")

2. agreed (regular -ed)

3. put ("put" is the same in all three forms)

3. Tell it like a history book — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the passive of the verb in brackets, in the correct tense. Read it aloud once you're done.

Little (know) about her early life. By 1807 the confederation (lead) by Ching Shih, and along the coast merchant ships (seize) almost at will. In 1810 an amnesty (grant), and she (allow) to keep her fortune.

is known · was led (by Ching Shih) · were seized · was granted · was allowed

Notice how the passive keeps the spotlight on the events — the ships, the amnesty, the fortune — while the doers stay in the background.

Task B · Outlaw or ruler? (4 minutes)

Ching Shih ran a criminal empire built on robbery — yet with taxes, laws and a strict code of conduct, it looked a great deal like a government. Was she a criminal, a rebel leader, or something in between? Build an argument, and decide what really separates an "outlaw" from a "state".

Task C · Knowing when to stop (4 minutes)

Ching Shih is remarkable partly because she quit at the top — negotiating her way out instead of fighting to the death. Tell a story, real or well-known, about someone who walked away at the right moment (or who didn't, and paid for it). Use the grammar: what was achieved, what was risked, what was finally agreed.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Ching Shih rose from the very bottom of her society to command tens of thousands. How much of that do you put down to her own genius, and how much to being in the right chaos at the right time?
  2. Her code protected female captives more strictly than most laws of the age protected women on land. What do you make of an outlaw fleet being, in some ways, ahead of the society around it?
  3. She was never punished — she was pardoned and kept her fortune. Was justice served, or cheated?
