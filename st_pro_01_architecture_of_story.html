Stories are up to 22 times more memorable than facts alone
Cognitive psychologist Jerome Bruner, Actual Minds, Possible Worlds

Here's what's interesting: when neuroscientists at Princeton put people in an fMRI scanner and had them listen to a story, the listener's brain activity began to mirror the storyteller's — a phenomenon called neural coupling. The more the brains synced, the better the listener understood.

Facts inform. Stories synchronise.

But not all stories work. The ones that stick share something in common — and it's not charisma or talent. It's structure. The architecture underneath.

Today we're going to name three structures that the most effective communicators use — usually without knowing it — and put them in your hands deliberately.

Three Structures

Every great story you've ever heard uses one of these three patterns — or a combination.

1. Situation → Complication → Resolution

This is the workhorse of professional storytelling — and the default structure at firms like McKinsey. It works because it mirrors how the brain processes problems.

Situation
Here's where we are
→
Complication
Here's what changed
→
Resolution
Here's what we do
In the wild
"We were the market leader for a decade. Then two startups undercut our pricing by 40% in six months. So we rebuilt our entire distribution model — and came back stronger."

Notice: the complication creates tension. Without it, there's no story — just a report.

2. Before → After → Bridge

The transformation story. This one works because it lets the audience feel the gap between what was and what could be — and then shows the path across.

Before
The old world
→
After
The new world
→
Bridge
How we get there
In the wild
"Three years ago, onboarding took six weeks and we lost 30% of new hires in the first quarter. Today, people are productive in ten days and retention is at 91%. Here's what we changed."

The trick: paint the "After" vividly enough that people want to cross the bridge.

3. Nested Loops

The master storyteller's structure. You open a story — then open a second story inside it — then a third. You close them in reverse order. Each layer deepens the meaning of the one before it.

Story A opens
Story B opens
Story C (complete)
Story B closes
Story A closes
In the wild

Think of any great keynote or TED talk that begins with a personal anecdote, then moves to a case study, then drops a core insight — and then returns to the case study with new meaning, and finally lands back on the opening anecdote transformed. Steve Jobs's Stanford commencement speech uses exactly this structure — three stories nested inside a larger narrative about connecting the dots.

Quick reality check: These structures aren't rigid templates. They're bones. The best storytellers flex them, combine them, break them intentionally. But they always know which structure they're working from — even when they're improvising.

Spot the Structure

Can you hear the bones inside the story?

Two versions, same content

Here are two ways someone might talk about a company's digital transformation. Read both, then we'll discuss what makes them different.

Version A

"We implemented a new digital strategy. It involved moving to cloud infrastructure, retraining 2,000 staff, and redesigning our customer portal. The project took 18 months and came in under budget."

Version B

"In 2019, our average customer waited 11 minutes on the phone to do something they should have been able to do in 30 seconds. We were haemorrhaging loyalty. So we tore up the playbook — retrained 2,000 people, rebuilt the portal from scratch, moved everything to the cloud. Today, 89% of customer interactions happen without a single phone call."

What's the difference? Discuss this before revealing the analysis.

Version A is a report — a list of things that happened. There's no tension, no stakes, no human element.

Version B is SCR in action: Situation (customers waiting 11 minutes), Complication (haemorrhaging loyalty), Resolution (rebuilt everything, now 89% self-service). Same facts. Completely different impact.

The structure didn't add information. It added meaning.

What's the structure here?

Read this excerpt from a CEO's all-hands speech. Before looking at the answer — what structure do you think she's using, and why?

"When I started this company, I used to pack boxes in my garage until 2am. I want to tell you about that — but first, let me tell you about someone you've never heard of. Maria Gonzalez, warehouse shift lead in Phoenix. Last month she solved a routing problem that saved us $2.3 million. She did it with a spreadsheet and a question nobody else thought to ask. That question — 'what if we're optimising for the wrong thing?' — is the same question I was asking myself in that garage fifteen years ago."

Nested loops. She opens with her own story (Story A — the garage), then nests Maria's story inside it (Story B — the routing problem). Maria's story completes, then she returns to Story A, now transformed — the same question, fifteen years apart. The nesting creates a connection between the CEO and a warehouse worker that no org chart could.

SCR — Situation, Complication, Resolution: Establish the norm, introduce what disrupted it, show what was done about it. The complication creates the tension.

BAB — Before, After, Bridge: Paint the old world, paint the new world, show the path between them. The gap between Before and After creates desire.

Quick Diagnosis

For each scenario, choose the structure that would work best — and be ready to explain why. Some are ambiguous on purpose.

A CEO needs to explain why the company is pivoting from hardware to software.
A manager needs to justify an over-budget project to the board.
A founder wants to show investors that her company's mission connects to her personal history.
A consultant presenting a turnaround strategy to a struggling retail chain.
A keynote speaker opening a conference about the future of healthcare.

The real point: There's rarely one "correct" structure. The skill is being able to articulate why you chose the one you did — and what changes if you choose differently. That's the difference between someone who tells stories by instinct and someone who can coach storytelling.

1. Situation-Complication-Resolution (SCR), Before-After-Bridge (BAB), Nested Loops.

2. SCR is about a problem that needs solving — the complication drives the story. BAB is about a transformation — the gap between old and new creates desire. SCR creates urgency. BAB creates aspiration.

3. When you need to connect ideas across different levels — personal and professional, individual and organisational, past and present. Nested loops create meaning through resonance between stories, not through linear argument.
