Making a Point: "Who Wouldn't Want That?"

Questions that don't ask for information — they make a point, often more persuasively than a flat statement.

Spot the pattern

Three things people actually say:

  • "Who wouldn't want a bit more sleep?"
  • "Why would I lie to you about something like that?"
  • "Is it really that surprising she got the job — she's brilliant."

None of these expect a real answer. Before you check: what is each speaker actually telling you?

Check the pattern CLICK
These are rhetorical questions — the speaker already has the answer and isn't waiting for one. "Who wouldn't want more sleep?" means everyone would. "Why would I lie?" means I wouldn't, and there's no reason to think so. "Is it really that surprising...?" means no, not really — but sounds softer and more persuasive than just stating it flatly.
Who / What + wouldn't + verb ...? → implies "everyone would"
Why would + subject + verb ...? → implies "there's no good reason to"

From flat statement to rhetorical question

"Everyone would jump at a job like that."
→
"Who wouldn't jump at a job like that?"

Same meaning, but the question sounds like an invitation to agree, not a claim you have to defend.

Sounding Sure: "Isn't It Obvious?"

Negative questions to persuade, agree, or show a little surprise — not to genuinely ask.

Spot the pattern

Three real reactions:

  • "Isn't it obvious she's the best person for the job?"
  • "Didn't I tell you this would happen?"
  • "Wouldn't it be quicker just to call them instead of emailing?"

What's each speaker really doing — asking, or something else? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Each one is a strong opinion dressed up as a question, inviting the listener to agree. "Isn't it obvious...?" = it's obvious, don't you think? "Didn't I tell you...?" = a little "I told you so". "Wouldn't it be quicker...?" = a suggestion that sounds more like a shared idea than an order. The negative makes it feel like you're both already agreeing.
Isn't / Didn't / Wouldn't / Aren't + subject + verb ...? = a strong opinion, checking the listener agrees
The same words can also be a genuine question ("Didn't you get my message?" — real doubt). Tone of voice and context tell your listener which one you mean.

Your turn

1. I'm sure it would be cheaper to book online.

Say it as a negative question: " be cheaper to book online?"

2. I definitely warned her about the traffic.

Say it as a negative question: " warn her about the traffic?"

3. It's obvious the meeting ran too long.

Say it as a negative question: " obvious the meeting ran too long?"

Think of a decision you feel strongly about — where to eat, what to watch, which route to take. Say two negative questions that make your opinion sound like an obvious, shared idea.

Reacting to News: Tags & Echoes

When a question isn't checking information — it's reacting to it.

Same-way tags: reacting, not checking

You already know a tag question that genuinely checks something — the statement and tag point in opposite directions ("You're coming, aren't you?"). Now look at these:

  • "So you're the new manager, are you?"
  • "Oh, you think that's funny, do you?"
  • "You've read the whole book already, have you?"

None of these are really asking anything — the speaker already knows. What's different about the tags here? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
The statement and the tag are both positive — "are you", "do you", "have you", not the negative "aren't you". A same-way tag doesn't check a fact; it reacts to news you've just heard, usually with falling intonation, and can sound surprised, doubtful or a little challenging depending on your tone.
positive statement + positive tag (are you? / do you? / have you?) = reacting, not asking
positive statement + negative tag (aren't you? / don't you? / haven't you?) = genuinely checking

From statement to reacting tag

"You already know, then."
→
"Oh, you already know, do you?"

Echo questions: buying a second to react

Three moments of real surprise:

  • "I quit my job today." — "You did WHAT?"
  • "We're flying to Tokyo tomorrow." — "You're flying WHERE?"
  • "She's marrying him in June." — "She's marrying him WHEN?"

Look at the word order in each reply. Does it look like a normal question? Say what you notice before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
An echo question keeps the original word order — it doesn't invert like a normal question. The surprising part is simply replaced by a question word, stressed and spoken with rising intonation at the end. It shows real surprise and buys you a second to react — much stronger than just saying "Really?"
[statement word order] + question word (stressed, at the end) ...?
"You're moving WHERE?" · "She said WHAT?" · "He did WHEN?"

Intonation carries most of the meaning here. A falling tag ("...are you?" ↘) usually just comments; a rising one (↗) can sound like real doubt. An echo question always rises and lands hard on the stressed word: "You did WHAT?"
