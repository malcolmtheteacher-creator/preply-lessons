Ten items for talking about yourself

Work out the meaning from the context and say it out loud before you open it.

1 She had a strong track record: three product launches in five years, all on time and all profitable.
Explain it in your own words first.
1. Show the meaning
track record (n) — everything a person has achieved or failed at in the past, taken together as evidence. Example: "He has a track record of turning struggling teams around."
2 He had never worked in finance, but negotiating with suppliers is a transferable skill, and the panel knew it.
Explain it in your own words first.
2. Show the meaning
transferable (adj) — able to be used in a different job or setting from the one where it was learned. Example: "Managing volunteers gave me transferable skills in motivating people."
3 The panel wanted tangible results, not adjectives: a number, a date, a signed contract.
Explain it in your own words first.
3. Show the meaning
tangible (adj) — real and measurable, so that it can be clearly shown. Example: "The clearest tangible outcome was a 20% drop in complaints."
4 Her answer about the failed project was unusually candid. She said exactly what she had got wrong.
Explain it in your own words first.
4. Show the meaning
candid (adj) — honest and direct, especially about something uncomfortable. Example: "To be candid, I underestimated how long the handover would take."
5 Losing their biggest client was a serious setback, but it forced them to rethink the whole service.
Explain it in your own words first.
5. Show the meaning
setback (n) — a problem that delays progress or makes a situation worse for a while. Example: "The biggest setback of my career taught me to ask for help earlier."
6 Within two minutes he had built real rapport with the panel; they were laughing and leaning forward.
Explain it in your own words first.
6. Show the meaning
rapport (n) — a warm, easy relationship in which people understand each other well. Example: "I build rapport with new clients by asking about their week first."
7 She isn't a distant manager. She is hands-on, and still reviews the code herself every Friday.
Explain it in your own words first.
7. Show the meaning
hands-on (adj) — directly involved in doing the practical work, not only directing it. Example: "I have hands-on experience of running the night shift."
8 He clearly knew the subject but couldn't articulate why his approach was better than the alternatives.
Explain it in your own words first.
8. Show the meaning
articulate (v) — to express an idea clearly in words. Example: "She struggled to articulate what made the team so effective."
9 Asked about a gap in her CV, she answered briefly, then pivoted to what she had learned during that year.
Explain it in your own words first.
9. Show the meaning
pivot (v) — to turn deliberately from one subject or direction to another. Example: "When the budget was cut, we pivoted to a smaller pilot."
10 The first three months were a steep learning curve: new software, new market, new language.
Explain it in your own words first.
10. Show the meaning
learning curve (n) — the rate at which someone has to pick up new knowledge in an unfamiliar situation. Example: "Moving into management was a steep learning curve."

Point, Proof, Payoff

Almost every strong interview answer has the same three moves.

🎯
PointAnswer the question in one sentence, first.
🧾
ProofOne specific episode: what you did, and a tangible result.
🔗
PayoffWhat that means for this role, here, now.

Hear it once

"How do you handle a steep learning curve?"

I get hands-on quickly and ask a lot of questions early. When I moved into logistics I spent my first fortnight on the warehouse floor rather than at my desk, and by month two I had rewritten the returns process, which cut handling time by a third. This role is a new sector for me, and that is exactly how I would approach it.

1 Where does each move begin and end? What would be lost if the point came last?

What went wrong here?

"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision."

"Well, there are always disagreements in any team, that's natural. We had quite a few last year around the restructure, and people felt differently about it. In the end we all worked through it and things settled down."

1 Diagnose it out loud. Which of the three moves are missing?
Compare your diagnosis

No point: we never learn what the speaker actually does when they disagree. No proof: "we" throughout, no single episode, no personal action, no result. No payoff: nothing links it to the role.

Rescue the answer

Say what is wrong with each, then deliver a better version yourself.

1 "So in 2021 we had this client, a big retailer, and they'd been with us for years, and the account manager had just left, and anyway there was this contract renewal coming up…"
Compare

No point up front. After twenty seconds the listener still doesn't know what this story is evidence of. Open with the claim, then tell the story.

2 "I'm very strong at managing change. It's something I've always been good at, and people often say so. I'm just naturally adaptable."
Compare

All point, no proof. Three claims and no episode, no action, nothing tangible. One specific example would outweigh all three sentences.

3 "I cut onboarding time from six weeks to four by pairing each new starter with a mentor. It was a really good project."
Compare

Point and proof are there, but no payoff. It ends on the past. Finish by linking it to what this role needs.
