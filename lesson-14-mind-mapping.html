A tree in a notebook

London, July 1837. A young naturalist, Charles Darwin, has been back from his long voyage on the Beagle for less than a year. His head is full of facts: finches from one island and mockingbirds from another, fossils, shells, bones, and a question he is not sure he dares to ask. His notebooks are full of lists.

On one page of a small notebook he stops listing. He writes two words at the top, I think, and underneath he draws something that is not a list at all.

What did Darwin do differently on that page?
He stopped writing a list and drew a branching shape.
Talk about it. When you think hard about something, do you write lists, draw pictures, or talk? Which helps you most?

It is a small, rough drawing: a single line that splits, and splits again, like the branches of a tree. Some of the branches end. Some keep going. It shows his idea that all living things are related, descending from common ancestors, with new kinds branching off over time.

The drawing is famous today. It shows the thing a list cannot show: how ideas connect. A list puts every item in a row, and every item looks the same size. A branching drawing shows which things belong together, which come first, and which are the trunk and which only twigs.

What does a branching drawing show that a list cannot?
How things connect: which belong together, which come first, and which are main branches and which twigs.
Talk about it. Think of a subject you know well. How would it look as a tree, with the trunk, the big branches and the twigs?

In the 1970s the writer and educator Tony Buzan popularised a way of taking notes in this branching form, which he called mind mapping. He explained it in a BBC series, Use Your Head, and in a book of the same name. A mind map starts from one central idea and spreads out in all directions, with a key word on every line.

A map is a good memory tool for another reason. It is a picture of a subject, and you already know what to do with pictures. In this lesson you will draw one and then turn it into a house.

The rules of a good map

A mind map is a single page with a central image in the middle and branches spreading out from it. Each branch carries one idea; smaller branches carry details.

A subject on one page: centre, five main branches, and one detail on each.
  1. Start in the middle. Draw the main idea as a picture or a word in a shape. Use the whole page.
  2. Give each main idea its own branch. Three to seven main branches is a good number.
  3. Use one key word per line. Not sentences. Keep the words short and write them on the branch.
  4. Add detail on smaller branches. Each sub-branch comes from a main branch.
  5. Use colour and small pictures. A different colour for each main branch.

From map to palace

A map shows you the structure. A palace lets you keep it. The two fit together because both are made of places.

  1. Choose a palace with a room for each main branch. Five branches, five rooms.
  2. Turn each branch into a picture and put it in its room. Metaphysics could be a huge glowing question mark floating over the hall.
  3. Turn each sub-branch into a picture in a place within the room. Detail goes on furniture and walls.
  4. Walk it. From room to room, branch by branch.

Make your own map

Choose a subject you know well (a hobby, a job, a place). On paper, draw a central image and at least four main branches, each with one key word and two details. Then write the branches here.
Centre: Cooking. Branches: Equipment (knives, pans), Methods (roast, boil, fry), Ingredients (veg, meat, spices), Meals (breakfast, lunch, dinner). Colour-code each branch.

Turn it into a palace

Take your map. Choose a palace with the same number of rooms, and write a picture for each branch, placed in its room.
Hall: Equipment, a knife the size of a car on the doormat. Kitchen: Methods, a pan dancing on the stove. Pantry: Ingredients, a pile of vegetables with sunglasses. Dining room: Meals, a table laid for the whole street.
