The Client Who Shouts

His launch has slipped for the second time, and he has stopped being polite about it. You're the one who has to pick up the phone.

The Scene 🎬

It's twenty past nine on a Tuesday. You've had one coffee. The phone rings, you see the name, and you already know what the next fifteen minutes are going to be, because the date has moved for the second time and this morning somebody had to tell him.

Here's the thing nobody trains you for: he isn't wrong. That's what makes it hard. If he were being unreasonable you could quietly file him under difficult and feel fine about yourself. But the launch has genuinely slipped twice, he genuinely stood up in front of his own board and repeated what you told him, and now he's the one who looks bad. The anger coming down the line is doing a job — it's the sound of a man who has run out of ways to protect himself.

So there are two easy exits, and both of them cost you the client. You can cave: promise Friday, promise anything, say whatever makes the shouting stop, and be back here in a fortnight with less credit than you've got now. Or you can defend yourself: explain the staffing, explain the testing, be technically right and completely useless to him. The third option is harder and it's the only one that works — you stay on the line, you let the anger be there without flinching or matching it, and you slowly turn a row into a conversation about what actually happens next.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When someone raises their voice at you, what does your body do first — go quiet, go cold, apologise too much, or fire back? Be honest; that reflex is the thing you're working on today.
  2. Think of a time someone was angry with you and was at least partly right. Did you say so out loud? What stopped you, if you didn't?

Who's who

You
The One Who Rings Him ☎️

You run this account. You didn't cause the delay on your own, but you're the human he gets, and there's nobody behind you to hide behind. Your job today isn't to fix the date — you can't. It's to still have him as a client on Friday.

The other voice
Gavin Doyle 🔥

Your client, whose launch has slipped for the second time and who has stopped being polite about it. He's not a monster; he's a man with a paid-for venue, a boss of his own, and a team he's already stood down once.

Watch the handling: where the speaker agrees instead of explaining, where they say what they don't know, and the exact moment they stop absorbing and start steering. That turn is small and easy to miss.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Gavin is worth playing properly: let him actually be angry. And notice how little the calm one says.

Gavin—no, sorry, let me — I'm going to say this. Twice. Twice now. I stood up in front of my board in March and I said it's fine, they're on it, it's in hand. And it wasn't in hand, was it. It was nowhere near in hand.
YouNo. It wasn't. I can hear how angry you are, and I'm not going anywhere. Say the whole thing.
GavinOh, that's — right. Very calm. Very good. Have you got a document for this? Is there a bit of paper in front of you that tells you what to say to Gavin when Gavin's shouting?
YouThere isn't, no. Shout at me if it helps, but I'd rather we used the time.
GavinThe time. Right. Yeah. Because time's the thing I've got loads of. The venue's paid for — the venue's paid for, the invitations went out a fortnight ago, and I've got Amanda ringing me every hour, on the hour, asking whether she stands her team down again, and I don't — sorry, what was I — the point is I've got nothing to tell her. Again.
YouYou're right to be annoyed. I'd be exactly the same. If I'd told my board it was in hand and then had to walk back in there twice, I'd be — honestly, I'd probably be worse than this.
GavinSo what actually happened? And don't do the thing where you—
YouCan I stop you for a second — not to argue, just so I've got it right. When you say the launch, do you mean the whole thing, or the bit Amanda's team needs?
GavinThe bit Amanda needs. That's — well, that's the whole problem, isn't it, that's what everything else hangs off. Everything else can wait.
YouRight. Okay. Then let me tell you what I know and what I don't. I know the build's late because we lost two people in May and I was slow replacing them. I know it's not in testing yet. GavinHonestly? Something I can forward. Two lines, from you, in your name, that I can put in front of Amanda and the board — that doesn't read like I've been sold the same story a third time.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Gavin sneers that there must be a piece of paper telling you what to say to him. The reply doesn't defend itself and doesn't get offended — what does it do instead, and why does that land better than insisting you're being sincere?

2. At one point Gavin makes the lie very easy to tell — he even builds in his own excuse for when it falls apart. What exactly was he asking for, and what was he given instead?

3. Right at the end, Gavin says what he actually wants. It isn't the thing he'd spent the whole call shouting about. What was it — and what does that tell you about what the row was really about?

1. It agrees in two words and then hands him a choice: keep shouting, or use the time. Arguing about whether you're sincere is a fight you can't win, because the more you protest the more managed he feels, whereas offering to absorb the shouting proves the point instead of claiming it.
2. He asked for Friday, and said that even if Friday turned out to be wrong he'd cope, as long as he had a date to walk in with. He was given a smaller promise that could actually be kept — a date by five o'clock — plus the reason for refusing, which is that he's already carried two of your dates into that room.
3. Two lines in your name that he can forward to Amanda and the board. He'd been shouting about a deadline, but what he needed was something that made him look like a man who was being told the truth. Most of the heat wasn't about the date at all — it was about standing in front of his own people with nothing.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the call you'd rather not take. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick, and these in particular have to come out of your mouth without you thinking about it, because you won't have any thinking spare.

Say it like you mean it. Every one of these dies if you say it in a bright, helpful, customer-service voice, because that voice tells him he's being processed. Go slower than feels natural and a little lower, and let your sentences end flat rather than rising, since a rise sounds like you're asking his permission. Don't rush into his silences — if he stops, let it sit for two seconds before you speak. And don't smile it away with a laugh in your voice; he can hear that, and it reads as though you find this less serious than he does.

🧱 Staying in the room

Phrase 1

He's a minute into it and there's no sign of a gap. You need to say something that doesn't try to stop him and doesn't sound frightened. Which line tells him his anger hasn't moved you an inch?

A. "Please calm down, and then we can have a proper conversation."
B. "I can hear how angry you are, and I'm not going anywhere."
C. "Maybe I should ring you back when things have settled a bit."
B — "I can hear how angry you are, and I'm not going anywhere."
The first half names what's happening without judging it, and the second half — "I'm not going anywhere" — means I'm not leaving, I'm not passing you to somebody else, and I'm not going to get upset and hang up. That matters, because an angry person half expects you to escape, and when you say plainly that you won't, there's much less to fight against. A tells him his feeling is the problem, which is exactly the wrong message when he's right; C is the escape he was expecting.
Phrase 2

He's mocking you for being calm. You're not going to argue about that, but you do want the call to go somewhere. Complete it — three words that don't ask him to stop, but offer the shouting to him as something you'll take, if it does him any good: "Shout at me ___ ___ ___, but I'd rather we used the time."

"Shout at me if it helps, but I'd rather we used the time."
This works because it refuses the two obvious replies. You're not telling him off for shouting and you're not pretending the shouting is fine — you're saying you'll carry it if it does him any good, which quietly makes it his choice rather than his weapon. Nearly everyone stops shortly afterwards, not because they've been told to, but because shouting at someone who has openly agreed to be shouted at stops feeling like it's doing anything.
Phrase 3

He's just listed everything this has cost him. You want to agree — but there's a difference between a line that manages his feeling and a line that says the feeling is correct. Which one is doing the second thing?

A. "I completely understand your frustration, and I do apologise for the inconvenience."
B. "There's really no need to take this personally, Gavin."
C. "You're right to be annoyed. I'd be exactly the same."
C — "You're right to be annoyed. I'd be exactly the same."
"You're right to be annoyed" doesn't say I've noticed your feeling, it says your feeling is the correct one to have, which is a much bigger thing to hand somebody and costs you nothing if it's true. Then "I'd be exactly the same" puts you in his chair rather than above it. A is the sentence he's heard from every call centre he's ever rung, and he'll hear it as a script; B tells him his reaction is a fault of his, which will make him angrier and, worse, is untrue.

✋ Getting a word in without arguing

Phrase 4

You have to break in — but the second you do, he'll assume you're breaking in to defend yourself, and he'll talk straight over you. Put it in order:

just so I've got it right.Can I stop younot to argue,for a second —
"Can I stop you for a second — not to argue, just so I've got it right."
The middle of it is the clever bit. "Not to argue" answers the accusation he hasn't made yet, so he doesn't have to brace himself, and "just so I've got it right" makes the interruption a favour to him rather than a defence of you. Asking permission and then saying what the interruption isn't costs you two seconds and buys you the floor.
Phrase 5

He wants to know what happened. You know some of it and you genuinely don't know the rest. Complete it — the first half is easy; the four words that finish it are the half most people leave out, because it means admitting the holes: "Let me tell you what I know ___ ___ ___ ___."

"Let me tell you what I know and what I don't."
Saying it in advance changes how everything after it is heard. Once you've promised him both halves, the things you do know sound like facts rather than a selection, and the gaps sound like honesty rather than dodging — whereas if you only offer the good half, he spends the whole call hunting for what you're leaving out. It also protects you later, because you've told him at the start which parts might move.

🪞 Owning it

Phrase 6

There are reasons — real ones, and they're not all yours. Every instinct says explain them. Don't. Put it in order:

It slipped,I'm not going toand that's on us.defend it.
"I'm not going to defend it. It slipped, and that's on us."
When a date slips it quietly moves later than planned, and that's on us means the responsibility is ours — "on" someone here means it's their fault to carry. Saying you won't defend it, and then not defending it, is the whole trick: the explanation may be true, but to him it's just the noise a company makes before it does the same thing again. Taking it plainly, in one short sentence, ends that part of the call instead of extending it.
Phrase 7

He's asking you to say something you can't stand behind, and he's made it easy — he's even said he'll forgive you if it turns out wrong. You're going to refuse. Which line refuses without making him wrong for having asked?

A. "I'm not allowed to give out dates that aren't confirmed, I'm afraid."
B. "I'd rather tell you something true than something you want to hear."
C. "You know I can't do that, Gavin. Come on."
B — "I'd rather tell you something true than something you want to hear."
It refuses him without making him wrong for asking, because the sentence is about what you'll allow yourself to say rather than about his behaviour. It also does something useful for later: you've now told him that when you do give him good news, it's the same kind of news as the bad, which is the only reason he'll believe the next thing you say. A hides behind a rule, so the honesty stops being yours and becomes something the company made you do; C is a refusal that tells him off for asking, and he asked because you've let him down twice.

🎯 Turning it into something useful

Phrase 8

The heat has gone out of it. There's a silence. You could apologise again, or list what you're doing about it — both are about you. Ask him one short question instead, in your own words: what would you ask?

"What would actually help you today?"
The two words doing the work are "actually" and "today". "Actually" quietly admits that plenty of what you could offer wouldn't help at all, and "today" shrinks it to something he can answer, because he probably can't tell you what would fix the whole mess but he does know what he needs before he goes home. Almost always the answer is smaller than the row was, and it's the first question in the whole call that he gets to decide.
Phrase 9

He wants the date. You haven't got the date. But you have got something — a small thing you can absolutely guarantee. Put it in order:

a date by five o'clock.I can promise youI can't promise Friday.
"I can't promise Friday. I can promise you a date by five o'clock."
The shape is what makes it work: the refusal comes first and is one short sentence, then the same verb comes back with something you can genuinely deliver, so he's left holding a promise rather than a no. Notice how small the promise is — not the outcome he wants, just a time by which he'll know. Small promises kept are the only way back from two broken big ones.
Phrase 10

You've said you'll ring at five. He half expects you to go quiet if the news is bad, because that's what people do. Complete it — two words meaning whether there's good news or none at all: "I'll ring you at five ___ ___, even if there's nothing to say."

"I'll ring you at five either way, even if there's nothing to say."
"Either way" means whichever way it turns out, and here it closes the escape route he's expecting you to take. The second half is the promise that actually rebuilds something, because the silence is what did the damage last time, not the delay — he could have handled a later date; what he couldn't handle was finding out late. A call with no news in it is still a call that proves you're there.

Not for "an angry client" — for the one you've actually got, or the one you know is coming. Write the real name, the real thing they're angry about, and say each answer out loud until it doesn't sound like a line.

The Twist

🎯 Ten to five

You had a plan and it was a good one: get the date, send the two lines, ring at five, live to fight another day. Here's what's on your screen at ten to five. The date isn't Friday and it isn't next week — it's the 8th, three weeks away. And the reason it's the 8th is that your own company sold him something it hadn't built, which you didn't know in March and do know now. Your director has just walked past your desk and said, without stopping, "don't put any of that in writing." He is expecting two lines he can forward to his board, and a call from you, in ten minutes. Start the timer. Ring him. You have to decide, out loud and in real time, what goes in the two lines, what you say and don't say about the 8th, and whether "I'd rather tell you something true" survives contact with your own boss. No rehearsing first — that's the exercise.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I let him be angry without flinching, apologising over and over, or getting cold.
  • I said he was right to be annoyed, and meant it, before I said anything else.
  • I didn't explain, defend, or blame anyone else — not even once, and not even when I had a good reason to hand.
  • I refused the date he wanted and gave him a smaller promise I can genuinely keep.
  • I asked him what would help, and the call ended with something he can use, not just something he'd heard.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that'll go wrong on a real call. Run the twist again and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Anger is nearly always the second feeling. Underneath Gavin's there's a man who repeated your words to people who trusted him, and it's embarrassment doing the shouting — which is why explaining yourself makes it worse, because the explanation is about you and the problem is about him.

The odd part is that these calls, handled properly, don't cost you the client. They're often where you get one. Anyone can be pleasant while things go to plan; he now knows what you do when they don't, and there's no other way he could have found that out.

None of which makes it pleasant. You'll still see the name on the screen and feel it in your stomach. You just pick the phone up anyway, and say the true thing, and ring at five.

Two bigger questions

  1. Where you work, is it safer to give a comforting date you'll miss or an uncomfortable one you'll hit? Be honest about what actually gets rewarded — and if it's the first, what does that do to everyone over a few years?
  2. "Stay calm while someone shouts at you" is fine advice for the person holding the account. Is it fine advice for a junior on a helpline being shouted at forty times a day? Where's the line between a professional skill and something a company is quietly asking its staff to absorb for free?
