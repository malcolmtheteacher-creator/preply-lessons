A Child's Eye on History

A revolution and a war, told by a girl who understands only half of what she sees — and a reader who understands more.

Today: you'll understand how a child narrator shapes a story about revolution and war, and use a grid of knowing to explain irony, so you can write and talk about a narrator's perspective with precision and the right amount of caution.
Before we start — talk these through:
  • How much did you understand about the world's big events when you were ten? What did you get wrong?
  • Why might an author choose a child to narrate something frightening — rather than an adult?
  • Have you ever looked back at an event and understood something you could not see at the time?

The vocabulary of this story

Ten items. Say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

Read the sentence, guess the meaning of the word in bold, then reveal.

The narrator is an adult looking back, but she tells the story through the eyes of the child she used to be.
narrator (noun) — the voice that tells a story; the person or character who is telling it.
The girl is naive: she believes what she is told at school until her parents show her another side.
naive (adjective) — too trusting, or lacking experience of how the world really works.
There is a quiet irony when a child proudly repeats the slogan of the very system that will harm her family.
irony (noun) — a gap between what is said or expected and what is actually true; often it makes the reader smile or wince.
With hindsight, the adult author can explain what the child could never have understood at the time.
hindsight (noun) — understanding an event only after it has happened and you can see the whole picture.

B · The vocabulary of political change

Same method: guess from the sentence, then reveal.

After the revolution, the old government was gone and a completely new set of rules began.
revolution (noun) — a sudden, large change in government or society, often brought about by the people.
People feared the new regime, because it controlled how they dressed, what they read and who they met.
regime (noun) — a government, especially one that is strict or that people did not choose freely.
The posters in the street were propaganda: pictures and slogans designed to make people believe one version of events.
propaganda (noun) — information, often biased or misleading, used to promote a political cause.

C · Looking and feeling

Three words you will need to discuss the child's view. Guess, then reveal.

The government called the dead soldiers martyrs: people honoured for dying for a cause.
martyr (noun) — a person who dies for their beliefs or for a cause, and is honoured for it.
She was bewildered by the new rules, which seemed to change from one week to the next.
bewildered (adjective) — very confused, unable to understand what is happening.
A story told from a child's perspective shows us only what the child can see, hear and understand.
perspective (noun) — the particular point of view from which something is seen or described.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — 1979: a country changes overnight

For many years Iran was ruled by a king, the Shah. By the late 1970s many Iranians were angry about his rule, about the secret police and about the gap between rich and poor. In January 1979 the Shah left the country, and after months of protest and uncertainty the monarchy was replaced by an Islamic Republic.

The change was a revolution: people from very different groups — students, workers, religious leaders, left-wing families — had demanded the end of the old order, and at first many of them celebrated. The family at the centre of Persepolis, who held left-wing, educated views, is among those who hope for a freer country.

In the book, Marji is about ten years old when it happens. The first volume shows how an ordinary child feels the revolution: not in speeches, but in what changes at school, on the street and at the dinner table.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened in Iran in 1979?

2. Who had demanded change before the revolution?

3. Through what does Marji experience the revolution?

1. The Shah left, and the monarchy was replaced by an Islamic Republic after a revolution.

2. Many different groups — students, workers, religious leaders and left-wing families.

3. Through everyday changes: at school, on the street and at home, not through speeches.

Discuss:
  • Why do revolutions often begin with many people who want different things? What can go wrong afterwards?

Part 2 — The new rules and the war

Soon the hopes of many people, including Marji's family, turned to worry. The new regime introduced strict rules about behaviour and dress. In schools, girls had to wear the veil, and boys and girls were separated. Books, music and parties that had been normal were now dangerous.

In September 1980 Iraq attacked Iran, and a long and very costly war began; it lasted until 1988. The state used propaganda to encourage young people to fight, and it called those who died martyrs. The book shows young boys being given plastic keys said to open the gates of paradise.

Not everyone believed these messages. At home, Marji's parents tell their daughter a different story from the one she hears at school, and they sometimes take risks to do so. Her uncle Anoosh, a former political prisoner, is arrested again and does not survive. This is where the book's lightness stops and the grief begins.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What changed in schools after the revolution?

2. When did the war begin and end?

3. Who tells Marji a different version of events from the school?

1. Girls had to wear the veil and boys and girls were separated.

2. It began in September 1980 and lasted until 1988.

3. Her parents, who tell her what they really think at home.

Discuss:
  • How should an author handle death and violence when the narrator is a child? What do you gain or lose by showing it through her eyes?

Part 3 — The girl who asks questions

Marji is curious and stubborn. She argues with her teachers, tests the official story against what she hears at home and sometimes holds imaginary conversations with God. Her questions — direct, sometimes funny, sometimes devastating — are how the book makes sense of an enormous subject.

But the book has two voices. One is the child, who is often naive or bewildered. The other is the adult author, looking back with hindsight, who chooses what to draw and what to leave out. Often the child sees only part of a situation, and we, with the adult's help, see more.

That gap between what the child understands and what the reader understands is where much of the book's irony and feeling lives. It lets Satrapi write about political violence without a lecture: a child's question can do the work of a whole essay.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Marji react to the official story at school?

2. What are the two voices in the book?

3. Where does the book's irony come from?

1. She questions it and compares it with what she hears at home.

2. The child, who is often naive or bewildered, and the adult author, looking back with hindsight.

3. From the gap between what the child understands and what the reader understands.

Discuss:
  • Does a child narrator make a political story more powerful or less reliable? Can it be both?

How the tool works

When a child narrates, there are always two people reading the same scene: the child, who sees part of it, and the reader, who may see more. The Two Levels of Knowing helps you say exactly what is happening in that gap.

Using it, step by step

  1. Child. What does the narrator, at that age, understand about this moment? Look at her words and her reaction.
  2. Reader. What do we know that she does not — from history, from earlier pages, from the adult author's captions?
  3. Place it. Put the moment on the grid: is it irony, shared understanding, mystery or hidden knowledge?
  4. Effect. Say what that does: By letting the child see only …, the author makes the reader …

Try it yourself

Each sentence describes an imagined moment in a story with a child narrator. Where does it sit on the grid?

1. A child proudly wears a badge from a march and calls the day a game. The reader knows the march ended in violence.
2. The child and the reader both realise, at the same moment, that a friend will not be coming back.
3. A child hears adults whispering a name; neither she nor the reader knows who it is.
4. A child explains calmly how to behave at a checkpoint — knowledge the reader has never needed.
5. A child laughs at a newspaper headline whose meaning the reader fully understands.
6. The adult narrator explains an event, and the child's reaction makes its meaning clear to both.
7. A character gives an order, but no one can tell yet who it comes from or why.
8. A child knows from experience which neighbours can be trusted; the reader must learn this from her.

1. Dramatic irony — the reader knows more than the child, and the contrast creates tension.

2. Shared understanding — both understand together; no gap between them.

3. Mystery — neither the child nor the reader has the information yet.

4. Hidden knowledge — the child knows more than the reader about this world.

5. Dramatic irony — the reader sees the seriousness the child misses.

6. Shared understanding — narrator, child and reader all understand together.

7. Mystery — the source and purpose are unknown to everyone.

8. Hidden knowledge — the child holds knowledge the reader lacks.

Where the tool breaks

The child is not the author. A moment of irony may come from the adult's choice to draw it that way, not from the child. Always ask who is controlling what we see.

Moments move. A scene can be mystery on one page and dramatic irony on the next once we learn more. Place the moment on the page you are analysing, not the whole book.

Do not call a narrator unreliable too fast. A child who sees only part of the picture is limited, not necessarily dishonest.

Talk it through:
  • Which box on the grid do you think a story about war uses most — and why?
  • Can you place a moment from a film or novel you know on the grid?

Language — Hedging an interpretation

A narrator's meaning is rarely certain, so strong writers use careful language: it suggests, it may be, it could be read as. This is not weakness. It shows that you can tell evidence from opinion.

1 · Verbs of interpretation. suggests, implies, hints at, invites us to, seems to: “The simple drawing suggests that the child does not fully grasp the danger.” Avoid proves or shows clearly unless the evidence is beyond doubt.
2 · Modal verbs. may, might, could: “This could be read as a criticism of propaganda.” Use must only for something that logically cannot be otherwise.
3 · Appears to, seems to. “The narrator appears to believe the slogan, yet the caption seems to mock it.”
4 · Frames for a claim. “It could be argued that…”, “One reading is that…”, “This is perhaps intended to…”.
5 · Do not hedge everything. If the evidence is clear, say so: “The caption states the date.” Hedge the interpretation, not the facts.

1. Choose the better word for an interpretation

Choose the word that fits the cautious style of analysis.

1. The child's calm voice (suggests / proves) that she does not understand the danger.

2. The empty gutter (may / must) be intended to make the reader supply the missing event.

3. The caption gives the year as 1980, so we can state that the scene (takes place / may take place) in 1980.

4. Her question (appears to / will) be innocent, but it exposes a contradiction.

5. It (could be argued / is proved) that the author uses simplicity to avoid exoticising her characters.

1. suggests (an interpretation, not a proof)

2. may (an interpretation, not a certainty)

3. takes place (the year is given — a fact, so no hedging)

4. appears to (a careful reading)

5. could be argued (a cautious frame for an interpretation)

2. Soften the claim

Each claim is too strong for what the evidence shows. Rewrite it, using one hedging word or phrase. (Model answers are suggestions; any careful version is fine.)

1. The drawing proves the child feels no fear.

2. The empty panel means the author wants us to feel guilty.

3. The child's laughter shows she is stupid.

4. The adult narrator is lying about what happened.

1. The drawing suggests the child feels little fear.

2. The empty panel may be intended to leave the reader uneasy.

3. The child's laughter suggests she does not yet understand the situation.

4. The adult narrator may be selecting and shaping what happened.

3. Where would a strong writer not hedge?

Choose the sentence that should NOT be hedged. Write the number.

1. (a) The caption names the year. (b) The caption might name the year. (c) The caption may well name the year. →

2. (a) The panel could possibly show a street. (b) The panel seems perhaps to show a street. (c) The panel shows a street, drawn in thick black lines. →

1. (a) — the caption actually names the year; that is a fact.

2. (c) — what the panel shows is visible; hedge what it means, not what it contains.

Now say three sentences about a narrator you know — a book or a film — using suggests, could be read as and appears to.
