Renting a Flat

The photos looked perfect. Now you're standing in the real thing — and the real work is asking the right questions before you sign anything.

The Scene 🎬

You've been flat-hunting for three weekends straight, and your inbox is a graveyard of places that were "cosy" (tiny), "characterful" (falling apart) or gone before you could reply. This one, though — a one-bedroom flat above a bakery, top of your budget but not over it — actually looks like somewhere you could live.

The letting agent meets you at the door, jangling a ring of keys and glancing at their watch. There's another viewing after yours. So you've got fifteen minutes to work out whether the photos were telling the truth, spot anything the listing conveniently left out, and — if you like it — see whether that rent has any give in it at all.

Because a flat is never just the flat. It's the bills, the deposit, the neighbours, the damp patch behind the wardrobe, and the small print you'll be living inside for a year. Time to find out what you're really signing up for.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of a place you've lived. What's the one thing you wish you'd asked before you moved in — and what did it end up costing you?
  2. When you walk into somewhere new, what tells you in the first ten seconds whether you could be happy there? Is it something you can see, hear, or just feel?

Who's who

You
The Flat-Hunter 🔑

Tired of viewings, but not desperate. You've got a budget, a few non-negotiables, and enough sense to smile while you ask the awkward questions.

The other voice
The Letting Agent 🏠

Polished, upbeat, a little rushed. Wants the flat off their books today — which means, if you play it right, there may be more room to move than they'll admit at first.

When you're the flat-hunter

🚪 Start warmly
"Thanks for fitting me in at such short notice."
"I'll be honest, I've got a couple of other viewings lined up today, but this one caught my eye."
🔍 Ask the facts
"Could you give me a rough idea of what the bills come to on top of the rent?"
"What's the notice period if I ever need to leave?"
⚠ Raise a concern
"I did notice a bit of damp by the window. Is that something that's been looked at, or…?"
"Could you tell me about the deposit?"
💷 Say the price is a stretch
"I love the flat, but it's a touch more than I was hoping to pay."
"Is there any movement on the rent at all?"
🤝 Offer something
"If I were to sign for two years rather than one, is that something you could reflect in the monthly rent?"
"Could you meet me halfway?"
🌟 Leave the door open
"I'm really keen. Could I take your number and come back to you first thing tomorrow, once I've slept on it?"
"Thanks so much for showing me round."

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Listen for the softeners, the hedges, and the moment a firm "the rent's fixed" quietly turns into "let me have a word with the landlord".

AgentCome on in! Mind the step. So — this is the one. South-facing, quiet street, and that bakery smell downstairs comes free with the flat. What do you think?
YouIt's lovely, actually — better than the photos, which is a first this month. Do you mind if I have a proper look round while I fire a few questions at you?
AgentFire away, that's what I'm here for. Kitchen's just been redone, as you can see, and the boiler's only two years old.
YouGood to know. Could you give me a rough idea of what the bills come to on top of the rent? I'm trying to work out the real monthly figure, if that makes sense.
AgentOf course — the last tenants said gas and electric came to about ninety a month, and it's council tax band C. Water's metered, so that's on usage.
YouThat's helpful, thanks. And just so I'm clear — the deposit's held in one of the government protection schemes, is it? I got caught out on that once.
AgentAbsolutely, all above board — it goes straight into a registered scheme and you get the certificate within thirty days. Nothing dodgy here.
YouGlad to hear it. I did notice a bit of damp coming through by the bedroom window, though — is that something that's been looked at, or…?
AgentAh — yes, spotted that myself. The landlord's booked someone to sort the seal on that window next week. It's on the list, I promise.
YouFair enough — I'd want that in writing before I moved in, if it came to it. Can I ask what the notice period is, on both sides, once the first year's up?
AgentTwo months from the landlord, one month from you, once you're past the fixed term. Pretty standard. Honestly, they're after a long-term tenant — no interest in churn.
YouWell, that's me — I'm not planning on moving again for a long time. Which brings me to the rent. I love the flat, but it's a touch more than I was hoping to pay. Is there any movement on it at all?
AgentIt's priced pretty keenly already, to be fair… but I hear you. What did you have in mind?
YouWell — if I were to sign for two years rather than one, is that something you could reflect in the monthly rent? You get your long-term tenant, I get a little breathing room. Could you meet me halfway?
AgentYou know what — that's not unreasonable, and a two-year tenant is gold to this landlord. I can't promise anything, but let me have a word with them this afternoon and come back to you.
YouThat's all I can ask. I'm really keen — genuinely the best thing I've seen. Could I take your number and come back to you first thing tomorrow, once I've slept on it?
AgentCourse you can. Sleep on it, I'll speak to the landlord, and let's talk in the morning. Mind the step on your way out — and grab a loaf downstairs while they're warm.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The rent is "a touch more" than the flat-hunter wanted to pay. What exactly do they offer the landlord in exchange for a lower rent — and why is that offer clever rather than just cheeky?

2. The agent says the damp "is on the list". The flat-hunter is polite about it — but not fully reassured. What single condition do they attach, and what does that tell you about them?

3. At the end, the flat-hunter loves the flat but doesn't agree to anything. How do they stay warm and keen while still keeping their options open — and why is that smart?

1. They offer to sign a two-year tenancy instead of one, in return for a lower monthly rent. It's clever because it gives the landlord exactly what they've said they want — a long-term, no-churn tenant — so the flat-hunter isn't just asking for a discount, they're trading something the other side values. Both sides win: "you get your long-term tenant, I get a little breathing room."
2. They say they'd want the damp repair "in writing before I moved in." That shows they're friendly but not naïve — a verbal promise on the doorstep isn't enough; they want it on paper where it counts.
3. They praise the flat sincerely ("genuinely the best thing I've seen"), so the agent knows they're a serious buyer worth chasing — but they ask to "sleep on it" and come back tomorrow rather than signing on the spot. That keeps the pressure on the agent to deliver on the rent, and leaves the flat-hunter free to walk away if the answer's no.

  1. Start warmly. Be friendly and show you are serious.
  2. Ask the facts. Get the real costs and terms.
  3. Raise a concern. Ask about a problem without accusing.
  4. Say the price is a stretch. Be honest, then open a door.
  5. Offer something. Trade something for a better deal.
  6. Leave the door open. Close without committing.

The Echo 🕰️

In September 1666, a spark in a baker's shop on Pudding Lane grew into four days of fire that ate the medieval City of London — some thirteen thousand houses gone, and with them the little wooden world of tenants, landlords and rented rooms that had stood for centuries.

What rose from the ash was a different city: wider streets, brick instead of timber, and a fresh scramble over who owned which plot, what the ground was worth, and what a landlord could charge to let it. The paperwork of modern renting — leases, terms, disputes over who fixes what — was forged in that rebuilding.

So when you stand in a flat above a bakery, haggling gently over the rent, you're playing a very London game. People have been doing it, on these streets, since long before the fire — and, it turns out, right through the flames.

Two bigger questions

  1. After the Great Fire, London had a rare chance to start again from nothing — and mostly rebuilt on the same old street lines anyway. When you get a blank slate, why is it so hard to build something genuinely new instead of copying what was there before?
  2. A rented flat is your home, but it isn't yours — someone else owns the walls, sets the rules, and can ask you to leave. Where's the line between a house and a home, and can a place you rent ever truly be "yours"?
