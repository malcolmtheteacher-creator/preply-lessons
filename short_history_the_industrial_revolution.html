The ten words, one by one

Say what each means, then reveal.

1. to harness — a river? the wind?
to harness (v) — to bring a natural force under control and put it to work: to harness steam, water or wind.
2. to mechanise — what happens to the job?
to mechanise (v) — to change a process so machines do work once done by hand. Noun: mechanisation.
3. a mill — what is made in one?
a mill (n) — a building of machinery for grinding grain or, here, for spinning and weaving cloth.
4. output — quality or quantity?
output (n) — the amount a person, machine or country produces in a given time. Opposite idea: input.
5. a workforce — one person, or many?
a workforce (n) — all the people who work for one company, industry or country, taken together.
6. urbanisation — what root hides inside it?
urbanisation (n) — the process by which more of a population comes to live in towns rather than the countryside. Adjective: urban.
7. squalor — poor, dirty, or both?
squalor (n) — filthy, degraded living conditions caused by poverty and neglect. Adjective: squalid.
8. exploitation — who, and to whom?
exploitation (n) — treating people unfairly to profit from their work. Verb: to exploit.
9. unrest — how far short of war?
unrest (n) — angry, restless dissatisfaction in a population, shown in protests, strikes or riots.
10. prosperity — stronger than comfort?
prosperity (n) — wealth and success, especially when shared widely across a society. Adjective: prosperous.

The story, in three parts

Part 1 — Why here, why then

For almost all of human history, the amount one person could make in a day had a ceiling, and that ceiling was muscle. A weaver worked at a hand loom in a cottage; a miller ground corn with the help of a stream or the wind. Then, in Britain from roughly the 1760s, something began that nobody planned and nobody yet had a name for. Within a century, cloth was being made in buildings the size of cathedrals, iron was being poured in quantities that would have astonished a medieval kingdom, and people travelled faster than a galloping horse. Historians argue about the dates, and some dislike the word "revolution" at all, since the change took three or four generations rather than a single year. But the shape of it is not in doubt: between the reign of George III and the reign of Victoria, Britain stopped being a country of villages and became a country of towns, and the way people made their living was rebuilt from the ground up.

Why Britain, and why then? It is safer to give several answers than one. Britain sat on abundant coal, much of it close to the surface and to water. Through a burst of canal-building from the 1760s, and later through the railways, that coal could be moved cheaply to wherever it was wanted. Changes in farming meant fewer hands were needed on the land, so labour was available. Capital and raw materials flowed in through a colonial and slave-trading empire: it must be said plainly that the cotton industry, the spearhead of the whole transformation, ran on cotton grown by enslaved people in the American South. Patent law gave inventors a reason to invent, and a culture of practical tinkering turned ideas into machines. Banks and a stable government made borrowing possible. No single one of these explains the whole thing, and you are free to disagree with the historians about which mattered most.

The device at the centre of the story was not invented in this period. Thomas Newcomen's atmospheric engine had been pumping water out of mines since the early eighteenth century; it worked, but it swallowed coal. In the 1760s James Watt, an instrument-maker in Glasgow, realised that enormous energy was being wasted by heating and cooling the same cylinder over and over, and solved the problem by adding a separate condenser. The idea reached the world thanks to the Birmingham manufacturer Matthew Boulton, who had the capital and the factory, and who went into partnership with Watt in the 1770s. Between them they made it possible to harness steam almost anywhere — not only beside a coal pit or a river. That is the hinge of the whole story: once power could be placed wherever an owner wished, output stopped depending on the weather or the flow of a stream, and depended only on how much coal could be fed to the fire.

1. Any three of: cheap accessible coal; canals and railways to move it; agricultural change freeing labour; capital and raw cotton from a colonial and slave-trading empire; patent law and practical invention; stable finance. No single factor is agreed on.

Part 2 — The mill, the mine and the railway

The first industry to be thoroughly mechanised was cotton. A run of inventions — Hargreaves's spinning jenny, Arkwright's water frame, Crompton's mule and later Cartwright's power loom — meant that thread and cloth could be produced faster than by any number of hands. The consequence was social as much as technical: work left the home. A hand loom could sit in a cottage; a water frame and an engine could not. So the work went to the machine, and the workers went with it. Arkwright's mill at Cromford, opened in the early 1770s, is often taken as the model. Inside, a large workforce kept the machinery running by day and, by artificial light, into the night. Time itself was reorganised: the seasons gave way to the factory bell, and lateness became an offence for which a worker could be fined. Twelve- and fourteen-hour days were ordinary, the machines were unguarded, and children — some no more than five or six — were employed precisely because they were small enough to crawl beneath the moving frames.

Beneath the mills lay the mines, and beneath the mines lay the whole system, because everything ran on coal. Men, women and children worked underground in the dark, dragging tubs of coal along low tunnels, with flooding, roof falls and gas explosions ever possible. Above ground, the towns swelled. Manchester, Leeds, Glasgow and Birmingham grew at a pace no authority was equipped to manage, and this sudden urbanisation created a crisis that had nothing to do with machinery at all. Back-to-back houses were thrown up without drains and without clean water; whole families shared a single room. The result was squalor of a kind that shocked even visitors who thought they knew what poverty looked like. Cholera arrived in the 1830s and returned again and again, killing rich and poor but the poor far more efficiently, and only slowly was the link between filth, water and disease understood and acted upon.

Then came the railway, and distance stopped meaning what it had always meant. The Stockton and Darlington line opened in 1825 and the Liverpool and Manchester in 1830, and within two decades Britain was laced with track. Goods that had crawled by canal now moved in hours; people who had never left their county travelled to the sea, to the capital, to a new job. The effect on time was stranger still. Every town had kept its own local noon, differing from London's by a few minutes — an irrelevance until a timetable made it a danger. The railway companies imposed a single time along their lines, and through that quiet decision the country was persuaded to standardise its clocks. It is a small example of a large truth: a change made for the convenience of an industry reached into every pocket in the country, and nobody had voted for it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the new machines force work out of the home and into the mill?

2. Name two ways the railway changed ordinary life beyond simply moving goods faster.

1. The new machinery — the water frame, above all the steam engine — was too large for a cottage, so workers came to the machine. Factory discipline came with it: the bell, the clock, fines for lateness, very long days.

2. Any two of: ordinary people could travel far beyond their county; distance collapsed; and the country had to standardise its clocks, since timetables could not work while every town kept its own noon.

Part 3 — The reckoning

People did not accept all this quietly. Between roughly 1811 and 1816, groups of skilled textile workers remembered as the Luddites broke into workshops at night and smashed the frames that were making their trade obsolete. They are often dismissed as men who simply hated machines; in fact their quarrel was with employers who used machinery to cut wages and replace trained hands with cheap unskilled labour. The government answered with troops and the gallows. In 1819 a huge crowd gathered at St Peter's Field in Manchester to demand parliamentary reform, cavalry were sent in, and a number of people were killed and hundreds injured — an event named Peterloo in bitter imitation of Waterloo. Out of this long unrest grew the trade unions. Reform came slowly: Factory Acts from the 1830s limited the hours children could work and sent inspectors to check; an Act in 1842 removed women and the youngest boys from underground work; and public health legislation from the late 1840s began at last to treat sanitation as a duty of government. What reformers called exploitation, mill owners called business.

Did ordinary people actually become better off? This is the great quarrel among historians of the period, and it has been running for over a century. One side points to cheaper cloth, cheaper goods, longer lives and a population growing as it never had before. The other points to evidence that real wages moved very little for decades after the machines arrived, while hours lengthened, cities poisoned their inhabitants and skilled trades were destroyed. The most careful modern answer is uncomfortable: the gains were real but late, so that a generation, perhaps two, paid the price for a prosperity their grandchildren enjoyed. Meanwhile the model spread. Belgium industrialised early, followed by France and the German states, each borrowing British techniques and engineers; the United States went further and faster; and from the 1870s Japan compressed the process into a few decades.

The echoes are still sounding, and they are not simple. Mass production eventually made the consumer economy possible — the ordinary abundance of clothes, tools, medicines and machines we take for granted. The movement it began has never stopped: for the first time in history, most of humanity now lives in cities rather than in fields. It created industrial capitalism and, in the same motion, organised labour, since factories that gathered thousands of workers under one roof also taught them that they could act together. It is no accident that Karl Marx wrote his analysis of capital in London, using the reports of British factory inspectors as evidence. And there is one echo that would have astonished everybody involved: the fossil-fuel economy begun in these decades is the root of the climate crisis we are now trying to escape. That is what makes the final accounting so hard — the same century that lifted so many out of hunger also lit a fire its grandchildren are still trying to put out.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were the Luddites really objecting to, and what did reform eventually deliver?

2. Summarise the historians' quarrel about living standards in two sentences.

1. Not machines as such, but employers using them to cut wages and replace skilled workers with cheap labour. Over decades came trade unions, Factory Acts limiting children's hours with inspectors to enforce them, the 1842 Mines Act, and public health legislation.

2. One side stresses cheaper goods, longer lives and a growing population; the other, real wages that barely moved for decades, longer hours, disease and destroyed trades. The likeliest answer: the gains were real but late, so one or two generations paid for what their descendants enjoyed.

Discuss:
  • The same transformation produced modern abundance and the climate crisis. Can one verdict be given on it — or is "was it worth it?" the wrong question to ask about history?

Grammar — means and instrument

This whole story answers the question how? How was the engine improved? By adding a separate condenser. How was coal moved? By means of canals. How did a country come to keep one time? Through the railway timetables. English has a small family of structures for the means or instrument by which something is done, and with them you can explain any change precisely.

How these structures are formed:
• by + -ing — the method as an action: "Watt saved fuel by adding a condenser."
• by means of + noun — formal; the system used: "Coal was carried by means of a canal network."
• through + noun — an abstract process over time: "Reform came through years of agitation."
• thanks to + noun — the cause we credit: "Thanks to Boulton's capital, the engine sold."
• with the help of + noun — assistance: "The mills ran at night with the help of gas light."
When we choose which:
• by + -ing is the default. Note the form: by adding — never by to add, never by add.
• by means of is formal and normally takes a noun: by means of steam.
• through suits long or abstract processes: through investigation, through legislation.
• thanks to assigns credit, so it carries an attitude — "thanks to slave-grown cotton, the mills prospered" reads as heavy irony.
• with the help of implies a supporting role: it helped, but did not act alone.
• by also marks the agent in a passive ("smashed by Luddites"). Same word, different job: agent = who, means = how.

1. Say how it was done

Join each pair of ideas using by + -ing.

1. Watt improved the engine. He added a condenser. → Watt improved the engine a condenser.

2. Owners raised output. They ran the mills at night. → Owners raised output them at night.

3. The Luddites protested. They broke the frames. → The Luddites protested the frames.

1. by adding · 2. by running · 3. by breaking

In every case, by + the -ing form: what follows is the method, not a second event.
