Today: We're exploring who Epicurus was — his life, his revolutionary school, and why his ideas were both loved and feared — so you can understand the human being behind the philosophy.

A Surprising Figure

When most people hear "epicurean" today, they think of fine dining and luxury. But Epicurus himself lived on bread, water, and occasionally cheese. He called a pot of cheese "a feast."

He founded one of the most influential philosophical schools in history — in a garden. He welcomed women and enslaved people as equals in an age when this was scandalous. And his ideas were so threatening to later authorities that they tried to erase him from history.

Let's meet him.

The Life of Epicurus

📍 Epicurus's World
Samos
Born here, 341 BCE. Greek island in the Aegean.
Athens
Studied here briefly. Later founded The Garden.
Asia Minor
Taught in Mytilene and Lampsacus before Athens.
341 BCE — Birth
Born on the island of Samos to Athenian parents. His father was a schoolteacher, his mother a "charm-seller" (possibly involved in religious rituals). Not from wealth or aristocracy.
323 BCE — Military Service
At 18, went to Athens for required military training. This was the year Alexander the Great died — the world was in turmoil. May have encountered the Academy (Plato's school) during this time.
322 BCE — Exile
Athenians were expelled from Samos. His family became refugees, settling in Colophon (modern Turkey). This experience of displacement and instability shaped his philosophy.
311-306 BCE — Early Teaching
Founded schools in Mytilene and Lampsacus. Began attracting devoted followers who would stay with him for life.
306 BCE — The Garden
Returned to Athens and purchased a garden just outside the city walls. Founded his school there — "The Garden" (Kepos) — where he taught until his death.
270 BCE — Death
Died at 72 after a painful illness (possibly kidney stones). His last letter describes being in agony, yet says he was happy remembering conversations with friends. Left the Garden to his followers in his will.
Your turn: Based on this timeline, what do you notice about Epicurus's life circumstances? How might exile, instability, and modest origins have influenced his philosophy?

Key patterns to notice:

  • Instability breeds philosophy: Epicurus lived through regime changes, exile, and the collapse of Alexander's empire. His emphasis on finding peace within oneself, independent of external circumstances, makes sense for someone who had everything external taken away.
  • Modest origins: Unlike Plato (aristocrat) or Aristotle (royal physician's son), Epicurus came from a schoolteacher's family. His philosophy doesn't require wealth, status, or power — just a garden, friends, and simple pleasures.
  • Lifelong community: The friends he made in Mytilene and Lampsacus stayed with him until death. Friendship wasn't abstract theory for Epicurus — it was his actual life.

The Garden (Kepos)

🌿

The Garden

A modest property outside Athens' walls. Not a formal academy — a home and garden where friends lived and studied together.

What Made It Revolutionary

  • Women as equals: Women could join as full members — unthinkable in Plato's Academy
  • Enslaved people welcomed: People like Mys were accepted as philosophical equals
  • Living together: Not just a school — a community (commune) practicing the philosophy daily
  • Simple living: Bread, water, vegetables. Cheese was a luxury. No pursuit of wealth.
💡
Why "Garden" and not "Academy"?
Plato's Academy and Aristotle's Lyceum were in grand public spaces. The Garden was deliberately modest and private — withdrawn from political life. This wasn't escapism; it was a philosophical statement: the good life doesn't require power, fame, or public recognition.

The Garden's Motto

According to ancient sources, the entrance to the Garden had an inscription:

Stranger, here you will do well to tarry; here our highest good is pleasure.

— Inscription at the Garden's entrance (reported by Seneca)

Your turn: Why might this inscription have been misunderstood by visitors? What do you think Epicurus meant by "pleasure" — and what do you think critics assumed he meant?

The Great Misunderstanding:

Critics assumed "pleasure" meant sensual indulgence — feasting, drinking, sexual excess. This misunderstanding has persisted for 2,300 years and gave us the modern word "epicure" (a connoisseur of fine food and drink).

What Epicurus actually meant: The highest pleasure is ataraxia — tranquility, freedom from disturbance. The absence of pain and anxiety. A calm mind among friends, simple food, philosophical conversation. Not the pursuit of pleasure, but the removal of suffering.

We'll explore this distinction deeply in later lessons on Epicurean ethics.

Hearing Epicurus's Voice

Epicurus was prolific — ancient sources credit him with about 300 scrolls. Almost all are lost. What survives gives us glimpses of his actual voice.

📜
What Survives:
• Three letters (to Herodotus, Pythocles, and Menoeceus)
• The Principal Doctrines (Kuriai Doxai) — 40 core teachings
• The Vatican Sayings — 81 maxims discovered in 1888
• Fragments quoted by other ancient authors
• Lucretius's De Rerum Natura — a Roman poem presenting Epicurean philosophy

From the Letter to Menoeceus

This letter is the best introduction to Epicurus in his own words. Here's how it opens:

Let no one delay the study of philosophy when young nor weary of it when old. For no one is either too young or too old for the health of the soul.

— Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus (opening)

Your turn: Epicurus calls philosophy "health of the soul." What does this metaphor suggest about how he viewed philosophy? How is this different from philosophy as "the pursuit of truth" or "logical argumentation"?

Philosophy as Medicine:

This is the key to understanding Epicureanism. For Epicurus, philosophy isn't an academic subject — it's therapy. The soul is sick (with fear, anxiety, false beliefs, endless desires), and philosophy is the cure.

This "therapeutic" model of philosophy was common in the Hellenistic period (Stoics and Skeptics used similar language), but Epicurus made it central. The famous Tetrapharmakos (Four-Part Cure) — which we'll study next lesson — takes the medical metaphor literally.

A philosophy that doesn't heal suffering is useless: "Empty is the argument of the philosopher which does not relieve any human suffering."

On Death

Death is nothing to us; for that which has been dissolved has no sensation, and that which has no sensation is nothing to us.

— Principal Doctrine 2

Your turn: This is one of the most famous (and controversial) statements in all philosophy. In your own words, what is Epicurus's argument? Do you find it convincing? What objections might someone raise?

The Argument:

  1. All good and bad consists in sensation (experience)
  2. Death is the end of sensation
  3. Therefore, death cannot be bad for the one who dies
  4. Therefore, fearing death is irrational

Common objections:

  • "But I fear the process of dying, not death itself" — Epicurus would say: fear pain, not death. They're different things.
  • "But I'll miss out on future goods" — Epicurus would say: You also "missed" the infinite time before you were born. Did that bother you?
  • "But my loved ones will grieve" — This is about their suffering, not yours. Help them while alive.

We'll examine this argument in depth in Lesson 14.

Key facts from Epicurus's life:

  • Born 341 BCE on Samos to modest family (father was a teacher)
  • Family exiled when he was about 19 — became refugees
  • Founded schools in Mytilene and Lampsacus before Athens
  • Bought The Garden in Athens in 306 BCE
  • Died 270 BCE, aged 72, after painful illness
  • Wrote ~300 scrolls, almost all lost

What made the Garden revolutionary:

  • Women accepted as equals
  • Enslaved people welcomed as philosophical equals
  • Living community, not just a school
  • Simple living — modest food, no pursuit of wealth
  • Withdrawn from political life

Pleasure (Epicurus vs. critics):

  • Critics assumed: sensual indulgence, feasting, excess
  • Epicurus meant: ataraxia — tranquility, absence of suffering, calm mind
