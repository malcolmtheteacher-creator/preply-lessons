Step 1 of 11

The First Read

Before we change a single word, hear it. A poem must be heard before it can be edited.

What to do
Read your poem aloud, all the way through, at performance pace. Don't stop to fix anything. After you finish, write one word that describes how it felt to read.
Step 2 of 11

Form Check — Is This Actually a Slam Poem?

Before we polish anything, let's confirm the form. A "slam poem" is a poem written to be performed aloud, not read silently — it grew out of the poetry slams started in Chicago in the 1980s, where poets compete in front of a live audience.

Slam has six tell-tale features. Run your draft against the checklist. Tick the boxes you hit.

The six-question form check
Click each one for the definition, then decide whether your poem ticks it. This is a skill you'll use on every poem you write from now on.
1. Written for the ear, not the eye
What this means
Each line can be read in one breath. The syntax is simple enough that someone hearing it once can follow. The audience never gets to re-read — every word has to land first time.
2. Personal stakes
What this means
The poem is about something that matters to you at gut level — identity, family, grief, fear, injustice. Detached, observational poems don't score in slam. The speaker's body has to be in the poem.
3. Repetition as the engine
What this means
Repeated words, phrases, or anaphora (the same opening across consecutive lines). Repetition is the rhythmic heartbeat of a slam piece — it's how the audience knows what to feel.
4. A central image or extended metaphor
What this means
One image or metaphor running through the whole poem. The audience leaves the room carrying one picture in their head. Without it, the poem feels like a list of feelings.
5. Free verse with strategic rhyme
What this means
No fixed meter, no fixed rhyme scheme. But rhymes used as weapons at moments you want to land hard. Slam isn't the limerick — rhyme should surprise the audience, not march them through it.
6. Length — 2 to 3 minutes performed
What this means
Typically 30 to 50 lines. Long enough to build emotional momentum, short enough to keep the audience hot. Most slam competitions cap individual pieces at 3 minutes.

If you ticked all six — you're writing in the right form. The rest of this workshop is polish.

If any are missing — that's where the next pass starts.

Step 8 of 11

The Ending — Circle Back to the Cage

This is the single biggest improvement we can make. Right now, the poem ends on a list of fears. The strongest slam endings return to the opening image, but with a shift in meaning. Your poem opened with a birdcage. The ending should bring us back there — changed.

Try this
Write a 3-line ending that returns to the birdcage. Answer this: at the end of your story, what has changed? Is the cage still locked? Are you still inside? Has the gold started to dull?
Stuck? Click for two possible endings to consider

Hopeful version:

The birdcage is still gold.
But the bird has learned the bars by heart —
And one day, gold bends too.

Resigned version:

The birdcage is still gold.
And I am still inside it.
Singing the song I was taught.

Which one feels true to where you actually are? That's the one to write.

Step 9 of 11

★ Beyond the Rubric — Three Lift Moves

The rubric tells you what your poem needs to pass. These three moves are what take a good slam piece to the level the audience remembers. They appear in championship-winning slam pieces but almost never in school work. Apply any two and your poem leaps.

Lift Move 1 — The Direct Address
Right now your poem stays inside "I" and "we." Add ONE line that speaks directly to the audience — break the fourth wall. It pulls them into the poem with you instead of leaving them as spectators.
Where it could land in your poem

Strong placement: just after the multilingual stanza, before "Somewhere between four languages…".

Three options to consider:

  • "Have you ever heard 'I love you' in a language you couldn't trust?"
  • "Tell me — when does love stop sounding like love?"
  • "You who have never run faster, studied harder, smiled wider — you wouldn't understand."

Why it works: the audience answers in their own head. They become part of the poem. Slam judges score this every time.

Lift Move 2 — The Volta (the turn)
Right now your poem moves in one direction — pressure escalating to fear. Add ONE pivot line: a "but," "until," "and then I learned," or "now." It gives the poem an arc instead of a straight line. The strongest slam pieces all turn somewhere.
Where it could land in your poem

Strong placement: after "And love began sounding like pressure", just before the final birdcage section. That's where the poem can pivot from "this is what they did to me" to "and here is what I now know."

Three options to consider:

  • "But here's what the cage forgot to tell me—"
  • "Until I learned that gold tarnishes too."
  • "And then one day I heard my own voice — and it wasn't the one I'd been taught to use."

Why it works: a poem that turns has shape. A poem that doesn't turn is a list. Judges reward shape.

Lift Move 3 — The Closing Beat
After your three-line ending, add ONE more line. Short — two or three words. A fragment. A breath. This is the move that turns a poem people clap politely for into one they erupt over.
How it works in your poem

If you've chosen the hopeful ending:

The birdcage is still gold.
But the bird has learned the bars by heart —
And one day, gold bends too.
[ pause ]
Watch me.

If you've chosen the resigned ending:

The birdcage is still gold.
And I am still inside it.
Singing the song I was taught.
[ pause ]
Still.

Why it works: the pause before is everything. After your main ending lands, the audience expects you to step back. Instead you give them one more — and THAT single beat is what they applaud. It is a craft signature of championship slam.

Step 10 of 11

Distribution — Two Voices, One Poem

You and your partner have two voices. The colour-coding (Yellow = together, Green = Yurim, Blue = Jesslyn) is the map. Get the map right and the poem performs itself.

The rule of thumb
Together (yellow) — opening lines, the climax ("Run faster / Study harder…"), the final lines. The universal moments.

Solo voices (green / blue) — the confessions, the personal images, the emotional shifts.

The multilingual stanza — should go to ONE voice (whoever's mother tongue those languages are). Code-switching only works as a personal voice, never as a chorus.
Step 11 of 11

The Final Read-Aloud Test

Now you have a full draft. Read it aloud — start to finish, at performance pace, with the distribution as planned. Mark any line where you stumble, run out of breath, or feel the energy drop.

What to listen for
• Lines too long for one breath → break them.
• Words that trip your tongue → swap them.
• Flat moments where the audience would lose interest → cut, image, or speed up.
• Moments you'd want the room to go silent → mark them.
• Moments you'd want the room to react → mark them.
One small thing to flag (spelling)

You have "burdens I can't bare". The word probably wants to be "bear" (to carry). Spoken aloud they sound identical, so for a slam performance it doesn't matter — but if your teacher marks the printed version, "bear" is the safer choice.
