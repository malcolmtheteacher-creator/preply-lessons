The Taj Mahal

The most beautiful building in the world — and the grief that built it.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of the world's most famous monument and discuss whether beauty can be born from sorrow — using relative and purpose clauses and the language of great buildings.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. We descended cold stone steps into the family mausoleum, where the marble coffins of long-dead nobles lay side by side in the dark.
a mausoleum / a tomb (n) — a large, impressive building made to hold the body of a dead person.
2. Too busy to paint it herself, the wealthy widow commissioned a famous portrait artist, signing a contract and paying half the fee in advance.
to commission (v) — to formally order and pay for a piece of work (a building, a painting) to be made.
3. When the news of her son's death arrived, the old woman sank to the floor in grief, weeping and unable to speak for the rest of that day.
grief (n) — very deep sadness, especially after someone has died.
4. Her kitchen counter was a single slab of polished white marble, cool to the touch and threaded with faint grey veins that caught the light.
marble (n) — a hard, fine stone, often white, used for grand buildings and sculpture.
5. A butterfly's wings have a striking symmetry: whatever pattern appears on the left appears again on the right, an exact mirror image.
symmetry (n) — the quality of having matching, balanced parts on either side of a centre. (adj: symmetrical)
6. The old craftsman bent over his workbench, carving tiny petals into the wood with the same patient hands that had shaped furniture for fifty years.
a craftsman / an artisan (n) — a worker highly skilled at making things by hand.
7. The garden was planted in memory of the soldiers — so that, long after they were gone, people would still remember their names.
in memory of (phrase) — done to honour and keep alive the memory of a person who has died.
8. In the centre of the city square stood a towering stone monument, and every year crowds gathered at its base to lay flowers and remember the fallen.
a monument (n) — a large structure built to remember a person or event, or simply admired as important.
9. After thirty years of failed attempts, the painter finally produced a masterpiece, and visitors now travel across continents just to stand before it.
a masterpiece (n) — a work of supreme skill; the finest thing of its kind.
10. Fashions come and go, but the band's enduring popularity has carried their songs from their grandparents' record players to the radios of today.
enduring (adj) — lasting over a very long time; not fading or weakening.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — An emperor and his wife

In the early seventeenth century, much of northern India was ruled by the Mughal emperors — a powerful dynasty of immense wealth and refined taste. In 1612, a young prince who would one day become the fifth emperor, Shah Jahan, married a woman named Mumtaz Mahal. Theirs was not merely a political marriage. The two were inseparable companions, devoted to each other in a way that the court itself remarked upon; she travelled with him everywhere, even on military campaigns.

Then, in 1631, tragedy struck. Mumtaz Mahal died giving birth to their fourteenth child. The emperor, who had loved her completely, was destroyed by grief. The court fell into mourning, and it was said that Shah Jahan's hair turned grey within months. But out of that sorrow came an extraordinary resolve: he would build, in memory of his wife, the most beautiful tomb the world had ever seen. That same year, he commissioned the monument we now call the Taj Mahal.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who were Shah Jahan and Mumtaz Mahal, and what kind of relationship did they have?

2. What happened in 1631, and how did the emperor respond?

1. Shah Jahan was the fifth Mughal emperor of India; Mumtaz Mahal was his beloved wife, whom he married in 1612. They were inseparable companions, devoted to each other.

2. Mumtaz Mahal died giving birth to their fourteenth child. Overwhelmed by grief, the emperor resolved to build her the most beautiful tomb in the world, and commissioned the Taj Mahal that year.

Discuss:
  • A ruler responded to private grief by ordering an enormous public building. What does that choice tell us about how love, power and memory were tied together for someone like Shah Jahan?

Part 2 — Building a masterpiece

Construction began in 1632 on the banks of the river in Agra, in northern India. What followed was one of the great building projects of the age. More than twenty thousand workers laboured on the site, and the finest craftsmen — masons, carvers, calligraphers, garden-designers — were gathered from across the empire and beyond. The whole structure was faced in dazzling white marble, brought from far away and inlaid with semi-precious stones in delicate patterns of flowers and script.

The design is famous above all for its perfect symmetry: every element on one side is mirrored exactly on the other, so that the eye finds no flaw and no end of balance. The Taj Mahal is regarded as the finest example of Mughal architecture — a graceful blend of Indian, Persian and Islamic styles, drawing the best of three traditions into a single harmonious whole. It was no quick triumph. The main mausoleum was finished around 1648, and the complete complex — its great gateway, its formal gardens, a mosque, and a matching mirror-building set opposite the mosque — was not finished until about 1653. In all, it took more than two decades to build.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where was the Taj Mahal built, and roughly how many people and how long did it take?

2. What two qualities is the building most admired for?

1. It was built in Agra, in northern India, beginning in 1632. More than twenty thousand workers laboured on it, and it took over two decades — the main tomb finished around 1648, the whole complex around 1653.

2. Its perfect symmetry and its white marble craftsmanship — it is the finest example of Mughal architecture, blending Indian, Persian and Islamic styles.

Discuss:
  • Twenty thousand people and twenty years went into one building. Can such an enormous cost in labour and money ever be justified by beauty alone — or does it need some other purpose?

Part 3 — What it became

Shah Jahan did not rest beside his wife by his own design. In his later years he was deposed and his final days were spent away from the throne he had once held. When he died, he was buried inside the Taj Mahal, laid to rest beside Mumtaz Mahal at last — the one place, perhaps, he had always meant to return to. The two tombs lie together at the heart of the monument he had raised for her.

In the centuries since, the Taj Mahal has become far more than the tomb of one woman. It is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, visited by millions, and for countless people around the world it has become the ultimate symbol of love — a thing of such enduring beauty that it seems to stand outside time altogether. The grief of one emperor, frozen into marble, has outlasted his empire, his name and very nearly the memory of the woman herself. What began as a private sorrow became, in the end, a masterpiece belonging to everyone.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Shah Jahan himself come to be buried in the Taj Mahal?

2. What is the Taj Mahal today, and what has it come to symbolise?

1. After his death he was buried inside the Taj Mahal, beside his wife Mumtaz Mahal, so that the two tombs lie together at the heart of the monument he built for her.

2. It is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site visited by millions, and for many people the ultimate symbol of love — an enduring masterpiece.

Discuss:
  • The building was made for one particular woman, yet today it belongs to the whole world as a symbol of love. Is something lost when a deeply personal monument becomes a famous attraction — or is that its truest success?

The grammar this story runs on: relative & purpose clauses

To describe a great building, you need to add information to your nouns and explain why things were done. Two tools do exactly that — and this story is full of both.

The two tools:
• Relative clauses add information about a noun, using who (people), which / that (things), where (places) and whom (people, as object): "Shah Jahan, who loved his wife deeply…", "the marble, which came from far away…", "Agra, where it stands…"
• Purpose clauses explain why something is done, using to + verb, in order to + verb, or so that + clause: "He built it to honour her," "…in order to remember her," "…so that people would never forget her."

1. Combine with a relative clause

Join each pair into one sentence using who, which / that or where. Write the whole new sentence.

1. Shah Jahan was an emperor. He loved his wife deeply.
→ Shah Jahan was an emperor ...

2. Mumtaz Mahal was a woman. She died in 1631.
→ Mumtaz Mahal was a woman ...

3. The Taj Mahal is in Agra. The river flows there.
→ The Taj Mahal is in Agra, ...

4. The marble was inlaid with stones. It came from far away.
→ The marble, ..., was inlaid with stones.

1. Shah Jahan was an emperor who loved his wife deeply.

2. Mumtaz Mahal was a woman who died in 1631.

3. The Taj Mahal is in Agra, where the river flows.

4. The marble, which came from far away, was inlaid with stones.

2. Purpose clauses — say why

Fill each gap with to, in order to or so that (more than one may be possible).

1. He built the Taj Mahal honour her memory.

2. Craftsmen carved every flower carefully the tomb would be perfect.

3. He raised the monument people would remember Mumtaz Mahal for ever.

4. White marble was chosen make the building shine in the light.

1. to (or: in order to) honour her memory.

2. so that the tomb would be perfect.

3. so that people would remember Mumtaz Mahal for ever.

4. to (or: in order to) make the building shine.

Remember: to / in order to are followed by a bare verb; so that is followed by a full clause (subject + verb).

3. Put them together — one rich sentence

Fill each gap so the sentence uses both kinds of clause. Read it aloud once you've finished.

The Taj Mahal, stands in Agra, was commissioned by Shah Jahan, an emperor loved his wife deeply, the whole world would remember her. The white marble, came from far away, was carved by craftsmen the building would be perfectly symmetrical.

which (place / thing) · who (person) · so that (purpose) · which (thing) · so that (purpose)

Notice how the relative clauses add detail to the nouns, while the purpose clauses explain why each thing was done.
