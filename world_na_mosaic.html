Melting Pot or Mosaic?

The USA calls itself a melting pot; Canada prefers a mosaic. Do these images describe reality — or national stories each country tells itself?

The stereotype: "The USA is a melting pot where everyone becomes the same; Canada is a perfect multicultural mosaic." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Two metaphors

The phrase "melting pot" became popular after Israel Zangwill's 1908 play The Melting Pot, which imagined immigrants blending into one new American people. Canada developed a different image: the "Canadian mosaic", the title of a 1938 book by John Murray Gibbon, in which groups keep their own colours within one picture. In 1971 Canada became one of the first countries to adopt an official multiculturalism policy.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where does "melting pot" come from?

2. When did Canada adopt official multiculturalism?

1. Israel Zangwill's 1908 play.

2. 1971.

Discuss:
  • Which metaphor do you find more attractive?

Part 2 — A history of arrivals

Between 1892 and 1954, roughly 12 million immigrants passed through Ellis Island in New York. Today about one in seven US residents is foreign-born, while in Canada, according to the 2021 census, the figure is about 23% — one of the highest in the G7. Many US immigrants must have felt pressure to assimilate, for example by changing their names or speaking only English to their children.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many people passed through Ellis Island?

2. What share of Canadians are foreign-born?

1. Roughly 12 million.

2. About 23%.

Discuss:
  • Why might immigrants change their names?

Part 3 — Neither perfect

Both images leave things out. Neither country began as an empty land: Indigenous peoples lived there for thousands of years, and both governments ran residential or boarding schools that tried to remove Indigenous languages and cultures. Enslaved Africans were brought to America by force. Both countries continue to debate racism and integration, and in practice many Americans keep their heritage and many Canadians blend cultures.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What do both metaphors leave out?

2. What did residential and boarding schools try to do?

1. Indigenous peoples and forced migration such as slavery.

2. Remove Indigenous languages and cultures.

Discuss:
  • Can a national story include painful history?

Grammar — Past Modals of Deduction

When we explain where a label or belief came from, we often deduce. Past modals show how certain we are.

How it works:
• must have + past participle — nearly certain: "The label must have come from the Cold War."
• might / may / could have — possible.
• can't have — nearly impossible.

1. How sure are we?

Complete each sentence about Melting Pot or Mosaic?. Use the word in brackets.

1. Immigrants at Ellis Island (feel) nervous. (must have)

2. Zangwill (expect) his title to become so famous. (can't have)

3. Some families (change) their names to fit in. (might have)

1. must have felt

2. can't have expected

3. might have changed

2. Complete with a past modal

1. Gibbon (want) a different image from the USA's. (must have)

2. Early settlers (understand) Indigenous cultures well. (can't have)

3. The policy (attract) more immigrants to Canada. (may have)

2. can't have understood

3. may have attracted

Going deeper

National metaphors are stories countries tell about themselves; they shape policy and pride, but they simplify. The contrast "melting pot vs mosaic" also helps Canada define itself against the USA. In reality, both societies mix assimilation and diversity, and both are still working through their histories.

Worth noticing: "Must have" = nearly certain; "might/may have" = possible; "can't have" = nearly impossible.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Ask about heritage respectfully. In North America, people often describe themselves with two identities ("Korean-American", "Italian-Canadian"). Let people tell you how they identify, don't ask "Where are you really from?", and learn whose traditional land you're on — many Canadian events begin by acknowledging it.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Melting pot or mosaic — which works better?
  • Why do countries need national stories?
  • How do you describe your own identity?
