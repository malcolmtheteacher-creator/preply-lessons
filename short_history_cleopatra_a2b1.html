Cleopatra

The last queen of Egypt — clever, powerful, and remembered for the wrong reasons.

Today: tell the story of Cleopatra, the last queen of Egypt, and talk about how we remember powerful women. Grammar: the second conditional (if + past, would).

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — A clever queen

Cleopatra was the last queen of ancient Egypt. She was born about 69 BC. Her family came from Greece, but they had ruled Egypt for almost three hundred years.

Cleopatra was very clever. She could speak many languages, and she understood people and politics very well. She was not just a beautiful face — she was a strong, intelligent ruler.

When her father died, Cleopatra and her younger brother shared the throne. But they did not agree, and her brother pushed her away. To get her power back, Cleopatra made an alliance with a powerful Roman: Julius Caesar. With his help, she became queen again. She and Caesar also had a son.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did Cleopatra's family come from? How long had they ruled Egypt?

2. How did Cleopatra get her power back?

1. Her family came from Greece, but they had ruled Egypt for almost three hundred years.

2. She made an alliance with a powerful Roman, Julius Caesar, and with his help she became queen again.

Talk about it:
  • Cleopatra needed a powerful friend (Caesar) to get her power back. Is it always good to have powerful friends? Why or why not?

Part 2 — Antony and the battle

Then Julius Caesar was killed in Rome. Cleopatra needed a new friend. She made an alliance with another powerful Roman, Mark Antony. They worked together, and they had children together.

But Antony had a rival in Rome: a young, ambitious man called Octavian. Octavian wanted all the power for himself. He could not attack Antony directly, so he used propaganda. He told the Roman people that Cleopatra was a dangerous foreign woman who controlled Antony. Many Romans believed him.

In 31 BC, there was a big sea battle at a place called Actium. Octavian's ships were smaller and faster. They defeated the ships of Antony and Cleopatra. Antony and Cleopatra escaped, but they had lost the war.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was Octavian, and what did he do to turn Rome against Cleopatra?

2. What happened at the battle of Actium?

1. He was Antony's rival in Rome. He used propaganda — he told the Romans that Cleopatra was a dangerous foreign woman who controlled Antony.

2. Octavian's smaller, faster ships defeated Antony and Cleopatra's ships. They escaped, but they had lost the war.

Talk about it:
  • Octavian used stories to make people afraid of Cleopatra. Can words and stories be as powerful as armies? Why?

Part 3 — The end of a queen

One year later, in 30 BC, Octavian's army came to Egypt. Antony thought Cleopatra was already dead, so he killed himself. Soon after, Cleopatra died too. She did not want to be Octavian's prisoner.

How did she die? The famous story says a snake bit her. But many historians think she used poison instead. We are not completely sure.

After Cleopatra died, Egypt became part of Rome. For a long time, people remembered Cleopatra only as a beautiful, dangerous woman — because her enemies wrote the story. But today, historians say she was something different: a clever and strong queen who kept Egypt rich and free for many years.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Cleopatra die? Are we sure?

2. How do historians today see Cleopatra differently?

1. The famous story says a snake bit her, but many historians think she used poison. We are not completely sure.

2. For a long time people saw her only as a beautiful, dangerous woman; today historians see a clever, strong queen who kept Egypt rich and free for many years.

Talk about it:
  • Cleopatra's enemies wrote her story. Is it fair that we remember her in their words?

Grammar: the second conditional

We use the second conditional to talk about an imaginary situation — something that is not real now, or is not likely.

The form: If + past simple, would + base verb.
Example: "If Cleopatra were alive today, she would be a famous leader."
The past tense here does not mean the past. It means "this is not real." We are just imagining.

1. Choose the right form

1. If I (be) a queen, I (need) clever friends.

2. If we (have) Cleopatra's own letters, we (know) her better.

3. If propaganda (not exist), it (be) harder to lie about people.

1. were (or "was") / would need

2. had / would know

3. did not exist / would be

2. Finish the sentences about the story

Put the verbs in the second conditional. Then read each sentence out loud.

1. If a leader today (speak) nine languages, people (be) amazed.

2. If you (be) Cleopatra, what (you / do) about Octavian?

3. If history (listen) to Cleopatra's words, the story (look) different.

1. spoke / would be

2. were (or "was") / would you do

3. listened / would look

Remember: if + past simple, then would + base verb. The past tense just means "this is imaginary."
