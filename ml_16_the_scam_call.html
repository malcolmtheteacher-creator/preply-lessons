The Scam Call

A polite man from your bank's fraud team. He knows your address, he's in no rush to be rude, and he'd just like the first four digits of your card. He isn't from your bank.

The Scene 🎬

Half nine on a Tuesday, and your phone goes. Unknown number. You pick up because you always pick up, and a man called Ryan says he's from the fraud team at your bank, and there are two payments sitting on your account this morning that don't look like you — one in a shop in Manchester, one online.

He's good. He's not shouty, he's not obviously foreign to your life: he knows your address, he has an agent number ready, and when you say you're suspicious he agrees with you and says he's glad. Everything he does is designed to make the next ten minutes feel like the most reasonable thing in the world, and every ten seconds he mentions the clock.

Here's the difficulty. If he really is your bank, then being awkward costs you an afternoon of hold music. If he isn't, then being polite costs you everything in the account. You can't tell which, and he knows you can't tell which — that gap is where he lives.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When an unknown number rings, do you answer it? Has that changed in the last few years — and if it has, what did you lose along with the nuisance calls?
  2. Think of a time you were pressured into saying yes to something on the phone or at the door. What made it hard to refuse — the argument, or just the fact that the other person was being nice?

Who's who

You
The One Who Picked Up 📞

Not stupid, not paranoid, and genuinely unsure. You'd quite like to be wrong about him. You also have no intention of reading anything out to a voice you've never heard before.

The other voice
"Ryan from the fraud team" 🎧

Calm, patient, faintly amused, never pushy for more than a sentence at a time. He knows your address. He agrees with every doubt you raise. He is not who he says he is.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Twice, Ryan agrees with the very doubt that should have finished him off — he even says he's glad about it. Why would a person trying to trick you do that, rather than argue?

2. Everything Ryan says is built to make ten minutes feel impossible. What exactly did he claim about the time, and what was he hoping you'd feel?

3. Right at the end, when nothing has worked, his manner changes. What does he do — and what does that tell you about him?

1. Because arguing with a suspicious person makes them more suspicious, whereas agreeing with them makes you sound like the one person on the phone who is on their side. When he says he's glad you're careful, he's borrowing the exact voice a real bank uses, and he's hoping that if he shares your doubt, you'll stop pointing it at him.
2. He said the payment would leave in about eight minutes and that the bank's own main line had a forty-minute queue, so that checking him properly would cost you the money. He wanted you to feel that thinking was itself the expensive option — which is why the ten-minute line works, because it refuses the deadline he invented.
3. He stops being helpful and starts handing you the blame: nothing he can do afterwards, and it's on you, not him. A real fraud team has no reason to make you feel guilty for ringing them back — that turn from charm to blame is the moment he tells you what the whole call was for.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for refusing a pleasant stranger who is in a hurry. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. The whole trick here is that your voice must not change. Anger tells him he's rattled you and gives him something to work with; apology tells him you can be moved. What beats him is boredom — the same friendly, slightly flat, faintly bored tone you'd use to turn down a second biscuit, repeated for as long as it takes. You're not fighting him. You're just not doing it.

🔍 Working out who you've got

Phrase 1

He's three sentences in and already halfway through a story. You want to stop the momentum and find out who this actually is — without sounding hostile. Which one does that?

A. "Who is this?"
B. "Sorry — before we go any further, who am I actually speaking to?"
C. "Is this one of those scam calls?"
B — "Sorry — before we go any further, who am I actually speaking to?"
A is blunt enough to sound rude, which pushes you into apologising and lets him take the call back. C accuses him, and an accusation is the one thing he has a prepared answer for. B works because "before we go any further" stops the story dead while sounding like ordinary admin, and "actually" quietly says you noticed he told you a job title rather than a name.
Phrase 2

He has just proved he's genuine by reading out something only your bank could know — your address. You don't buy it, and you want to say so lightly rather than call him a liar. Put the pieces in order:

have I.but I've no wayI'm not being funny,of knowing that,
"I'm not being funny, but I've no way of knowing that, have I."
"I'm not being funny, but…" is a very British warning that something blunt is coming and no offence is meant by it, and it lets you say the blunt thing without the row. Then the tag "have I" turns your refusal into a fact you're both looking at together, rather than a charge against him — you're not saying he's lying, only that from your side of the phone the two possibilities look identical.
Phrase 3

You'd like to know how a stranger came to have this number in front of him at half nine in the morning — asked casually, as if it barely matters, because a casual question is harder to dodge than a demand. Put the pieces in order:

out of interest?How did you getthis number,
"How did you get this number, out of interest?"
"Out of interest" means "I'm only asking out of curiosity" — it takes the weight out of the question so that it sounds like small talk. That's precisely why it's useful: he was ready for an interrogation and this isn't one, so he either answers it properly or he flounders, and the flounder tells you everything.

🚫 Not proving anything to him

Phrase 4

He wants four digits. Only four. He's right that it isn't much — and that's the trap, because the next request will also not be much. Which refusal shuts the whole category, not just this one request?

A. "I can't give you that, sorry."
B. "How do I know you won't misuse it?"
C. "I'm not going to be reading anything out over the phone, I'm afraid."
C — "I'm not going to be reading anything out over the phone, I'm afraid."
A and B both argue about this particular request, which invites him to make it smaller until you agree. C isn't a decision about four digits at all; it's a rule about the phone, and rules are much less tiring to hold than decisions, because there's nothing left to negotiate. The "I'm afraid" keeps it courteous while giving away nothing.
Phrase 5

You want to grant that he might be genuine, and refuse him anyway — so that your no doesn't depend on deciding he's a liar. Complete the chunk: "You'll appreciate I can't just ___ ___ ___ ___ it."

"You'll appreciate I can't just take your word for it."
To take someone's word for something is to believe it purely because they said it, with nothing behind it. Starting with "you'll appreciate" is the clever part, because it hands him the reasonable position — of course a sensible man in his job would understand — so that disagreeing with you would mean admitting he's unreasonable.
Phrase 6

Time to name what you'll do instead — and the safe route is a number he had no part in giving you. Put the pieces in order:

on the numberI'd ratherring you backon the back of my card.
"I'd rather ring you back on the number on the back of my card."
This is the one move that settles the question, because it uses a number that was printed long before he rang and that he cannot have chosen. "I'd rather" makes it a preference rather than an accusation, which lets you say it in the same friendly voice as everything else — and a real bank will simply say yes.

⏱️ Taking the clock off him

Phrase 7

He says the queue is forty minutes and the money leaves in eight, so checking him will cost you the lot. Throw his own word back at him. Complete: "If it's genuinely that urgent, it'll ___ ___ ___ in ten minutes."

"If it's genuinely that urgent, it'll still be urgent in ten minutes."
Repeating his own word back is what does the damage, because a real emergency survives a ten-minute delay and an invented one doesn't. The sentence quietly points out that the deadline is the only thing forcing you to skip the checking, which is a strange coincidence — and you've said all that without once suggesting he made it up.
Phrase 8

He offers to sort it himself if you'll just confirm your last purchase. You'd like to decline the favour and take the job somewhere he can't reach — cheerfully, the way you'd wave away an offer of help with the washing-up. Put the pieces in order:

through the app.No, I'm alright, thanks —I'll sort it
"No, I'm alright, thanks — I'll sort it through the app."
"I'm alright, thanks" is how British English turns down help without giving a reason, and giving no reason is the point, since a reason is something he can argue with. To "sort" something is to deal with it and be done with it, so the sentence takes the whole problem out of his hands and puts it somewhere he has no way of following you.

👋 Getting off the phone

Phrase 9

He insists it has to be now, and it has to be by phone. Without accusing him of anything, offer him a route a real bank could take easily and a stranger couldn't take at all. Say it out loud — what would you offer him?

e.g. "You're welcome to put it in writing."
It sounds like an offer, and it is one — that's what makes it so hard to argue with. A letter costs a real bank nothing and costs him the two things he depends on, which are speed and being a voice with no address behind it. "You're welcome to" stays warm and gives him the choice, and then he has to explain why the choice is no good.
Phrase 10

He's started saying it'll be your fault. There is nothing left to discuss, and you don't want to be rude or to sneak away apologising. Which ending is it?

A. "Sorry, I've got to go, sorry — bye, sorry."
B. "Right — I'm going to hang up now. Nothing personal."
C. "You're a criminal and I'm reporting this call."
B — "Right — I'm going to hang up now. Nothing personal."
A leaves a gap he can talk into, and every apology is an invitation to keep going. C is a row, and a row is still a conversation. B announces the ending instead of asking permission for it, and "nothing personal" is the small courtesy that lets you finish it without heat — you're closing a call, not winning an argument.

🔑 Make them yours

These aren't only for scam calls. They're for anyone who is friendly, in a hurry, and would like something from you before you've had time to think. Pin three of them to your own life so you'll actually reach for them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"You'll appreciate I can't just take your word for it." — where in your life does someone expect to be believed simply because they said it?

"If it's genuinely that urgent, it'll still be urgent in ten minutes." — think of a deadline someone else invented to hurry you. What was it?

"Right — I'm going to hang up now. Nothing personal." — a conversation you should have ended and didn't. Where would you have ended it?

The Twist

☎️ You ring the number on the card

No script this time. You hang up on Ryan, you turn the card over, you dial the number printed on the back yourself — the one plan you had, the one thing everybody tells you to do. It rings twice. "Fraud team, Ryan speaking. Oh — hello again. I did say it'd be quicker to stay on." Your safe route has just answered in his voice, and you have no idea whether that's because he never let the line go, or because he really does work there and you've spent four minutes being rude to a man doing his job. Start the timer. You cannot ring the card number — you've tried it. Decide what you now trust, say it out loud to him, and see whether you can get off this call without either handing him anything or apologising your way back into it.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

The strange thing about a call like this is that nothing technical is being attacked. Your password holds, your bank's computers hold. What gets picked is your manners — the habit, learned young and mostly worth keeping, of being helpful to a polite person who has asked you a small question.

That's why it's so hard, and why it isn't a matter of intelligence. Clever people are robbed this way every day, usually while thinking how reasonable they're being. The pressure never arrives as pressure; it arrives as a nice man who is on your side, and a clock, and one tiny request at a time.

Which leaves a small, slightly sad skill worth having: the ability to be unhelpful to someone who has done nothing wrong yet, and to be alright about it if you turn out to have been unfair. Ten wasted minutes and one mildly embarrassing apology is a low price. He's counting on you thinking it's a high one.

Two bigger questions

  1. Being trusting is what makes someone easy to rob, and it's also what makes them good to know. If you trained yourself out of it, what would you lose — and would the trade be worth it?
  2. When someone is tricked like this, we tend to say they should have known better. Whose fault is it really — the person who picked up, the bank that rang them from a number they'd never seen before, or a phone system where anyone can pretend to be anyone? And why is blaming the victim so much more comfortable than the alternatives?
