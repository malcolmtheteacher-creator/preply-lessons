Bad News

Your friend's mother died on Tuesday. You're outside his flat holding a bag of shopping, and you have no idea what to say. Good — because there isn't anything.

The Scene 🎬

Rafa's mum died on Tuesday morning. You found out on Tuesday afternoon, and it is now Friday, and you have not been round. Not because you don't care — you've thought about almost nothing else — but because every sentence you've rehearsed on the way to work has sounded either useless or, somehow, slightly insulting.

So this evening you went to the supermarket instead of thinking, and you came out with far too much: a roast chicken, two bags of pasta, biscuits, a bunch of flowers you're now not sure about, some tinned soup you bought purely because your hands needed a job. It's in a bag by your foot. You're standing outside his door. You still haven't got a sentence.

Here's the thing worth knowing before you knock: there isn't one. There is no sentence. The people who help are not the ones who find the right words — they're the ones who turn up, sit down, and don't perform. Everything your brain is offering you right now (she's in a better place, at least she didn't suffer, I know exactly how you feel, time heals) is a way of making your discomfort stop. Put the lot down. Pick up the shopping. Knock.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of a time something bad happened to you. What did people say that actually helped — and what did people say that made it worse, even though they meant well? Be specific; the exact words matter.
  2. Have you ever avoided someone because you didn't know what to say to them? How long did you leave it, what were you afraid of, and what happened in the end?

Who's who

You
The One at the Door 🛍️

Three days late and holding four kilos of shopping you can't explain. Frightened of saying the wrong thing, which is exactly why you nearly didn't come. You're here now. That's the whole job.

The other voice
Rafa 🚪

Four days in. Oddly calm, oddly practical, mostly talking about paperwork because paperwork is the only bit that has instructions. Don't mistake the calm for coping.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Rafa mentions something a friend called Ana said to him. What was it, and what does his reaction tell you about lines like that?

2. One of Rafa's turns is a single syllable. What had just been said to him, and why is that tiny answer the most important thing he says?

3. Rafa says nobody has asked him a particular kind of question. What have people been asking instead, and what's the difference?

1. Ana said "she's at peace now", and Rafa "nearly —" and then stops, because he doesn't want to say how much it annoyed him about someone who meant well. It's the classic well-meant disaster: it tells the bereaved person how to feel, and it's really there to make the speaker comfortable, so it lands as a small insult even when it isn't one.
2. He'd been insisting he was alright — the list, being efficient — and the visitor said he didn't have to be alright with them. After a long pause, all he says is "Mm." It's the moment the performance stops, and it's tiny precisely because there's nothing underneath it to say; anything longer would have been him coping at you again. The silence around it is doing most of the work.
3. Everyone has been asking how he is, which he has to perform an answer to. Nobody had asked whether he wanted to talk about her — which is the question that lets him choose, and the one that gets him talking properly.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten things to say when there's nothing to say. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Tone and pace. Flat and ordinary is the target here, which will feel wrong: no special soft voice, no head tilt, no meaningful eye contact. Grief makes people feel like an exhibit, and a performance of sympathy makes it worse, so talk to them roughly the way you always did. Then the harder half — slow down and stop early. Leave the gaps. Let a silence run past the point where it's comfortable, because the urge to fill it is your discomfort, not their need, and almost every wrong thing anyone says at a funeral is said to make a pause stop.

🚪 Arriving with nothing

Phrase 1

The door opens. You have four seconds and no sentence. Which opener is actually honest about that — and doesn't hand him a job to do?

A. "She's at peace now, that's the main thing."
B. "I'm not going to say anything useful. I just didn't want you to be on your own."
C. "I know exactly how you feel — I lost my dad two years ago."
B — "I'm not going to say anything useful. I just didn't want you to be on your own."
A decides for him how he's supposed to feel about it, which is not yours to decide. C is the commonest well-meant disaster of the lot, because it turns his loss into a conversation about yours, and he now has to be interested in your dad. B admits the thing everyone is pretending isn't true — that you've got nothing — and then gives the only real reason you came, which is that he shouldn't be sitting there alone.
Phrase 2

You're holding a bag with a roast chicken, two kinds of pasta and some soup you can't account for. Explain it honestly and a bit stupidly, in three short pieces. Put them in order:

I panicked in the shop.I brought food.It's a bit much.
"I brought food. It's a bit much. I panicked in the shop."
Fact, then apology for the fact, then the honest reason — which is that shopping was something to do with your hands when there was nothing to do with your mouth. "It's a bit much" and "I panicked" let him laugh if he can and ignore it if he can't. Food matters at this point because eating is one of the first things people stop bothering with, and because it gives you both something to look at other than each other.

🙊 Not performing

Phrase 3

He's told you something and you've got nothing back. You could reach for a comforting line, or you could just say the true thing. Which one is honest rather than helpful-sounding?

A. "Everything happens for a reason, I really believe that."
B. "I don't know what to say."
C. "Try and remember the good times, that's what she'd want."
B — "I don't know what to say."
A is unanswerable and slightly monstrous, since the reason on offer is his mother dying, and C hands him homework and speaks for her while it's at it. B sounds like a failure and isn't one: it's the only sentence in the room that's true, and it quietly tells him you're not going to perform at him. Most people who say it apologise afterwards. Don't — it's the phrase, not a gap where the phrase should be.
Phrase 4

You've begun and abandoned about four sentences and you can hear yourself doing it. Rather than pretending it isn't happening, name it and give up — out loud. Complete the chunk: "I keep ___ sentences and they're all rubbish, so I'll stop."

"I keep starting sentences and they're all rubbish, so I'll stop."
"Keep starting" is the giveaway — starting, not finishing, over and over, which is exactly what your mouth does when you're frightened of getting it wrong. Saying it aloud does two useful things at once: it lets him off having to rescue your half-sentences, and it makes the silence that follows deliberate rather than awkward, so nobody has to fill it.

👂 Letting them lead

Phrase 5

Everyone else has been asking how he is. Ask instead about her — and build a genuine escape hatch into the question, so "no" is an easy answer. Put it in order:

about her,Do you wantabsolutely not?orto talk
"Do you want to talk about her, or absolutely not?"
The whole weight sits on "or absolutely not" — without it, the question is a polite request that he'll feel obliged to say yes to. With it, refusing is offered as the equally normal answer, so he can actually choose. It's also, unusually, a question about the person who died rather than about how he's coping, and people are often desperate to talk about her and can't find anyone who'll let them.
Phrase 6

He's said yes. Now open it up — but "tell me about your mum" invites a eulogy, and he'll give you the polished version. Ask for the small, unflattering, real stuff instead. What do you say? Say it out loud, then type it.

e.g. "Tell me about her. Anything. The annoying stuff, ideally."
"Anything" removes the pressure to choose something significant, and "the annoying stuff, ideally" is doing the real work, because it gives him permission to describe a person rather than a saint. The half-seven phone calls, the "I know it's early" — that's who she actually was, and remembering her properly is a lot more comforting than remembering her respectfully.

🚫 Not fixing

Phrase 7

He's just told you something genuinely unbearable. Your instinct is to find the redemptive angle. Don't. Which reply refuses to?

A. "At least she didn't suffer, though — you have to hold on to that."
B. "That's just awful. I'm not going to try and make it less awful."
C. "It'll get easier, I promise. Time heals everything."
B — "That's just awful. I'm not going to try and make it less awful."
A and C are both small acts of tidying up: they take the size of the thing and shrink it, so that you can cope with hearing it. B does the opposite and agrees with him, and then — this is the bit that matters — promises not to argue with him about it. It's a relief, because the bereaved spend most of their week being talked out of how bad it is by people who can't stand how bad it is.
Phrase 8

He's insisting he's fine. He's got a list. He's being efficient. He is very obviously not fine — but you're not going to argue, you're going to remove the requirement. Complete the chunk: "You don't ___ ___ be alright with me."

"You don't have to be alright with me."
"Have to" is the whole point, because being alright is a duty he's been performing all week for colleagues and neighbours and Colin, and you're telling him he can put it down here. Notice what it doesn't do: it doesn't say "you're not alright", which would be arguing with him about his own feelings. It just removes the obligation and then waits.

🪑 Staying

Phrase 9

You're going to stay, and you need him to know that staying costs him nothing — no chat, no hosting, no being interesting. Put it in order:

entertain me.I'll sit here.have toYou don't
"I'll sit here. You don't have to entertain me."
To "entertain" someone is what a host does — keep them talking, keep them comfortable — and it's exactly the job the bereaved end up doing for every visitor, which is why so many of them stop answering the phone. Offering to be in the room without being a guest is, on the whole, the most useful thing on this list, and it asks nothing of you except tolerating a quiet flat.
Phrase 10

You're leaving, and "let me know if you need anything" puts the work on him and means he'll never ring. Name a day instead, and take the pressure off it. Complete the chunk: "I'm coming back Thursday. You don't have to ___ ___ ___."

"I'm coming back Thursday. You don't have to answer the door."
Two moves in one breath. "I'm coming back Thursday" is a decision rather than an offer, so he doesn't have to ask, and asking is the thing people can't do. "You don't have to answer the door" then makes it safe, because some days he won't be able to face anyone, and knowing that's allowed is what makes the visit bearable to look forward to. You leave the shopping on the step and go home. That's fine — you still turned up.

🔑 Make them yours

These aren't for a role play. They're for the phone call you'll get one day about someone you know, and you'll want them ready, because you won't be able to think. Pin three of them to real people. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I'm not going to say anything useful. I just didn't want you to be on your own." — who in your life has had something bad happen that you've been quietly avoiding? Name them, and name what you've been afraid of saying.

"Do you want to talk about her, or absolutely not?" — rewrite this for someone you actually know. Whose name goes in it?

"I'm coming back Thursday. You don't have to answer the door." — turn a vague "let me know if you need anything" into one specific day. Which day, and what will you bring?

The Twist

😳 The thing people don't say out loud

One more round, no script. Halfway through, Rafa says it: he's relieved. It was four years, and the last one was horrible, and on Tuesday morning what he felt first — before anything else — was relief. And now he thinks he's a monster, and he's watching your face to see if you agree. Every comforting thing you had loaded is now useless: "she's at peace" is what he just said and he hates himself for it, and anything reassuring — "that's completely normal", "anyone would feel that" — will sound like you weren't listening, because he hasn't asked for a verdict. Start the timer. Stay in the room with it. One rule: you lose the moment you make it alright.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said less than he did — and I let at least one silence run on past comfortable.
  • I never once tried to make it better, find a bright side, or say "at least".
  • I asked about her, not just about how he's coping — and I made "no" an easy answer.
  • I took away his job of being fine, without arguing that he isn't.
  • I named a specific day I'm coming back, and I didn't make him ask me for anything.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Everyone knows someone who was brilliant when it happened, and everyone knows someone who crossed the road. The odd part is that they're usually the same sort of person, and the one who crossed the road wasn't cold — they just couldn't think of anything to say and were terrified of getting it wrong in front of someone already in pieces.

Which is how the fear produces the worst outcome available. Saying a clumsy thing is survivable, and mostly forgotten; the bereaved will tell you they can barely remember what anyone said. What they remember, with total clarity and for the rest of their lives, is who came and who didn't. Nothing is the only unforgivable option, and it's the one that fear keeps choosing.

It's worth noticing that no culture has ever left this to individual judgement. Sitting shiva, the wake, the nine nights, the forty days, the endless tea — the details differ wildly, but the instruction underneath is the same everywhere, and it isn't about words. It's a rota. It tells you to go to the house, and it tells you what to carry, precisely so that you never have to be clever, only present.

Two bigger questions

  1. Where you're from, what actually happens when someone dies — who goes to the house, when, what do they bring, and what are they expected to say? What would strike a British visitor as strange about it, and what would strike you as strange or cold about the way it's done here?
  2. Is our fear of saying the wrong thing really about the other person at all? Consider how much of what gets said at a funeral is aimed at the mourner, and how much is the speaker trying to get out of the room — and whether a culture that talks about death less has made that fear better or considerably worse.
