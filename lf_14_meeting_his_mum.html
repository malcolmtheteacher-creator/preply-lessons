Meeting His Mum

She's been cooking since nine, there's a dog, there's too much food, and there are questions. You'd like her to like you. You'd also rather not hand over your entire life between the starter and the pudding.

The Scene 🎬

Sunday, about half twelve. This is Danny's mum's house — the house you've been hearing about for four months, and it is exactly as described, right down to the dog and the tiles nobody has ever got round to replacing. Carol has been in the kitchen since nine. There is enough food for eleven people. There are four of you, and one of you is a dog.

You'd like her to like you. She'd like to like you. Neither of you would dream of saying so, so instead there's wine you didn't ask for, a story about Danny aged nine, and a slow, warm, perfectly friendly stream of questions. Some of them are easy. Some of them are about the future. And one or two are questions Danny hasn't answered either — which is how you find out you're being asked in his place.

Nothing here is hostile. That's what makes it hard. You can't be blunt with someone who's been standing over a hot oven since nine on your behalf, and you can't tell her things that aren't yours to tell. So you do the other thing: you answer warmly, you answer honestly, and you don't quite answer.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of the last time you were a guest in someone's home for the first time. What did you do in the first ninety seconds — and what were you hoping they'd decide about you?
  2. When someone asks you something you'd rather not answer, which are you: the one who changes the subject, the one who gives a vague answer, or the one who says it's private? Which of the three do you find easiest to forgive in other people?

Who's who

You
The One Being Auditioned 🍽️

Four months in, and you'd rather like this to work. You want to be warm, you want to be honest, and you'd quite like to leave without having promised anybody anything.

The other voice
Carol, Danny's Mum 🥘

Up since seven, cooking since nine, apologising for all of it. She isn't nosy, exactly. She's just been told almost nothing for four months and she's making the most of what's finally in front of her.

The Conversation 💬

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Carol asks about the relationship twice — once gently, once much more directly. The guest answers the first one and doesn't really answer the second. What did they add to that second answer to stop it landing as a refusal?

2. There is one question the guest doesn't answer at all — they hand it to someone who isn't even in the room. What was the question about, and why was handing it over the honest thing to do rather than the cowardly one?

3. Twice the guest turns Carol's attention back onto Carol. What were the two moments, and what did they have in common?

1. They said they'd rather not say, and then immediately offered her an answer at a later date — ask me in a year — which turns a wall into a wait. They also gave her the reason, which was that they'd rather find out than promise her something they can't be sure of yet, so she hears honesty rather than a door closing.
2. The job in Leeds, and whether it meant the two of them moving. It's Danny's news, and answering would have meant telling his mother something he had chosen not to tell her himself — so passing it to him isn't dodging, it's leaving his news where it belongs, and the guest points out he's a few feet away, which makes it an offer rather than an excuse.
3. The kitchen, at the door, and the recipe, at the table. Both take her out of the questioner's chair and put her somewhere she's happy to be, and — this is why they work — both are true. A compliment you mean isn't a tactic, even when it's also useful.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the two hours in which you are being decided about. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is what makes it stick.

Say it like you mean it. Deflection lives or dies on warmth, because the same words can be a shrug or a gift depending entirely on the tune. "We'll see" said briskly, with your eyes somewhere else, is a snub; the same two words said slowly, with a bit of a laugh in them, are an answer. So slow down, look at her, and let her hear that you're enjoying being asked — even while you're not telling her.

🚪 Through the door

Phrase 1

You're on the doorstep. She's been on her feet since nine and she's more nervous than you are. Which greeting makes the whole morning feel worth it to her?

A. "Thanks for the invite."
B. "Sorry we're late, the traffic was unbelievable."
C. "Thank you so much for having me — I've heard a lot about this kitchen."
C — "Thank you so much for having me — I've heard a lot about this kitchen."
"Thank you for having me" is the standard formula and it does its job, but the second half is where the work happens, because it tells her she has been talked about warmly in a house she's never been inside. The kitchen matters too: it's her territory, and it's where she's been standing all morning, so noticing it is a way of noticing her without saying anything so direct that either of you gets embarrassed. A is thin and could be said to anybody, and B makes your arrival about your own bad morning.
Phrase 2

She's refusing to sit down and there are four pans going. You want to offer help in a way she can accept without admitting she needs it. Complete the chunk: "Can I give you a ___ with anything?" — one word, and it turns a big offer into a small one.

"Can I give you a hand with anything?"
To "give someone a hand" means to help them, and it's much lighter than "can I help you", which can accidentally suggest you think she's struggling. "With anything" is the other half of the trick, because you're not proposing a job — you're leaving her to choose one, or to wave you away, which people almost always do while thinking well of you for asking.

🍷 More of everything

Phrase 3

There's a glass of wine in your hand already and she's halfway to the fridge for a beer to go with it. You're driving later. Which refusal actually stops her, without going cold and without telling a lie you'll be stuck with for years?

A. "No, I'm alright."
B. "That's very kind, but I'm honestly fine with the one."
C. "No thanks, I don't really drink."
B — "That's very kind, but I'm honestly fine with the one."
"That's very kind" takes the offer properly before it turns it down, so she's thanked rather than refused, and "fine with the one" says the drink already in your hand is enough — it's a full answer, which is why she stops. "Honestly" is small but it's doing a lot: it tells her you're not just being polite, which is exactly what she'd otherwise assume. A is flat and she'll ask again in ten minutes; C is a lie that this family will believe about you forever.

😅 The family stories

Phrase 4

She's telling you a story about him aged nine, and you already know the ending. You want to show you're in on it — fondly, not wearily. Put the pieces in order:

about six times,He's told mewell briefed.that storyso I'm
"He's told me that story about six times, so I'm well briefed."
To be "briefed" is to be given all the information you need before you go into something, the way a lawyer or a soldier is briefed, so calling yourself "well briefed" turns the repetition into a joke the two of you are now inside together. It also quietly tells her something lovely, which is that her son tells this story at home — that the family she's worried about being left out of is one you already live in a bit. Note the vagueness of "about six times": an exact number would sound like a count, and a count sounds like a complaint.

🙊 When the questions get pointed

Phrase 5

"So. You two. How's it all going?" You want to give her something real and warm without opening the whole file. Complete it: "It's ___ ___, but it's going well." — two words; the phrase means it's too soon to judge, and the second word is the plural of a twenty-four-hour period.

"It's early days, but it's going well."
"Early days" says a thing is too new to be judged yet, and it's the most useful four letters in this entire lunch, because it declines to predict anything while sounding like an answer rather than a dodge. The second half is what saves it from being cold: she gets a genuine, warm report — it's going well — and what she doesn't get is a promise, which is the only part you were ever going to withhold.
Phrase 6

She's pushed harder: do you see it going anywhere, long term? You genuinely don't know, and you're not going to invent an answer to make her happy. Which reply refuses without blaming her for asking?

A. "I hope so!"
B. "That's a bit personal."
C. "I wouldn't like to say, honestly — ask me in a year."
C — "I wouldn't like to say, honestly — ask me in a year."
"I wouldn't like to say" puts the reason for the silence in you rather than in her question, which means nobody has been told off: you're not saying she shouldn't have asked, only that you'd rather not commit words to it. Then "ask me in a year" rescues the whole thing, because it's warm, it's a bit of a joke, and it promises her an answer at a date — so what she hears is "not yet" instead of "no". A hands her a hope you might not be able to keep, and B, however reasonable, tells her she's crossed a line, and she'll spend the rest of the afternoon apologising.
Phrase 7

Now she asks about something that is his news, not yours — a job, a plan, something he hasn't told her. You need to pass it on without it feeling like a wall. Put the pieces in order:

that one.answerI'lllet him
"I'll let him answer that one."
"I'll let him" sounds generous rather than evasive, as though you're handing him a turn he's owed instead of getting rid of a question you don't want. The real work is done by "that one", because it marks this single question out from all the others you've cheerfully answered — you're not closing the conversation, you're closing one item in it, and that's why she doesn't feel shut out.

🥘 Turning it round

Phrase 8

The food's in front of you and it's good, and she's been apologising for it since you arrived. Say the thing that both means it and gets her talking about herself for a bit instead of you. In your own words — what do you say?

e.g. "This is genuinely lovely — is it your recipe?"
"Genuinely" is carrying the compliment, because she has heard the polite version of that sentence from every guest she's ever fed, and the one word separates you from all of them. But the question is the better half: a recipe always has somebody behind it — a mother, a neighbour, a magazine from 1994 — so you've handed her a subject she'd love to be asked about, and the interrogation quietly becomes a conversation. It only works if you mean it, which, luckily, you do.

👋 Leaving well

Phrase 9

She's eyeing the washing-up and there's still pudding she's threatening you with. You want to release her — and yourself — without either of you saying "I want to go now." Put the pieces in order:

let youon.I'dgetbetter
"I'd better let you get on."
"To get on" here means to carry on with what you were doing, and the whole phrase is polite because of who it's about: you haven't said you'd like to leave, you've said you don't want to hold her up, which lets both of you out of the room without anyone admitting anything. "I'd better" adds the sense that you're being made to go by the clock rather than choosing to, which is a small fiction everybody is happy to accept.
Phrase 10

Coat on, dog at your knees, front door open. One sentence, and it's the one she'll repeat to him on the phone next week. Which one does it?

A. "Thanks for lunch."
B. "Thank you, really — you've made it very easy."
C. "Well, that was nice."
B — "Thank you, really — you've made it very easy."
"Really" does the job of a whole sentence, because it lifts this thank-you out of the pile of automatic ones she's been collecting all day. And the second half answers a question she was too proud to ask out loud — whether you'd been dreading it, whether she'd been too much — so you're telling her she was kind before you're out of the door, at the one moment she can't argue back. A is what you'd say to a colleague who bought the sandwiches, and C sounds like a review.

🔑 Make them yours

Answering warmly without answering fully is a skill you'll use long after this lunch — with colleagues, with neighbours, with anyone who asks a fair question at the wrong moment. Pin three of these to your own life so you'll actually reach for them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"It's early days, but it's going well." — something new in your life that people keep asking about. What is it, and what's your version of this line?

"I wouldn't like to say, honestly — ask me in a year." — the question you get asked that you genuinely can't answer yet.

"This is genuinely lovely — is it your recipe?" — whose home, kitchen or office will you next be a guest in? What could you honestly ask them about?

The Twist

😬 The kitchen door closes

One more round — no script. Danny is outside on the phone to his brother, and Carol quietly pushes the kitchen door to with her foot. Then, not smiling: "While he's out there. Is he alright? Because he's not telling me anything, he's not been to work in three weeks, and you'd know." Every phrase you've just practised is now the wrong tool — a warm half-answer here will tell her something is very wrong, and a full one tells his mother what he chose not to. She's not being nosy any more; she's frightened, and she's asking the only person who can see him every day. Start the timer. You have about a minute before he comes back in, and you have to choose: what do you actually say — and can you say it so that she's not left more frightened than when she asked?

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said something true about her house or her food, and I meant it.
  • I turned down the second drink so clearly that she didn't ask again — and stayed warm doing it.
  • When I didn't answer, I gave her a reason, or a date, or both — never just a stop.
  • I kept his news his, without making it sound like a secret.
  • The last thing she heard from me was about her, not about me.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

There's a version of this lunch where you tell Carol everything. She's delighted for about a day. Then she knows things her son didn't tell her, and you've become the person in the family who tells her things — a job with no end date and no way out.

There's another version where you give her nothing at all, and she decides you're cold, and that decision will sit in the corner of every Christmas for the next twenty years.

So most of us do the third thing, which gets called dishonest and mostly isn't. Answering warmly without answering fully is how you stay loyal to two people at once when one of them has been up since seven cooking for you.

Two bigger questions

  1. When your partner's family asks about the relationship, whose information is it? Is there anything about the two of you that you'd say you have the right to tell on your own — and who decides where that line is?
  2. Carol is asking because she's been told almost nothing for four months. Is keeping a family at arm's length from your life a kindness to them, a kindness to yourself, or just easier than the alternative? Where would you draw the line with your own?
