The Wright Brothers

Two bicycle makers from Ohio who solved a problem the whole world had failed to crack — and taught us how to fly.

Today: you'll tell the story of how two self-taught brothers invented the aeroplane — and discuss why the world refused to believe them at first — using the language of invention and the grammar of expectation.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. His garage was a happy mess of half-built radios and bicycle parts, and he was happiest there, a born tinkerer with grease on his hands and a screwdriver in his pocket.
a tinkerer (n) — someone who likes to experiment with and repair machines in a hands-on, practical way.
2. The idea took over her life completely; it became an obsession she thought about at breakfast, at work, and lying awake at three in the morning.
an obsession (n) — an idea or desire that fills the mind and will not let go. (adj: obsessed)
3. Watch a soaring bird bank into a turn: it tilts by wing-warping, twisting one wingtip up and the other down until the whole body leans into the curve.
wing-warping (n) — the Wrights' method of twisting an aircraft's wings to control roll and turn.
4. A design can look perfect on paper yet be deeply flawed — one hidden mistake in the figures, and the whole machine fails.
flawed (adj) — containing a fault or mistake that spoils something otherwise sound.
5. She checked every figure three times, repeated each test, and threw out any result she could not reproduce; her colleagues teased her about such rigour, but her work never failed.
rigour (n) — careful, exact thoroughness; refusing to accept anything that is not properly proven.
6. The little boat carried no sail and no oars; a small motor at the stern gave it all its propulsion, pushing it steadily upstream against the current.
propulsion (n) — the force that drives a vehicle or machine forward. (v: to propel)
7. Anyone can hold a note for a second, but the singer held it sustained across the whole bar, steady and unbroken, until the audience gasped.
sustained (adj) — kept going steadily over a period of time, rather than brief or interrupted.
8. When the salesman promised the pills would cure everything, the old farmer folded his arms, raised one eyebrow, and gave him a long sceptical look.
sceptical (adj) — doubtful; not willing to believe something without strong proof. (n: scepticism)
9. Instead of explaining how the blender worked, the shop assistant said nothing, plugged it in, and began to demonstrate it, turning a bowl of fruit into smooth juice before our eyes.
to demonstrate (v) — to show clearly, by doing it, that something works or is true. (n: demonstration)
10. For years they had laughed at her warnings; when the bridge finally cracked exactly where she had said it would, she felt a quiet, bittersweet vindication.
vindication (n) — proof that someone doubted or blamed was right after all. (v: to vindicate)

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The bicycle shop and the buzzards

Wilbur and Orville Wright were brothers from Dayton, Ohio, who ran a modest bicycle business — repairing, selling and eventually building their own machines. Neither had been to university; what they shared was a sharp, restless curiosity and a habit of solving problems with their hands. The bicycle trade taught them something that proved crucial: a moving machine does not need to be perfectly stable, because a skilled rider can balance it. Most inventors of the age were chasing aircraft that would fly themselves. The Wrights came to believe the opposite — that the pilot, like a cyclist, must learn to control the machine.

Powered flight was an old obsession, and a long graveyard of failures. The brothers read everything they could, then watched the sky. Studying how buzzards held their balance in a gust, Wilbur noticed they twisted the tips of their wings. Back at the shop, idly twisting a long cardboard box, he saw how a wing might do the same — and so wing-warping was born, a way of rolling the aircraft left or right by gently twisting its wings. Pitch, roll and yaw: they were beginning to see flight as three problems of control, not one problem of lift.

But when they built gliders from the published flight data of the day, the wings refused to lift as the books promised. The figures, they realised, were flawed. Rather than trust them, the brothers built their own wind tunnel — a wooden box with a fan — and tested over two hundred miniature wings inside it, with a rigour no rival came close to. By 1902 they had their own accurate tables, and a glider that truly answered to its pilot.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the bicycle trade teach the Wrights that shaped their whole approach to flight?

2. Why did they build their own wind tunnel instead of trusting existing flight data?

1. That a moving machine need not be self-stabilising — a skilled rider (or pilot) can balance and control it. So they focused on control, not on a machine that flew itself.

2. The published figures were flawed: gliders built from them didn't lift as promised. So the brothers tested over 200 wing shapes themselves and built their own accurate tables.

Discuss:
  • The Wrights had no scientific training, yet they out-thought the experts. What can amateurs sometimes do that established professionals can't?

Part 2 — Twelve seconds at Kitty Hawk

For their flying ground they chose Kitty Hawk, on the wind-blown sandy coast of North Carolina, where steady breezes and soft dunes gave them lift and a forgiving place to land. Their camp sat near Kill Devil Hills. There they fitted their machine, the Flyer, with a light petrol engine — built in their own shop with their mechanic, Charlie Taylor — and two propellers they had designed from their wind-tunnel theory.

On the morning of 17 December 1903, the wind was cold and strong. The brothers tossed a coin to decide who would fly first; Orville won. At about half past ten, with Wilbur running alongside to steady the wingtip, the Flyer lifted off the rail and into the air. It stayed up for just twelve seconds and travelled about a hundred and twenty feet — a distance you could almost throw a stone. It was brief, low and wobbling. It was also the first sustained, powered, controlled flight in history. They flew three more times that day, taking turns, the longest lasting fifty-nine seconds and covering over eight hundred feet. Then a gust caught the Flyer on the ground and damaged it, and the day's flying was done.

Five witnesses saw it. A handful of newspapers ran garbled, half-believing reports; most ignored it altogether. The brothers had changed the world before breakfast, and the world had barely noticed.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was windy, sandy Kitty Hawk a good place to test the Flyer?

2. What exactly made that first twelve-second flight historic — and how did the world react?

1. Steady strong winds gave the machine lift, and the soft sand dunes near Kill Devil Hills gave a forgiving surface to take off from and land on.

2. It was the first sustained, powered and controlled flight (Orville at the controls after winning the coin toss). The world barely reacted — only a few, often garbled, newspaper reports.

Discuss:
  • One of the most important moments in human history was watched by just five people and largely ignored. Why do world-changing breakthroughs so often go unnoticed at the time?

Part 3 — Fliers or liars?

Kitty Hawk had proved flight was possible; it had not yet proved it was useful. Back home over Huffman Prairie near Dayton, the brothers kept refining the machine, and in 1905 their Flyer III could bank, turn, circle the field and stay up for over half an hour at a time. They now had a genuinely practical aeroplane. Then, strangely, they stopped flying altogether — refusing to demonstrate it until they had secured their patents and a buyer, terrified that rivals would simply copy their hard-won secrets.

The silence cost them dearly. With no public flights to see, the press grew openly sceptical; one famous headline as good as asked whether they were "fliers or liars." For nearly three years two men who had genuinely conquered the air were dismissed by many as fantasists or frauds.

In 1908 they finally answered the doubters in the only language that mattered. Wilbur shipped a Flyer to France and, at Le Mans, flew banking turns and effortless figure-of-eights before astonished crowds; Orville flew for the United States Army back home. The mockery collapsed into wonder. Former critics issued public vindication and open apologies, and the brothers became world-famous almost overnight. They had been right all along — and now, at last, everyone could see it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the brothers stop flying for nearly three years, and what did their silence cost them?

2. How, in the end, did they silence the doubters?

1. They refused to demonstrate the practical 1905 Flyer III until their patents and a buyer were secured, fearing imitators. In the silence the press turned sceptical and called them frauds ("fliers or liars").

2. By demonstrating in public in 1908 — Wilbur's banking turns and figure-eights at Le Mans, Orville flying for the US Army — which turned mockery into vindication and made them world-famous.

Discuss:
  • They chose secrecy to protect their invention, and it nearly destroyed their reputation. When you've made something remarkable, is it better to protect it or to show the world at once?

The grammar this story runs on: talking about the future

The Wrights' story is one of ambition, evidence and bold claims about what was coming. English picks a different form depending on why we are looking forward — a prediction, an intention, or a fixed arrangement.

The three tools:
• will — a prediction or a spontaneous decision: "This machine will change the world."
• going to — an intention already decided, or a prediction backed by evidence: "We are going to fly this machine"; "Look at those clouds — it is going to rain."
• present continuous — a fixed future arrangement, already on the calendar: "They are testing the Flyer at dawn."

1. will vs going to — prediction and intention

Reach for will for a prediction or a decision made on the spot; reach for going to for an intention already in place, or a prediction the evidence is pointing to.

1. Wilbur was certain: "One day people (travel) across oceans by air." (will / going to)

2. "We (build) our own engine," Orville announced — the plan was already settled. (will / going to)

3. The wind is rising fast at Kitty Hawk; the Flyer (be) hard to control today. (will / going to)

1. will travel (a prediction about the distant future)

2. are going to build (an intention already decided)

3. is going to be (a prediction the evidence points to)

2. present continuous — fixed arrangements

Use the present continuous for a future plan that is already arranged — a time, a place, a commitment.

1. The brothers (test) the Flyer at first light tomorrow.

2. A reporter (come) to Huffman Prairie on Friday to watch them fly.

3. We (not / demonstrate) the machine until the patent is signed.

1. are testing

2. is coming

3. are not demonstrating

3. Put them together — narrate a paragraph

Fill each gap with the form the meaning needs (will, going to, or present continuous). Read it aloud once you've finished.

Orville checks the sky. "We (fly) at dawn — it's all arranged," he says. Wilbur nods: "Look at this steady wind; the Flyer (lift) cleanly today." He pauses, then decides on the spot: "I (run) alongside the wingtip myself." Neither doubts that this small machine (change) the world.

are flying (a fixed arrangement) · is going to lift (a prediction the evidence points to) · will run (a decision made on the spot) · will change (a confident prediction)

Notice how each form answers a different question: is it arranged, is the evidence already there, or is it a prediction or a sudden choice?

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • A goal you set yourself that friends or family said was impossible — they were sure it would never happen, but you were certain you'd get there in the end.
  • A career change, business or move abroad you made against everyone's advice — they thought it would fail; you were convinced it was going to work out.
  • The electric car: for years people laughed and said it would never replace petrol — they were wrong, and it would go on to reshape the whole industry.
  • Vincent van Gogh, who sold almost nothing in his lifetime — nobody believed his paintings would ever matter, yet they would later become some of the most loved in the world.
  • The smartphone or the internet — early critics insisted it was a passing fad that would never catch on, sure it would soon be forgotten.
  • A musician, athlete or company from your own country that was dismissed at the start — everyone assumed they'd fade away, but they were going to prove the doubters spectacularly wrong.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Two brothers, working alone in a bicycle shop, beat governments and famous scientists to powered flight. What does that tell us about where great ideas really come from?
  2. They were certain they would succeed long before they had any proof. Where is the line between useful self-belief and simply fooling yourself?
  3. Flight shrank the world — for travel, but also for war. When you invent something this powerful, how much responsibility do you bear for what others do with it?
