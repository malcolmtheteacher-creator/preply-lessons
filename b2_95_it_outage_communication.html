Explaining a Service Outage to the Business

B2 Level · IT & Systems · Crisis Communication

IT & Systems · Dewey 004

First Thoughts

It is 9:42am on Tuesday. Something has gone wrong with your core system. Business users cannot log in. The phones are ringing. Your engineers are investigating — but the business does not want your engineers. They want you, and they want to know what is happening.

Today is about explaining outages in plain English — honest, calm, and without jargon — so that the people who rely on your systems feel informed, not panicked.

Take a moment with each question below. Share as much detail as you like — specific examples, what you would actually say, what tripped you up last time.

1. When a system goes down in your organisation, who is the first non-technical person who has to be told? How soon do they hear?

2. What is the difference between a good outage message and a bad one? Can you give a real example of each?

3. Why do so many outage emails use phrases like “intermittent issues” or “service degradation”? Do you think they help or hurt?

4. If you have no update to give, what should you say?

Quick opener — pick whichever you prefer:

  • Someone once said: “Users will forgive the outage. They will not forgive the silence.” Do you agree?
  • Have you ever sent an update that said less than you actually knew? Why?

an outageA period when a system is not working or available.
downtimeThe total time a system is not usable.
to be affectedTo feel the impact of the problem. “Users in Europe are affected.”
root causeThe underlying reason for a problem — not just the symptom.
a workaroundA temporary way to keep working while the real fix is in progress.
to restore (a service)To bring the system back to normal working order.
an ETA (estimated time to resolution)The time by which you expect the problem to be fixed.
a post-mortem / incident reportA written review, after the fact, of what went wrong and what you learned.

Key Language

Useful chunks of language for this conversation. Read each phrase aloud and think about when you would actually reach for it.

First message — we know something is wrong

  • “We are aware of an issue affecting [system] and we are investigating.”
  • “Some users are currently unable to log in. Our engineers are on it.”
  • “We will send the next update within fifteen minutes, even if we do not have new information yet.”

Saying what you know — and what you don’t

  • “Here is what we know right now. Here is what we do not yet know.”
  • “We do not yet have a root cause. We expect to know more in the next thirty minutes.”
  • “I would rather tell you less, accurately, than more, inaccurately.”

Giving an ETA safely

  • “Our current best estimate for full restoration is 11:30.”
  • “I want to be clear that this is an estimate, not a commitment — I will update you if anything changes.”
  • “If we are not back up by 11:30, expect another update from me at that time.”

After the fix — closing calmly

  • “Service was fully restored at 11:17 and has been stable since.”
  • “We will share a short incident report tomorrow with the root cause and the steps we are taking.”
  • “Thank you for your patience — and for the constructive way you reported the issue.”

The Scenario

The situation: Your company’s main sales CRM went down at 9:42 this morning. Around two hundred sales people cannot log in. You manage the IT team. Your engineers are investigating but do not yet know the cause. The Head of Sales has messaged you: “Are we losing deals?” The CEO has messaged: “What is going on?”

Your task: Handle the next hour. Write (or role-play) three messages: the first alert, a 30-minute update, and the all-clear. Each message should be short, honest, and in plain English.
