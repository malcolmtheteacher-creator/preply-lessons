What makes a great lecture?

Research from Harvard's Derek Bok Center for Teaching and Learning found that students remember only 10-20% of a standard lecture after 3 days. But lectures that use specific techniques can push retention to 60-80%.

The difference is not what the lecturer knows. It's how they deliver it.

Today you'll learn a framework called REACH that covers the 5 decisions every effective lecturer makes -- whether they're teaching psychology, engineering, history, or medicine.

Why this matters for you

Giving a lecture in English when it's not your first language adds an extra layer. You need to:

  • Sound clear and confident (not just correct)
  • Use signposting language so students can follow your structure
  • Handle questions without losing your flow
  • Make complex ideas accessible to undergraduates

REACH gives you a system for all of this.

R

Road Map -- Tell them where you're going

Students follow better when they know the structure in advance.

What it means: In the first 2 minutes, give your audience a clear outline of what you'll cover, how long it will take, and what they'll be able to do by the end.

Strong Road Map

"Today we're covering three things: first, what inflation actually is and why prices rise; second, two real-world examples -- Germany in the 1920s and Zimbabwe in the 2000s; and third, how central banks try to control it. By the end, you should be able to explain inflation to someone who's never studied economics. We'll finish with time for questions."

Weak Road Map

"OK so today we're going to talk about inflation, there's quite a lot to cover so let's just get started..."

YOUR TURN: You're giving a 30-minute lecture on any topic from your field. Write or say your Road Map opening (3-4 sentences).

Language for Road Maps

"I've divided today's lecture into three parts..."

Clear structure signal -- students can take notes by section

"By the end of this session, you should be able to..."

Sets expectations -- students know what success looks like

"I'll leave 5 minutes at the end for questions, but feel free to raise your hand if anything is unclear as we go."

Manages interaction expectations from the start

E

Explain Simply -- Break it down for non-experts

If undergrads can't follow you, the lecture fails -- no matter how expert you are.

What it means: Take your expert knowledge and make it accessible. Use analogies, concrete examples, and simple language before introducing technical terms.

Strong Explanation

"Think of a cell like a factory. The nucleus is the manager's office -- it holds all the instructions. The mitochondria are the generators -- they produce the energy. And the membrane is the security gate -- it controls what comes in and what goes out. That's the basic structure of every cell in your body."

Weak Explanation

"The cell consists of a phospholipid bilayer membrane surrounding cytoplasm containing organelles including ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, and mitochondria, each with distinct biochemical functions..."

YOUR TURN: Choose a concept from your field that students find difficult. Explain it using an everyday analogy. Say it out loud -- don't just write it.

Language for Explaining

"In other words..."

Signals you're about to rephrase something complex in simpler terms

"Think of it like..."

Introduces an analogy -- bridges expert knowledge to student experience

"Let me give you a concrete example..."

Moves from abstract to specific -- where real understanding happens

A

Activate -- Get students involved

Passive listening kills attention after 10-15 minutes. Activation resets the clock.

What it means: Every 10-15 minutes, make students DO something: think-pair-share, quick poll, prediction question, brief writing task. A lecture is not a monologue.

Strong Activation

"Before I tell you what happened next, I want you to predict: it's 1962, the Cuban Missile Crisis. You're President Kennedy. Soviet ships are heading towards Cuba carrying nuclear missiles. Your generals are saying 'bomb them now.' What would you do -- and why? Take 20 seconds to decide. ... OK, hands up -- who said attack? Who said negotiate? Who said something else entirely?"

Weak Activation

"Any questions? ... No? OK, let's continue..." [continues monologue for 40 more minutes]

YOUR TURN: Design an activation moment for a lecture on your topic. What would you ask students to DO (not just listen to)?

Language for Activating

"Before I tell you the answer, I'd like you to predict..."

Creates curiosity gap -- students are invested in hearing the answer

"Turn to the person next to you and discuss..."

Think-pair-share -- low stakes, high engagement

"Take 30 seconds to write down..."

Brief writing forces individual processing before group discussion

C

Check -- Verify understanding

"Does everyone understand?" is not checking. Nobody will say no in a room of 200.

What it means: Use specific questions, quick quizzes, or application tasks that reveal whether students actually understand -- not just whether they're nodding.

Strong Check

"I've just explained three types of bridge structure. Without looking at your notes, can anyone name all three? ... Good. Now -- if I asked you to design a bridge to cross a 200-metre valley in an earthquake zone, which type would you choose? Why?"

Weak Check

"Does that make sense? OK great, moving on..."

YOUR TURN: You've just explained a key concept in your field. Write a checking question that would reveal whether students truly understand it (not just recognise it).

Language for Checking

"Without looking at your notes, can someone explain..."

Tests recall, not recognition -- much more powerful

"Here's a scenario -- which theory applies here, and why?"

Application question -- reveals deep understanding vs surface learning

H

Hook -- Open strong, close strong

Students remember the first and last 5 minutes best (primacy/recency effect).

What it means: Open with something that creates curiosity or surprise. Close with a clear takeaway and a forward-looking question. Never open with admin or close with "well, that's all we have time for."

Strong Opening Hook

"Right now, somewhere in the world, a bridge is being crossed by 10,000 vehicles. The engineer who designed it made one critical decision 30 years ago that determines whether it holds or collapses. Today, we're going to learn what that decision was."

Strong Closing Hook

"So here's the one thing I want you to leave with: great design isn't about complexity -- it's about constraint. The question isn't 'how much can I add?' but 'what can I remove and still have it work?' Next week, we'll put that principle to the test with your own designs."

YOUR TURN: Write an opening hook for a lecture in your field. Make it surprising, curious, or provocative. Say it out loud with confidence.

Language for Hooks

"What if I told you that..."

Creates a curiosity gap -- audience leans in

"So if there's one thing I want you to take away from today..."

Strong closing -- gives students a single anchor to remember

Lecture Opening A: Introduction to the Industrial Revolution

"Good morning everyone. Take a look at this image. This is Manchester in 1850 -- a city where the average life expectancy was 26 years. Not because of war or plague, but because of factories. [pause] How did something designed to make life better end up making it so much worse?

Today we're covering three things: first, the economic forces that triggered industrialisation; second, the human cost -- working conditions, child labour, and public health; and third, how the backlash against those conditions created the labour rights we have today.

Let me start with a simple idea. Industrialisation is what happens when a society shifts from making things by hand to making things by machine. Think of it like this: imagine you bake bread at home -- you can make maybe 10 loaves a day. Now imagine a machine that makes 10,000. That's incredible, right? But what happens to all the bakers?"

ANALYSE: Which parts of REACH can you find? Which are missing? Label each paragraph.

Lecture Opening B: Introduction to Nutrition Science

"OK, so, welcome to Nutrition Science. Right. Today we need to cover, um, quite a lot actually. We'll look at macronutrients and micronutrients, and different dietary guidelines, and also some stuff about metabolism. There's a lot to get through so I'll try to go quickly.

So, macronutrients. These are the big ones -- proteins, carbohydrates, and fats. Your body needs them in large amounts. Micronutrients are things like vitamins and minerals -- you need them in smaller amounts but they're still important.

Does that make sense? Good. Let's move on to dietary guidelines..."

ANALYSE: What's wrong here? Which REACH components are weak or missing? How would you fix this opening?

Key insight

Lecture A uses H + R + E in the first 3 minutes. The audience is hooked, oriented, and already learning. The A (Activate) and C (Check) come at the 12-15 minute mark.

Lecture B has a vague road map, no hook, a surface-level explanation, and a false check ("Does that make sense?"). An undergraduate would zone out within 90 seconds.

REWRITE: Take Lecture B's topic (Nutrition Science) and write a better opening using at least H + R + E. Say it aloud as if you're standing in front of 100 undergrads.

Guided Practice: Build a Mini-Lecture

How this works

We'll build a 5-minute lecture segment together, step by step. First I'll show you one (I DO), then we'll build one together (WE DO), then you'll create your own (YOU DO).

I DO: Watch how the framework works

Topic: "Why do cities flood?"

H - Hook (Click to see the model) +

"In July 2021, the German city of Ahrweiler disappeared under water in less than 6 hours. 180 people died. The strange thing? Engineers had been warning about exactly this for 20 years. So why did nobody listen? Today we're going to find out why cities flood -- and why it keeps getting worse."

Why it works: Specific real event, human stakes, creates a puzzle ("why did nobody listen?"), sets up the lecture.

R - Road Map (Click to see) +

"We'll look at three things: the natural causes of flooding -- rainfall, river systems, and geography; the human causes -- urbanisation, deforestation, and poor drainage design; and what cities are doing about it, from Tokyo's underground cathedral to Rotterdam's water plazas."

Why it works: Three clear sections, mixes science with real solutions, the examples create curiosity ("underground cathedral?").

E - Explain Simply (Click to see) +

"So here's the basic problem. Think of a city like a giant umbrella turned upside down. In nature, when rain falls, the ground absorbs it -- soil, grass, trees all soak up water like a sponge. But when you cover everything with concrete and tarmac, the water has nowhere to go. It just runs off -- faster and faster -- until it all arrives at the lowest point at the same time. That's a flood."

Why it works: Analogy (upside-down umbrella, sponge), everyday language before technical terms, builds understanding step by step.

A - Activate (Click to see) +

"OK, I want you to look at these two aerial photos -- one from 1970, one from today. Same city, same river. Take 30 seconds: what differences do you notice? What do you think has changed about the flood risk? Turn to the person next to you and compare your observations."

Why it works: Visual comparison, prediction task, pair discussion. Students apply the concept they just learned to real evidence.

Mixed Practice -- Apply REACH in Different Situations

These scenarios require different REACH components

For each situation, decide which part of REACH is needed and produce a response. The situations are mixed on purpose -- you need to diagnose what's needed, not just follow a sequence.

Scenario 1: You're 15 minutes into your lecture. Students are looking at their phones.

Which REACH component is needed here? What would you say/do?

See analysis +

A (Activate) -- You've been talking too long without involvement. Interrupt yourself with a quick task: "OK, before I go further -- take 30 seconds and write down the key difference between X and Y. No looking at slides." This resets attention.

Scenario 2: You're about to introduce a complex theory that has 4 stages.

Which REACH components do you need? What would you say?

See analysis +

R (Road Map) + E (Explain Simply) -- Before diving in, give the structure: "This theory has 4 stages. I'll take you through each one with an example." Then use an analogy to make the first stage accessible before adding technical detail.

Scenario 3: You've just finished explaining a concept and several students look confused.

What do you do? Which REACH component? Write exactly what you'd say.

See analysis +

C (Check) + E (re-Explain) -- Don't just ask "any questions?" Use a specific check: "Let me see if I explained that clearly. Can someone give me an example of [concept] from everyday life?" If they can't, re-explain with a different analogy.

Scenario 4: It's the last 3 minutes of your lecture. You have one more slide but you're running out of time.

What do you prioritise? Which REACH component matters most right now?

See analysis +

H (Hook -- the closing) -- Skip the extra slide. A strong close is more important than more content. "So if there's one thing I want you to take away from today..." Give the single most important idea and a forward-looking question for next time.

Scenario 5: A student asks a question you don't fully know the answer to.

This isn't a specific REACH component -- but what would you say? Write the exact words.

See analysis +

Strong responses: "That's a really interesting question. I'm not 100% sure about the specifics, but my understanding is... Let me look into it and get back to you next week." or "I don't know the exact answer to that -- does anyone else have thoughts?" Honesty builds more credibility than bluffing.

Useful phrases: "That's a great question -- let me think about that for a moment..." / "I'd need to check the specifics, but broadly speaking..." / "That's beyond the scope of today's lecture, but I'll follow up on that."
