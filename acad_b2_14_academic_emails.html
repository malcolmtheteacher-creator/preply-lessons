Academic Email Basics

Email is your primary communication tool at university. Professors expect professional, clear emails. A poorly written email can damage your academic relationships.

When to Email Your Professor

  • Asking about assignment clarifications
  • Requesting an extension or special accommodation
  • Asking to meet during office hours
  • Following up on feedback
  • Notifying of illness or emergency affecting coursework
  • Asking questions you couldn't resolve in lectures or office hours

When NOT to Email

  • Questions answered in the syllabus or lecture slides
  • Asking to be excused from participation
  • Complaints about grades (request a meeting instead)
  • Emergencies (call the main office)
  • Vague requests ("Can you help me with this?")

Email Structure

  1. Subject line: specific and descriptive
  2. Greeting: formal
  3. Opening: state your purpose clearly
  4. Body: provide context and relevant details
  5. Closing: professional
  6. Signature: full name and student ID (if requested)

Response Time

Professors typically respond within 24-48 hours on weekdays. Email during business hours (8am-5pm) when possible. Don't expect responses to evening or weekend emails immediately.

Tone and Register

Academic emails should be:

  • Formal: "I would like to request..." not "Can I get...?"
  • Polite: Please, thank you, I appreciate
  • Clear: Get to the point quickly
  • Brief: 3-4 paragraphs maximum
  • Correct: Check grammar and spelling

Language for Academic Emails

Formal Greetings

Preferred: "Dear Professor [Last Name]:" or "Dear Dr. [Last Name]:"

Also acceptable: "Hello Professor [Last Name],"

Avoid: "Hi [First Name]", "Hey", "Dear Sir/Madam" (too impersonal)

Common Email Purposes and Language

Requesting clarification:
"I have a question about the assignment. Could you clarify what you mean by 'analyze the data in context'? Should I be comparing it to other studies?"

Requesting an extension:
"I am writing to request a two-day extension on the essay due Friday. I am currently dealing with an unexpected family matter that has affected my ability to complete the work to my usual standard. I hope this is possible."

Requesting to meet:
"Would it be possible to meet during your office hours to discuss my feedback on the midterm? I would like to understand where I lost points. I am available Tuesday and Thursday afternoons."

Asking about grades:
"I received a grade of C on the essay but I am not sure why. Could we schedule a meeting to discuss the feedback in more detail?"

Notifying of absence:
"I am writing to inform you that I will be absent from the seminar on Wednesday due to illness. I will complete the reading and catch up on any material I miss. Please let me know if there are additional steps I should take."

Closing lines:
"Thank you for your time." / "I appreciate your help with this." / "I look forward to hearing from you."

Formal sign-off:
"Best regards," / "Yours sincerely," / "Thank you,"

Common mistake: Over-apologizing ("I'm so sorry to bother you..."). You're a student doing legitimate coursework. A simple, clear email is professional and appropriate.

Email Rewrite Practice

Informal Email — Make it Academic

Original (Informal):

Hi Prof Smith,
I missed the last class and was wondering if u could send me the notes? Also I didn't understand the assignment at all. Like what am I supposed to analyze? Can u explain? Thanks so much!

-Alex

Revised (Academic):

Dear Professor Smith,

I am writing to request the lecture notes from the class I missed on Wednesday due to illness. Additionally, I have a question about the assignment. Could you clarify what you mean by "analyze in context"? Should I be comparing the results to existing research, or analyzing them within the case study framework?

Thank you for your help.

Best regards,
Alex [Student ID]
Too Casual — Add Formality

Original (Too casual):

Hello,
I did bad on the exam and don't know why. Can we talk about it? I put in a lot of effort and think I should have done better. What happened?

Thanks,
Jordan

Revised (Formal and constructive):

Dear Professor Smith,

I received my exam feedback and would like to discuss my performance with you. I put considerable effort into preparation and would like to understand where my analysis was lacking. Would it be possible to schedule a meeting during your office hours?

I am available on Tuesday at 2pm or Thursday at 3pm.

Thank you,
Jordan [Student ID]
Missing Details — Add Specificity

Original (Too vague):

Dear Professor,

I need help with the assignment. Can you help me?

Thanks

Revised (Specific):

Dear Professor Smith,

I am working on the literature review for Essay 2 due next Friday. I am having difficulty determining how many sources I should cite and how to organize them thematically. Should I aim for 10-15 sources, or is this flexible? Any guidance would be helpful.

Thank you,
Alex [Student ID]

Email Best Practices

Before You Send

  • Read it aloud to check tone and clarity
  • Check for spelling and grammar errors
  • Verify you're using the correct email address
  • Double-check any attachments are included if you mentioned them
  • Make sure your subject line is descriptive
  • Read it one more time before hitting send

Email Mistakes That Hurt Your Grade

  • Asking questions answered in the syllabus (shows you didn't read it)
  • Complaining about grades instead of asking to understand them
  • Requesting extensions at the last minute without good reason
  • Multiple emails about the same question (shows you didn't wait for a response)
  • Demanding tone instead of requesting politely

If You Make a Mistake in an Email

Send a brief follow-up: "I apologize; I sent the previous email with an error. The correct information is..." Professors understand people make mistakes. A quick correction shows professionalism.

Responding to Professor Feedback

If your professor sends you feedback via email:

  • Reply within 24 hours thanking them
  • If you have questions, ask in a follow-up email
  • Use their feedback constructively on future assignments
  • Don't argue with the feedback via email (request a meeting if you disagree)

Remember: Email is a formal communication tool in academic settings. First impressions matter. A clear, professional email shows respect for your professor's time and establishes you as a serious student.
