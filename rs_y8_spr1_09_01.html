🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: A theodicy is a defence of God that explains how God can be good and powerful while evil exists. Example: The Free Will Defence — God allows free choice, which sometimes leads to evil. Or the Soul-Making Theodicy — suffering allows spiritual growth.

Question 3: How does Buddhism approach suffering differently from Christianity?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Buddhism teaches that suffering (dukkha) is part of life (First Noble Truth). Rather than justifying why God allows suffering, Buddhism focuses on its cause (craving) and the path to end it (the Eightfold Path). Buddhism doesn't need theodicy because it doesn't have a creator God.

Question 4: How does Humanism respond to evil and suffering?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Encounter: What Are Rites of Passage?

Why Do Humans Mark Life's Big Moments?

Think about the big moments in life: a baby is born, a teenager becomes an adult, two people get married, someone dies. In every culture around the world, people mark these moments with ceremonies and rituals. These aren't just parties — they are meaningful events that change how a person is seen by their community.

A rite of passage is a ceremony that marks the move from one stage of life to another. It's more than a celebration. It's a formal process where a person's role and status in their community actually changes.

Van Gennep's Three-Stage Model

In 1909, a French researcher called Arnold van Gennep noticed something remarkable. Despite huge cultural differences, rites of passage across the world all follow the same three-stage pattern:

Stage 1: Separation

The person is taken out of their normal life. They leave behind their old role. This might mean leaving home, changing clothes, or being physically separated from family.

Example: A bride leaves her parents' home on her wedding day.

Stage 2: Transition

The person is "in between" — no longer in their old role, but not yet in their new one. This is where the real change happens. They might learn new things, face challenges, or go through special rituals.

Example: The wedding ceremony itself — the couple are neither single nor yet married.

Stage 3: Incorporation

The person re-enters their community in their new role. Everyone recognises their changed status. They receive new rights and responsibilities.

Example: The couple are announced as married and celebrated by family and friends.

Key idea: Van Gennep called the middle stage "liminality" — from the Latin word limen, meaning "threshold." The person is standing on the threshold between their old life and their new one. This in-between time is where the deepest change happens.

Communitas: The Bond of Shared Experience

Anthropologist Victor Turner studied the transition stage closely. He noticed that when people go through a rite of passage together, they form a powerful bond. He called this communitas — a deep feeling of unity and equality among people sharing the same experience.

Think of it this way: soldiers who train together, students who survive exam season together, or young people going through a religious ceremony side by side — they often feel a lasting connection. Normal social differences (wealth, status, popularity) fall away during the shared experience.

Four Types of Rites of Passage

Birth Rites

Ceremonies welcoming a child into the family and community. Examples: Christian Baptism, Islamic Aqiqah, Hindu Namakarana (naming ceremony), Jewish Brit Milah.

Coming-of-Age Rites

Ceremonies marking the transition from child to adult. Examples: Jewish Bar/Bat Mitzvah, Christian Confirmation, Hindu Upanayana (Sacred Thread).

Marriage Rites

Ceremonies uniting two people and often joining families. Examples: Islamic Nikah, Christian wedding, Hindu Vivaha, Jewish Kiddushin.

Death and Mourning Rites

Ceremonies honouring the dead and supporting the living through grief. Examples: Christian funeral, Islamic Janazah, Jewish Shiva (seven days of mourning), Hindu Antyeshti (cremation).

Religious vs. Secular Rites

Religious Rites

Rooted in faith traditions, often involving prayers, sacred texts, and divine blessing. The change is understood as both social and spiritual. Examples: Baptism, Bar Mitzvah, Aqiqah.

Secular Rites

Not based on religion but still formally marked. Examples: graduation ceremonies, 18th birthday celebrations, civil marriages. These serve similar purposes — marking change, bringing community together — but without religious meaning.

Something to consider: Even in modern secular societies where fewer people follow a religion, humans still create ceremonies for life's big moments. Graduation ceremonies, milestone birthdays, and retirement parties all follow a similar pattern to traditional rites of passage. This suggests that the need to mark important transitions might be something deeply human — not just a religious tradition.

Comparing Coming-of-Age Rites Across Traditions

Each tradition marks adulthood differently. What do these differences tell us about what each religion values?

Hindu Upanayana vs. Christian Confirmation

Similar: Both mark the transition to adulthood. Both involve preparation and a public ceremony.

Different: Upanayana focuses on learning and the sacred thread. Confirmation focuses on personal spiritual commitment and anointing with oil.

Jewish Bar/Bat Mitzvah vs. Islamic Coming of Age

Similar: Both mark the point when a young person becomes responsible for religious law. Both happen around age 13.

Different: Bar/Bat Mitzvah has a major public ceremony with Torah reading. In Islam, there is no single ceremony — at puberty, the obligations of Islamic law simply apply.

Evaluate: Do We Still Need Rites of Passage?

The Question

In modern Britain, many people don't go through formal rites of passage. You might get a birthday party when you turn 18, but it's not a structured coming-of-age ceremony. Some families practise religious ceremonies, others don't. So the question is: Do we still need rites of passage, or are they outdated traditions?

Argument 1: Yes — Rites of Passage Are Still Important

Every culture in history has created rites of passage because they meet real human needs. They give people a clear moment when their life changes, surrounded by community support. Without them, major transitions can feel confusing and lonely. Young people today often say they don't know when they become "adults" — there's no clear marker. Rites of passage provide that marker. The fact that even secular people create ceremonies (graduation, 18th birthday parties) shows we still need them.

Argument 2: No — Modern Life Has Moved On

In modern societies, laws and institutions mark life changes clearly: you turn 18 and legally become an adult; a marriage certificate makes marriage official; a death certificate records someone's passing. We don't need ceremonies to make these changes "real." Also, elaborate ceremonies can be expensive and exclude people who can't afford them. Religious rites don't mean much to people who aren't religious. Modern life has replaced the function of rites of passage with legal systems and institutions.

A Third Perspective

Some people argue that rites of passage remain important, but they can take new forms. Graduation ceremonies, for example, follow the same pattern as traditional rites — special clothing (cap and gown), a ceremony, community recognition, and a changed status. Perhaps what matters is not whether the rite is religious or traditional, but that important life changes are marked with intentional ceremony and community support.

Your evaluation: Which perspective do you find most convincing? Why? A strong answer will acknowledge the strengths of different viewpoints before explaining your own position. There's no single right answer — what matters is that you can give reasons for your view.

Key Terms

Rite of Passage
A formal ceremony marking the transition from one life stage or social role to another.
Separation
The first of van Gennep's three stages — the person is removed from their normal life and old role.
Liminality
The middle stage — being "in between," neither in the old role nor the new one. From the Latin limen (threshold).
Incorporation
The final stage — the person re-enters their community in their new role with public recognition.
Communitas
The deep bond and sense of equality that forms between people sharing a rite of passage together. Identified by Victor Turner.
Arnold van Gennep
French anthropologist (1873–1957) who identified the universal three-stage structure of rites of passage.
Victor Turner
Anthropologist (1920–1983) who studied liminality in depth and identified the concept of communitas.
Religious Rite
A rite of passage rooted in religious tradition, involving divine blessing and spiritual significance.
Secular Rite
A rite of passage that marks social transitions without religious content — e.g. graduation, civil marriage.
Initiation
The process of formally admitting someone into a new group, status, or tradition through a structured ceremony.
Central Insight: Rites of passage exist in every human culture because they serve real needs — marking life changes, creating community support, and helping people understand who they are becoming. Van Gennep's three-stage model (separation, transition, incorporation) appears everywhere because it works: it creates the conditions for genuine change. In the coming lessons, you'll see this pattern at work in Hinduism, Islam, Christianity, and Judaism.
