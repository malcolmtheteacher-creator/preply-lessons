Resigning

The letter is in your bag and Gilberto is across the desk. He backed you when you were useless. He is about to be hurt and then, worse, generous — and generosity is what makes people accept counter-offers they regret for two years.

The Scene 🎬

The difficulty isn't that he's difficult. It's that he's been good to you.

You've accepted a job somewhere else. It's signed. You're going.

Gilberto gave you a chance when your CV didn't merit one, covered for you in March when you deserved worse, and has told other people you're the best thing on his team. In about four minutes he is going to make you an offer that is real, generous, and constructed out of four years of goodwill — and taking it would be a decision made out of guilt rather than judgement.

The opposite failure is just as available: going cold and businesslike to protect yourself, and repaying four years of decency with a form. What you want is warm and completely immovable, which is harder than either.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for leaving a job you've been happy in, without being talked out of it. They work for resigning, ending a contract, or leaving any long commitment. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Warm and firm are not opposites here — the warmth is what makes the firmness bearable. But any wobble in your voice will be heard as an opening, and he will reach for it, kindly. Practise the second sentence out loud until it comes out level, because that's the one that decides how the next twenty minutes go.

⏱️ The first thirty seconds

Phrase 1

The job: warning him it's coming and signalling respect, in one line. Which opening?

A. "So, I've been doing a lot of thinking recently about where I am and where I want to be…"
B. "I'm going to make this quick, because you deserve that more than a build-up."
C. "I'm afraid I've got some bad news."
B — "I'm going to make this quick, because you deserve that more than a build-up."
A is the long preamble everyone reaches for, and it's cruel — he'll work it out in the first six words and then sit through the rest. C is melodramatic and makes him think someone's ill. B does the whole job: it warns him, it explains why there's no preamble, and the reason is a compliment. Then say it immediately.

Take it anywhere: "I'm going to make this quick, because you deserve that more than a build-up."
Phrase 2

The job: saying the actual word. Complete it:

"I've accepted a job somewhere else. I'm ___."

"I've accepted a job somewhere else. I'm resigning."
Two sentences and both are necessary. The first is the fact; the second is the word, and the word matters — "I'm thinking about moving on" or "I've been approached" both leave a door open that you'll then spend twenty minutes trying to shut. Accepted is doing quiet work too: it's already done, so there's nothing to persuade you out of.

Take it anywhere: say the actual word — resigning, leaving, stopping — in the first thirty seconds.

🚪 Closing it before it opens

Phrase 3

The job: heading off the counter-offer before he's built one. Put the pieces in order:

trying to fix somethingThis isn't a negotiation. I'd hate for you tothat isn't broken.spend a week
"This isn't a negotiation. I'd hate for you to spend a week trying to fix something that isn't broken."
The second sentence is what makes the first one kind rather than cold. You're framing the refusal as consideration for his time — and "something that isn't broken" is a compliment about the job, which stops the whole thing sounding like a complaint. Say it early, before he has invested in an offer he'll then have to withdraw.

Take it anywhere: "This isn't a negotiation — I'd hate for you to spend a week on something that isn't broken."
Phrase 4

The job: making it final in three words. Complete it:

"It's ___. I've told them yes."

"It's signed. I've told them yes."
Facts, not feelings. A manager can argue with a decision and cannot argue with a signature — and the second sentence closes the last gap, because "signed" alone might still be walked back. Short and flat is right here; any elaboration sounds like justification, and justification invites debate.

Take it anywhere: "It's signed. I've told them yes."

💬 Being honest without an exit interview

Phrase 5

The job: taking money off the table, believably. Complete it:

"It's not about the money, and I'm not saying that to be ___."

"It's not about the money, and I'm not saying that to be polite."
Everybody says the first half and most of them mean the opposite, so on its own it functions as an invitation to make a better offer. The second half is what makes it land — you're naming the polite version and rejecting it, which tells him not to spend the next ten minutes on salary bands.

Take it anywhere: "…and I'm not saying that to be polite." Attach it to any statement people routinely say insincerely.
Phrase 6

The job: giving the real reason, about yourself rather than about him. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "I'd stopped being any use to you. I could feel it before you could."
Honest, specific, and impossible to fix with an offer — which is exactly why it ends the negotiation. Notice it's a statement about your own performance, not his management, so there's nothing for him to defend. An exit interview's worth of grievance would achieve less and cost you the reference.

Take it anywhere: "I'd stopped being any use." Any leaving where the honest reason is drift rather than grievance.

🎁 Saying the thing you owe him

Phrase 7

The job: the thank-you — specific, and admitting you've never said it. Complete it:

"You ___ me when I hadn't earned it, and I've never said thank you properly."

"You backed me when I hadn't earned it, and I've never said thank you properly."
To back someone is to support them publicly, usually at some risk to yourself. "When I hadn't earned it" is what makes this specific rather than a card — it names the actual thing he did, which was take a chance he didn't have to. And admitting you've never said it is more honest than pretending you have.

Take it anywhere: "You backed me when I hadn't earned it." Say it to someone before you need to.
Phrase 8

The job: the hardest sentence — making clear the leaving is not his fault, without reopening the decision. Complete it:

"I'm not leaving because of you. If anything, you're the reason I ___ this long."

"I'm not leaving because of you. If anything, you're the reason I stayed this long."
The sentence he'll remember. "Because of you" would let him fix something; this version tells him the truth instead — he did everything right, and it kept you two extra years. It closes the door on the negotiation while paying the debt in full, and every word of it is something people actually say.

Take it anywhere: "I'm not leaving because of you — if anything, you're the reason I stayed this long." For every good boss, landlord and team you ever leave anyway.

🤝 Handling the counter-offer and the sting

Phrase 9

The job: refusing a genuinely good offer without insulting it. Which version?

A. "It's too late for that now, I'm afraid."
B. "If you'd offered me that a year ago it would have worked. That's on both of us."
C. "Honestly, that's not really enough anyway."
B — "If you'd offered me that a year ago it would have worked. That's on both of us."
A is a door slammed and it stings. C reopens the negotiation and tells him a bigger number might do it, which is the opposite of what you want. B does the honest thing: acknowledges the offer was real and good, explains why it's arrived too late, and — crucially — takes half the blame with "that's on both of us". You never asked for it either.

Take it anywhere: "If you'd offered me that a year ago it would have worked. That's on both of us."
Phrase 10

The job: being generous about everything except the one thing. Complete it:

"Ask me for anything on the way out. ___ ___."

"Ask me for anything on the way out. Except staying."
A lovely closing move: total generosity with one clearly marked exception. It offers him everything that's actually useful — a proper handover, training someone, finishing the account — while making absolutely clear the one door is shut. Said warmly it's almost affectionate, and it gives him something to do with the next three months other than mourn.

Take it anywhere: "Ask me for anything. Except ___." Any refusal where you want to give ground everywhere else.

🔑 Make them yours

These work for leaving a job, a committee, a band, a shared project — anywhere you're going and somebody will try to keep you. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"This isn't a negotiation." — what have you announced and then been talked out of?

"You backed me when I hadn't earned it." — who did that for you? Have you told them?

"Ask me for anything. Except ___." — where could you give ground on everything but one thing?

⚡ The twist

Gilberto doesn't counter-offer at all. He listens, nods, and says: "Good. You should have gone last year — I've thought so for months. I didn't say anything because I needed you on Dunmore."

He means it warmly and it's an admission that he kept you somewhere he knew you'd outgrown. Everything you armoured yourself against never comes, and you're now holding a goodbye instead of a fight. What do you say?

The Echo 🕰️

In January 1915 the Endurance froze into the Weddell Sea. Shackleton had set out to cross Antarctica on foot, had spent years raising the money and staking his name on it, and after months watching the ice crush his ship he stood on the floe and told the crew the expedition was over.

It is the decision he's remembered for. Not the crossing — he never made it — but the moment he let go of the thing he'd built his reputation on, and turned the whole enterprise into getting twenty-eight men home. Which he did, every one of them.

Leaving something you were good at is not failure, and the hardest part is rarely the going. It's saying it out loud to people who'd rather you stayed.
