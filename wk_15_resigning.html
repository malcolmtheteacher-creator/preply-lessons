Resigning

The letter is in your bag. Across the desk is the man who backed you when you were useless — and in about nine seconds you are going to hurt him.

The Scene 🎬

You asked for five minutes. It's a Tuesday, it's the small room with the broken blind, and the letter is folded in the side pocket of your bag where you can feel it against your knee. You've been carrying it for four days. You have signed a contract with another company, you start in seven weeks, and none of that is in doubt.

The difficulty is sitting opposite you, and his name is Gilberto, and he is not the difficulty because he's difficult. He's the difficulty because he has been good to you. He hired you when your CV was thin and your English was worse. He put his own name behind you in a room you weren't in. In March, when you made the kind of mistake that ends people, he took it upstairs himself and it never came back down, and the two of you have never once talked about it.

So there are two ways to get this wrong. The first is the counter-offer: he will try to fix it, because that's what he does, and what he offers will be real — not a bribe, not a delaying tactic, an actual good offer he has probably been holding for you anyway. You will be tempted. And if you say yes, you'll be saying yes out of guilt rather than judgement, and you'll be back in this chair by autumn. The second way to get it wrong is the opposite one: to armour up, go cold and efficient, hand over a form and a date, and repay four years of decency with a piece of paper.

Somewhere between those two is the conversation you actually want to have.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of someone at work who was good to you early on, before you'd done anything to deserve it. What exactly did they do — and have you ever told them?
  2. If your company offered you more money tomorrow to stay somewhere you'd already decided to leave, would you take it? Be honest, and say what the deciding factor would actually be.

Who's who

You
The One Who's Leaving 🚪

Decided, signed, and dreading the next four minutes. You owe this man honesty and warmth, and you owe yourself a decision that survives contact with his kindness. Both, in the same conversation.

The other voice
Gilberto 🗝️

Your manager for four years. Not a villain, not a saint — a decent man who is about to be hurt and will respond to being hurt by trying to fix it. Everything he offers you will be genuine. That's what makes it dangerous.

First: read the conversation in the next tab, and time one thing — how long it takes before the word resigning is actually said out loud. It's inside the first thirty seconds, before any explanation, before any softening. That costs something: there's no gentle landing, and the shock lands whole. But it buys the only thing that matters here, which is that the decision is a fact in the room rather than a topic. Everything said after it is warmth. Nothing said after it is up for discussion.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Gilberto's offer is generous and it arrives fast — but he insists it wasn't invented on the spot. What does he claim about where the offer came from, and why does he bother saying that at all?

2. There's a moment where the thank-you goes wrong. What does the speaker say about how it came out — and what do they do immediately afterwards instead of giving up on it?

3. Gilberto says one genuinely cold thing. What was the shape of it, how quickly does he take it back, and what reason does he give for having said it?

1. He says it had been sitting in a drawer waiting for the review in April, and that the person leaving had been top of his list since before Christmas — so it isn't panic. He says it because he can hear how a fast counter-offer sounds, and he wants it heard as something already owed rather than something bought in a hurry. Which, awkwardly, makes it harder to refuse.
2. They say it came out like something off a card — greeting-card language, warm-sounding and hollow. What they don't do is stop there and let the bad version stand. They say it again, plainly and worse: he covered for them, they never said it out loud, and they're saying it badly now. The second attempt is clumsier and much more true.
3. A small cruelty about four years being reduced to a paragraph. He pulls it back inside the same turn, and the reason he gives is that he's cross and the person leaving is the only one in the room — which is an apology and an admission that the anger isn't really aimed where it's landing.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten lines for the hardest four minutes of a working year. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Warm and firm are not opposites here. The warmth is the thing that makes the firmness survivable, which is why going cold is not the safe option even though it feels like it — a person who has been decent to you for four years and gets a flat, procedural exit will read that as the answer to a question he didn't ask. But the firmness has to be genuinely firm, because any wobble in your voice will be heard as an opening, and it will be heard instantly. If your tone says "convince me", he will spend the next fortnight trying, out of kindness, and you'll have to break his heart twice. So: gentle in the voice, immovable in the content. The kindness goes into how you say it and into everything you owe him. It does not go into the decision.

⏱️ Saying it in the first ten seconds

Phrase 1

You've sat down. He's waiting. You have one opening line, and its job is to warn him that something is coming and then not make him wait for it. Which one does that?

A. "I'm so sorry, I know the timing's awful and I've been agonising about this for weeks and I really, really didn't want to be sitting here—"
B. "I'm going to make this quick, because you deserve that more than a build-up."
C. "Before I start, there are a few things about how this department is run that I think you ought to hear."
B
A build-up is the slow approach to bad news — the throat-clearing, the "so, I've been thinking a lot lately", the two minutes of context before the sentence that matters. It feels kind, and it isn't, because the other person worked out what was coming during your first breath and now has to sit politely through the rest of it while their stomach drops. Calling it "a build-up" and then refusing to do one is how you show respect in the first three seconds. A is the apology, which makes him manage your feelings before he's had a chance to have his own. C files a complaint on the way in, which turns a resignation into a grievance and poisons everything after it.
Phrase 2

Now the sentence itself. Two short statements, no cushion between them — the first is the fact, the second is the word. Put it in order:

a jobI'm resigning.somewhere else.I've accepted
"I've accepted a job somewhere else. I'm resigning."
Two sentences where most people would write six. "Accepted" is doing quiet work, because it's past and it's finished — you didn't apply, you didn't consider, you accepted. And then the word itself, on its own, with nothing after it. Plenty of people never actually say resigning; they say they've been approached, or that they're thinking about their options, and the conversation becomes a negotiation without anyone deciding to negotiate. Say the word.

🚪 Closing it before it's negotiated

Phrase 3

You can see him gathering himself to solve you. You want to stop that before it starts, and the second sentence does it by pointing at the waste rather than at him. Complete the chunk: "This isn't a negotiation. I'd hate for you to spend a week trying to fix something that ___ ___."

"…that isn't broken."
The move here is that you take the blocking sentence — "this isn't a negotiation", which on its own is quite hard — and immediately hand him a reason that's about protecting his time rather than refusing his help. And "isn't broken" is doing something specific: it tells him there's no fault to find and no lever to pull, because a person who thinks you're unhappy will keep hunting for the unhappiness. Not broken. Just finished.
Phrase 4

He asks, carefully, whether there's anything at all that might change your mind. He's hoping. Which answer closes the door without slamming it?

A. "Well — it depends what you can offer, really."
B. "It's signed. I've told them yes."
C. "I mean, nothing's ever completely certain at this stage, is it."
B
Four words and a fact, and notice that neither half is about him or about your feelings — it's just what has already happened. "It's signed" is checkable and final; "I've told them yes" adds the human half, that other people are now relying on you, which quietly explains why you can't be argued back. A invites the auction you said you didn't want, and once you've invited it you've agreed the decision is for sale. C sounds humble and is actually the cruellest of the three, because it leaves him a crack of light and he will spend a fortnight walking towards it.

🩶 Being honest without an exit interview

Phrase 5

The offer was real and generous, and you have to refuse it in a way that doesn't sound like the polite lie everyone tells. Put it in order:

to be polite.and I'm not saying thatIt's not about the money,
"It's not about the money, and I'm not saying that to be polite."
The first half is a sentence so worn out that nobody hears it any more — it's what everyone says while meaning the opposite. So the second half exists purely to knock down the thing he's already thinking, which is that you're being nice about the number. Naming the suspicion is usually more convincing than protesting against it, because you've shown him you know exactly how you sound.
Phrase 6

He asks what it is, then. There's a version of this answer that's a list of complaints about the company, and it's the wrong one. The right one puts the problem inside you, and admits you saw it coming before he did. Say it out loud, in your own words, before you reveal anything.

"I'd stopped being any use to you. I could feel it before you could."
This is honest without being a complaint, which is the whole trick of leaving well: the subject of both sentences is I, so there's nothing for him to defend and nothing for him to fix. "Any use to you" is generous as well as true, because it frames the problem as something that was happening to his team rather than something being done to you. And the second sentence gives him the one thing he'd otherwise lie awake over, which is whether he should have noticed. He couldn't have. You were doing the work fine.

💛 Saying the thing you actually owe him

Phrase 7

The debt. One word goes in the gap — the thing a manager does when they put their own name and credit behind you in a room you're not in, before you've proved anything. Complete it: "You ___ me when I hadn't earned it, and I've never said thank you properly."

"You backed me when I hadn't earned it…"
To back someone is to risk your own standing on them — you back a horse, and if it loses, that's your money gone. It's much bigger than "supported" or "helped", because it says he had something to lose and spent it on you. Then "when I hadn't earned it" refuses the comfortable version where you were obviously worth it all along, and "properly" admits the thanks you have said were the small automatic kind that don't count. Say this even if it comes out wrong. Especially if it comes out wrong.
Phrase 8

He's braced for the usual thing, which is that people leave managers. One word turns that on its head — the word you'd use for something you did against a good reason to stay. Complete it: "I'm not leaving because of you. I'm leaving ___ you, which is worse for both of us."

"I'm leaving despite you…"
"Despite" means the good reason was there and you went anyway, so this sentence pays him the biggest compliment available in the room and takes away his last comfort in the same breath — he doesn't get to be the cause, which means he doesn't get to be the cure. "Worse for both of us" is the honest bit: it's worse for him because there's nothing to correct, and worse for you because it would have been so much simpler to leave someone you didn't like.

🪤 Handling the counter-offer

Phrase 9

He says the promotion was real — he'd have given it to you. He isn't lying, and it lands. You need to agree with him completely and still not take it. Put it in order:

That's on both of us.it would have worked.If you'd offered me thata year ago
"If you'd offered me that a year ago it would have worked. That's on both of us."
The first sentence concedes everything — the offer was good, it would have held you, he wasn't wrong — while making the point that timing was the whole thing and timing has already gone. Then "that's on both of us": when something is on you, it's your fault, it goes on your account. Saying it's on both of you splits the blame deliberately, which stops the conversation becoming either an accusation or a confession, and it happens to be true, because he didn't offer and you didn't ask.
Phrase 10

He's landed. He's decent about it, and now he asks what he can do for you. Which answer gives him everything except the one thing?

A. "Nothing, honestly. Let's just get through the seven weeks."
B. "Ask me for anything on the way out. Except staying."
C. "Anything at all, really — I mean, within reason, obviously. We can talk about it."
B
Note that it flips his question round: he asked what he could do for you, and you answer by offering yourself instead. On the way out means during the leaving — the notice period, the handover, the last weeks — and by making yourself completely available in it you turn the exit from a loss into a debt you're paying. And then the last two words keep the door shut, warmly, without another speech. It's the same firm decision as everything above; it just arrives wrapped in an offer. A is a kind of cold politeness that leaves him nothing; C reopens the negotiation you closed eight minutes ago.

The Twist

🎯 He doesn't fight

Run it once more, and this time Gilberto doesn't counter-offer. There's no money, no Lisbon, no drawer. He hears the word, sits back, and says: "Good. You should have gone last year — I've been waiting for you to work that out." And he's warm, and he means it, and he's a bit relieved for you. Every sentence you armoured yourself with is now sitting in your lap with nothing to do: the closing line, the refusal, the "this isn't a negotiation" — all of it useless, because nobody's negotiating. Start the timer and keep going anyway. You came in for a fight and you've been handed a goodbye instead, and a goodbye is much harder, because there's nothing to push against and the only thing left to do is say true things to a man who is pleased for you. Find out what you say when you're not defending anything.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said out loud. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said the word "resigning" inside the first thirty seconds, with no build-up in front of it.
  • I closed the decision before he could start negotiating it, and I didn't reopen it later.
  • I refused a genuinely good offer without pretending it wasn't good.
  • I was honest about why without turning it into a list of complaints about the company.
  • I said the thing I owed him out loud, even though it came out badly.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that'll cost you in the real room. Run the twist again and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Most people who accept a counter-offer are gone within a year anyway, and the reason is fairly plain once you say it out loud: the offer fixes the number, and the number was rarely the thing. What it does fix, brilliantly and for about six weeks, is the guilt — which is why it works in the moment and stops working shortly afterwards. And it costs something that's hard to see on the day, because from then on you're the person who was talked into staying, and both of you know the price of you now, and nobody ever quite forgets which of you asked.

We say people leave managers, not jobs, and that's often true. But the opposite happens more than anyone admits — people stay for a manager long after the job stopped fitting, out of loyalty to someone who would be genuinely upset to learn they'd been the reason. Decency can hold you in the wrong place for years, and it's an odd sort of debt, because the person you owe it to would forgive it instantly if you ever asked.

Which leaves the real debt, the one you can't pay by staying. Someone backed you before you'd earned it. You cannot give that back to them — they don't need it and it isn't a loan. You can only say it out loud, badly, while you're leaving, and then do the same thing for someone else in about ten years' time when it's your name on the line and theirs on the CV that isn't good enough yet.

Two bigger questions

  1. If a company only offers you what you're worth at the moment you try to leave, what does that tell you about what they thought you were worth the day before? And is that a reason to go, or just a reason to have asked sooner?
  2. Do we owe loyalty to people or to organisations — and what happens when a good manager becomes the main reason to stay somewhere that's stopped being good for you? At what point does staying out of decency turn into a favour nobody asked for?
