What Is the Eucharist?

The Eucharist (also called Communion, the Lord's Supper, or Mass) is a central act of Christian worship. Believers consume bread and wine (or grape juice) to remember and celebrate Jesus's death and resurrection. It's called a sacrament—a sacred action that connects believers to Jesus and to each other.

Key Scripture: Luke 22:19–20 (Last Supper) — Jesus said, "Do this in remembrance of me."

1 Corinthians 11:23–26 — Paul describes the Eucharist as proclaiming Jesus's death until He returns.

Catholic Mass & Transubstantiation

Church: Catholic (and some Anglican/Orthodox)

Belief: Transubstantiation — the bread and wine literally become the body and blood of Christ. The substance (what it is) changes, not the appearance.

  • The priest says the words of consecration: "This is my body"
  • The bread and wine physically transform into Christ's body and blood
  • Consuming them means receiving Jesus Himself into your body
  • This is a true sacrifice—re-presenting Christ's one sacrifice on the Cross
  • Only baptized Catholics usually receive Communion
  • Frequency: Often weekly in Mass

Why it matters: Connects Catholics directly to Jesus. The Eucharist is the climax of the Mass and the most sacred moment.

Protestant Communion & Symbolism

Church: Many Protestant churches (Reformed, Baptist, Pentecostal)

Belief (Zwingli's view): The bread and wine are symbolic — they represent the body and blood but are not literally transformed. Communion is a remembrance and commitment ceremony, not a sacrifice.

Alternative (Calvin's view): Spiritual presence — the bread and wine are symbols, but Christ is spiritually present to those who consume them with faith.

  • The bread and wine remain bread and wine
  • They remind us of Jesus's death for our sins
  • Participating is a declaration of faith and community
  • More frequent in some churches; less in others
  • Open to all baptized believers (sometimes anyone who believes)

Why it matters: Emphasizes faith over ritual. The believer's faith makes the meal meaningful, not the priest's words.

Orthodox Divine Liturgy

Church: Orthodox

Belief: Mystery of transformation — The bread and wine are transformed into Christ's body and blood, but Orthodox theologians don't explain how it happens. It's a divine mystery beyond human understanding.

  • The liturgy (set prayers and ritual) is ancient and unchanging
  • The priest faces the altar with the people, not facing the congregation
  • Icon veneration and incense are prominent
  • Communion is central, but requires confession and fasting beforehand
  • The Eucharist is the continuation of Christ's presence on earth

Why Different Beliefs About One Practice?

All Christians agree the Eucharist is important and recalls Jesus's death. They disagree on how it works:

  • Catholic: Emphasizes the transformation itself as a miracle. The priest's words (authorized by the Church) make it happen. Transubstantiation shows how literally real Christ's presence is.
  • Protestant (Reformed): Emphasizes personal faith and memory. The bread and wine are signs, not magical. What matters is your faith and remembrance, not the substance of the elements.
  • Orthodox: Holds both together—it's a real transformation (like Catholic) but a mystery we don't explain (different from Catholic scholastic theology). It's mystical, not mechanical.

Common ground: All believe the Eucharist:

  • Commemorates Jesus's death
  • Brings believers into communion (connection) with Jesus and each other
  • Is sacred and central to Christian life
  • Should be taken seriously, not casually

Evaluate (12 marks)

Question: "The Eucharist is the most important act of Christian worship." Evaluate this statement with reference to Christianity.

Guidance:

  • For: Jesus instituted it; it's central to most Christian services; it directly recalls His death and resurrection; it unites the believer with Christ and the community; it appears in the New Testament frequently
  • Against: Prayer might be more important; preaching God's word is essential; some churches (Quakers, Salvation Army) don't use Eucharist; individual faith matters more than the ritual; some argue daily prayer is more important than weekly Communion
  • Nuance: Different traditions prioritize differently. Catholics see it as central; Protestants may prioritize preaching; Quakers prioritize the Holy Spirit directly
  • Conclusion: It's very important but perhaps not THE most important—that depends on your theological tradition
