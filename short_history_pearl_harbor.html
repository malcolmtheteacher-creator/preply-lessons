Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. By dawn the whole fleet had gathered in the bay — dozens of grey ships of every size, anchored row upon row, more than the harbour had ever held at once.
a fleet (n) — a large group of warships (or other ships) operating together under one command.
2. He came from a long line of sailors and had spent thirty years in naval service, first on small patrol boats and later commanding ships of the line.
naval (adj) — relating to a navy, or to warships and the sailors who serve on them.
3. The deck of the aircraft carrier stretched out flat and enormous, long enough for planes to take off and land far out at sea, hundreds of miles from any airfield.
an aircraft carrier (n) — a huge warship with a flat deck from which aircraft take off and land.
4. The submarine fired a single torpedo; it cut a white line through the water toward the ship and struck the hull below the waterline with a tearing explosion.
a torpedo (n) — an underwater missile that travels through the sea and explodes against a ship's hull.
5. Listeners worked through the night to intercept the enemy's coded messages, pulling the signals out of the air before they ever reached their destination.
to intercept (v) — to stop, catch or secretly listen to something — a message, a signal or an aircraft — while it is on its way somewhere.
6. The generals sat over the maps for hours, weighing the latest intelligence — reports from spies, photographs from the air, decoded radio traffic — before deciding where the enemy would strike.
intelligence (n) — secret information gathered about an enemy's plans, strength or movements.
7. After the ambassador read the message aloud, there was no longer any doubt: the two countries were at war, and this was the formal declaration of war that made it official.
a declaration of war (n phrase) — an official, public statement by a country that it is now at war with another.
8. They had not started the fight, but once their city was bombed the people demanded retaliation — they wanted the enemy struck back just as hard.
retaliation (n) — action taken to hurt or punish someone in return for harm they did to you; striking back.
9. Within hours the order went out to mobilise: factories switched to making weapons, reservists were called up, and trains full of soldiers began rolling toward the coast.
to mobilise (v) — to gather and prepare troops, resources and a whole country for war or action.
10. The battleship was the pride of the navy — vast, heavily armoured, bristling with enormous guns that could hurl a shell the weight of a car for twenty miles.
a battleship (n) — the largest, most heavily armed and armoured type of warship of that era.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A quiet harbour and a gathering storm

By late 1941 most of the world was already at war, but the United States had stayed out of it. On the far side of the Pacific, however, the relationship between America and the Empire of Japan was breaking down fast. Japan was expanding across Asia, and in response the United States had cut off its oil and frozen its trade. To Japan's leaders, that left two choices: back down, or strike first and seize what they needed before America could stop them. In great secrecy, the navy chose to strike.

Their target was Pearl Harbor, the home of the United States Pacific Fleet, on the Hawaiian island of Oahu. It was meant to be a knockout blow — to cripple the American fleet in a single morning and buy Japan time to build an empire. A force of six aircraft carriers slipped across the northern Pacific in radio silence, keeping to empty waters where no one would see them, edging ever closer to Hawaii.

Warning signs reached Washington, but they were scattered and unclear. Codebreakers had intercepted Japanese messages; everyone knew war was coming somewhere. Yet almost no one in command believed Japan would dare to reach all the way to Hawaii. So on the morning of Sunday, 7 December 1941, the great ships lay peacefully at anchor along "Battleship Row," their crews half-asleep, the bands preparing to play. No one was ready.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Japan's leaders decide to attack the United States?

2. Why was Pearl Harbor caught by surprise, even though warnings existed?

1. America had cut off Japan's oil and trade in response to its expansion; Japan chose to strike first and seize what it needed before America could stop it.

2. The warnings were scattered and unclear, and almost no one in command believed Japan would dare to attack as far away as Hawaii — so the fleet lay unready at anchor.

Discuss:
  • The clues were there but no one joined them up. Why is it so hard for people in charge to believe a warning they don't want to be true?

Part 2 — "A date which will live in infamy"

At 7:48 in the morning, Hawaiian time, the first wave of Japanese aircraft roared in out of the rising sun — fighters, dive-bombers and torpedo planes, more than 350 of them in two waves. To the sailors below, the planes seemed unreal until the bombs began to fall and the torpedoes ran toward Battleship Row. Within minutes the harbour was a wall of fire and smoke.

The battleship USS Arizona took a bomb deep into its forward magazine and exploded with a force that lifted it out of the water; more than 1,100 of its crew died in seconds, almost half of all the Americans killed that day. Ship after ship was hit. In under two hours it was over. Around 2,400 Americans were dead, nearly 1,200 more were wounded, and much of the Pacific Fleet lay sunk or burning in the shallow water.

And yet the blow was not as complete as it looked. By chance, the fleet's three aircraft carriers were all out at sea that morning and escaped untouched — and in the war to come, it was the carriers, not the battleships, that would decide everything. The Japanese also left the fuel tanks and repair yards standing. The next day, President Franklin Roosevelt stood before Congress and called 7 December "a date which will live in infamy." Within an hour, the United States had declared war on Japan. It was no longer watching the world war from the outside. It was in it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to the USS Arizona, and roughly how many Americans died that day?

2. Why was the attack, despite the destruction, less complete than it first appeared?

1. A bomb reached its magazine and it exploded, killing more than 1,100 of its crew; around 2,400 Americans died in all that day.

2. The three aircraft carriers were at sea and survived, and the fuel tanks and repair yards were left intact — and the carriers would prove decisive in the war.

Discuss:
  • The attack succeeded and yet missed what mattered most. How often do you think a great "victory" turns out, later, to have been a mistake?

Part 3 — The long echo

Japan had hoped to frighten America into staying out of its way. It did the opposite. The shock of Pearl Harbor united a divided country overnight; men queued outside recruiting offices, factories switched to weapons, and the whole nation mobilised for war. The admiral who planned the raid, Isoroku Yamamoto, is said to have feared exactly this — that all the attack would do was wake a sleeping giant and fill it with a terrible resolve.

He was right. America's entry turned the Second World War into a truly global conflict and, in time, helped decide it. The road ran on for nearly four more years — across the Pacific island by island, and finally to the atomic bombs over Hiroshima and Nagasaki in 1945. The cost, on every side, was almost beyond counting.

Pearl Harbor never faded from memory. The wrecked Arizona still lies where it sank, with most of its crew inside, and a white memorial now floats above it, drops of oil still rising from the hull after more than eighty years. "Remember Pearl Harbor" became one of the most powerful phrases in American history. It is remembered as the morning a single, sudden attack pulled a reluctant nation into the deadliest war the world has ever known — and changed the course of the century.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. In what sense did the attack achieve the opposite of what Japan intended?

2. What still marks the place where the USS Arizona sank?

1. Instead of frightening America into staying out, it united the country and brought it fully into the war — "waking a sleeping giant," as Yamamoto reportedly feared.

2. A white memorial floats above the sunken ship, which still holds most of its crew, and oil continues to rise from the hull after more than eighty years.

Discuss:
  • A surprise attack meant to weaken an enemy ended up strengthening their will. What does that tell you about the limits of fear as a weapon?

The grammar this story runs on: reported questions & commands

A story like this is full of people asking, wondering, ordering and warning. To retell it, you rarely quote them word for word — you report what they asked and what they told others to do. English changes the word order, drops the question mark, and usually shifts the tense one step back. Get these moves right and your retelling sounds smooth and natural.

Reported QUESTIONS — three changes:
• Yes/no questions → use if or whether: "Is the fleet ready?" → Commanders asked whether the fleet was ready.
• Wh- questions → keep the question word, but use statement word order: "Why were the warnings ignored?" → Washington wondered why the warnings had been ignored.
• No do-support, no question mark, and backshift the tense: is → was, did ... ignore → had ignored, can → could.
Reported COMMANDS — verb + object + to-infinitive:
• Orders: "Strike at dawn." → Tokyo ordered the pilots to strike at dawn. (also: told / instructed someone to do)
• Negative commands & warnings: "Don't break radio silence." → The admiral told the crews not to break radio silence. ("Watch out for…" → warned someone not to…)
• The pattern is always told / ordered / warned + someone + (not) to + verb. There is no "that."

1. Report the question

Rewrite each direct question as a reported question. Start with the words given and mind the word order, the tense and the lost question mark.

1. "Is the fleet ready?" → Commanders asked whether .

2. "Where are the carriers?" → The captain wanted to know where .

3. "Why did we ignore the warnings?" → Washington later wondered why .

4. "Can the radar see them?" → The young operator asked if .

1. … whether the fleet was ready (is → was; no question mark)

2. … where the carriers were (statement word order; are → were)

3. … why they had ignored the warnings (no do-support; did ignore → had ignored)

4. … if the radar could see them (can → could)

2. Report the command or warning

Rewrite each order using told / ordered / warned + someone + (not) to.

1. "Strike at dawn." (Tokyo → the pilots) → Tokyo ordered .

2. "Don't break radio silence." (the admiral → the crews) → The admiral told .

3. "Watch out for torpedo planes!" (the lookout → the gunners) → The lookout warned .

4. "Report to your stations." (the officer → the sailors) → The officer told .

1. Tokyo ordered the pilots to strike at dawn.

2. The admiral told the crews not to break radio silence.

3. The lookout warned the gunners (to watch out) for torpedo planes / warned the gunners that torpedo planes were coming.

4. The officer told the sailors to report to their stations.

3. Put it together — report the scene

Fill each gap so the reported version is correct. Read the finished paragraph aloud.

When the strange planes appeared, a young officer asked whether (they / be) American. His commander could not say, and wondered why no one (warn) them. Moments later, headquarters ordered every ship (open) fire, and told the crews (not / abandon) their posts. Afterwards, the whole nation asked how this (can) have happened.

asked whether they were American · wondered why no one had warned them · ordered every ship to open fire · told the crews not to abandon their posts · asked how this could have happened

Notice: questions lose the question mark and take statement word order; commands become "verb + someone + (not) to".
