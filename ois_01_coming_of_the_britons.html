A number to hold in your mind

900,000 years. That's how long human beings have been walking on the land we now call Britain. In 2013, archaeologists found human footprints of that age at Happisburgh, Norfolk — the oldest ever discovered outside Africa. Britain's story is immeasurably older than any book can contain.

And yet the book we are following begins not with archaeology, but with something far more lovely — a fairy tale about the god of the sea.

II. Neptune's Island

H.E. Marshall knew that every great history needs a great beginning. She chose to open not with dates, but with a story — the ancient legend of how Britain came to be named.

Once upon a time there was a giant called Neptune. When he was quite a tiny boy, Neptune loved the sea. All day long he played in it, swimming, diving, and laughing gleefully as the waves dashed over him. As he grew older he came to know and love the sea so well, that the sea and the waves loved him too, and acknowledged him to be their king. At last people said he was not only king of the waves, but god of the sea. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter I

Neptune had a wife, Amphitrite, and many sons. As each son grew old enough to reign, Neptune gave him an island to rule. His fourth son was the one he loved most — and his name would echo down the centuries.

Neptune's fourth son was called Albion. When it came to his turn to receive a kingdom, a great council was called to decide upon an island for him. Now Neptune and Amphitrite loved Albion more than any of their other children. This made it very difficult to choose which island should be his. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter I

Mermaids and mermen came from all corners of the ocean, each describing their own island. But none was good enough for the favoured son. Until, at last, a single mermaid appeared — more beautiful than all the rest.

At last a little mermaid swam into the pink and white coral cave in which the council was held. She was more beautiful than any mermaid who had yet come. Her eyes were blue as the sky and the sea. Her hair was as yellow as fine gold. Her voice was as clear as a bell and as soft as the whisper of the waves upon the shore.

'O Father Neptune,' she said, 'let Albion come to my island. It is a beautiful little island. It lies like a gem in the bluest of waters.' — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter I

Neptune went to see for himself. And when he saw the green island with its white cliffs, he rose upon a great wave and cried out in joy.

This is the island of my love. Albion shall rule it and Albion it shall be called. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter I

Albion ruled for seven years, until he was killed in a fight with Hercules. The island was left without a king for a long age. Then, Marshall tells us, a prince arrived from far away.

For many years after Albion's death the island had no ruler, until a prince called Brutus came sailing from the far-off city of Troy. He landed upon the shores of the white island and there he fought and conquered the giants. He made himself king, not only over Albion, but over all the islands which lay around. He called them the kingdom of Britain or Britannia after his own name, Brutus, and Albion he called Great Britain because it was the largest of the islands. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter I

Marshall ends her first chapter with characteristic honesty:

Some people think this is only a fairy tale. But however that may be, the little island is still sometimes called Albion, although it is nearly always called Britain. In this book you will find the story of the people of Britain. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter I
The historian's note: Marshall's myth comes from Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (1136), which mixed scraps of history with a great deal of creative invention. The real origin of the name "Britain" is probably the Celtic word Pritani or Pretani — meaning "the painted ones" or "the tattooed people" — which the Greek explorer Pytheas used around 325 BC when he visited these islands and called them the Pretannic Isles. The Romans later adapted this into Britannia. But Marshall was right to begin with myth. Every nation tells stories about where it came from, and those stories reveal how a culture sees itself.

III. The Deep Past

Marshall leaps from the founding myth straight to the Romans. But the real story of Britain before Rome stretches back far deeper than she ever imagined.

A Timeline of Ancient Britain

c. 900,000 BC

First human footprints in Britain — discovered at Happisburgh, Norfolk in 2013. The oldest human footprints found outside Africa.

c. 40,000 BC

Modern humans (Homo sapiens) arrive — during the Ice Age, when a land bridge still connects Britain to the continent.

c. 8,000 BC

Britain becomes an island. Rising seas after the last Ice Age flood the land bridge (Doggerland). Britain is cut off from Europe.

c. 3,000 BC

Stonehenge begins. Construction starts on the monument that would take over 1,500 years to complete — before the Pyramids of Giza.

c. 800–500 BC

Iron Age and Celtic culture. Celtic languages, art, hillforts, and tribal kingdoms spread across Britain.

c. 325 BC

Pytheas of Massalia circumnavigates Britain — the first written description of the islands. He calls them the Pretannic Isles.

55 BC

Julius Caesar lands in Britain. But that is a story for our next chapter...

The People of Ancient Britain

When Marshall does describe the ancient Britons, she paints them as wild cave-dwellers:

Before the Romans came, the Britons had been very ignorant and wild. In many parts of the country they wore no clothes at all. Instead, they stained their bodies blue with a dye called woad. Their houses were only little round huts, with a hole in the middle of the roof which let some light in and the smoke of the fire out. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter VI
The historian's note: This is the picture the Victorians loved to paint, but it is dramatically oversimplified. By the Iron Age, the Britons were skilled farmers growing wheat, barley, and oats. They kept cattle, sheep, pigs, and horses. They built well-constructed roundhouses with thatched roofs — warm and weatherproof. Their ironwork and bronze art (like the magnificent Battersea Shield, found in the Thames) rivalled anything in the classical world. British tin was traded across Europe and the Mediterranean. These were not cave-dwellers — they were part of a sophisticated, connected world.

Marshall also tells us about the Druids:

The ancient Britons were heathen. They worshipped the oak-tree and the mistletoe. The British priests were called Druids. It is said that they received their name from Druis, who was a very wise king of Albion in far-off times. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter VI
The historian's note: What we know about Druids comes mainly from Roman writers like Caesar and Tacitus, who had their own reasons for portraying them in particular ways. Caesar described them as learned philosophers who memorised vast quantities of verse and believed in the transmigration of souls. They were the intellectual class of Celtic society — judges, astronomers, healers, and keepers of oral tradition. And contrary to the popular image, Stonehenge was built roughly 2,500 years before the Druids existed. The Victorian picture of white-robed Druids at Stonehenge is an 18th-century invention, largely by the antiquarian William Stukeley.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Why begin with a fairy tale?

Marshall opens a history book with a myth about Neptune and mermaids. Was this a mistake — or was she doing something clever? What do origin myths tell us about a nation?

When does Britain's story start?

Is it 2,000 years old (the Romans)? 5,000 (Stonehenge)? 900,000 (Happisburgh footprints)? When does a nation's history actually begin?

The "savage" question

Marshall describes the ancient Britons as wild cave-dwellers. The archaeology shows sophisticated farmers and artists. Why would a 1905 writer paint them as savages?

Stonehenge without writing

The people who built Stonehenge left no written records, no names, no books. Yet their monument still stands 5,000 years later. What does that tell us about memory and legacy?

Britain as an island

For most of human history, you could walk from Britain to France. How might being cut off by the sea have shaped the character of the people and nation?

The power of names

Albion, Britain, Pretannic Isles — every name for this island tells a different story. What does the name a place carries reveal about who named it and why?
