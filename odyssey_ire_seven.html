The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Fifths and kingdoms

The Irish word for a province, cóiced, means "a fifth" — an old idea that Ireland had five parts: Ulster, Leinster, Munster, Connacht and Meath. In reality the number of major kingdoms changed over time, and big ones such as Munster were often split. Lists of "seven" or "five" kingdoms are simplifications that help us remember.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does cóiced mean?

2. Were the numbers fixed?

1. A fifth.

2. No — they changed over time.

Discuss:
  • Why do people like neat numbers in history?

Part 2 — Kings with opposition

After Brian Boru, provincial kings who claimed the High Kingship were often called "kings with opposition", because someone always challenged them. The O'Briens of Munster, the Mac Lochlainns of the north and the O'Connors of Connacht, who rose in the 12th century, all competed.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were "kings with opposition"?

2. Name two competing families.

1. High Kings who were always challenged.

2. O'Briens, Mac Lochlainns, O'Connors (any two).

Discuss:
  • Is constant competition good for a country?

Part 3 — Toirdelbach Ua Conchobair

Toirdelbach Ua Conchobair, who was king of Connacht from 1106 to 1156, became the strongest king in Ireland. He built bridges and castles and used fleets on the River Shannon. In the same century, church synods such as Kells (1152) reorganised the Irish Church into dioceses. His son was Rory O'Connor.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Toirdelbach build?

2. Who was his son?

1. Bridges, castles and fleets.

2. Rory O'Connor.

Discuss:
  • Can infrastructure make a leader powerful?

Grammar — Relative Clauses

To pack a lot of information into one sentence — who someone was, where something happened — we use relative clauses.

How it works:
• who for people, which for things, where for places, whose for possession.
• Non-defining clauses add extra information with commas: "Edward, who was deeply religious, rebuilt Westminster Abbey."
• Defining clauses identify which one — no commas.

Going deeper

The 12th century was a time of real change in Ireland: castles, church reform, new monasteries such as the Cistercians at Mellifont (1142). Ireland was not "frozen" before the Normans came.

Worth noticing: Use "whose" for possession, and commas for non-defining clauses that add extra information.
Learning from the past — for today: Infrastructure is power. Toirdelbach's bridges and fleets mattered as much as his battles. In your life, the "bridges" — routines, tools, contacts — often decide what you can achieve.
