Today we're working on the three parts of a paragraph so you can write one that is clear, focused and well supported.

Why it matters

A paragraph is a group of related sentences that discuss one (and usually only one) main idea. It can be as short as one sentence or as long as ten. The number of sentences is unimportant; it should be long enough to develop the main idea clearly.

A paragraph may stand by itself. In academic writing you often write one to answer a test question such as: "Define management by objective, and give one example of it from the reading you have done for this class." A paragraph may also be one part of a longer piece of writing such as an essay or a book.

We mark a paragraph by indenting the first word about a half inch (five spaces on a computer) from the left margin.

Gold

Concluding sentenceSignals the end and leaves the reader with the important points. (Some paragraphs don't need one.)

The topic sentence

Every good paragraph has a topic sentence that clearly states the topic and the controlling idea. It is the most important sentence in the paragraph: it briefly shows what the paragraph will discuss, so the writer knows what to include (and leave out) and the reader knows what to expect.

Three points to remember

1. It is a complete sentence, with at least one subject and one verb. These are not complete sentences:
  • Driving on freeways.
  • How to register for college classes.
  • The rise of indie films.
2. It has a topic and a controlling idea.
TopicDriving on freewaysControlling idearequires skill and alertness.
TopicRegistering for college classesControlling ideacan be a frustrating experience for new students.
TopicThe rise of indie filmControlling ideais due to several factors.
indie films: independent films, not made in or by Hollywood studios
3. It is the most general statement in the paragraph. Think of a restaurant menu. You want to know more than "salad", but you don't need every ingredient. "Potato salad? Mixed green salad? Fruit salad?" A reader wants to know generally what to expect, not all the details in the first sentence.
Which of these is a good topic sentence for a paragraph about Arabic words in English?
Why are the others wrong?
The first is too general: it could introduce a paragraph about any language. The third is too specific: it gives a detail that could support the topic sentence but cannot be the topic sentence.

Where does the topic sentence go?

Usually (but not always) it is the first sentence. Readers used to English-language writing want to know what they will read about as soon as they start. Here is a paragraph that begins with its own topic sentence:

Synonyms

Synonyms, words that have the same basic meaning, do not always have the same emotional meaning. For example, the words stingy and frugal both mean "careful with money." However, calling someone stingy is an insult, but calling someone frugal is a compliment. Similarly, a person wants to be slender but not skinny, aggressive but not pushy. Therefore, you should be careful in choosing words because many so-called synonyms are not really synonymous at all.

Sometimes the topic sentence comes at the end, after a series of examples or facts. Where is it here?

Medical Miracles to Come

By the year 2009, a vaccine against the common cold will have been developed. By the same year, the first human will have been successfully cloned. By the year 2014, parents will be able to create designer children. Genetic therapy will be able to manipulate genes for abilities, intelligence, and hair, eye, and skin color. By 2020, most diseases will be able to be diagnosed and treated at home, and by 2030, cancer and heart disease will have been wiped out. These are just a few examples of the medical miracles that are expected in the next few decades.

The two parts of a topic sentence

The topic names the subject. The controlling idea limits it to something you can discuss in one paragraph.

TopicConvenience foodsControlling ideaare easy to prepare.

The reader expects to hear how easy they are to prepare, perhaps with examples such as canned soup or frozen dinners.

Controlling ideaImmigrants have contributed many delicious foods toTopicU.S. cuisine.

The reader expects ethnic foods popular in the United States: tacos, egg rolls, sushi, baklava, pizza.

Don't pack in controlling ideas that are unrelated. Compare:

Too many ideas
Indie films are characterized by experimental techniques, low production costs, and provocative themes.
Good
Independent films are characterized by experimental techniques.

Supporting sentences

Supporting sentences explain or prove the topic sentence. One of the biggest problems in student writing is that ideas aren't supported enough. Use specific details to be thorough and convincing: examples, statistics, and quotations.

Compare the two paragraphs

Paragraph A: without support

Although some people think that red-light running is a minor traffic violation that is no worse than jaywalking, it can, in fact, become a deadly crime. Red-light runners cause accidents all the time. Sometimes people are seriously injured and even killed. It is especially a problem in rush hour traffic. Everyone is in a hurry to get home, so drivers run red lights everywhere. The police do not do much about it because they are too busy. The only time they pay attention is when there is an accident, and then it is too late. In conclusion, running a red light is a serious offense.

Paragraph B: with support

1Although some people think red-light running is a minor traffic violation that is no worse than jaywalking, it can, in fact, become a deadly crime. 2Red-light runners cause hundreds of accidents, including deaths and injuries as well as millions of dollars in damages. 3Each year more than 900 people die, and nearly 200,000 are injured in crashes that involve red-light running. 4Motorists run red lights all the time. 5For example, in Fairfax, Virginia, a five-month-long survey at five busy intersections revealed that a motorist ran a red light every 20 minutes. 6Red-light runners are seldom caught. 7According to the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, "Communities don't have the resources to allow police to patrol intersections as often as would be needed to ticket all motorists who run red lights" ("Q&A").

jaywalking: crossing a street where there is no marked area for it

Examples

Examples are the easiest kind of support, because you can often take them from your own knowledge and experience. They also make writing lively, and readers remember a memorable example. Words that introduce examples: for example, for instance, such as.

Facts, statistics and quotations are explained in more detail in Chapter 3.

A model supported with examples

Language and Perception

Although we all possess the same physical organs for sensing the world—eyes for seeing, ears for hearing, noses for smelling, skin for feeling, and mouths for tasting—our perception of the world depends to a great extent on the language we speak, according to a famous hypothesis3 proposed by linguists Edward Sapir and Benjamin Lee Whorf. They hypothesized that language is like a pair of eyeglasses through which we "see" the world in a particular way. A classic example of the relationship between language and perception is the word snow. Eskimo languages have as many as 32 different words for snow. For instance, the Eskimos have different words for falling snow, snow on the ground, snow packed as hard as ice, slushy snow, wind-driven snow, and what we might call "cornmeal" snow. The ancient Aztec languages of Mexico, in contrast, used only one word to mean snow, cold, and ice. Thus, if the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis is correct and we can perceive only things that we have words for, the Aztecs perceived snow, cold, and ice as one and the same phenomenon.4

hypothesis: theory that has not been proven · phenomenon: thing or event (plural: phenomena)
1. What is the main idea? What part of the topic sentence expresses it?
Answer
Our perception of the world depends to a great extent on the language we speak.
2. What examples support it?
Answer
The many Eskimo words for snow (falling, on the ground, packed hard, slushy, wind-driven, "cornmeal"), and the single Aztec word for snow, cold, and ice.
3. Which words and phrases introduce the examples?
Answer
"A classic example of … is", "For instance", and "in contrast" (introducing the second example).

The concluding sentence

A concluding sentence does two jobs. It signals the end of the paragraph, and it leaves the reader with the most important ideas, either by summarizing the main points or by repeating the topic sentence in different words.

A paragraph does not always need one. For single paragraphs, especially long ones, it helps; but in a multi-paragraph essay not every paragraph needs a concluding sentence.

End-of-paragraph signals followed by a commaSignals not followed by a comma
Finally, · Lastly, · In brief, · Therefore, · In conclusion, · Thus, · Indeed, · To sum up, · In short,The evidence suggests that … · There can be no doubt that … · These examples show that … · We can see that …
Notes
1. Many writing teachers think In conclusion and In summary are overused.
2. Don't use At last as a signal. It means "at the end of a long period of time": At last, you've come home.

Two models

Greeting Cards

Have you noticed how many different kinds of greeting cards you can buy these days? In the old days, the local drugstore had one rack display, maybe five or six basic kinds of cards. You could walk into the store and choose an appropriate card in five minutes or less. Nowadays, however, the display space for greeting cards is as big as a soccer field, and it may take an hour or two to hunt down exactly the right card with exactly the right message. There are at least 30 categories of birthday cards alone: birthday cards for different ages, from different ages, for different relatives, from different relatives, for different genders, from different genders, from a couple, from the office, for dog owners, for cat owners, and so on. There are cards for getting a job, for retiring from a job, for acquiring a pet, for losing a pet, for becoming engaged, for breaking up. There are also greeting cards to send for no reason—"Thinking of you" or "Just because" cards. The newest type of card is the "encouragement card." An encouragement card offers comforting thoughts and helpful advice to someone who is sad or distressed in these troubled times. In short, there is now a greeting card for every possible life event and for a few nonevents as well.

A Hawaiian Legend

Native people create legends to explain unusual phenomena in their environment. A legend from the Hawaiian island of Kauai explains how the naupaka flower, a flower that grows on beaches there, got its unusual shape. The flower looks like half a small daisy—there are petals on one side only. The legend says that the marriage of two young lovers on the island was opposed by both sets of parents. The parents found the couple together on a beach one day, and to prevent them from being together, one of the families moved to the mountains, separating the young couple forever. As a result, the naupaka flower separated into two halves; one half moved to the mountains, and the other half stayed near the beach. This story is a good example of a legend invented by native people to interpret the world around them.

1. In which paragraph does the concluding sentence summarize the main points, which are not specifically stated in the topic sentence?
2. In which paragraph does the concluding sentence paraphrase the topic sentence?
3. Which signals are used?
Answer
Greeting Cards ends with In short,. The Hawaiian Legend has no formal signal: "This story is a good example…" works as the ending without one.

Never introduce a new idea

Incorrect: "In conclusion, we now have more variety of greeting cards to choose from, but they are also becoming very expensive."
Incorrect: "In conclusion, there are many other legends like this one in Hawaii."
Why?
Price is not mentioned anywhere else in the greeting-card paragraph, and other Hawaiian legends are not discussed in the legend paragraph. A conclusion should only remind the reader of what has been said.

Now compare with the chapter's own summary:

1. A good topic sentence is a complete sentence with a subject, a verb and a controlling idea; it is neither too general nor too specific; and it is usually the first sentence.
2. Good supporting sentences explain or prove the topic sentence, are specific and factual, and can be examples, statistics or quotations.
3. A good concluding sentence signals the end and briefly summarizes the important points or restates the topic sentence in different words.
