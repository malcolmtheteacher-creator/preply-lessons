Thousands of Languages: Multilingual by Default

Most people in Sub-Saharan Africa speak at least two or three languages, and many switch between them in a single sentence.

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Most people in Nigeria are multilingual.
multilingual (adj) — able to use several languages. Example: “Most people in Nigeria are multilingual.”
2. Her mother tongue is Yoruba, but she also speaks English.
mother tongue (n) — the first language a person learns as a child. Example: “Her mother tongue is Yoruba, but she also speaks English.”
3. Swahili is a lingua franca in much of East Africa.
lingua franca (n) — a language used between people who do not share a mother tongue. Example: “Swahili is a lingua franca in much of East Africa.”
4. Code-switching is natural for many city speakers.
code-switching (n) — moving between two or more languages in one conversation. Example: “Code-switching is natural for many city speakers.”
5. Nigerian Pidgin is spoken by millions of people.
pidgin (n) — a simple mixed language used for communication between groups. Example: “Nigerian Pidgin is spoken by millions of people.”
6. Yoruba is a tonal language with three tones.
tonal (adj) — using the pitch of the voice to change the meaning of a word. Example: “Yoruba is a tonal language with three tones.”
7. South Africa has twelve official languages.
official language (n) — a language that a government uses for laws and public services. Example: “South Africa has twelve official languages.”
8. Many indigenous languages are now taught in primary school.
indigenous (adj) — originally from a place, not brought from outside. Example: “Many indigenous languages are now taught in primary school.”

The stereotype: “Africans speak ‘African’ — and communicate in simple tribal languages.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Not one language, but about 2,000

Africa has about 2,000 languages, nearly a third of the world's total, and Nigeria alone has more than 500. They belong to several large families, including Niger-Congo, which includes Swahili, Yoruba and Zulu, as well as Afro-Asiatic, which includes Amharic and Hausa. Many are tonal: in Yoruba, the same syllable can have different meanings depending on whether the voice rises, stays level or falls. Others, such as Zulu and Xhosa, contain click consonants, which are written with letters like c, q and x. Some, such as Hausa, Swahili and Yoruba, are spoken by tens of millions of people, and a few are spoken by only a handful of elders.

Part 2 — Local, regional and international

In practice, many people use several languages in different parts of daily life: a mother tongue at home, a regional lingua franca in the market and an international language at school or work. Swahili is spoken by well over a hundred million people across East Africa and is an official language in Tanzania, Kenya and Uganda, among others. English, French and Portuguese came with colonialism, and remain official languages in many countries, so school can be in a language that a child does not speak at home. South Africa recognises twelve official languages, including sign language since 2023. In some countries, languages such as Hausa and Swahili are now also used in broadcasting and in schools.

Part 3 — Mixing and creating

City speakers constantly mix languages. In Nairobi, young people speak Sheng, a mixture of Swahili and English with words from other languages, and in Nigeria, tens of millions speak a form of Pidgin English. South African Tsotsitaal blends Afrikaans, English and Zulu. This code-switching is not a sign of poor language: it is a skill, and it creates new expressions, songs and jokes. Many speakers wish their governments did more to support local languages in education and online, and some prefer to read the news in their mother tongue if they have a choice. Writers, rappers and technology companies are increasingly producing content in languages that were once rarely written.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

2. Is code-switching a sign of weak language skills?

1. A mixture of Swahili and English spoken in Nairobi.

2. No. It is a skill and a source of creativity.

Discuss:

Grammar — Wishes and Preferences — Wish, If Only, Would Rather

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
Wishes and preferences:
• wish + past simple for unreal present situations: “I wish I spoke Swahili.”
• wish + would for change: “I wish the schools would teach in our mother tongue.”
• if only + past perfect for regrets: “If only I had learned Zulu as a child.”
• would rather + infinitive: “I'd rather read the news in my own language.” With a different subject: “I'd rather you spoke slowly.”

Going deeper

The stereotype that Africans speak “African” treats about 2,000 languages as one. It also hides something remarkable: that being multilingual is the ordinary condition. The debate is less about whether to speak African or international languages than about which language to use for which purpose, and who gets to decide. Education in a language that children do not understand is a real challenge, and many educators argue that learning first in the mother tongue helps children master the others later. Preserving local languages, while giving people access to international ones, is one of the central questions of the region.

Worth noticing: Wishes and preferences: I wish + past simple (present wishes), I wish + would (annoyance or change), If only + past perfect (regrets), would rather + infinitive (preference).
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Never ask which “African language” someone speaks. Ask: “Which languages do you use at home, and which at work?” You will often be surprised by the answer, and a few words in the local greeting will always be appreciated.
