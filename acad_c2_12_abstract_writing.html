What Makes an Abstract Get Accepted?

Reviewers see 200+ abstracts. They spend 90 seconds on each. They're asking: Is this work addressing a real problem? Is the approach novel? Will the results matter?

Bad abstracts describe what you did. Good abstracts answer: What gap exists? Why does it matter? What did you find?

Your Turn Look at your abstract. Count how many sentences describe your method. Now count sentences that explain the problem and results. Is it balanced?

Six Moves for Abstract Success

1 The Gap Statement +
Start by naming a problem or gap that the field recognizes. Not your problem. The field's problem. This immediately signals relevance.
Weak

"This paper examines the effects of social media on teenagers."

Strong

"While research links social media use to increased anxiety in adolescents, most studies rely on self-reported usage data. We lack reliable metrics for *how* adolescents engage with these platforms—what they're actually doing online."

The Move

Start with consensus → Name what we don't know → Make the gap specific

2 Framing the Contribution +
Don't just say what you did. Say *why* you did it that way. What makes your approach different from what others have tried?
Weak

"We conducted interviews with 40 teenagers about their social media use."

Strong

"We developed a novel method combining real-time screen tracking with ethnographic interviews. This captures both what adolescents *do* and how they *interpret* their own actions—something neither method alone can achieve."

The Move

Name your method → Explain why it's suited to the gap → Show what it reveals that others can't

3 Results That Compel +
Give your most surprising or significant finding first. Not all findings. The one that changes thinking.
Weak

"We found that girls and boys used social media differently. Girls spent more time on messaging. Boys spent more time on gaming platforms."

Strong

"We discovered that adolescents actively curate different identities across platforms—not a unified self, but strategic code-switching. Anxiety emerges not from total usage, but from *managing* these multiple selves."

The Move

Find the finding that contradicts or expands existing understanding → State it boldly → Suggest its implications

4 The "So What" Sentence +
Your abstract must end by answering: Why should someone in your field care? What changes as a result of this work?
Weak Ending

"This research contributes to the literature on adolescent development."

Strong Ending

"These findings suggest that interventions targeting social media anxiety need to focus not on total usage, but on helping adolescents manage identity work across platforms. This reframes the problem in clinical and educational settings."

The Move

Name the implication → Point to how practice or theory changes → Be specific

5 Writing for a Broad Audience +
Conference program committees include scholars from adjacent fields. Technical jargon will exclude them. Translate your concepts into accessible language.
Too Narrow

"We employ critical discourse analysis and multimodal semiotics to interrogate the performativity of identity constitution in networked publics."

Accessible

"We examine how adolescents perform different versions of themselves on different social media platforms, and how managing these performances affects their mental health."

The Move

Use concrete verbs (perform, manage, build) → Replace jargon with action → Keep sentences short

6 Cutting Without Losing Substance +
Conference abstracts are often 250-350 words. You must be ruthless about scope. Every sentence must earn its place.
Fat

"While much research has been done in recent years on the topic of adolescents and social media, we still lack a clear understanding of how exactly these platforms affect developing minds."

Lean

"Existing research links social media to adolescent anxiety, but lacks real-time data on how teens actually engage with these platforms."

The Move

Cut hedging (while, much, recent) → Remove redundancy → Make every word earn its place → Strong verbs over weak ones

Your Senior Colleague Reads Your Draft

"Here's what I'm hearing in your abstract," they say. "You've done interesting work. But you've buried the lead. You spend two sentences on context, three on method, and one tiny sentence on what you actually found. Flip that ratio."

The Revision Challenge

Take 5 Minutes Pull your rejected abstract. Rewrite it using the structure: (1) The gap, (2) Your approach & why it's different, (3) Your key finding, (4) The so-what. Aim for 250-300 words.

Read It Aloud

Now read your revised abstract aloud—as if you're pitching it to someone at a conference reception. Listen for:

  • Does the first sentence make them curious?
  • Can you say it smoothly without tripping on jargon?
  • Does the ending make the work sound important?
Speak Your Revised Abstract (2-3 Minutes) Read it aloud. Don't rush.

The Follow-Up Question

Your colleague asks: "Now tell me—if you had to cut this to 150 words for a different conference, what would you keep?"

Priority Ranking

MUST KEEP The problem + your key finding + the implication
GOOD TO KEEP One detail about your method (the novel part)
CUT FIRST Literature review, secondary findings, methodological details
Answer (1.5 Minutes) What's the essence of your abstract? What could you cut and still have a compelling pitch?

The Hard Questions

After you submit a revised abstract, you'll face reviewer feedback. Work through these common challenges.

Reviewer: "Your abstract claims your method is novel, but similar approaches exist in [adjacent field]. Why should we see yours as a contribution?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Acknowledge the adjacent work, then show *how* your application is new.

Answer: "That's fair—similar methods exist in [field]. But applying them to [your domain] is new. The key difference: previous work looked at [aspect A]. I'm looking at [aspect B], which requires [technical adaptation]. This shift reveals something about [your field] that hasn't been visible before."

Reviewer: "Your results are interesting but preliminary. With only [small sample size], can you really claim to have answered the question?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Reframe the scope. Show what your sample *can* answer.

Answer: "You're right that my sample is modest. But exploratory research doesn't require statistical power—it requires depth. My 15 cases are intensive: multi-year ethnographic study. This lets me show *how* [mechanism] works in practice. That's different from proving *how often* it happens. This work opens a pathway for larger studies."

Reviewer: "The abstract doesn't explain what you actually *found*. It tells me your approach but not your results. Why should I be interested?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Go back and rewrite. Lead with findings, not methods.

Answer: "That's the best feedback. I got the structure backwards. Let me rewrite the abstract to lead with: 'We found that [key finding]—not what existing theory predicts.' Then explain how we discovered it. The method only matters if it reveals something surprising."

Reviewer: "This seems like work that would interest specialists in your field, but what's the broader significance? Why should a general conference care?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Find the human or policy implication, not just the disciplinary one.

Answer: "Valid point. The disciplinary contribution is [X]. But the broader significance: this changes how [practitioners/policymakers/educators] think about [real-world problem]. For instance, current approaches assume [Y], but my findings suggest [Z], which means we need to [practical change]."

Reviewer: "The abstract is too jargon-heavy for a general audience. Can you say what you actually did in simpler terms?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Strip jargon without losing precision. Use concrete verbs and nouns.

Answer: "Good catch. Instead of '[theoretical jargon],' I should say '[what actually happens].' Instead of '[methodological term],' I'll say '[what I actually did].' Let me rewrite using verbs—observed, tracked, compared—not nouns that hide meaning."
