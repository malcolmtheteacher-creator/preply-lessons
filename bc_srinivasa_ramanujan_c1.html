Srinivasa Ramanujan

The self-taught clerk from Madras whose notebooks astonished Cambridge — and who leaves us asking where great ideas actually come from.

You talk first — before you read anything

This is a talking lesson, not a reading one. So before a single fact, the floor is yours.

Start here · 2 minutes

Just his name and one number: 1729. Have you heard of Ramanujan — perhaps from the film The Man Who Knew Infinity, or from a maths teacher? Do you think genius is mostly born or mostly made? Just talk — no maths needed.

Now meet him — one beat at a time

Three short beats. After each one, you do the talking.

Srinivasa Ramanujan was born on 22 December 1887 in Erode, in southern India, and grew up in the town of Kumbakonam. His father was a clerk in a sari shop. In 1903, aged 16, he got hold of a library copy of G. S. Carr's Synopsis, a collection of about 5,000 mathematical results. He won a scholarship to college — and then lost it, because he was so absorbed in mathematics that he failed most of his other subjects.

Over to you:
  • He lost his scholarship by caring about only one thing. Should education reward people who are brilliant at one thing and hopeless at the rest — or is that asking for trouble?

By 1912 he was working as a clerk at the Madras Port Trust, doing mathematics in his spare time. He wrote to leading mathematicians at Cambridge. Two of them sent his papers back without comment. On 16 January 1913 he wrote to G. H. Hardy: “I have no University education but I have undergone the ordinary school course… I am striking out a new path for myself.” Hardy at first suspected a fraud. Then he looked more closely: some of the results, he said, “defeated me completely; I had never seen anything in the least like them before”.

Over to you:
  • Two experts binned his letter; the third nearly did. How many brilliant ideas do you think are lost because they arrive from the “wrong” person in the “wrong” format?

He sailed for England on 17 March 1914 and spent nearly five years in Cambridge working with Hardy and J. E. Littlewood. In 1918 he was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society — only the second Indian, and one of the youngest Fellows in its history — and the first Indian Fellow of Trinity College. But he was seriously ill. He returned to India in 1919 and died on 26 April 1920, aged 32. His illness was diagnosed at the time as tuberculosis; a 1994 study suggested it was more likely a liver infection, hepatic amoebiasis.

Over to you:
  • He spent nearly five years far from home, during a world war, and much of that time seriously ill. What do you think success cost him?

Intuition and proof — and you do the thinking out loud

Ramanujan's story raises one big question: how do we know something is true? I will hand it to you in small pieces. After each piece, you talk — I will mostly listen.

Ramanujan filled notebooks with nearly 3,900 results — mostly identities and equations — but he rarely wrote down how he got them. Paper was expensive, so he did much of his working on a slate and copied only the final results. Carr's book, which shaped him, also stated results without proofs. Many of his results turned out to be completely new. Some were already known. A few were wrong.

Put yourself there

Imagine you are Hardy. A stranger sends you nine pages of astonishing claims with almost no explanations. Some look impossible. What do you do — and what would make you take him seriously?

For Hardy — an atheist and, in Wikipedia's phrase, “an apostle of proof and mathematical rigour” — a result without a proof was not yet mathematics. Ramanujan relied strongly on intuition. He credited his insight to his family goddess, Namagiri, and an acquaintance later reported him saying, “an equation has no meaning for me unless it expresses a thought of God.” Hardy thought Ramanujan's religious belief had been romanticised; the mathematician Bruce Berndt insists there was nothing mystical about his method — the work is all recorded in his notebooks.

Over to you:
  • Is intuition just very fast reasoning that we can't see — or something genuinely different from logic?
  • In your own job or studies, when is “I just know it's right” good enough — and when is it dangerous?

Hardy left one famous story. He visited Ramanujan, who was ill in Putney, and remarked that his taxi's number, 1729, seemed “rather a dull one”. “No,” Ramanujan replied, “it is a very interesting number; it is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways.” (1729 = 1³ + 12³ = 9³ + 10³.) Hardy said Littlewood once remarked that every positive integer was one of Ramanujan's personal friends.

The question his life leaves us is not whether intuition or proof matters more — it is how many intuitions never find their Hardy.

Vocabulary — click each card to reveal

Ten words you need to discuss Ramanujan properly — and that work just as well for any serious conversation about talent, knowledge and recognition. Tap to reveal the meaning and an example.

intuition / intuitive
noun / adjective
The ability to understand something immediately, without conscious reasoning.
"He looked at the formula and knew it was true — pure intuition, with no proof yet."
rigour / rigorous
noun / adjective
Extreme care and exactness, especially in checking that every step is correct.
"Hardy insisted on rigour: every claim had to be proved step by step."
self-taught
adjective
Having learned something on your own, without formal teaching. (A formal noun: an autodidact.)
"With no degree and one borrowed book, the self-taught clerk wrote to the best mathematicians in Cambridge."
prodigy / prodigious
noun / adjective
A young person with exceptional natural ability; prodigious means remarkably great.
"A teenager working through 5,000 results from one borrowed book sounds like the classic prodigy story."
conjecture
noun / verb
A statement believed to be true but not yet proved.
"Many of his results were conjectures that other mathematicians spent years proving."
to verify / verification
verb / noun
To check that something is true or correct.
"Before Hardy could believe the letter, he and Littlewood had to verify the results."
to dismiss
verb
To decide something is not worth considering. Collocation: dismiss something out of hand.
"Two professors dismissed his letter without a word of comment."
gatekeeper
noun
A person or institution that controls access to something, such as a profession or publication.
"Cambridge's gatekeepers almost kept him out — until one of them opened the door."
unorthodox
adjective
Different from what is usual or traditionally accepted.
"His methods were unorthodox: results first, proofs (perhaps) later."
recognition
noun
Public acknowledgement of someone's achievement. Collocations: gain / win recognition; long overdue recognition.
"His election to the Royal Society brought recognition, but he had little time left to enjoy it."

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that he could prove far more than he ever wrote down.
  • It might well be that Hardy needed Ramanujan as much as Ramanujan needed Hardy.
  • I suspect his isolation was both a weakness and a strength.
  • You could argue that the system nearly failed him — and only got lucky.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say every field needs its Hardys, not just its Ramanujans.
  • What strikes me most is how close the story came to never happening.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, talent today is easier to spot — or is it harder?

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure intuition and proof are really opposites.
  • I take the point, but doesn't that romanticise the lonely genius?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…

A · The Letter Test (3–4 min)

You run hiring at a top company or university. An application arrives from someone with no degree, no references and an unusual format — but the work inside looks either brilliant or crazy.

Argue one side: should gatekeepers take the time to check unusual applications, even if 99 out of 100 are nonsense? Build a real case — give your reasons, raise the strongest objection to your own view, then answer it.

B · Proof or Insight (3–4 min)

Ramanujan often knew a result was true before he could show why. Today, AI systems and experienced doctors sometimes give answers they cannot fully explain.

Should we act on a result we trust but cannot explain — and who should carry the risk if it's wrong? Make the case with one concrete example.

Open discussion

  1. After today, has anything shifted in how you see Ramanujan — or in how you think about talent?
  2. His life depended on one stranger reading one letter carefully. Who has been your “Hardy” — or whose “Hardy” could you be?
  3. Is there a Ramanujan in every school who is failing the wrong subjects? What should education do about it?
  4. He saw his mathematics as connected to his faith; Hardy didn't believe that at all — yet they worked brilliantly together. Can people with opposite worldviews really collaborate well? Give an example.
  5. If you could ask Ramanujan one question, what would it be? And what do you think he would say back?
  6. Who is the next writer or thinker you would put in front of me — and what is the one idea of theirs I need to understand?
