BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Memento

A man who can no longer make new memories hunts his wife's killer — in a film that runs backwards. The condition is real, and so are the patients it was built from.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Memento — a man who cannot hold on to anything that happens to him, and who is being steered by everyone around him — discover how much of its neurology is genuinely true, and master the linking and discourse markers English uses to organise time, contrast and result: meanwhile, subsequently, in the meantime, however, nevertheless, consequently, whereas, by contrast, at which point. For a story told out of order, they are exactly the right tools.
Before we begin — talk it through: How much of who you are is made of what you remember? If you woke tomorrow with no memory of the last five years, would you still be the same person? And have you ever been certain of a memory that turned out to be wrong?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. The tape was badly damaged, so the recording came through fragmented: three seconds of speech, then silence, then a burst of noise, then nothing.
fragmented (adj) — broken into small separate pieces that don't join up into a whole.
2. The photographer spent nine winters in those mountains and never once got a picture: the snow leopard is famously elusive.
elusive (adj) — very hard to find, catch, reach or pin down.
3. She labelled every box, dated every photograph, cross-referenced each one against a numbered list, and checked the whole system twice a month. She was meticulous.
meticulous (adj) — extremely careful and precise about small details.
4. One driver said the van was dark green; a second driver, who had never met the first, said the same, and the petrol-station camera corroborated both of them.
to corroborate (v) — to support a statement with independent evidence that shows it is true.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. futile is closest in meaning to: (a) pointless   (b) urgent   (c) expensive
(a) pointless — futile (adj) means having no chance of succeeding or achieving anything, however hard you try.
6. oblivious is closest in meaning to: (a) deeply offended   (b) completely unaware   (c) barely awake
oblivious (adj) — (b) completely unaware: not noticing or knowing about something that is happening around you. Usually oblivious to something.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. When the accounts refused to balance, he fabricated a folder of receipts overnight and hoped nobody would look closely.
To fabricate something is: (a) to repair it; (b) to invent or forge it; (c) to pay for it in advance.
(b) — to fabricate (v) — to make up false information, evidence or a story and present it as real.
8. She never took a penny herself, but she knew exactly what her colleagues were doing every night and said nothing, which made her complicit.
To be complicit is: (a) to be involved in something wrong along with other people; (b) to be entirely innocent of it; (c) to be officially in charge of it.
(a) — complicit (adj) — sharing in the guilt of a wrong act, often by helping quietly or by staying silent. The noun is complicity.

D. Two more ways in — odd one out, and the natural partner word

9. Three of these four words belong to the same idea — paying someone back for a wrong. Which is the odd one out?
(a) retribution   (b) revenge   (c) vengeance   (d) forgiveness
(d) forgiveness is the odd one out. retribution (n) — punishment or harm inflicted on someone as deserved payment for what they did. It is the most formal and the most moral-sounding of the three.
10. Which verb makes the natural English phrase?
He had been cheated twenty years earlier, and he still ______ a grudge against the man who did it.
(a) makes   (b) holds   (c) takes
(b) holds — we say hold a grudge (also bear a grudge). a grudge (n) — a feeling of resentment that you keep alive long after the event that caused it.

The film, in three parts

Christopher Nolan's Memento (2000). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A man, a body and a system

Leonard Shelby used to investigate claims for an insurance company. Then, one night, intruders broke into his home. His wife was attacked and killed; Leonard was struck hard on the head. He survived — but since that night he has been unable to keep anything new. His old life is perfectly intact: his childhood, his job, his wife's face, the sound of her voice. Everything after the attack lasts a few minutes and then simply dissolves. He walks into a room and cannot say why he came, or how long he has been standing there.

So he builds a system, and he is meticulous about it. Polaroid photographs of every person, car and building that matters, each one annotated in his own handwriting. Notes folded into his pockets. And, for the facts he refuses to risk losing, tattoos across his chest, his arms and his legs — a numbered list of what he believes he knows about the man who killed his wife. His body has become his filing cabinet.

Nolan tells the story in a way that puts you inside the condition rather than watching it. The colour scenes run in reverse order: each one begins where the previous one ended, so you arrive, exactly as Leonard does, in the middle of a situation with no idea how you got there or who anybody is. Running between them, in black and white, is a second strand that moves forwards — Leonard alone in a motel room, talking on the telephone about a former insurance client called Sammy Jankis, a man he says had the same condition. The two strands meet at the end of the film, which is the beginning of the story.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What can Leonard still remember, and what can he not?

2. How does the structure of the film put the viewer inside his condition?

1. Everything from before the attack is intact — his identity, his childhood, his wife. Nothing after it survives more than a few minutes.

2. The colour scenes run backwards, so each scene drops you into a situation with no memory of what led to it; a black-and-white strand runs forwards, and the two meet at the end.

Discuss:
  • If you had ten minutes of memory and one tattoo, what would you have written on you — and why that, rather than anything else?

Part 2 — Everybody has a use for him

Leonard is hunting a man he knows only as John G. Two people attach themselves to him. Teddy is friendly, talkative, endlessly helpful, and always seems to know more than he is saying. Natalie works in a bar; her partner has disappeared, and she has problems of her own. Both of them are warm to Leonard. Both of them are steering him.

In the film's most uncomfortable sequence, Natalie works out precisely how weak he is. She insults his dead wife until he loses control and strikes her; then she walks calmly outside, waits for the memory to disappear, and comes back in as his friend and ally — bruised, and blaming somebody else. He believes her instantly, because he has nothing left to check her against. It is not a violent scene so much as a chilling one: we watch a person being edited.

Guided by information he has been fed, Leonard tracks down and kills a man called Jimmy Grantz in the basement of an abandoned building. Almost immediately, small details — the dead man's clothes, his car, a name he said before he died — stop fitting the story Leonard has been told. Something is wrong, and Leonard has no way of reconstructing what. Because the film is running backwards, we have already seen the consequences of this killing before we understand its cause.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Natalie discover that Leonard can be manipulated, and what does she do with that discovery?

2. What goes wrong after Leonard kills Jimmy Grantz?

1. She provokes him into hitting her, then waits outside until he has forgotten it and returns as his injured friend, blaming someone else — and he believes her completely.

2. Details of the dead man stop matching the story Leonard was given, and he cannot reconstruct what really happened or who fed him the information.

Discuss:
  • Natalie, Teddy and others all use Leonard. Is a person who cannot remember being wronged actually being harmed? Where would you draw the line?

Part 3 — The truth at the end, which is the beginning

Cornered, Teddy finally tells Leonard what he says is the truth. He is a police officer. Leonard found and killed his wife's real attacker more than a year ago, with Teddy's help — Teddy even took the photograph. Since then, Teddy has simply been recycling him, pointing him at a new "John G." whenever it suits, because Leonard is a weapon that forgets it has been fired.

And Sammy Jankis, Teddy says, is a story Leonard has partly rebuilt. In Teddy's account, Leonard investigated Sammy as an insurance claim, decided his memory loss was psychological rather than physical, and refused to pay. Sammy had no wife who died of an overdose. It was Leonard's own wife who survived the break-in — and who later died after asking, again and again, for the insulin injections that Leonard, unable to remember giving the last one, kept giving her. The grief in the story is real; the name on it may not be.

Whether Teddy is telling the truth is left deliberately open. What is not open is what Leonard does next. He hears the facts, decides he cannot live inside them, and destroys them: he burns the photograph, writes himself a false note, and deliberately adds Teddy's number plate to the list of "clues" tattooed on his body — knowing that a future version of himself will read it as proof and go hunting again. For about ninety seconds he understands everything. Then he chooses, consciously, the world he would rather wake up in.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Teddy claim about Leonard's revenge and about the story of Sammy Jankis?

2. What does Leonard deliberately do with that information at the end?

1. That Leonard already killed the real attacker a year ago and has been steered at fresh targets ever since; and that the Sammy Jankis story hides Leonard's own part in his wife's death.

2. He burns the evidence, writes a false note and tattoos Teddy's number plate onto himself, so that his future self will start the hunt again.

Discuss:
  • Leonard lies to himself on purpose, because the lie gives him a reason to get up. Is that madness, or is it something almost everybody does in smaller ways?

Grammar — linking & discourse markers

A story told backwards is impossible to retell without signposts. These are the words that tell your listener when something happened relative to something else, which way the argument has just turned, and what followed from what. Get them right and a confusing plot becomes clear; get them wrong and your listener is as lost as Leonard.

1. Sequence and simultaneity — putting events in time:
• meanwhile — at the same time, in another place or another strand of the story: "Leonard is photographing the motel sign. Meanwhile, Teddy is watching him from the car park."
• in the meantime — in the gap between two known events, while you wait: "The motel room will not be ready until three; in the meantime, he sits in reception with his coat on."
• subsequently — after that; formal, and common in reports and case histories: "The patient recovered from the infection and was subsequently transferred to long-term care."
• at which point — and at that exact moment, something changed: "She waited outside for ten minutes, at which point he had forgotten the whole quarrel."
2. Contrast — turning the argument:
• however — but; starts a new sentence, formal: "He has no memory of the last ten minutes. However, he knows exactly who he is."
• nevertheless — despite that, this is still true; concedes a point and then holds its ground: "The notes are unreliable. Nevertheless, he goes on believing them."
• whereas — a direct comparison between two things, inside one sentence: "A tattoo lasts for ever, whereas a note can fall out of a pocket."
• by contrast — introduces the opposite side of a comparison you have just set up: "The film's colour scenes move backwards. By contrast, the black-and-white scenes move forwards."
3. Result — what followed from what:
• consequently — as a direct result of that: "Nobody wrote a name on the back of that photograph. Consequently, it is useless to him."
Position and punctuation — the part learners get wrong:
• However, Nevertheless, Consequently, Subsequently, Meanwhile, By contrast, In the meantime normally open a new sentence and take a comma after them. They can also sit inside a sentence between two commas: "He was, nevertheless, completely certain."
• Whereas is different: it is a conjunction. It joins two clauses inside one sentence and cannot stand alone at the start of a new one.
• At which point also cannot start a sentence. It hooks onto the clause before it, usually after a comma.
• However is not but: don't write "He tried, however he failed." Either use a full stop or a semicolon before it.

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

Films love amnesia and almost always get it wrong. Memento is the exception that neurologists actually recommend. The condition Leonard has is a real, well-documented one, and the two most famous people who lived with it reshaped everything science knows about human memory.

🧠 Anterograde amnesia — and why it is not the Hollywood kind

Anterograde amnesia is the inability to form new long-term memories after the event that caused the damage. Everything from before is still there: your name, your childhood, your skills, your personality. That is the opposite of the usual film version, in which somebody is knocked on the head, forgets who they are, wanders off with a new identity, and is cured by a second knock. In real medicine, memories of the past are lost in retrograde amnesia, and total loss of personal identity belongs to a different, much rarer psychiatric picture.

In 2004 the clinical neuropsychologist Sallie Baxendale reviewed cinema's portrayals of memory loss in the BMJ and found that almost none of them corresponded to any real neurological condition. Memento was one of the very few she picked out as accurate. The usual real causes are not fist fights: brain infection (herpes simplex encephalitis in particular), stroke, oxygen starvation, severe thiamine deficiency, and surgery on the medial temporal lobes.

Henry Molaison — the patient who taught the world how memory is built

Henry Gustav Molaison was born in Connecticut on 26 February 1926. He developed epilepsy in childhood, possibly after a bicycle accident at seven; by his late twenties the seizures were disabling despite very high doses of medication. On 1 September 1953 the neurosurgeon William Beecher Scoville performed a bilateral medial temporal lobectomy, removing structures on both sides of his brain including most of the hippocampus, the entorhinal and parahippocampal cortex, and the amygdalae. It was experimental. It was also, in a way nobody foresaw, catastrophic.

The seizures improved. But Molaison could no longer lay down new memories at all — his span was around thirty seconds. He kept his intelligence, his manners, his childhood and his sense of humour, and he lost every day as it happened. He was known to the world only as "H.M." until his death on 2 December 2008; his full name was published only afterwards. He lived for many years in a care home in Windsor Locks, Connecticut, and took part in research for over half a century — never once able to remember having done it before.

From 1957 the neuropsychologist Brenda Milner studied him, and made the discovery that split memory in two. She gave him a mirror-drawing task: trace between the lines of a star while seeing only its reflection, which reverses every movement your hand makes. Day after day his accuracy improved, exactly like any healthy learner — and every single time, he insisted he had never attempted it before. Something in him was learning. Nothing in him was remembering. After his death his brain was preserved, cut into 2,401 thin slices and reconstructed as a digital atlas that was made freely available to researchers.

Clive Wearing — the man whose music outlived his memory

Clive Wearing, born on 11 May 1938, was a distinguished English musicologist, conductor, tenor and keyboard player. He had been chorus master of the London Sinfonietta, worked in music broadcasting, and specialised in Renaissance music, editing the works of the sixteenth-century composer Orlande de Lassus. On 27 March 1985, in his mid-forties, he contracted herpes simplex encephalitis. The infection destroyed his hippocampi and much of his medial temporal lobes.

What was left was a memory measured in seconds — somewhere between about seven and thirty. His carers gave him a diary. He filled page after page with the same discovery: that he had just this moment become conscious for the first time, each earlier entry crossed out as a mistake, hundreds of near-identical pages, still being written more than twenty years later. When his wife Deborah comes into the room he greets her with overwhelming joy, as though after years apart — and does so again if she steps out for a minute. She wrote about their life in her memoir Forever Today (2006), and the neurologist Oliver Sacks described him in a 2007 essay, "The Abyss".

And yet: sit him at a keyboard and he plays. He sight-reads music he cannot remember seeing, performs complex piano and organ pieces, and conducts a choir with the authority he had before the illness — Sacks described his musical powers as essentially intact, and his playing as still full of feeling and structure. For the length of a piece of music he is not a patient. When the last chord dies, so does the memory of having played it.

Two memories, not one — and what the film gets right

Molaison and Wearing demonstrate the same split. Declarative memory (also called explicit) is the memory you can put into words: facts, and events that happened to you. It depends on the hippocampus and the surrounding medial temporal lobe, which is why damage there destroys it. Procedural memory (also called implicit) is the memory in your hands: skills, habits and conditioned responses, learned by doing and repeating. It runs on different machinery, and it survives. H.M.'s mirror drawing and Wearing's Lassus are the same phenomenon in two different lives.

Memento is unusually well built on this. Leonard keeps his identity and his whole pre-injury life, as real patients do. His everyday failures are the real ones: losing the thread mid-sentence, finding himself running and not knowing whether he is chasing or being chased. And the film's central plot device is scientific rather than invented — Leonard's insurance test of Sammy Jankis, picking up objects of which some are electrified, is a version of a genuine clinical logic. A person with organic amnesia would learn, without any conscious recollection, to avoid the ones that hurt. That is procedural learning, and it is why Leonard himself can be conditioned, shaped and steered by people all through the film without ever knowing it. The neuroscientist Christof Koch has called the film the most accurate portrayal of the different memory systems in popular media.

Where it dramatises. First, the cause: a single blow to the head producing pure, permanent, isolated anterograde amnesia is very rare — the real culprits are infection, stroke, oxygen loss and surgery. Second, and much bigger, the independence. Leonard drives, rents rooms, dresses well, holds conversations and runs a multi-day investigation alone. People with damage that dense need constant care; Molaison lived in an institution, and Wearing has been looked after for four decades. Third, the note system. Wearing's diary is the real-world version of Leonard's Polaroids and tattoos, and it does not work as a substitute memory — it records the same "first" awakening over and over. To use a reminder you have to remember that you have reminders, and that is precisely the part that is broken. Leonard's elegant, functioning archive is the film's most beautiful lie.

One last real detail. The film came from a short story, "Memento Mori", written by Christopher Nolan's brother Jonathan Nolan and published in Esquire in March 2001 — after the film had already premiered at Venice in September 2000. In the story the character is called Earl, and he lives in an institution, which is rather closer to how this actually goes.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, which part of Leonard's condition did you assume was invented for the film — and which turns out to be textbook neurology?
  • H.M. and Clive Wearing were people, not case studies, and both spent decades being observed. What do we owe patients whose misfortune advances science?
  • Clive Wearing can play a piece he cannot remember learning. If your skills survive but your memories don't, how much of "you" is still there?
  • The film keeps the science and invents the independence. Is that a fair trade for a story — or does it quietly mislead people about what these lives are like?

Task B · The comfortable lie (4 minutes)

At the end Leonard hears the truth, decides he cannot live with it, and deliberately destroys it so that his future self will believe something easier. Build an argument: is a person ever entitled to choose a comforting lie over an unbearable truth? And is Leonard a victim of his injury, or a man who is fully responsible for what he does with it? Bring in examples you know — people who refuse a diagnosis, families who keep a secret, official histories that leave things out.

Task C · Memory, evidence and identity (4 minutes)

Leonard trusts his notes more than his mind. We now keep our lives on our phones. Is a written or photographed record a better memory than a human one — or does something important disappear when we outsource remembering? Make your case.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Whether photographs of an event make you remember it better, or replace the memory of it.
  • How much a court should trust an eyewitness, given that human memory can be rewritten.
  • Whether societies, like people, choose which version of their past to keep — and who decides.
  • A famous historical event where the popular version and the documented version disagree.
  • Whether the ability to forget is a fault in the brain or one of its best features.
  • A film or book that changed the way you think about how the mind works.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Henry Molaison learned a skill he could never remember practising. If your hands know something your mind does not, who exactly is the one who learned it?
  2. Everyone in Memento uses Leonard, and he never finds out. Does a wrong that the victim can never know about still count as a wrong?
  3. The neurology in this film is broadly right; the idea that a man like Leonard could live alone and run an investigation is not. Which distortion matters more — getting the science wrong, or getting the life wrong?
