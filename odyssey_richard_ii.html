The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The poll tax charged every adult the same.
a poll tax (n) — a tax on every person.
2. The Peasants' Revolt began in 1381.
a revolt (n) — a violent protest against authority.
3. The rebels marched on London.
a rebel (n) — a person who fights authority.
4. They demanded an end to serfdom.
serfdom (n) — a system where peasants were tied to a lord's land.
5. The king chose to pardon the rebels.
to pardon (v) — to forgive officially.
6. He later decided to revoke the pardons.
to revoke (v) — to cancel officially.
7. His later rule became tyrannical.
tyrannical (adj) — cruel and unfair in using power.
8. He chose to confiscate his cousin's inheritance.
to confiscate (v) — to take property officially.
9. He probably starved to death in prison.
to starve (v) — to die from lack of food.
10. The Wilton Diptych is a beautiful painting of him.
the Wilton Diptych (n) — a two-panelled painting.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The revolt

Richard II became king at ten in 1377. In 1381, after a harsh poll tax, peasants rose in Essex and Kent. Led by Wat Tyler, they entered London, burned the Savoy Palace and killed the Archbishop of Canterbury.

At Smithfield the fourteen-year-old king rode out to meet them. Tyler was killed by the Mayor of London, and Richard, it is said, rode forward telling the rebels that he would be their leader.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What caused the revolt?

2. What did Richard do at Smithfield?

1. A harsh poll tax (and wider anger at serfdom).

2. Rode out, and after Tyler was killed, told the rebels he would be their leader.

Discuss:
  • Was the boy king brave or reckless?

Part 2 — Promises broken

Richard promised the rebels freedom from serfdom and pardons. Once they had gone home, the promises were revoked and leaders executed. A chronicler reported him saying, "Serfs you are, and serfs you shall remain."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Richard promise?

2. What happened afterwards?

1. Freedom from serfdom and pardons.

2. The promises were revoked and leaders executed.

Discuss:
  • Should promises made under pressure be kept?

Part 3 — Tyranny and fall

In later years Richard punished his enemies and ruled in an increasingly tyrannical way. In 1399, when his cousin Henry Bolingbroke's father died, Richard confiscated Henry's inheritance. Henry invaded while Richard was in Ireland, and Richard surrendered. Parliament accepted his deposition, and he died in Pontefract Castle in 1400, probably starved.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Richard confiscate in 1399?

2. How did Richard die?

1. Henry Bolingbroke's inheritance.

2. In Pontefract Castle, probably starved, in 1400.

Discuss:
  • Why did taking Henry's inheritance backfire?

Grammar — Reported Speech

Much of what we know about the past comes from chroniclers reporting what people said. Reported speech moves the tense back and changes the pronouns.

How it works:
• "I will return," he said → He said that he would return.
• "We have lost," she said → She said that they had lost.
• Reporting verbs: claimed, promised, warned, insisted, denied.

1. Report the words

2. He promised, "You are free." → He promised that they free.

Going deeper

Richard's reign shows two sides of leadership: courage at Smithfield, then broken promises and arbitrary rule. His deposition opened the way to the Wars of the Roses.

Worth noticing: Shakespeare's Richard II gives him the famous lines on the "sceptred isle" — spoken, in fact, by John of Gaunt.
Learning from the past — for today: Trust is spent once. Broken promises — to workers, customers or friends — are remembered for a long time. Promise only what you can keep.
