Swapping Verbs: Casual vs Careful

The fastest way to shift register: swap a two- or three-word phrasal verb for a single, more formal verb that means almost the same thing.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday messages, each said two ways:

  • "Let's put off the meeting till Monday." → "Let's postpone the meeting until Monday."
  • "I'll look into it and get back to you." → "I'll investigate and respond to you."
  • "Can we sort this out over the phone?" → "Can we resolve this over the phone?"

Before you check: what's swapped, and what stays the same?

Check the pattern CLICK
The meaning barely moves — but the two- or three-word phrasal verb (put off, look into, sort out) gets replaced by a single verb (postpone, investigate, resolve). The phrasal verb sounds relaxed and immediate; the single verb sounds careful and a little more distant. Neither is "more correct" — you pick based on who's listening.
put off → postpone
look into → investigate
sort out → resolve

Closing the Distance: Passives and Nominalisation

Watch what happens to "I" and "we" as a message gets more formal.

Spot the pattern

Three admissions, moving from personal to distant:

  • "I broke the vase, sorry!" → "The vase was broken — I'm very sorry."
  • "We messed up the invoice." → "Errors were made in preparing the invoice."
  • "We forgot to submit the form on time." → "The failure to submit the form on time was an oversight."

What happens to "I" and "we" as each sentence gets more formal? Say your answer before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
"I" and "we" quietly disappear. The passive voice removes the doer (was broken, were made), and nominalisation turns the action itself into a noun (failure, oversight, error) so nobody has to be the grammatical subject of the mistake. It creates distance — useful for softening blame in writing, but odd and evasive if you did it out loud to a friend.
Personal (informal): I / we + active verb — "I broke it."
Distanced (neutral): subject + was / were + past participle — "It was broken."
Nominalised (formal): a noun for the mistake (failure, oversight, error) — "The failure ... was an oversight."

Precision and Politeness

Formal register does two more things: it swaps vague words for exact ones, and it softens requests and refusals so they land gently.

Spot the pattern

  • "Can you do me a favour and cover my shift?" → "I was wondering whether you might be able to cover my shift."
  • "The room was kind of gross, loads of dust everywhere." → "The room was unclean; there was a significant amount of dust on every surface."
  • "We can't do the discount, sorry." → "I'm afraid we're not able to offer the discount on this occasion."

Two things change on the right. What happens to (a) vague words like "kind of" and "loads of", and (b) the request or refusal itself? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
(a) Vague quantifiers get replaced by precise ones — formal register commits to specifics instead of "kind of" and "loads of". (b) Requests and refusals get softened with hedges — I was wondering whether, I'm afraid, might be able to — so the demand or the "no" feels less direct, even though the meaning is identical.
Vague (informal): a bit, kind of, loads of, stuff, tons of
Precise (formal): a significant amount, several, an exact figure
Softening a request: I was wondering whether... / Would you be able to... / I'd be grateful if...
Softening bad news: I'm afraid... / Unfortunately... / I regret to say...
One nuance worth noticing: contractions aren't automatically informal. Formal spoken English (a phone call, face to face) usually keeps them — "I'm afraid we won't be able to..." It's formal writing — a letter, an official email — that tends to spell everything out in full.
