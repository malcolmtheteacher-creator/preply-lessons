Tutankhamun

A forgotten boy king who became the most famous pharaoh of all.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The forgotten boy king

Around 3,300 years ago, in ancient Egypt, a boy became a god. His name was Tutankhamun, and he was made pharaoh while he was still a child — perhaps only eight or nine years old. We know little for certain about his short life. He ruled for only a handful of years and died while he was still a teenager, before he had the chance to do anything that history would long remember.

In his own time, Tutankhamun was a minor king. He left behind no great temples, no famous battles, no legend. Within a few generations his name had faded; later pharaohs left him off their lists of kings, and the desert quietly swallowed his memory. For more than three thousand years he was, to all the world, forgotten — one obscure name among hundreds, lost in the sand of the Valley of the Kings.

And yet today he is the most famous pharaoh who ever lived. Every detail of the story you are about to read happened not because of how he lived, but because of where he was buried — and because of the man who, thousands of years later, would refuse to stop looking for him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What kind of king was Tutankhamun in his own lifetime?

2. Why was he forgotten for so long?

1. A minor, unimportant king who became pharaoh as a child and died as a teenager, having ruled for only a few years and left no great monuments or victories.

2. He achieved little that history recorded, later pharaohs left him off their king-lists, and over three thousand years his name and tomb were lost in the desert.

Discuss:
  • Tutankhamun is famous for how he was buried, not for anything he did. Is it strange that someone can become world-famous for reasons that have nothing to do with their own life?

Part 2 — "Wonderful things"

In November 1922, a British archaeologist named Howard Carter was running out of time. For years he had been searching the Valley of the Kings, funded by a wealthy patron, Lord Carnarvon, who was growing impatient and close to abandoning the whole project. Almost everyone believed the valley had given up all its secrets long ago. Then Carter's workers uncovered a stone step, then another, then a whole staircase descending to a sealed doorway. After all the years of searching, something untouched lay behind it.

On 26 November 1922, Carter made a small hole in the sealed door and held up a candle to look inside. Behind him, Lord Carnarvon asked, "Can you see anything?" For a long moment Carter could not speak. Then he answered: "Yes — wonderful things." The flickering candlelight had fallen on gold. Against all the odds, the tomb was almost intact: where nearly every other royal tomb had been emptied by robbers thousands of years before, this one had survived.

Inside lay over five thousand objects: golden treasures, chariots, statues, and great nested shrines and coffins, one inside another. At the centre was the mummy of the boy king himself, his face covered by a solid-gold mask that would become one of the most recognisable images on earth. It was the most complete ancient Egyptian tomb ever found — and as the news travelled, it became a global sensation.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was Carter almost out of chances when he made the discovery?

2. What made this tomb so extraordinary compared with other royal tombs?

1. He had searched the Valley of the Kings for years with little to show for it, and his backer, Lord Carnarvon, was impatient and close to ending the funding.

2. It was almost intact — barely touched — while nearly every other royal tomb had been robbed; it held over 5,000 objects, including the boy king's mummy and a solid-gold mask.

Discuss:
  • "Yes — wonderful things" is one of the most famous sentences in archaeology. Why do you think a single short phrase can capture a huge moment so well?

Part 3 — The curse that never died

A few months after the discovery, in 1923, Lord Carnarvon died suddenly. The cause was an infected mosquito bite, but the timing was irresistible. Newspapers, hungry for an even bigger story, invented one: a "curse of the pharaohs," an ancient punishment said to strike down anyone who disturbed the king's rest. Every later illness or death connected, however loosely, to the tomb was added to the legend, and the world half-believed it.

The curse, of course, is a myth. There was no inscription threatening death, and most of those present at the opening lived long, ordinary lives — Howard Carter himself worked on for years and died of natural causes well over a decade later. What the story really shows is how easily superstition fills the space where mystery and money meet.

And yet the myth has never quite gone away. A hundred years later, the curse is still repeated in films, books and documentaries, travelling alongside the real wonder of the discovery. Tutankhamun, the forgotten boy king, is now immortal — remembered not only for his golden treasures, but for a spooky story that was never true.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the idea of a "curse" begin?

2. What evidence shows the curse is a myth?

1. When Lord Carnarvon died suddenly in 1923 (from an infected mosquito bite), newspapers invented the story of a "curse of the pharaohs" that punished those who disturbed the tomb.

2. There was no curse inscription, and most people present — including Howard Carter, who lived on for years and died naturally — were unharmed.

Discuss:
  • The curse was invented, yet it has lasted a hundred years. Why do you think a good myth can outlive the truth so easily?

The grammar this story runs on: the passive, reported speech & deduction

This is a story of things being done — a tomb discovered, treasures found, a curse invented — and of things being said about a man we can only guess at. Three tools carry it.

The three tools:
• The passive — when the action matters more than who did it: "the tomb was discovered in 1922," "the treasures were found intact." Form it with be + past participle.
• Reported speech — telling what someone said, one step back in time: "Carter said that he could see wonderful things," "it was said that a curse struck the tomb."
• Modals of deduction — for guessing about the past from evidence: "he must have been only a child," "the robbers can't have known the tomb was there."

1. The passive — when the action matters most

Turn each active sentence into the passive. Keep the same tense.

1. Carter discovered the tomb in 1922. → The tomb in 1922.

2. Workers found over 5,000 objects inside. → Over 5,000 objects inside.

3. Newspapers invented the curse. → The curse by newspapers.

1. was discovered (the focus is the tomb, not Carter)

2. were found

3. was invented (you can keep the agent with by when it matters)

2. Reported speech — telling what was said

Put the words into reported speech. Remember to shift the verb one step back.

1. Carter said, "I can see wonderful things." → Carter said that he wonderful things.

2. Carnarvon asked, "Can you see anything?" → Carnarvon asked Carter anything.

3. People said, "A curse strikes the tomb." → It that a curse struck the tomb.

1. could see (can → could)

2. if / whether he could see (yes/no question → if/whether, no question word order)

3. was said (the impersonal passive report: "it was said that…")

3. Modals of deduction — guessing about the past

We can't ask Tutankhamun anything, so we deduce. Fill each gap with must have, can't have or might / may have + past participle, using the evidence in brackets.

1. He became king as a small boy, so he (be) very young when he died.

2. The tomb was almost untouched, so robbers (empty) it.

3. We aren't sure how he died, so an illness or an accident (cause) it.

1. must have been (strong certainty from evidence)

2. can't have emptied (impossibility — the treasure was still there)

3. might / may have caused (a real possibility we can't confirm)

This is the voice-and-deduction strand — the language of describing what was done, reporting what was said, and reasoning about what we cannot know for certain. You'll put it straight to work in the Speak tab.
