1. A single cargo of pepper and nutmeg could make its owners rich for life; if the ship sank, the cargo went to the bottom with it.

4. Every year the company paid out part of its profit to its owners; this payment, a dividend, was what many buyers really wanted.
dividend (n) — a part of a company's profit that is paid to its shareholders. Example: "The company paid a dividend of two euros per share."

speculate (v) — to buy things such as shares in the hope of selling them quickly at a higher price, taking a big risk. Example: "Many people speculated on the price of the new stock."

frenzy (n) — a state of wild, uncontrolled excitement or activity. Example: "The crowd worked itself into a frenzy of buying."

The story, in three parts

Part 1 — Ships, spices and a clever idea

In the early 1600s the richest trade in Europe was in spices: pepper, nutmeg, cloves and cinnamon, which grew thousands of miles away in Asia. A voyage from the Netherlands to the Spice Islands and back took more than a year, and the dangers were enormous: storms, disease, shipwreck and pirates. One cargo could make a merchant rich for life. One lost ship could just as easily ruin him.

In 1602 the Dutch government made a decision that changed financial history. Instead of letting rival merchants send out their ships separately, it merged several small trading companies into one giant organisation, the Dutch East India Company, known by its initials, the VOC. And to raise the huge sums needed, it did something unusual: it invited ordinary people, not only the wealthy, to put in money and receive shares in return. A share is a small piece of ownership of a company. If the voyages made a profit, the shareholders received a dividend.

Shares solved two problems at once. First, they spread the risk: with so many owners, the loss of one ship could not ruin any single person. Second, and more cleverly, shares could be sold. A shareholder who needed his money did not have to wait for the ships to return; he could simply sell his share to someone else. Soon traders were meeting in Amsterdam, first in the street and later around the Beurs, a building made for merchants, to buy and sell VOC shares every day. Historians often describe the result as the world's first modern stock exchange.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did sharing the ownership of a voyage make it less risky for each person?

2. What was the second, cleverer advantage of shares in the VOC?

1. With many owners, the loss of one ship could not ruin any one person: the risk was spread over everyone who owned a share.

2. Shares could be sold to someone else, so owners did not have to wait for the ships to come back to get their money out. This created a market for trading shares.

Discuss:
  • Would you have bought a VOC share in 1602? What would you have wanted to know first?

Part 2 — Why the price moves

Here is how it works. Imagine a small company that owns one bakery. It has 100 shares, and today one share costs 10 euros, so the whole company is worth 1,000 euros on the market. This figure, the price of one share multiplied by the number of shares, is called the company's market value.

Now the news arrives: the bakery will open in five new cities. More people want to buy shares, and fewer want to sell. Buyers offer 11 euros, then 12, and the price rises. Nothing about the bakery has changed yet; what has changed is what people expect. A week later an oven breaks and the bakery closes for a month. Now owners want to sell and nobody wants to buy. Sellers accept 9 euros, then 8, and the price falls.

That is the key idea: a share price is not a fact about the company. It is the latest agreement between buyers and sellers. A shareholder can make money in two ways, from dividends and by selling a share for more than they paid. To follow the whole market at once, people invented the index. In 1896 the American journalist Charles Dow published the first version of what became the Dow Jones average, calculated from the share prices of just twelve companies. Today there are thousands of indexes, and the news announces the main ones every day.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. In the bakery example, why does the price go up, even though the bakery has not changed yet?

2. In what two ways can a shareholder make money?

1. Because people expect the company to be worth more. More people want to buy and fewer want to sell, so demand rises and so does the price.

2. From dividends (a part of the profit paid to owners), and by selling a share at a higher price than they paid for it.

Discuss:
  • The bakery did not change, but the price did. Who gains and who loses when prices move because of expectations alone?

Part 3 — The bubble

Sometimes expectations run far ahead of reality. In 1720 a British company called the South Sea Company, which held a monopoly on trade with South America and had also taken over part of the government's debt, saw its share price climb from about £128 in January to roughly £1,000 by August. All of London talked about nothing else. Servants, doctors, clergymen and lords scrambled to buy before the price rose again, and clever promoters opened new companies for schemes that were completely absurd.

Even the great scientist Isaac Newton was caught in the frenzy. He sold some of his shares early and made a profit, but as the price kept rising he bought back in at a far higher price, and when the bubble burst he lost a great deal of money, probably around £20,000, though accounts differ. By the end of the year the price had fallen to a fraction of its peak.

The pattern has repeated again and again, in the crash of 1929 and in the dot-com collapse of 2000. Each time there was a good story, the price rose, and more people joined in because the price was rising. Over very long periods, however, studies of the American market find that shares have grown by roughly seven per cent a year after inflation, with long, painful falls on the way, and past results are never a promise. A market is not a machine; it is a story that millions of strangers update every second.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to the South Sea Company's share price in 1720?

2. What mistake did Newton make, according to the story?

1. It rose from about £128 in January to around £1,000 by August, then fell back to a fraction of its peak by the end of the year.

2. He sold early and made a profit, but then bought again at a much higher price. When the bubble burst, he lost a great deal of money.

Discuss:
  • Why do people join a bubble even when they suspect that the price cannot rise for ever?

The grammar this story runs on: likelihood and prediction

A market is a machine for guessing the future: will this price rise, will that company pay, will the bubble burst? English has a rich set of phrases for saying how sure you are about the future, and choosing the right one is the difference between sounding careful and sounding reckless.

The scale, from certain to doubtful
• Certain: is bound to / is sure to / is certain to + verb — "If profits fall for years, the price is bound to fall."
• Very likely: is very likely to + verb · It is very likely that + clause — "It is very likely that demand will stay high."
• Likely: will probably + verb · is likely to + verb — "The price will probably rise."
• Possible: may well / might / could + verb — "Prices may well fall sharply."
• Doubtful: is unlikely to + verb · is very unlikely to + verb — "Nobody is likely to predict the exact date."
• Almost impossible: is highly unlikely to / almost certainly won't.
Careful with position
• The adverb goes after will but before won't: "It will probably rise" · "It probably won't rise."
• Likely is an adjective, not an adverb: "It is likely to rise" or "It is likely that it will rise" — not "It likely will rise".
• Never bound alone: always bound to + verb. It suggests a result that follows naturally from the situation.

1. Choose the right phrase

Complete each sentence with one of these: bound · unlikely · probably · may well. Use each once.

1. If a company loses money for years, its share price is to fall as well.

2. Almost nobody can predict next year's price exactly. It is very that anyone will get it right.

3. I'm not certain, but I think demand will stay high this week; it's more likely than not.

4. Prices have doubled in a year. Some experts warn that they fall sharply; it's quite possible, though nobody knows.

1. bound (a natural result: "is bound to")

2. unlikely ("very unlikely that")

3. probably ("will probably stay": after will)

4. may well (quite possible, not certain)

2. Choose the correct form — a, b or c

1. Everyone is selling and nobody is buying. The price is ___ to fall.
  (a) unlikely   (b) bound   (c) probably
(b) — a natural result of the situation: is bound to fall. Probably is an adverb and cannot follow is in this pattern.
2. It is possible, but not very likely. The company is ___ to pay a dividend this year.
  (a) unlikely   (b) sure   (c) bound
(a) — "possible, but not very likely" means unlikely. Sure and bound would mean it is certain.
3. The bubble ___ much longer.
  (a) won't probably last   (b) probably won't last   (c) probably will not to last
(b) — with won't, the adverb goes before: probably won't. After will, it goes after: will probably.
4. The harvest was terrible, so ___ the price of grain will rise.
  (a) it likely   (b) likely is   (c) it is likely that
(c) — it is likely that + a full clause.

3. Make a prediction — write your answer first

Read the situation. Write a sentence that says how likely the result is, using a phrase from the scale.

1. A company has lost money for five years in a row. What will probably happen to its share price?

2. A new company promises to double its profit every year for ever. How believable is that?

3. Almost everybody wants to buy a share, and almost nobody wants to sell. What is the price bound to do?

1. Its price is likely to fall. / It will probably fall. / It is bound to fall (if nothing changes).

2. It is highly unlikely to happen. / It is very unlikely that the company can keep that up for ever.

3. The price is bound to rise. / It is very likely to rise.

Check: did you match the phrase to how sure you are, and put the adverb in the right place?
