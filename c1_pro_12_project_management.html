Project Management Communication

C1 Level · Operational English

The PM World: Why Projects Fail

Projects go wrong for many reasons—but most failures trace back to communication breakdowns.

Reflect:

  • What's the last project you've seen go off the rails? What happened?
  • How did the team find out things were going wrong?
  • What would have made a difference?
  • What's the difference between a project being "on track" and actually being on track?

The Hidden Costs of Project Failures

Budget overruns: If a project goes 20% over budget, that's millions on large projects. But worse is the loss of credibility. Sponsors stop trusting your estimates.

Schedule slips: Missing deadlines cascades downstream. Other projects get delayed. Teams get demoralised. Your reputation suffers.

Scope creep: "Just one more thing" compounds. Before you know it, you're delivering a different product than what was commissioned.

Communication failure: When stakeholders hear about problems from someone other than the project leader, trust erodes. When they hear it late, anger flares.

Team burnout: When a project runs over, people work harder and longer. If they're caught off guard by problems, they feel unheard and disrespected.

The Role of Communication

Good project management is, fundamentally, clear communication. You're managing:

  • Expectations (scope, timeline, quality standards)
  • Information (who knows what, when, and why it matters)
  • Confidence (stakeholders, sponsors, and team members believe you have control)
  • Problems (being transparent about risks and issues early)

The best project managers aren't necessarily the most technical or organised. They're the ones who communicate relentlessly, honestly, and with sufficient warning when things change.

Think about this: In a project you've been part of, what information came too late? What would have happened if it had arrived sooner?

Vocabulary & Key Concepts

Master the language of project management to communicate with precision and credibility.

Scope Creep Uncontrolled expansion of a project's requirements beyond what was originally planned.
Milestone A significant point in a project timeline marking completion of a major phase or deliverable.
Deliverable A tangible output or product produced by a project; what's being handed over.
Risk Register A document listing identified risks, their impact, probability, and mitigation strategies.
Blocker An issue or dependency that stops work from progressing; requires immediate escalation.
Sprint A fixed time period (typically 1-2 weeks) in Agile where a team commits to deliver specific work.
Retrospective A meeting where teams reflect on what went well, what didn't, and what to improve next.
Stakeholder Anyone affected by or who influences the project—sponsors, users, team members, executives.

Communicating Project Status Clearly

The Red-Yellow-Green framework: Stakeholders need to know status at a glance. Is the project on track (green), at risk (yellow), or in trouble (red)?

Be specific about what's wrong: Don't just say "at risk." Say "We're three weeks behind schedule on the API development because the vendor didn't deliver as promised. This impacts our go-live date unless we extend by two weeks or reduce scope."

Show ownership: When problems occur, explain what you're doing about them. "We've identified this issue and are implementing X solution, which will get us back on track by Y date."

Distinguish probability from impact: "There's a 40% chance we'll need additional budget (probability) because the third-party integration is more complex than expected. If it happens, we'll need an extra £50,000 (impact)."

Lead with recommendations: If there's a problem, come with options. "We can either extend the timeline by two weeks, reduce the feature set, or increase the budget by £30,000 for additional resources."

Communicate early and often: Better to say "we might have a problem" when you first sense it than to wait until it's certain and much larger. Gives people time to react and adjust.

Scenario: The Crisis Email & Steering Committee Meeting

Your project is three weeks behind schedule and 20% over budget. You need to communicate this to the steering committee—both via email and verbally. Let's work through it.

10:00

The Situation

Your enterprise system migration project launched three months ago with an 8-month timeline and a £2M budget. It was supposed to go live in 5 months (2 months from now). Last week, your infrastructure vendor missed a critical handoff. Your team has been scrambling, but it's now clear: you'll need at least three more weeks, and you'll need to hire contractors to stay as close to the original budget as possible.

Task 1: Write the status update email to the steering committee (CFO, CIO, Head of Operations). Keep it to 4 short paragraphs. Be honest, clear, and focused on solutions.

Example structure:

Subject: [Project Name] Status Update – Schedule Impact & Proposed Actions

Paragraph 1 (The issue): State the problem clearly and take responsibility without making excuses. "The enterprise system migration will require an additional three weeks beyond our target go-live date. This is due to delays in the vendor's infrastructure handover, which impacted our testing timeline."

Paragraph 2 (Why it matters): Explain the business impact. "This moves our go-live from [date] to [date]. It delays the projected £1.5M savings by three weeks and affects the Q3 operational efficiency gains we communicated."

Paragraph 3 (Your solution): Show what you're doing about it. "We're bringing in specialist contractors to accelerate testing and validation. This will cost an additional £85K, bringing total project cost to £2.085M—still within our contingency."

Paragraph 4 (The ask): Be clear about what you need. "We need approval from the steering committee by [date] to proceed with contractor engagement. I'm confident the revised timeline is achievable. Happy to discuss options on [meeting]."

Task 2: Prepare Your Verbal Explanation

You have 3 minutes to brief the steering committee in person. What are your key messages? How do you maintain credibility while acknowledging the miss? Write bullet points for what you'd say.

Effective approach:

Open with accountability: "This timeline extension is my accountability. We should have flagged this risk three weeks ago when the vendor first signalled delays."

Explain what happened (briefly): Don't over-explain or blame. "The vendor's infrastructure integration took longer than estimated. We've adjusted our testing approach and timeline accordingly."

Show you have control: "We've implemented additional checkpoints and brought in specialist resources to de-risk the remaining work."

Address the cost: "The delay costs us £85K in contractor fees, but it's within our contingency and significantly cheaper than a failed migration."

Be decisive: "I recommend we approve the extended timeline and contractor budget. I'm confident we'll go live by [new date]."

Show confidence without arrogance: "I understand this isn't the update we wanted. Here's what I'm doing to make sure there are no further surprises."

Project Culture & Communication

How you communicate as a project leader shapes team culture and determines how problems get surfaced.

Reflection questions:

  • What kind of project leader do you want to be? How does that show up in your communication?
  • How do you create a culture where people surface problems early?
  • When you've made a significant project mistake, what made it better or worse?
  • How do you balance confidence (we've got this) with honesty (things are hard)?
  • What's the difference between transparency and overloading people with information?

The Trust Bank Account

Every project leader has a trust bank account with stakeholders and team members. When you deliver on commitments, you make deposits. When you miss deadlines or surprise them with bad news, you make withdrawals.

The interesting thing: the deposits come from consistent, honest, transparent communication—not perfection. Leaders who admit problems early, explain them clearly, and take action often maintain more trust than leaders who hide problems until they're undeniable.

Your reputation as a project leader isn't built on never having problems. It's built on how you communicate when problems occur.

Creating Psychological Safety

The best teams surface issues early because they trust that reporting problems won't result in blame. As a leader, you can create this by:

  • Rewarding bad news: When someone brings you a problem early, thank them. Show it's the right move.
  • Focusing on solutions: "What do we do about this?" not "Why didn't you catch this?"
  • Taking accountability: When things go wrong, own your part before looking elsewhere.
  • Not punishing people for reasonable risks: If the team took a calculated risk and it didn't work out, that's learning, not failure.

Consider this: Think of a project where things went wrong. Did the team feel safe reporting problems? How did that affect the outcome? What would you do differently as a leader?
