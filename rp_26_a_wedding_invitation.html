A Wedding Invitation

An old friend is getting married and they want you there. Now to say yes with your whole heart — or bow out without breaking one.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

Your phone lights up on a quiet Tuesday evening. It's one of your oldest friends — the one you've laughed with since school — and the moment they say your name, you can hear the grin down the line. There's news, and it's the good kind.

They're engaged. And you're on the guest list. In the next two minutes you'll need to sound as delighted as you feel, then — without spoiling the mood — sort out the small mountain of practical questions a wedding always brings: can you bring someone, what on earth do you wear, and is there a gift they'd actually like.

And if, deep down, you already know you can't be there? Then the real skill kicks in: how to say no to the happiest invitation of the year, and still have a friend at the end of the call.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. What's the best wedding you've ever been to — and what made it stick in your memory, the ceremony, the food, or the chaos afterwards?
  2. Someone invites you to an event you really don't want to attend. Are you a "kind white lie" person or a "tell them the truth" person — and does it depend on who's asking?

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how the warmth never stops, even while the boring-but-necessary questions get asked and answered.

YouOh, stop it — that's the best news I've heard all year! I'm absolutely thrilled for you both. Honestly, I couldn't be happier for the two of you. Tell me everything — how did they do it?

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The guest asks four practical questions in a row without the call ever feeling like a form to fill in. What are the four things they sort out — and what small trick keeps each question from sounding cold?

2. "Genuinely, your being there is the only present we're after." Does this mean the guest shouldn't bring a gift? What's really going on with that line?

3. Look at the guest's very first reply. Before a single practical question, what does the guest do — and why is the order (this first, questions later) so important?

1. The plus-one, the dress code, the gift/registry, and where to stay. Each one is wrapped in a softener — "before I forget the sensible questions", "I hope you don't mind me asking", "one last thing" — so it lands as a friendly aside, not a demand.
2. Not really — it's a warm, modest thing couples say so guests don't feel obliged. Politely, you still bring or send something; the registry link is your answer to what. It's generosity meeting generosity.
3. They explode with joy and accept whole-heartedly first — "thrilled", "couldn't be happier", "wouldn't miss it for the world" — before anything practical. Warmth first tells the friend the answer is a delighted YES; the logistics are just tidying up afterwards.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the happiest phone call of the year — how to celebrate, how to ask the awkward bits, and how to say no without losing a friend. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🎉 Celebrating the news

Phrase 1

Your friend has just announced they're engaged. A flat "oh, nice" would break their heart. Which reply matches your delight to theirs and makes them feel truly celebrated?

A. "Oh, congratulations."
B. "That's good, when's the wedding?"
C. "Stop it — that's the best news I've heard all year! I'm absolutely thrilled for you both."
C — "Stop it — that's the best news I've heard all year! I'm absolutely thrilled for you both."
"Stop it" here is playful, not literal — pure joyful disbelief. "Thrilled for you both" folds in the partner too. Big news deserves a big reaction; don't rush to the logistics.
Phrase 2

You want to say that no news could possibly make you more delighted for them. There's a warm, common way to say it built on the word "happier". Fill the gaps: "I couldn't ___ ___ for the two of you."

"I couldn't be happier for the two of you."
A little piece of grammar doing emotional work: "couldn't be happier" = it's not possible to be happier than I am. Swap the noun for any occasion: "I couldn't be prouder / more excited / more grateful."

💌 Saying yes with your whole heart

Phrase 3

You want to accept in a way that says "nothing on earth would keep me away." Reassemble the classic idiom that does exactly that:

miss itfor theI wouldn'tworld
"I wouldn't miss it for the world."
The warmest possible yes — it puts the event above absolutely everything. Pair it with a playful "try and stop me" for extra sparkle. Perfect for weddings, big birthdays, anything that matters.
Phrase 4

You want to reassure them, casually and firmly, that they can rely on you being there. Which sounds more like a warm promise between friends, and less like a business email?

A. "I will confirm my attendance at the earliest opportunity."
B. "Consider me there — front row, tissues at the ready."
B — "Consider me there — front row, tissues at the ready."
"Consider me there" = treat it as already settled. The playful detail ("tissues at the ready") paints the picture and shows you care. A is correct English, but it belongs at work, not on a joyful phone call.

❓ Asking the practical things — gently

Phrase 5

You'd love to bring your partner, but you don't want to presume — a plus-one isn't guaranteed. What polite question asks permission rather than announcing a decision?

"Would it be alright if I brought someone?"
"Would it be alright if I…" is the gold-standard way to ask permission softly — and note the grammar: after "if I", we say brought (past form) for this polite, hypothetical feel. Add "…or is it strictly a solo affair?" to give them an easy out.
Phrase 6

You have no idea what to wear and don't want to be the only one in a suit — or the only one not in one. Complete the question that checks without any fuss: "Is there a ___ ___ I should know about?"

"Is there a dress code I should know about?"
"…I should know about" is a lovely soft tag — it assumes there might be one without demanding an answer. Follow it with "…or shall I just turn up looking respectable?" to keep it light.
Phrase 7

Asking about gifts can feel awkward — you don't want to sound like you're only there for the shopping list. Reorder this tactful way in:

you're hopinganythingis therefor, gift-wise?in particular
"Is there anything in particular you're hoping for, gift-wise?"
"…gift-wise" is a neat, casual way to name the awkward topic. Prefacing it with "I hope you don't mind me asking" softens it further, and lets them point you to a registry without either of you feeling odd.

🙏 Bowing out — without breaking a heart

Phrase 8

Sometimes the answer has to be no. You want the regret to land BEFORE the refusal, so it never sounds careless. Which opening does that better?

A. "We can't come, sorry."
B. "I'm so sorry — I don't think we're going to be able to make it, much as we'd love to."
B — "I'm so sorry — I don't think we're going to be able to make it, much as we'd love to."
The regret comes first and the "no" is cushioned on both sides. "Make it" softens "attend"; "much as we'd love to" proves the wish is real. A drops the bad news like a stone.
Phrase 9

You need to give a genuine-sounding reason for the clash — something already fixed, so it's clearly nobody's fault. There's a wry little opener meaning "unluckily, as it happens." Complete it: "As ___ would have it, we're already away that weekend."

"As luck would have it, we're already away that weekend."
"As luck would have it" frames the clash as bad timing, not a snub — a gentle shrug at fate. Follow with a real reason ("we're already committed to…") so it never sounds like an excuse you invented on the spot.
Phrase 10

You've said no — but you refuse to let the warmth drain out of the call. How do you sign off so they know you'll be with them in spirit, even from a distance?

A. "Anyway, have a good one."
B. "We'll be raising a glass to you from afar — and we want every single photo."
B — "We'll be raising a glass to you from afar — and we want every single photo."
"Raising a glass from afar" keeps you part of the celebration despite the miss; asking for photos shows you still care how the day goes. A is flat — a missed chance to end on love rather than logistics.
Quick test: run the whole call out loud twice. Once say YES — celebrate, accept, ask your four questions. Once say NO — regret first, a real reason, and a warm goodbye. When both flow, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

💔 The one you can't say yes to

One more round — no script, no easy way out. Your closest friend rings with the same joyful news… but you already know you can't go. Pick your reason and commit to it: it clashes with your own sister's wedding the very same day; or it's abroad and money is tight this year; or you've a work trip booked that can't move. Start the timer and give the hardest yes-turned-no there is: match their joy first, congratulate them properly, then let the regret land BEFORE the refusal, give a real and gentle reason, and find a way to stay part of the celebration from afar — a video call on the day, a gift, a promise to see the photos. One rule: they must come off the phone still feeling loved, not brushed aside.

The Echo 🕰️

In February 1840, a young queen married her cousin Albert — and did something quietly revolutionary. Instead of the royal silver and heavy colour of the day, Victoria walked down the aisle in a simple white gown. The image travelled the world, and within a generation "the white wedding" was what a wedding was supposed to look like.

Nearly two centuries on, you're on the phone sorting out a plus-one and a dress code for a wedding at a vineyard — and half the traditions you're navigating trace back, in part, to one determined woman and her wardrobe choice.

She set a fashion that outlived an empire. You just have to decide whether to wear the jacket.

Two bigger questions

  1. So many wedding "rules" — the white dress, the speeches, the seating plans — are barely a century or two old, yet they feel ancient and compulsory. Why do we cling so tightly to traditions we know were invented? Would you keep them or throw them out?
  2. Victoria married for love, publicly and unusually for a monarch of her time. Today a wedding invitation can also be a small test of a friendship — who's invited, who accepts, who bows out. What does the way we handle these invitations reveal about how much a relationship really means to us?
