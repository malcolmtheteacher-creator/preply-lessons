Oceania: Stereotype vs Reality

8 lessons on the cultures of Australia, New Zealand and the Pacific — each one opens with a stereotype and finds out what is really true.

Kangaroos, “G'day mate”, laid-back surfers and paradise beaches: Australia, New Zealand and the Pacific Islands attract plenty of stereotypes, and thousands of islands and some of the world's oldest living cultures are often reduced to a postcard. Start with the Background lesson, then work through the topics. Each lesson asks three questions — what does the stereotype say, what is the reality, and why does the idea exist? — and finishes with practical tips for working, travelling and making friends across cultures.

Read this first: who lives in Oceania, and how the many cultures connect.

BackgroundOceania: Oceans, Islands and the World's Oldest Living Cultures
Background
Oceania covers a vast area, mostly ocean, and includes rich and small countries, some of the oldest continuous cultures on Earth and some of the most linguistically diverse places anywhere. Australia is only part of the picture.
The stereotype: “Oceania is just Australia, with some beaches and a few islands nearby.”

🧭 Stereotype vs Reality7

Culture, cultural differences and the stories we tell about each other.

1Deadly Animals and Cuddly Koalas: The Real Wildlife Story
Wildlife & nature
Most of Australia's and New Zealand's wildlife is found nowhere else, and most people live in cities. Dangerous animals exist, but they rarely cause harm; the greater story is the threat that humans and introduced animals pose to the wildlife itself.
The stereotype: “Everything in Australia can kill you, and everyone lives surrounded by kangaroos.”

2“No Worries, Mate”: The Real Aussie and Kiwi English
Language & slang
Australian and New Zealand English are real varieties with their own vocabulary, abbreviations and humour. Some of the famous phrases are rarely used, and everyday speech is full of shortenings, understatement and words from Indigenous languages.
The stereotype: “Everyone in Australia says ‘G'day mate’ and puts shrimp on the barbie.”
Phrasal Verbs — Separable and InseparableOpen →
3More Than the Haka: Māori Culture Today
Indigenous culture
Māori make up around one in six New Zealanders. Their language is an official language and has been revived, their culture shapes national life, and the 1840 Treaty of Waitangi is still debated and applied today.
The stereotype: “Māori culture is just the haka and tattoos — something from the past.”
Past Perfect and Past Perfect ContinuousOpen →
4Laid-Back or Lazy? Work, Fairness and “She'll Be Right”
Attitudes & work
Working hours in both countries are close to those of other wealthy countries, and the relaxed style often hides serious effort. Informality, a belief in a “fair go” and a habit of understatement shape the culture.
The stereotype: “Australians and New Zealanders are lazy: they just surf, drink beer and take it easy.”
Adverbs of Degree and Understatement — Quite, Rather, Pretty, A Bit, Not BadOpen →
5Sport as a Way of Life: More Than Cricket and Surfing
Sport & identity
Sport is central to national identity, but the favourite games differ by place, class and background. Small Pacific countries produce an unusual share of the world's best rugby players, and sport has also been a stage for Indigenous pride and difficult debates.
The stereotype: “Everybody in Australia and New Zealand is a surfer who loves sport.”
Past Continuous and Past Simple — Telling a Story About a MatchOpen →
6Paradise Under Pressure: Life on a Pacific Island
Islands & climate
Pacific island nations are diverse, modern societies with their own languages, politics and economies. Many depend on fishing, farming and remittances, and some face a serious threat from rising seas and stronger storms.
The stereotype: “Pacific islands are tropical paradises where nothing happens and everyone lives the same simple life.”
Likelihood and Prediction — Likely To, Bound To, May Well, Could PotentiallyOpen →
7The Flat White and the Café: A Surprising Coffee Culture
Food, drink & migration
Australia and New Zealand have some of the world's most admired café cultures, shaped by post-war migrants from Italy and Greece. The flat white, claimed by both countries, is now served around the world.
The stereotype: “Australians and New Zealanders only drink beer and eat meat pies, and know nothing about coffee.”
