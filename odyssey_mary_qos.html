The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — France

Mary became queen in December 1542, six days old. At five she was sent to France. In 1558 she married the French heir, Francis, and in 1559 became queen of France. But Francis died in 1560, and in 1561 Mary, aged eighteen and a widow, returned to a Scotland that had become Protestant.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. When did she go to France?

2. Why did she return?

1. At five, in 1548.

2. Her husband Francis II had died.

Discuss:
  • How strange would Scotland feel to her?

Part 2 — Three men

In 1565 she married her cousin Lord Darnley. In 1566 Darnley and others murdered her secretary, David Rizzio, in front of her. In February 1567 Darnley's house at Kirk o' Field exploded, and he was found strangled in the garden. Three months later Mary married the Earl of Bothwell, the chief suspect. The nobles rose against her, and she was forced to abdicate.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was killed in front of Mary?

2. Why was the Bothwell marriage disastrous?

1. Her secretary David Rizzio.

2. He was the main suspect in Darnley's murder.

Discuss:
  • Why did Mary marry Bothwell? (Love, force or politics?)

Part 3 — Fotheringhay

In 1568 Mary fled to England, hoping her cousin Elizabeth I would help. Instead she was held for nineteen years. Catholic plots formed around her. In 1586 letters in the Babington Plot linked her to a plan to kill Elizabeth. She was tried and, on 8 February 1587, beheaded at Fotheringhay. Her motto was "In my end is my beginning" — and her son became king of England.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the Babington Plot?

2. How was her motto proved true?

1. A plan to murder Elizabeth, linked to Mary by letters.

2. Her son James became king of England.

Discuss:
  • Did Elizabeth have a choice?

Grammar — Past Perfect & Sequencing

History is a chain of causes. To show that one thing happened before another in the past, English uses the past perfect.

How it works:
• Past simple tells the main line: "The Danes attacked at Christmas."
• Past perfect (had + past participle) reaches further back: "By then, Alfred had already escaped into the marshes."
• Signal words: by the time, already, before, after, when, until.

1. Past simple or past perfect?

Complete each sentence about Mary, Queen of Scots. Use the word in brackets.

1. By 1561, Mary (lose) her husband.

2. Before Darnley died, Rizzio (be) murdered.

3. She married Bothwell after Darnley (die).

1. had lost

2. had been

3. had died

2. Put the earlier event in the past perfect

1. By 1587, she (spend) nineteen years in captivity.

2. When she fled, she (abdicate).

3. She was tried because the letters (be) found.

1. had spent

2. had abdicated

3. had been

Going deeper

Mary has been seen as a romantic victim, a reckless queen, and a Catholic martyr. Most historians see her as clever but unlucky, and often badly advised, in a world of ruthless men.

Worth noticing: Mary used the French spelling "Stuart", which became the usual spelling for her descendants.
Learning from the past — for today: Your associations decide your reputation. Mary lost Scotland not because of what she did, but because of whom she married. Choose your partners and allies as if your future depends on them — it might.
