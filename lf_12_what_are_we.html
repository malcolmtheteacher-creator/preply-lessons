What Are We?

Four months, two toothbrushes, and not one word about what any of it is. The plates have been cleared. The question is still sitting there.

The Scene 🎬

It's a Tuesday, it's the small Italian place near the station, and the waiter has just taken the plates away. You've been seeing Dan since March. Neither of you has ever said what that means, because there has never been a convenient moment, and because saying it out loud might break something that currently works rather well.

And yet. You've stopped replying to other people. You've caught yourself doing the sums — four months, and you still don't know whether you're his girlfriend, his boyfriend, his partner, his mate, or a name he goes a bit vague about at work. It isn't that you're unhappy. It's that you've started spending real energy on not asking, and that energy has to go somewhere.

So: the pudding menu is coming, and you have about four minutes. You don't want a ring. You don't even want a word. You want to know that there is one on its way — and, failing that, you want to know that too, tonight, rather than in October.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Is there a right moment to ask someone what you are to each other, or is the wait always just fear dressed up as good timing? How long is too long?
  2. Some people need the word — partner, girlfriend, boyfriend — and some think words like that flatten the whole thing. Which are you, honestly, and where did that come from?

Who's who

You
The One Who Asks 🍷

Not upset, not issuing an ultimatum, just tired of guessing. You'd like an honest answer more than you'd like a flattering one — and you'd rather find out over pudding than over another four months.

The other voice
Dan 🥄

Fond of you, genuinely. Also the sort of man who hopes things will quietly sort themselves out if left alone, and who has never once volunteered a sentence about feelings without being asked a direct question first.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how the asking is done, not what comes back — how the question is made small before it's asked, how one person puts their own cards down before asking to see anyone else's, and how the whole thing is kept under five minutes on purpose.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's clumsier than it looks on the page; let it be clumsy. Listen for how the question stays a question and never becomes a demand.

YouUm. Yeah. Can I ask you something slightly awkward?

DanRight. Mm. Where you are. Okay.
YouWhat would you call this, if someone asked you? Like, if someone at your work said "who's that, then" — what do you actually say?

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Before asking Dan anything at all about other people, the asker says something about themselves. Why do it in that order rather than just asking the question?

2. Dan never actually gives a label by the end of the meal. So what does he give — and why is it enough for the conversation to close well?

3. Twice, the asker deliberately makes the question smaller than it could have been. Find both moments. What does shrinking the ask buy them?

1. They own up to being exclusive first, which means the question that follows is an exchange rather than an inspection — Dan is being invited to match something, not audited. It also removes the trap: whatever he answers, they've already been honest, so nobody is bargaining with information.
2. He gives a timescale, and an admission that he'd feel rubbish calling them "a friend". That's enough because the asker never asked for the word tonight — they asked to know one was on the way, and a week with a real reason attached answers exactly that.
3. First when they say they're not trying to pin him down, only to know where they stand; second when they say they don't need a label today. Shrinking it means Dan can answer without feeling cornered, so what comes back is likelier to be true rather than whatever gets him out of the restaurant.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for asking someone where you stand without turning it into a summit meeting. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Every one of these can be ruined by the voice it's said in. Said quickly and quietly, they sound like you're apologising for asking; said slowly with a hard edge, they sound like a solicitor's letter. The tune you want is the one you'd use to ask what time the train goes — level, unhurried, faintly amused at yourself. If your voice stays calm, the question stays a question.

🫣 Opening the awkward door

Phrase 1

You need to signal that something uncomfortable is coming, without making him think somebody has died or that he's about to be dumped over the bread basket. Which opener does the least damage?

A. "We need to talk about us."
B. "Can I ask you something slightly awkward?"
C. "Don't take this the wrong way, but I've got a few questions."
B — "Can I ask you something slightly awkward?"
A is the sentence people fear most in English, because it almost always comes before bad news. C is worse than it looks: "don't take this the wrong way" tells him there is a wrong way, and "a few questions" sounds like an interview. B works because "slightly" shrinks the thing before he can inflate it, and because asking permission hands him a small piece of control at exactly the moment he'd otherwise feel he had none.
Phrase 2

Now put the four months on the table — but casually, as though you're half-guessing rather than reciting a figure you've been carrying around since April. Put the pieces in order:

doing this —four months now?We've beenwhat,
"We've been doing this — what, four months now?"
The little "— what," in the middle is doing nearly all the work: it pretends you're counting on the spot, when of course you know exactly. "Doing this" is deliberately vague, because naming it is the very thing you're asking him to do. And ending as a question invites him to agree with something harmless before anything harder arrives.

🧭 Taking the pressure off before he panics

Phrase 3

He's tensed up. Tell him this isn't an ultimatum — you're not trying to trap him into a promise tonight, you only want to know your own position. Complete it: "I'm not trying to ___ ___ ___, I just want to know where I am."

"I'm not trying to pin you down, I just want to know where I am."
To pin something down is to hold it still so it can't move — which is precisely the fear you're heading off, so naming it out loud takes most of the air out of it. Then notice the second half: "where I am", not "where we are". You've made the question about your own position, which is something he can answer without signing anything.
Phrase 4

He's apologising for how uncomfortable this all is. Justify the discomfort — say why you'd choose it. Which line explains your reasoning rather than just excusing yourself?

A. "Sorry, I know this is a horrible thing to bring up over dinner."
B. "I've been meaning to ask you this for ages, actually."
C. "I'd rather have an awkward five minutes than wonder for another month."
C — "I'd rather have an awkward five minutes than wonder for another month."
A apologises, which quietly agrees that you were wrong to ask; B admits you've been sitting on it for ages, which makes the whole thing sound heavier than five minutes. C sets the two costs side by side — five awkward minutes against a month of wondering — so that asking looks like the sensible choice rather than a demand. It also puts a ceiling on the conversation: five minutes, not the rest of the evening.

🔍 Asking the actual question

Phrase 5

Here's the clever one. Instead of asking him what he feels — which he'll dodge — ask him what word he uses when you're not in the room. Put the pieces in order:

if someoneyou call this,What wouldasked you?
"What would you call this, if someone asked you?"
This works because it's not really about the someone. It moves the question from his feelings, which he can honestly claim not to know, to his vocabulary, which he uses every day — and whatever word he reaches for tells you more than an hour of talking about feelings would. It's also softer, because he's answering as if on someone else's behalf.
Phrase 6

Now the direct one. Ask about other people — and make plain, in two words, that you'll accept whichever answer is true. Complete it: "Are you seeing other people? It's fine ___ ___, I'd just rather know."

"Are you seeing other people? It's fine either way, I'd just rather know."
"Either way" means whichever of the two answers turns out to be the real one — so you've said, in two words, that you're not fishing for a particular reply. That's what makes the honest answer possible: if he thinks only one answer is allowed, you'll get that one whether it's true or not, and you'll have learned nothing.

🃏 Putting your own cards on the table

Phrase 7

Before you take anything from him, give something. Say two things in one sentence: that there's nobody else for you, and that staying that way hasn't cost you any effort — you didn't grit your teeth through it. Don't say "I love you" and don't ask him for anything back. What do you say?

"I'm not seeing anyone else, and I've not wanted to."
The first half is a fact; the second half is the part that matters, because "I've not wanted to" says the exclusivity was never a sacrifice you're now presenting him with a bill for. Two flat statements, no request attached — which is exactly why he's likely to answer honestly instead of defensively.

🎯 Sizing the ask so it can be answered

Phrase 8

This is the sentence the whole conversation exists for. You want to name what you actually need — which is less than he's braced for. Which one is the real ask?

A. "So are we together or not? Simple question."
B. "I don't need a label today, but I'd like to know one's coming."
C. "Honestly, I'm fine with whatever you want to call it."
B — "I don't need a label today, but I'd like to know one's coming."
A forces a yes or no tonight, and a cornered person's yes is worth very little. C gives away the whole thing and leaves you exactly where you started, still wondering. B splits the difference: you drop the demand for the word, and keep the demand for a direction. He can meet that without panicking, and you still get an answer worth having.
Phrase 9

Give him room — but put a soft edge on it, so "later" doesn't quietly become "never". Put the pieces in order:

too long, yeah?You don't have tobut don't leave itanswer right now —
"You don't have to answer right now — but don't leave it too long, yeah?"
The two halves pull against each other on purpose: the first is generous, the second stops the generosity being taken as permission to say nothing for another four months. And "yeah?" at the end turns the instruction into a request for agreement, which is why it lands as kindness rather than a deadline.

🍮 Landing it and letting him breathe

Phrase 10

He's answered. Now close it — fast, without a speech, and get the evening back. Say the shortest possible version of "that was enough for me", then change the subject entirely. Complete it: "Right. Good. That's all I ___ — pudding?"

"Right. Good. That's all I needed — pudding?"
"That's all I needed" is the whole conversation, filed and shut, in four words. The real skill is what follows it: jumping straight to pudding proves you meant the five minutes, so he learns that being honest with you costs him one short conversation rather than a whole evening. People tell the truth far more readily to someone who doesn't make them pay for it.

🔑 Make them yours

These aren't only for restaurants and romance. Anywhere someone is being vague and you've stopped being able to plan around it, the same three moves work: shrink the ask, put your own cards down first, then close it quickly. Pin three of them to your own life so you'll actually reach for them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I'm not trying to pin you down, I just want to know where I am." — where in your life are you currently guessing, and about what?

"I'd rather have an awkward five minutes than wonder for another month." — one question you've been putting off. What's the wondering actually costing you?

"I don't need a label today, but I'd like to know one's coming." — the smaller version of something you want. What's the least you'd genuinely settle for?

Your Turn 🎭

Now the plates are yours. Speak every answer out loud FIRST, then type it. Use the timer — across a table you get about two seconds, not two minutes, and the pause is part of the message.

The Twist

😬 He answers straight away

Run it once more, timer on — and this time Dan doesn't ask for a week. He puts his spoon down and says, kindly and without a trace of cruelty: "I'm not seeing anyone else and I don't want to. But I don't want a label, and I'm not going to want one in a month either. I like this exactly as it is. Is that a problem?" No stalling, no villain, nothing to push against — he's told you the truth on the first ask, and it isn't the truth you'd rehearsed. Your neat little plan needed him to be vague; he wasn't. So now you have to say something honest out loud, at the table, in the next ten seconds: is that enough for you, or isn't it? Not "let me think about it" — he gave you a straight answer and he's owed one back. One rule: you don't get to say "it's fine" unless it is.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I asked the real question out loud — I didn't hint and hope he'd guess it.
  • I said where I stood before I asked where he stood.
  • I made it clear both answers were allowed, and I meant it.
  • I asked for the smallest thing that would genuinely do — not the biggest.
  • I closed it quickly and let the evening carry on, instead of squeezing it for more.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist again and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

The strange thing about the four months is how much work goes into not asking. You rehearse it in the shower. You draft it and delete it. You decide the moment isn't right, which is true, because no moment ever is.

And what you're protecting, mostly, isn't the relationship — it's the version of it you've built in your head, which stays perfectly intact as long as nobody checks. Asking risks a smaller, truer answer than the one you've been imagining.

Which is the trade, in the end. A few awkward minutes buys you something real to stand on, whatever shape it turns out to be. The alternative isn't safety — it's just a longer wait for the same conversation, with more invested in it by then.

Two bigger questions

  1. Does naming a relationship change it, or only describe it? Think of a couple you know who never used the word, and one who used it early — did the word do anything at all?
  2. People say "I'd rather know" far more often than they behave as though it's true. When has not knowing genuinely been better for you — and, looking back, was it better, or just easier?
