Unwritten Social Rules We All Follow

C1-C2 Level | 50 minutes | 5 Key Expressions | SERIES 2 FINALE

The Rules Nobody Taught You

Research shows we follow hundreds of unwritten social rules every day—and we don't even realize it

You're in an elevator. Someone gets in. Without thinking, you both:

  • Face the door (never face the back wall)
  • Stand as far apart as possible
  • Look at the floor numbers, your phone, anywhere but each other
  • Stay silent unless you know each other

Who taught you these rules? Nobody. You just know. And if someone breaks them—stands too close, faces the wrong way, stares at you—it feels deeply wrong, even though logically, they're not doing anything harmful.

Sociologist Erving Goffman called these "interaction rituals"—the invisible choreography that makes social life possible. We learn them through observation and experience, and we enforce them through discomfort, embarrassment, and social pressure.

Examples of unwritten rules that exist in most cultures:

In queues/lines:

  • Wait your turn (no cutting)
  • Maintain personal space
  • Don't leave gaps someone could misinterpret as a spot to enter
  • If someone lets you cut ahead, profusely thank them

In public spaces:

  • Don't make prolonged eye contact with strangers
  • Headphones = "don't talk to me" signal
  • On public transport, don't sit directly next to someone if other seats are available
  • Hold doors for people close behind you

In conversation:

  • Take turns speaking (interrupting is rude)
  • Make occasional eye contact but don't stare
  • Match the other person's energy level somewhat
  • Laugh at jokes even if they're not funny (to be polite)
  • Ask "How are you?" but don't expect a real answer

Research by psychologist Robert Cialdini shows that we follow social norms even when they don't make logical sense, simply because everyone else does. In one famous experiment, people were more likely to litter in a dirty environment than a clean one—the norm of "everyone litters here" was more powerful than personal values about cleanliness.

Cultural variations are fascinating. In Japan, slurping soup is polite (shows appreciation); in Western countries, it's rude. In Finland, silence in conversation is comfortable; in Brazil, it's awkward. In some Middle Eastern cultures, arriving exactly on time is considered rude; in Germany, it's expected.

What happens when someone breaks the rules? Sociologists call it "norm violation"—and it makes us incredibly uncomfortable. When researchers deliberately broke elevator norms (facing the wrong way, standing too close), other passengers became anxious, left early, or tried to restore order.

Anthropologist Kate Fox notes that most social rules exist to manage the tension between our need for connection and our fear of intimacy. We want to be around people, but not too close. We want to interact, but not too deeply. Social rules give us a safe middle ground.

Think About It:

  • Have you ever felt uncomfortable because someone broke an unwritten rule? What did they do?
  • Are there social rules in your culture that foreigners often break accidentally?
  • Have you ever broken a social rule on purpose? What happened?
  • Why do we follow rules that nobody explicitly teaches us?

Talking About Social Norms

Click each expression to see how it's used

1. "Unwritten rule"
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: A social expectation or norm that everyone follows but isn't officially stated or recorded. People just "know" these rules through social learning.
Example:
"There's an unwritten rule in our office that you don't heat fish in the microwave. Nobody told me explicitly, but I learned fast when I saw people's reactions!"
NOW YOU TRY:
Think of an unwritten rule in your workplace, school, or culture. Describe it: "There's an unwritten rule that..."
2. "It's just not done"
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: Something is socially unacceptable even though there's no explicit rule against it. Used to explain why certain behaviors are avoided without needing to explain why.
Example:
"In Britain, you could push to the front of a queue, but it's just not done. People would be horrified, even though there's no law against it."
NOW YOU TRY:
Think of something that's technically allowed but socially unacceptable. Say: "You could [do X], but it's just not done because..."
3. "Common courtesy"
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: Basic polite behavior that is expected in social situations. Small acts of kindness or respect that "everyone should know."
Example:
"Holding the door for someone behind you is just common courtesy. It takes zero effort and makes life slightly more pleasant for everyone."
NOW YOU TRY:
Describe a behavior you consider common courtesy. When do people fail to show it? How do you feel about that?

Breaking & Following Rules

4. "Social faux pas"
Formality: Neutral/Formal
Meaning: An embarrassing social mistake; doing something that violates social etiquette or norms. From French, literally "false step." Pronounced "foh-pah."
Example:
"I made a huge social faux pas at the dinner party—I started eating before the host. Everyone just stared at me. I didn't know that was a rule!"
NOW YOU TRY:
Have you ever committed a social faux pas? What happened? How did you realize you'd made a mistake?
5. "Go against the grain"
Formality: Informal/Neutral
Meaning: To do something different from what most people do; to act contrary to social norms or expectations. Originally from woodworking—cutting against the grain is harder.
Example:
"She really goes against the grain in our culture—she decided not to get married or have kids, and people can't understand it. But she's happy, so who cares?"
NOW YOU TRY:
Describe a time when you or someone you know went against the grain. What social expectation did they challenge?

All 5 Expressions:

  • "Unwritten rule" — social expectation everyone knows
  • "It's just not done" — socially unacceptable behavior
  • "Common courtesy" — basic polite behavior expected
  • "Social faux pas" — embarrassing social mistake
  • "Go against the grain" — challenge social norms
