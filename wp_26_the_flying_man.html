The Flying Man

In which a Persian physician imagines a man floating in darkness, sensing nothing—and proves the soul exists

A Number to Hold in Your Mind

600 years. That is how long after Avicenna's death his medical textbook, the Canon of Medicine, remained the standard in European universities. A Persian physician, writing in Arabic, in the 11th century, would shape how doctors in Renaissance Europe and beyond understood the human body. This is one measure of his brilliance. But his real contribution to philosophy goes deeper: he proved, through a single thought experiment, that the soul is not a myth or a metaphor. It is a reality that knows itself.

This lesson is about the moment a great mind realized that consciousness itself was the most undeniable fact of existence. It anticipates Descartes’ famous declaration by six centuries—and goes even further.

II. The Story—A Genius in Three Careers

Ibn Sina was born in Bukhara (modern Uzbekistan) in 980 AD, at the height of the Islamic Golden Age. While Europe slept through the Dark Ages, the Islamic world was translating, preserving, and advancing the entire intellectual heritage of Greece, Persia, and India. Avicenna (his Latin name) would become the greatest synthesizer of this age.

The Man

His father noticed the boy’s extraordinary abilities and made him study with the best teachers in Bukhara. By age 16, Avicenna had mastered law, theology, and philosophy. By 18, he had turned to medicine and, in a remarkable claim, healed the sick emir (local ruler)—and was given access to the royal library as payment. He used it ruthlessly, working through the nights, absorbing everything. “I read the book of the soul ten times,” he would later say, referring to Aristotle’s psychology, “until I understood it and its difficulties.”

His life was turbulent. He fled persecution, worked as a court physician, was imprisoned, wrote continuously through it all. By his death in 1037, he had authored over 450 works on medicine, theology, logic, metaphysics, and natural philosophy. His two masterpieces were the Canon of Medicine and the Metaphysics section of his magnum opus, the Healing (al-Shifa).

The Thought Experiment: The Flying Man

Imagine a man created in perfect form, floating in empty air, unable to see, hear, smell, taste, or feel anything. His limbs do not touch each other; there is no sense of his body at all. Would he know that he exists?

The human being, if in the condition we have described, would affirm the existence of his essence, though he would not affirm the existence of any of his limbs or organs... His essence is thus known absolutely. — Avicenna, Metaphysics of the Healing

Avicenna’s answer is unequivocal: yes. Even with no sensory experience whatsoever, the flying man would know that something is aware, thinking, existing. This knowledge is not derived from the senses. It is immediate, undeniable, present to consciousness itself.

Why does this matter? Medieval thinkers debated whether the soul could be known directly or only through its effects on the body. Avicenna proved it could be known directly—to consciousness itself. This makes the soul (the thinking self) more certain than any physical object, because physical objects are known through the senses (which can deceive), but the self is known to itself immediately. He had found the ground of certainty in being itself.

The Three Doctrines

Avicenna built his system on three pillars:

1. The Flying Man Proves the Soul

Consciousness knows itself directly. This is more certain than the body, which is known through the senses.

2. The Distinction of Essence and Existence

God is the only being whose essence includes existence. Everything else has existence but doesn’t essentially have to exist. God is necessary; everything else is contingent. This became the foundation of medieval theology.

3. The Emanation of Intelligences

Reality overflows from God through a chain of intelligences (pure spirits), each of which knows God and emanates the next. This synthesizes Neoplatonism with Islamic theology.

♦ ♦ ♦

What Makes Avicenna Crucial

Avicenna stands at the meeting point of three worlds: Greek philosophy (Plato and Aristotle, which he knew deeply), Islamic theology, and the emerging medieval Christian thought of the West. He proved that reason and revelation could be integrated without contradiction. He systematized metaphysics in a way that would dominate Islamic, Jewish, and Christian theology for centuries. And he gave medicine a rational, empirical foundation that anticipated modern science by 800 years.

But for our course, his greatest gift is this: the Flying Man proves that the self—consciousness itself—is the most undeniable reality. You cannot doubt that you exist, even if you doubt everything else.

III. The Ideas—What Avicenna Really Proved

The Flying Man is more than a curiosity. It solves a fundamental problem and opens a new one. Let’s trace the three great ideas.

Idea 1: The Soul is Self-Evident

Consciousness knows itself immediately, prior to any bodily sensation.

This is revolutionary. The ancient materialists said the soul (mind) was just the body or its product. Avicenna said no—the soul is known to itself directly. Close your eyes, stop sensing, remove all external input, and ask: does the I still exist? Obviously yes. The self is not a conclusion from premises; it is a fact given directly to consciousness. This makes consciousness the foundation of all knowledge.

Descartes would echo this 600 years later: I think, therefore I am. But Avicenna had already said it.

Idea 2: The Essence-Existence Distinction

To have an essence (what something is) is different from existing (that something is).

A human essence (rationality, mortality, embodiment) does not necessarily exist. I could have never been born. But God’s essence is identical with existence—God cannot not exist. This distinction became crucial for all later medieval metaphysics. It explains why the universe needs God: without God, nothing has to exist.

This leads to a question: if I know my own existence, what grounds that existence? Answer: something or someone whose very nature is to exist. That is God.

Idea 3: The Self is Real but Dependent

You are a substance, and you know you exist. But you did not create yourself. Therefore, you are created.

The Flying Man proves the self is real. But the self cannot explain its own existence (that is the essence-existence problem again: the self’s essence does not guarantee its existence). So the self points to God. You are a being who knows that it is, but not a being who had to be. You are contingent on something necessary.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Question Avicenna Left

Avicenna proves the soul is real and self-aware. But what does the soul need? Avicenna’s answer is intellectual perfection: the highest good is to know God through rational contemplation. This is the happiness of the wise. But a deeper question waits in the shadows: Can the soul be perfected by knowledge alone? What if the soul is broken—wounded by guilt, shame, disconnection? Can reason heal that?

Think about this: Avicenna proves you know you exist. But do you know why you exist? What your existence is for?

This is the Question of Purpose and Healing. The soul knows itself, yes. But the soul also knows shame, guilt, fear of death, sense of exile from the real. Can knowledge alone heal these? Or does the soul need something else—forgiveness, reconciliation, love? The great philosophers will disagree. But watch for where the answer appears.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

The Flying Man’s Knowledge

Avicenna claims the flying man would know he exists even with no sensation. Do you agree? Try it: close your eyes and remove all sensation from your awareness. Is the knowledge of existing still there?

Six Hundred Years Before Descartes

Descartes is famous for “I think, therefore I am.” But Avicenna said almost the same thing, six centuries earlier. Why do we remember Descartes and forget Avicenna?

The Essence-Existence Split

Avicenna says your essence (what you are) is different from your existence (that you are). Your essence doesn’t guarantee your existence—you could have never been born. Does this separation make sense?

Knowledge and Happiness

Avicenna believed the highest happiness is intellectual: knowing God through reason. Do you agree? Can knowledge alone make a person truly happy? What else might the soul need?

The Golden Age

Avicenna lived during the Islamic Golden Age when Arab scholars were translating and preserving the entire Greek tradition while Europe slept. What changed? Why did this age end?

Medicine and Philosophy

Avicenna was both a brilliant physician and a profound metaphysician. Can the same person excel at both? What does medicine teach philosophy, and vice versa?

The Soul’s Wound

The lesson hints that knowledge alone may not heal the soul’s deepest wounds. What wounds do you think the soul carries? Can philosophy heal them?
