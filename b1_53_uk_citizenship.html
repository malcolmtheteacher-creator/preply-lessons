What Do You Need?

To apply for British citizenship through naturalisation, you must meet several requirements. First, you need to have lived in the UK legally for at least five years. During that time, you must not have been outside the UK for more than 450 days in total, and no more than 90 days in the final year before your application.

You must also hold Indefinite Leave to Remain (ILR) — sometimes called "settled status" — for at least one year before applying. This is permanent permission to live in the UK, and it comes before citizenship.

You need to demonstrate your English language ability at B1 level or above. This can be done through an approved Secure English Language Test (SELT), a degree taught in English, or by being a national of a majority English-speaking country.

You must pass the Life in the UK Test, a computer-based test covering British history, values, government, and society. The test costs £50 and is taken at an official test centre. You need to score at least 75% — that means 18 out of 24 questions correct.

Finally, you must be of good character — no serious criminal record — and intend to continue living in the UK.

Check Your Understanding

1. How long do you normally need to live in the UK before applying?

At least five years.

2. What is ILR, and do you need it before applying for citizenship?

ILR stands for Indefinite Leave to Remain — permanent permission to live in the UK. Yes, you need to have held ILR for at least one year before applying for citizenship.

3. What English level do you need, and can you show it without taking a test?

You need B1 level or above. Yes — if you have a degree taught in English, or you are from a majority English-speaking country, you may not need to take a separate test.

4. What happens if you fail the Life in the UK Test?

You can retake it — there is no limit on the number of attempts. However, you must pay the £50 fee each time.

5. What does "good character" mean in the context of this application?

It means you do not have a serious criminal record and you have generally respected the laws of the UK.

The Citizenship Ceremony

If your application is approved, you are invited to a citizenship ceremony at your local council. At the ceremony, you make an oath of allegiance (a religious promise) or an affirmation (a non-religious promise) to the King and to the UK. You then receive your certificate of naturalisation and become a British citizen.

Life in the UK — Practice Questions

These are the kinds of questions you will find in the real test. The official handbook covers British history, values, government, and everyday life. Try each question before revealing the answer.

Real Test Format 24 questions  |  45 minutes  |  Pass mark: 18/24 (75%)  |  Cost: £50

Values & Principles

1. Which of the following is a fundamental value of the UK?

A) Compulsory military service    B) The rule of law    C) A state religion for all citizens    D) Single-party government

B) The rule of law. The UK's fundamental values are: democracy, the rule of law, individual liberty, and mutual respect and tolerance for those with different faiths and beliefs.

2. What is the Magna Carta?

A) The UK national anthem    B) An agreement that limited the power of the king, signed in 1215    C) A list of British kings and queens    D) The first British constitution

B) An agreement that limited the power of the king, signed in 1215. The Magna Carta established that the king was not above the law — it is one of the most important documents in British history.

History

3. In what year did the Second World War end?

A) 1943    B) 1944    C) 1945    D) 1946

C) 1945. The Second World War ended in 1945 — in Europe in May (VE Day) and in the Pacific in August (VJ Day).

4. Which famous playwright wrote Romeo and Juliet?

A) Charles Dickens    B) Geoffrey Chaucer    C) William Shakespeare    D) John Milton

C) William Shakespeare. Shakespeare (1564–1616) is considered the greatest writer in the English language. He wrote 37 plays and 154 sonnets.

5. What was the name of the movement that fought for women's right to vote in the early 20th century?

A) The Chartists    B) The Suffragettes    C) The Trade Unionists    D) The Levellers

B) The Suffragettes. Women campaigned for the right to vote in the early 1900s. Women over 30 first gained the vote in 1918, and full equal voting rights came in 1928.

6. Which British monarch reigned for the longest time in history?

A) Queen Victoria    B) King George III    C) Queen Elizabeth II    D) King Henry VIII

C) Queen Elizabeth II. Queen Elizabeth II reigned from 1952 to 2022 — 70 years — making her the longest-reigning British monarch. She was succeeded by King Charles III.

Government & Law

7. What is the name of the building where the UK Parliament meets?

A) Buckingham Palace    B) 10 Downing Street    C) The Palace of Westminster    D) The Tower of London

C) The Palace of Westminster. This is also known as the Houses of Parliament. It contains the House of Commons (elected MPs) and the House of Lords (appointed members).

8. How often must a general election be held in the UK?

A) Every 3 years    B) Every 4 years    C) Every 5 years    D) Every 6 years

C) Every 5 years. Under the Fixed-term Parliaments Act (now revised), elections must be held at least every five years. The Prime Minister can call an earlier election with parliamentary approval.

9. Which court is the highest court in the UK?

A) The Crown Court    B) The High Court    C) The Court of Appeal    D) The Supreme Court

D) The Supreme Court. The UK Supreme Court was established in 2009 and is the final court of appeal for civil cases in the whole UK and criminal cases in England, Wales, and Northern Ireland.

10. What is the role of the House of Lords?

A) It proposes and votes on all new laws    B) It reviews and can suggest changes to laws passed by the House of Commons    C) It chooses the Prime Minister    D) It controls the national budget

B) It reviews and can suggest changes to laws passed by the House of Commons. The House of Lords can delay laws but cannot permanently block them. Members are not elected — they are appointed or sit by hereditary right.

Society & Culture

11. What are the capital cities of the four countries that make up the UK?

London (England), Edinburgh (Scotland), Cardiff (Wales), Belfast (Northern Ireland).

12. What is the national flower of England?

A) The daffodil    B) The thistle    C) The shamrock    D) The rose

D) The rose. Each of the four UK nations has its own flower: England = rose, Scotland = thistle, Wales = daffodil (or leek), Northern Ireland = shamrock.

13. Which saint's day is celebrated on 23 April in England?

A) St Andrew    B) St David    C) St George    D) St Patrick

C) St George. St George is the patron saint of England (23 April). St Andrew is Scotland's patron saint (30 November). St David is Wales's (1 March). St Patrick is Northern Ireland's (17 March).

14. What is the Church of England?

A) A Catholic church founded by Henry VIII    B) The Protestant established church in England, with the monarch as its head    C) A non-conformist church with no connection to the state    D) A church founded by the Puritans in the 17th century

B) The Protestant established church in England, with the monarch as its head. Henry VIII broke away from the Roman Catholic Church in the 1530s, creating the Church of England. The Archbishop of Canterbury is its spiritual leader.

15. What does the NHS stand for, and what is it?

National Health Service. The NHS was created in 1948 and provides free healthcare to everyone in the UK, funded by taxes. It is one of the largest employers in the world.

16. Which of these is NOT a responsibility of a citizen of the UK?

A) Obeying and respecting the law    B) Treating others with respect    C) Voting in every election by law    D) Supporting and looking after your family

C) Voting in every election by law. Voting is a right and is encouraged, but it is NOT a legal obligation in the UK. Citizens are expected to obey the law, pay taxes, treat others with respect, and look after their family.

Everyday Life

17. At what age can people vote in a UK general election?

A) 16    B) 17    C) 18    D) 21

C) 18. You must be 18 or over to vote in a UK general election. Note: in Scotland and Wales, 16-year-olds can vote in devolved elections.

18. What is a magistrate?

A) A type of police officer    B) A volunteer judge who deals with less serious criminal cases    C) A Member of Parliament    D) An official who processes citizenship applications

B) A volunteer judge who deals with less serious criminal cases. Magistrates (also called Justices of the Peace) are unpaid members of the public trained to hear cases in Magistrates' Courts — the first level of the criminal court system.

19. What is the name of the official document that records UK births, marriages, and deaths?

A) The Electoral Register    B) The Census    C) The Register Office    D) The Civil Registry

C) The Register Office. Births, marriages, and deaths are officially recorded at a Register Office (also called a Registry Office). Marriages can also take place there.

20. How is the UK's National Insurance (NI) number used?

A) It is your passport number    B) It is a unique number that records your tax and National Insurance contributions throughout your working life    C) It is needed to open a bank account    D) It is used to access the NHS

B) It is a unique number that records your tax and National Insurance contributions throughout your working life. You need an NI number to work legally in the UK and to claim benefits or a state pension.

21. Which of the following is a devolved matter in Scotland?

A) Defence    B) Immigration    C) Education    D) Foreign policy

C) Education. Education is a devolved power, meaning Scotland's Parliament makes its own decisions. Defence, immigration, and foreign policy are reserved matters controlled by the UK Parliament.

22. What is the Commonwealth?

A) A trading agreement between European countries    B) A group of 54 countries, most with historical links to the British Empire, working together on shared goals    C) The shared government of England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland    D) An organisation created after the Second World War for global peace

B) A group of 54 countries, most with historical links to the British Empire, working together on shared goals. The Commonwealth promotes democracy, human rights, and development. The UK monarch is the Head of the Commonwealth.

23. What sport was first played at Wimbledon?

A) Cricket    B) Lawn tennis    C) Croquet    D) Polo

B) Lawn tennis. The first Wimbledon Championship was held in 1877. The tournament is held every summer and is the world's oldest and most prestigious tennis tournament.

24. Which of the following statements about the UK is correct?

A) The UK has a written constitution in a single document    B) The UK is a constitutional monarchy where the monarch has no political role    C) The UK is a federal state where each nation has equal powers    D) The UK's head of government is the monarch

B) The UK is a constitutional monarchy where the monarch has no political role. The King is the head of state but does not make political decisions. The UK does NOT have a single written constitution — it relies on laws, conventions, and court decisions.
