The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — The boy in the library

Abraham Maslow was born in New York in 1908. His parents were immigrants, and the family was poor. He was the oldest of seven children.

Maslow was not a happy child. He was lonely, and life at home was difficult. He spent his free time in public libraries, because he loved books.

Later he studied psychology at university. At that time, most psychologists studied problems: fear, illness, sad people. Maslow wanted to ask a different question. What do healthy people need to grow?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Where was Maslow born, and when?

2. Where did he spend his free time?

3. What question did he want to ask?

1. In New York, in 1908.

2. In public libraries.

3. What do healthy people need to grow?

Discuss:
  • Why do you think a lonely child asked this question?

Part 2 — Five levels

In 1943 Maslow wrote a famous paper. In it, he described five groups of needs.

First, the body: food, water, sleep. Second, safety: a home, money, no danger. Third, love: family, friends, a group where you belong. Fourth, respect: from other people, and for yourself. And fifth, at the top: becoming the best person you can be.

His main idea was simple. When a need is full, it stops pulling you. A hungry person thinks about food. A person with food starts to think about safety, then about friends.

But Maslow also said something people often forget: the levels are not a strict staircase. People need many things at the same time.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What are the five groups, from the bottom?

2. What happens when a need is full?

3. What did Maslow say about the order?

1. Body, safety, love, respect, and becoming your best.

2. It stops pulling you; you start to think about the next one.

3. It is not a strict staircase — people need many things at the same time.

Discuss:
  • Can a person with very little money still be happy? Why?

Part 3 — Somebody else's pyramid

Here is the surprise. Maslow never drew a pyramid. Not in 1943, not later.

The pyramid came in 1960, from a different man: Charles McDermid, a psychologist who worked with companies. He put Maslow's five levels in a triangle, in an article for managers about money and work.

Managers loved the picture. It was easy to draw and easy to remember, and soon it was in books all over the world. But the triangle also changed the idea. It looks like a staircase: finish level one, then go up. That is exactly what Maslow did not say.

Today the pyramid is everywhere — and it belongs to a man most people have never heard of.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Did Maslow draw the pyramid?

2. Who drew it, and when?

3. What problem does the triangle picture cause?

1. No, never.

2. Charles McDermid, in 1960.

3. It looks like a staircase, but Maslow said the levels are not a strict order.

Discuss:
  • Is a simple picture better, even if it is not completely correct?

How the tool works

Five levels. The most basic needs are at the bottom. Remember: it is a guide, not a staircase.

Using it, step by step

  1. Think about one person: you, a friend, or somebody new in your country.
  2. Start at the bottom. Ask: is this need full?
  3. Find the lowest level with a problem. Problems at the top are hard to fix when the bottom is empty.
  4. Do one thing for that level this week.

Try it yourself

Which level is it? Choose, then check.

1. A safe flat and a job with a contract.
2. Winning a prize at work, in front of your team.
3. Eight hours of sleep and clean water.
4. Learning the guitar at sixty, only because you want to.
5. Friends you can phone at midnight.
6. A job title that other people admire.

1. Safety — you are protected; nothing is dangerous.

2. Respect — other people show they think you are good.

3. Food, water, sleep — the body.

4. Growth — becoming the best person you can be.

5. Love & friends — people who are close to you.

6. Respect — it is about the opinion of others.

Where the tool breaks

It is not a staircase. People need friends even when life is hard.

Different cultures, different order. For many families, the group comes before the person.

It is difficult to prove. Scientists are not sure the order is always the same.

Talk it through:
  • Would you change the order for your life?
  • Which level is the hardest in a new country?

Grammar — need and want

This lesson is about what people need and what they want. Both words are useful every day — and they have easy patterns.

need / want + noun
“I need a new phone.”  “She wants a coffee.”
need / want + to + verb
“I need to sleep.”  “They want to move to Spain.”
need / want + person + to + verb
“My boss wants me to work on Saturday.”  “I need you to help me.”
Negative and question: “I don't need a car.”  “Do you want to come?”
Polite: in a shop or a restaurant, I'd like is softer than I want. “I'd like a coffee, please.”
