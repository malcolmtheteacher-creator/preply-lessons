Listen in on this conversation

Customer: Hi, I'm here about my laptop.

Assistant: Good news — it's been fixed. The screen had to be replaced, but everything will be done by five o'clock.

Look again at what the assistant says. They never mention who fixed the laptop or who replaced the screen. Why not, do you think? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
Because it doesn't matter who did it — the customer only cares about the laptop. English uses the passive to put the important thing first and leave out the "who". That's why the passive is everywhere in everyday conversation: news, updates, promises, rules.
Today: three passive forms — has been done, will be done, and must / can / might be done — so you can give news, explain plans, and talk about rules in everyday conversation.

Giving News: "It's been fixed"

For news and updates — something happened, and the result matters now.

Spot the pattern

Three pieces of everyday news:

  • "Guess what — my bag has been found!"
  • "The kitchen's been painted — come and see."
  • "Have you heard? The match has been called off."

Before you check: what three ingredients appear in every bold phrase?

Check the pattern CLICK
has / have + been + past participle (found, painted, called off).
Note: "the kitchen's been painted" = the kitchen has been painted. In speech we almost always shorten it.
has / have + been + past participle

From "someone did it" to "it's been done"

"Someone has taken my seat."
→
"My seat has been taken."

The thing you care about ("my seat") moves to the front. "Someone" disappears — you don't know or don't care who it was.

Plans & Promises: "It'll be done by Friday"

For the future — deliveries, repairs, arrangements. The classic promise you hear in every shop.

Spot the pattern

Three promises you might hear this week:

  • "The sofa will be delivered on Monday."
  • "Your photos will be printed by four o'clock."
  • "Don't worry — you'll be told as soon as we know."

What's the structure this time? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
will be + past participle (delivered, printed, told). Just one "be" — never "will been", never "will be being".
For plans, "going to" works the same way: "The heating is going to be replaced next month."
will be + past participle
is / are going to be + past participle

Rules & Maybes: "It must be done" / "It might be delayed"

For rules, instructions and possibilities — with must, can, should, might.

Spot the pattern

  • "This medicine must be kept in the fridge." (a rule)
  • "Don't panic — it can be fixed." (a possibility)
  • "The flight might be delayed — check before you leave." (a maybe)

Same question: what's the structure?

Check the pattern CLICK
modal (must / can / should / might / could) + be + past participle. The modal changes the meaning; the "be + participle" part never changes.
must / can / should / might / could + be + past participle

From instruction to rule

"You can wash this jacket in the machine."
→
"This jacket can be washed in the machine."

The rule is about the jacket, not about "you" — so the jacket goes first.

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: "going to" for plans already decided ("I'm going to visit my parents this weekend") and the present continuous for fixed arrangements ("I'm meeting Ana at six"). "Will" works for decisions made right now ("I'll take the blue one").
