The Man Who Asked Doctors to Wash Their Hands

Ignaz Semmelweis discovered how to stop mothers dying in childbirth. The discovery was simple, free, and proven. Medicine destroyed him for it.

Today: you'll follow medicine's most painful story — and you'll master the grammar of looking back at mistakes: they should have listened; thousands of lives could have been saved.
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • Why is it so hard to accept evidence that you yourself are causing harm?
  • Does the way a message is delivered matter as much as the message? Think of an example.
  • What do we owe people who were right too early?

The vocabulary of this story

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Within days of giving birth, the young mothers developed high fever and infection, and many died. The disease was called childbed fever — today we would call it sepsis.
sepsis (n) — a life-threatening reaction to infection spreading through the body. Adjective: septic. The killer at the centre of this story — including, in the end, of its hero.
2. The hospital kept careful records of births and deaths in each clinic. The death rate — the mortality rate — told a story nobody wanted to read.
the mortality rate (n) — the percentage of patients who die. Maternal mortality = deaths of mothers. This story is one of the first in history driven by comparing the numbers.
3. Between the autopsy room and the delivery ward, the doctors carried something invisible on their hands. Semmelweis called it cadaverous particles — matter from the dead.
a cadaver (n) — a dead body used for medical study; adjective cadaverous. He had no word for "bacteria" — germ theory did not exist yet. He described the effect twenty years before anyone could explain the cause.

B · Which word means…?

Three words: ward · autopsy · disinfect. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which word means a large hospital room where patients stay?
a ward (n) — a maternity ward, to be admitted to a ward. The two maternity wards of Vienna are the story's stage.
2. Which word means the examination of a dead body to find the cause of death?
an autopsy (n) — to perform / carry out an autopsy. In 1840s Vienna, doctors went straight from autopsies to delivering babies. Hold that thought.
3. Which word means to kill the germs on something using chemicals?
to disinfect (v) — noun: disinfectant. Semmelweis used chlorinated lime — chosen because it removed the smell of the autopsy room, which was the only measure he had.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. His colleagues were offended by the theory: it said that they — gentlemen and healers — were carrying death. Offended means…
  (a) convinced   (b) hurt and insulted   (c) confused
(b) — offended (adj) — to take offence at something. The theory's problem was not its evidence but its accusation: accepting it meant accepting guilt.
2. After years of rejection, his letters became furious and insulting — he called professors murderers. His tone undermined his own case. To undermine means…
  (a) to strengthen   (b) to weaken from below, gradually   (c) to explain
(b) — to undermine (v) — to undermine an argument / someone's authority / your own credibility. One of the story's hardest questions: how much did the messenger damage the message?

D · Word building & collocation

1. You know to prove. Build the family: scientific ___ (noun) · the theory remained ___ (negative adjective: without proof) · the numbers ___ him right in the end (past).
Proof (n) · unproven (adj) · the numbers proved him right. Also: to prove a point, the burden of proof — which his critics said lay with him, and which his numbers had already met.
2. One verb completes all three: to ___ a rule on someone (force it) · to ___ handwashing in the clinic · the professor ___ strict discipline.
to impose — to make something compulsory by authority. To impose a rule / a requirement / discipline. Semmelweis could impose handwashing only inside his own clinic — and that boundary is the whole tragedy.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — Two doors, two fates

In the 1840s, the Vienna General Hospital ran the largest maternity clinic in the world — two clinics, in fact, side by side, admitting women on alternate days. The First Clinic was staffed by doctors and medical students; the Second by midwives. And everyone in Vienna knew a terrible secret about them: the door you entered decided your chances of living. In the midwives' clinic, roughly one mother in twenty-five died of childbed fever. In the doctors' clinic, the rate ran between one in ten and, in the worst months, nearly one in five. Women begged on their knees to be admitted to the midwives. Some preferred to give birth in the street and arrive afterwards — and, mysteriously, those street births survived more often than the women delivered by doctors.

The official explanations were confident and useless: bad air, cosmic-atmospheric influences, the emotional fragility of women, even the psychological effect of a priest walking through the ward with his bell. Ignaz Semmelweis, a young Hungarian assistant in the First Clinic, found every explanation absurd for the same simple reason: both clinics shared the same air, the same city, the same priest. Whatever was killing the mothers walked through only one door.

In 1847 the answer arrived through grief. His friend and colleague Jakob Kolletschka cut his finger during an autopsy and died days later — and at his autopsy, Semmelweis saw the same pattern of infection that killed the mothers. The chain assembled itself in his mind: doctors and students began their mornings dissecting corpses, then went — hands rinsed at best — to examine labouring women. The midwives never touched a cadaver. The doctors were not failing to save the mothers. They were killing them, carrying death from the autopsy room on their hands.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the difference between the two clinics — in staff and in numbers?

2. How did Kolletschka's death reveal the answer?

1. The First Clinic was run by doctors and students, the Second by midwives. Mortality was roughly 4% with the midwives and 10–18% with the doctors — so much worse that women begged to avoid the First Clinic or gave birth in the street.

2. He died of the same infection as the mothers after cutting himself during an autopsy. The link: doctors went from dissecting corpses to examining women; midwives never touched cadavers. The doctors' own hands were the carrier.

Discuss:
  • The data — two clinics, two rates — had been public for years. Why did it take a friend's death, not the numbers, to make someone see?

Part 2 — The proof, and the insult

Semmelweis acted immediately. From May 1847, everyone entering his ward had to wash their hands in chlorinated lime — a harsh disinfectant chosen simply because it destroyed the smell of the autopsy room. The result was almost embarrassing in its speed: within months, the First Clinic's mortality fell to the midwives' level, and in some months to below one per cent. For two years the numbers held. It was, by any modern standard, one of the great controlled demonstrations in medical history: same clinic, same staff, same patients — one changed variable, one transformed outcome.

And medicine said no. The rejection had reasons, and it is important to hear them honestly. Semmelweis could not explain why handwashing worked — germ theory was still twenty years away, and "invisible cadaver particles" sounded like superstition to men trained in chemistry and anatomy. His finding contradicted the entire framework of disease as imbalance and bad air. And it carried an unbearable implication: that the profession's own hands — the hands of gentlemen — had killed thousands of mothers. One professor famously replied, in effect, that a doctor's hands could not transmit disease because a gentleman's hands are clean. Politics helped too: Semmelweis was Hungarian in an Austrian institution in the year of revolutions, and his patron lost the internal power struggle. His position was not renewed. He went home to Hungary — where, given charge of a maternity ward in Pest, he did it again: mortality collapsed to below one per cent.

Then the story darkens. Instead of building alliances, Semmelweis spent years refusing to publish properly, then published in 1861 a long, rambling book that mixed brilliant evidence with attacks on his critics. When the establishment ignored it, he wrote open letters to Europe's leading obstetricians that crossed every line: I denounce you before God and the world as a murderer, he told one professor. He was not wrong about the deaths. But he had made agreement impossible: to accept his theory now was to accept his verdict.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the handwashing experiment show, and why was it so strong as evidence?

2. Give three honest reasons the profession rejected it.

1. Chlorinated-lime handwashing cut the First Clinic's mortality from up to 18% to around 1–2% within months, holding for two years — same staff and patients, one changed variable. He then repeated the result in Pest.

2. He couldn't explain the mechanism (germ theory didn't exist); the claim contradicted the whole medical framework of the day; and it accused the profession itself of killing its patients — an intolerable implication, delivered with increasing fury.

Discuss:
  • "To accept the theory was to accept the verdict." Could Semmelweis have separated the two — and how would you deliver evidence that convicts your own audience?

Part 3 — The asylum, and the reflex

By 1865 Semmelweis was breaking. Contemporaries described obsession, public outbursts, behaviour his family could no longer manage — whether from psychiatric illness, neurological disease or two decades of being right alone, no one can now say. That summer, colleagues lured him to a Viennese asylum on a pretext. When he understood and tried to leave, the guards beat him, put him in a straitjacket and locked him in a dark cell. He died two weeks later, aged forty-seven, of sepsis from his untreated wounds — killed by the same kind of infection he had spent his life fighting. Almost nobody attended the funeral. His successor in Pest discontinued the handwashing, and mortality rose sixfold.

Vindication came within a generation, wearing other men's names. Pasteur demonstrated germs; Lister built antiseptic surgery on that theory and was made a baron; by the 1880s the profession that had destroyed Semmelweis practised, as routine, exactly what he had begged of it — now that it arrived with a mechanism and without an accusation. History has since repaid him with statues, stamps, a university in Budapest, and a term of art: the Semmelweis reflex — the instinct to reject new evidence because it contradicts what we believe and who we believe we are.

What keeps the story alive is not the injustice but the arithmetic of the delay. Between his proof in 1847 and general acceptance lie some thirty-five years, and childbed fever killed on the order of a thousand mothers a day across Europe and America in that era. The deaths that could have been prevented number beyond counting — and they were not prevented by the reasonable behaviour of reasonable men. Every hospital hand-hygiene poster today is his monument, and his question remains open in every field: when the evidence says we are the problem, what do we do — and what did they do to the last person who told us?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Semmelweis die, and what happened to his reform in Pest?

2. What is the "Semmelweis reflex", and why did acceptance come so easily a generation later?

1. Lured into an asylum, beaten by guards when he tried to leave, he died two weeks later at 47 — of sepsis from his wounds. His successor abandoned handwashing and mortality rose sixfold.

2. The instinct to reject evidence that contradicts existing beliefs — especially beliefs about ourselves. A generation later, Pasteur's germ theory supplied the mechanism and Lister's antisepsis carried no accusation, so the same practice was adopted as routine.

Discuss:
  • The same reform failed with an accusation and succeeded with a mechanism. What does that teach about how to introduce any change that implies past error?

Grammar — should have & could have

This story is told, and argued about, almost entirely in two structures: what people should have done, and what could have happened. Master them and you can judge any past decision precisely.

should have + past participle — the right action, not taken (or the wrong one, taken):
• "The professors should have tested his method." (they didn't)
• "He shouldn't have called them murderers." (he did)
In speech it compresses: should've /ˈʃʊdəv/, shouldn't've.
could have + past participle — the possibility that existed:
• "Thousands of lives could have been saved." (possible, didn't happen — note the passive)
• "He could have published properly in 1848." (the chance was there)
Negative: couldn't have = it was impossible: "He couldn't have known about bacteria — the science didn't exist."

The pair working together is the voice of every fair post-mortem: "He couldn't have explained the mechanism — but the profession should have trusted the numbers, and the deaths could have stopped in 1847." Possibility first, judgement second.

Complete each sentence with should have or shouldn't have + the verb in brackets.

2. Semmelweis (wait) fourteen years to publish his book.

3. His successor in Pest (stop) the handwashing programme.

1. should have taken   2. shouldn't have waited   3. shouldn't have stopped

2. could have, couldn't have

Complete each sentence. Watch for the passive in number 3.

1. He (not / explain) the mechanism — germ theory was twenty years away.

2. With allies instead of enemies, he (win) acceptance within a decade.

3. Historians agree that enormous numbers of mothers (save) if the method had spread in 1847. (passive)

Number 3 is the sentence this whole story exists to teach: could have + been + past participle.

3. The fair verdict — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with should(n't) have or could(n't) have + the verb, then read the verdict aloud.

A fair judgement has to face both sides. Semmelweis (not / know) about bacteria — nobody could in 1847 — but the profession (trust) his numbers, because the evidence was as strong as any of its time. The professors (test) the method in their own clinics within months; instead they defended their honour. Semmelweis himself (publish) earlier and (not / insult) the very men he needed to convince. And the mothers? They (live). That is the sentence that ends every argument about this story.

couldn't have known · should have trusted · could have tested / should have tested · should have published · shouldn't have insulted · could have lived

Notice the shape of fairness: one "couldn't have" for each side's real limits, "should have" for the choices, and the final "could have" for what was lost.

Going deeper

A note on the record: the mortality figures are rounded from the hospital's own records; the asylum's brutality is documented from his autopsy; his final illness remains debated — and the "gentleman's hands" quotation, like much else, has been polished by generations of retelling.

What the story actually teaches. First, that evidence does not carry itself. Semmelweis had the strongest data of his era and lost; Lister, twenty years later, had a theory, allies and tact — and won with weaker initial evidence. The uncomfortable rule: a truth arrives at the speed of its packaging, and the messenger who refuses to package it is choosing, however nobly, to delay it.

Second, the accusation problem. The theory demanded that its audience accept not just an idea but guilt — that their own hands had killed. Humans do not update beliefs that convict them; they attack the evidence. Any reformer whose data says "you caused this" faces the same wall, and the practical lesson from Lister's success is that the same reform can be offered as future improvement rather than past crime. Whether that repackaging is wisdom or a lie of kindness is worth arguing about.

Third, the reflex is not theirs — it is ours. The story is always told as a tale about arrogant 19th-century professors, which is itself a comfortable evasion. Every field now has its own two clinics somewhere: a comparison nobody runs, a number nobody compares, because the answer might convict the people who would have to act on it. The honest use of Semmelweis is not to pity him but to ask where we are currently standing in the story — and the uncomfortable prior is: not where we think.

Talk it through:
  • Lister repackaged the same reform without the accusation and won. Was that available to Semmelweis — and is softening the truth to make it acceptable a virtue or a compromise?
  • "A truth arrives at the speed of its packaging." Agree or attack, with one modern example.
  • Where are the "two clinics" in your own field — a comparison that exists but is never made? Who benefits from not making it?
  • The profession later built statues to the man it destroyed. What do such monuments actually do — repair, apologise, or inoculate against the next Semmelweis?

  2. What would the 1847 equivalent of a modern safety-reporting system have needed to look like for the numbers to win without a martyr?
