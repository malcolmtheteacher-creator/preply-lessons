When you argued, you instinctively did some of these things:

  • Made a claim — took a position
  • Used evidence — facts, examples, comparisons
  • Made a logical connection — explained why your evidence proved your point
  • Possibly qualified your position — "in most cases...", "to some extent..."

That's the structure of every academic argument ever written. Today you're going to make it deliberate.

6 Moves Every Arguer Needs

These aren't abstract theory — they're weapons. A weak argument uses one or two of these by accident. A strong argument uses all six deliberately. Click each to see what it does and how it sounds.

Move 1 The Claim +

Your central assertion. It must be debatable — not a fact, not an opinion, but a position that can be argued. Specific beats vague. "Governments should ban X" beats "X is bad."

In court

"The evidence will show that the defendant acted with both intent and full knowledge of the consequences."

In academic argument

"Universities should prioritise interdisciplinary research over single-discipline specialisation."

Move 2 The Evidence +

The factual basis for your claim. Data, research, cases, examples. Specific beats vague — "73% of breakthrough innovations emerged from cross-disciplinary teams" is evidence. "Many studies show" is not.

In court

"Security footage places the defendant at the scene at 11:43pm. The victim's phone records confirm a call was made at 11:40pm."

In academic argument

"Cross-disciplinary teams produced 73% of breakthrough innovations in the past decade, compared to 31% from single-discipline units (Nature, 2022)."

Move 3 The Warrant +

The logical bridge. Why does your evidence support your claim? This is the most overlooked move — most people state evidence and assume the connection is obvious. It never is. Say it explicitly.

In court

"Because the defendant had financial motive AND physical presence, there is no innocent explanation that accounts for both facts simultaneously."

In academic argument

"Because complex problems require multiple perspectives, cross-disciplinary teams can conceive solutions that any single discipline, however expert, cannot."

Move 4 The Qualifier +

Scope and honesty. A strong argument doesn't claim more than its evidence supports. Qualifying your claim makes it more convincing, not less — it shows intellectual honesty and makes it harder to attack.

In court

"While this evidence alone is not conclusive, it is highly probative when considered alongside the three witness statements that corroborate the same timeline."

In academic argument

"For most applied research — particularly in health, climate, and policy — interdisciplinary approaches tend to generate superior outcomes."

Move 5 The Steel Man +

Present the strongest version of the opposing argument — then explain why your position still holds. This is the opposite of a straw man. It shows you've thought seriously, and it disarms the best counterattack before it arrives.

In court

"The defence will argue that security footage timestamps are unreliable. Let me address that directly: the timestamp was corroborated independently by three witnesses who did not know each other."

In academic argument

"One might fairly argue that deep specialisation produces the foundational research that interdisciplinary teams depend on. This is true — and it is precisely why we advocate for balance, not abolition of specialisation."

Move 6 The Rebuttal +

When the other side attacks, you don't simply deny. You name the flaw in their attack and explain it. "That misrepresents my position" is not a rebuttal. Showing how it misrepresents it is.

In court

"My learned colleague suggests the evidence is merely circumstantial. But six independent pieces of circumstantial evidence, each pointing to the same conclusion, constitute a pattern — not a coincidence."

In academic argument

"While it's true that interdisciplinary projects show higher early-stage failure rates, those studies conflate failure to publish with failure to innovate — they are not the same metric."

Choose Your Motion

Select the motion you'll argue. You don't choose your side — the more interesting challenge is to argue whichever side you're assigned. Pick the motion that interests you most.

Cross-Examination

Six challenges to your argument. They appear one at a time. You have 45 seconds per response. Don't retreat — defend, qualify, or concede precisely what needs to be conceded and no more.

Each challenge targets a specific weak point. Use the moves from the toolkit.

Fallacy Spotter

Which of these did the cross-examination try to catch you on? Tap each card to see the definition.

Hasty Generalisation
Drawing a broad conclusion from too little evidence. "Studies show X" — which studies? How many? Under what conditions?
Slippery Slope
Assuming one step inevitably leads to an extreme outcome without showing why each step is necessary or likely.
Straw Man
Attacking a weaker version of the opposing argument rather than the strongest version. Wins the battle, loses the war.
False Cause
Assuming that because A preceded B, A caused B. Correlation and causation are not the same thing.
Begging the Question
Using your conclusion as evidence for itself. "This policy is good because it produces good outcomes" — that's circular.
False Dichotomy
Presenting only two options when more exist. "Either we ban this entirely or we allow it completely" — almost always wrong.

The trial is over. Now think about what happened in the room — not just what you said, but how you argued.

Introducing a claim: "The argument I want to make is..." · "What I'm arguing is not X but specifically Y..." · "The position I'll defend is..."

Connecting evidence to claim: "The reason this matters is..." · "This is significant because..." · "The logical implication of this is..."

Qualifying: "In most contexts..." · "For the majority of cases..." · "This applies particularly when..." · "To the extent that..."

Steel-manning: "The strongest counterargument would be..." · "One might fairly object that..." · "To be precise about what I'm conceding..."

Rebuttals: "That argument misrepresents my position because..." · "Even if we accept that... it doesn't follow that..." · "The studies cited conflate X with Y — these are not the same."
