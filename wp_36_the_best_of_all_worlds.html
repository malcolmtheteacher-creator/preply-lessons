The Best of All Possible Worlds

In which the last great polymath builds a universe from windowless mirrors, invents calculus on the side, and insists that God chose the best of all possible worlds — even this one

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716), one of the greatest minds in the history of human thought. Philosopher, mathematician, diplomat, engineer, linguist, logician. He invented calculus independently of Newton. He built a mechanical calculating machine. He dreamed of a universal language of thought. And he invented a system in which reality is made of windowless, mirror-like spiritual atoms called monads, each reflecting the entire universe from its own perspective. His theodicy—the argument that this is the best of all possible worlds—was so famous that Voltaire spent sixty pages mocking it. By the end, you will have locked three new dates in memory and placed Leibniz in Location 36 of your palace. You will understand why Voltaire laughed, and you will glimpse a question that haunts all of us: how can we reconcile suffering with divine love?

A problem to hold in your mind

The Problem of Evil. If God is all-powerful and all-good, why does suffering exist? Why do the innocent suffer? Why evil?

This is one of the oldest questions in philosophy and theology. Leibniz answered it directly in his Theodicy (1710): God could have created any world. God, being perfect, chose the best one. Therefore, this world—despite suffering—must be the best option available. Evil exists because free will is better than puppetry, and a world with freedom allows sin.

But notice: this is an argument. An intellectual answer. Later, when Jesus enters the story, he does not argue. He enters suffering. He weeps. He dies. The philosophical theodicy and the incarnational answer are profoundly different responses to the same question. Hold both in mind. By lesson's end, you will see why.

II. The Story — The Last Great Polymath

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz was born in Leipzig in 1646. His father was a professor of moral philosophy. His mother wanted him to be a priest. Instead, he became something rarer than either: a mind of such universal scope that he could credibly contribute to nearly every field of human knowledge. He was the last great polymath—the last person to master nearly all the learning of his age.

A Life of Brilliance and Neglect

Leibniz was born in the chaos of the Thirty Years' War. Protestant and Catholic powers clashed. The new science was emerging—Galileo, Descartes, Newton. Leibniz was educated in classics, theology, and philosophy at Leipzig and Altdorf. By fifteen, he was exploring logic. By twenty-six, he had completed a law doctorate and written a dissertation on combinatorics.

He spent his career moving between courts. Diplomat. Librarian. Engineer. He traveled to Paris, where he met the greatest mathematicians of the age. He corresponded with nearly every major mind of Europe. He proposed schemes for peace, plans to reunite the churches, improvements to mathematics. Most of his ideas were never published in his lifetime. He died in 1716, nearly alone, largely unrecognized. It took centuries for the world to understand what he had done.

The Calculus War. Both Leibniz and Newton invented calculus independently. Leibniz published first (1684). Newton had discovered it earlier but kept it secret. A bitter priority dispute raged for decades, poisoning both men. History now credits both. But Leibniz's notation—dy/dx for the derivative, ∫ for the integral—won. We use his symbols today. In this, the underdog's victory was complete.

The Three Core Ideas of Leibniz's Philosophy

Leibniz's mature philosophy, especially in works like the Monadology (1714) and Theodicy (1710), rests on three revolutionary ideas. Let’s explore them one by one.

1. The Monads — Reality as Windowless Mirrors

What is reality made of? Leibniz rejected both pure materialism and pure mechanism. He proposed something entirely new: the monad—a simple, indivisible, spiritual substance. Not physical atoms. Not abstract ideas. Monads.

Each monad has three astonishing properties:

  • Windowless. Monads do not causally interact with each other. They have no doors or windows through which external influences enter. Each monad is completely isolated from every other monad.
  • Mirror-like. Yet paradoxically, each monad perfectly mirrors or expresses the entire universe from its own unique perspective. A single monad contains within itself a complete representation of all things. You are a monad. Inside you, somehow, is a perception of the entire cosmos.
  • Alive. Even apparently dead matter is composed of monads. All monads are in some sense alive or perceptual, even if we cannot detect their inner life. Consciousness goes all the way down.

Imagine you are a monad. You contain within yourself a complete perception of the entire universe. Every event that happens anywhere is perceived by you. But distant events appear to you as dim, unconscious perceptions. Your own body's sensations are bright and clear. You are isolated from every other monad, yet perfectly synchronized with them. You are a window onto infinity, closed to the outside but containing the whole.

A monad is a substance which is a unity, a being endowed with perception and appetite. Perception is the internal state of the monad representing an external thing. Appetite is a tendency of one perception to pass into another. — Leibniz, Monadology, §14-15

2. Pre-established Harmony — The Cosmic Synchronization

This raises an obvious problem. If monads do not causally interact, how does anything work together? How do my thoughts align with my body movements? How does the universe form a coherent whole?

Leibniz's answer: Pre-established Harmony. God, at the moment of creation, coordinated all monads so perfectly that they operate in complete synchrony without ever touching each other. It is like a thousand clocks, each independent, all set to display the exact same time. Or like a thousand orchestras, each musician playing their score without hearing the others, yet all in perfect harmony because God pre-composed their pieces.

God has created the soul in such a way that everything which must happen to it derives from its own nature, and it is perfectly expressed by its own concept. Now, since the concept of the soul contains all that it shall ever happen to it, all its appearances and thoughts are born within it, and nothing can happen to it from without. There is nothing external that acts upon it excepting God alone. — Leibniz, Discourse on Metaphysics, §14

This is one of the most ambitious metaphysical solutions ever proposed. It preserves both the isolation of monads and the unity of the cosmos. But it is also deeply strange. If nothing touches anything, how can we act? How can we affect the world? Leibniz says we do affect it, but only in the sense that God has already pre-orchestrated everything so that our actions and their effects are synchronized—as if by divine watchmaking.

3. Theodicy and the Principle of Sufficient Reason

Now we come to the question that made Leibniz famous and then infamous. If God created the world, and God is all-good and all-powerful, why does evil exist?

Leibniz tackled this head-on in his 1710 work Theodicy—a full defense of God's justice. His answer rests on the Principle of Sufficient Reason: Everything that exists must have a reason for existing.

When God created the world, God faced an infinite set of possible worlds. God, being perfect, had to choose according to reason. Which world would a perfect God create? The best one. Therefore, this world—the actual world, with all its suffering—must be the best of all possible worlds.

Why is this the best? Because:

  • Free will requires the possibility of sin. A world of free beings is better than a world of puppets.
  • Suffering is an inevitable consequence of freedom.
  • Therefore, the best possible world includes both goodness and suffering.
  • We live in that world. We must trust divine wisdom.
God has chosen the world which is the best possible, that is to say, the one which is at the same time the most perfect as a whole. For as all things are connected in each possible world, it was impossible to improve any part in it without spoiling the whole. — Leibniz, Theodicy, §201-204

The Candide Catastrophe

Leibniz's theodicy became famous—but not for the reasons he hoped. In 1759, nearly fifty years after Leibniz's death, the French satirist Voltaire published a novel called Candide. Its protagonist, Candide, follows a philosophical tutor named Dr. Pangloss, who insists throughout the novel that "all is for the best in this best of all possible worlds."

Over the course of the novel, Candide and Pangloss experience earthquakes, shipwrecks, slavery, disease, mutilation, and every conceivable form of human suffering. And through it all, no matter what happens, Pangloss repeats his mantra: "All is for the best."

Voltaire's satire is savage and brilliant. He shows that Leibniz's answer, while logically elegant, is morally obscene. It seems to excuse the inexcusable. It offers no comfort to someone actually suffering. It turns philosophy into something cynical and cruel. The novel devastated Leibniz's reputation. For centuries, Leibniz was remembered as the fool that Voltaire mocked.

Was Voltaire Fair? Not entirely. Leibniz was more sophisticated than Dr. Pangloss. He did not claim that suffering is good. He claimed it is the price of free will in the best possible world. But Voltaire's critique captures something true: no intellectual argument can comfort genuine suffering. The problem of evil cannot be solved by philosophy alone.

Unfinished Dreams

Beyond his main philosophical work, Leibniz harbored grand dreams that mostly went unrealized:

  • The Universal Language. He dreamed of a characteristica universalis—a symbolic system in which all human knowledge could be expressed and all disputes could be settled by calculation. It was a dream of reducing all thought to mathematics. He never completed it, but it launched a tradition that led eventually to symbolic logic and programming languages.
  • The Stepped Reckoner. In 1673, he designed a calculating machine capable of multiplying, dividing, adding, and subtracting. Too complex to manufacture in his time, it was a harbinger of computers.
  • European Peace. He proposed plans to reunite the Protestant and Catholic churches through rational argument. It failed. But it showed his deepest conviction: that reason could heal even the deepest rifts.

III. The Ideas — Windowless Mirrors, Infinite Harmony, and Divine Choice

Leibniz's philosophy is strange, intricate, and oddly modern. His ideas shaped mathematics, logic, and metaphysics for centuries. Let’s break down three core insights and see why they still matter.

Idea 1: Monadology — The Ultimate Nature of Reality

Reality is not made of inert matter. It is made of simple, spiritual, perceptual units called monads, each reflecting the entire universe from its own perspective.

This is a radical departure from materialism. It says the world is not ultimately made of particles bouncing around according to mechanical laws. Matter is only appearance. Underneath is something more fundamental—spirit, perception, life.

Modern quantum mechanics echoes this in unexpected ways. Some interpretations (like the relational interpretation) suggest that properties of particles only exist relative to observers. Each particle is, in a sense, a perspective on reality. Leibniz was saying something like this in 1714.

Leibniz was trying to reconcile two insights: mechanism (the world follows laws) and vitalism (the world is alive). Monads are the reconciliation. Each is both alive and perfectly synchronized with mathematical law. This gives us a universe that is both rational and living.

Idea 2: The Identity of Indiscernibles

Two things are identical if and only if they have exactly the same properties. Therefore, no two things in the universe can be exactly alike.

This sounds simple but has stunning implications. Because each monad has a unique perspective on the universe, each monad is absolutely unique. The universe contains infinite diversity. There are no duplicates.

This principle rules out certain symmetries and suggests that individuality goes all the way down. Leibniz used it to argue against the void and against absolute space. For him, space is not an empty container. Space is the order of things. And since every thing is unique, no two regions of space are identical. The universe is infinitely rich.

Idea 3: Sufficient Reason and Theodicy

Nothing happens without a reason. God, being rational and perfect, chose to create the world that best satisfies all divine perfections—therefore, this is the best of all possible worlds.

This principle shapes how we think today. In science, we assume every event has a cause. In ethics, we demand justification. All of this comes partly from Leibniz. But it also forces a conclusion: if we accept Sufficient Reason fully, every evil must exist for a reason chosen by God.

Leibniz chose to interpret this comfortingly: God chose the best option available. But Voltaire and others found it monstrous. How can God's wisdom excuse suffering? How can we know that God chose the best? Is this not mere speculation?

The Thesis Seed: Two Different Answers to Evil

Leibniz's theodicy is one of the most ambitious philosophical attempts to solve the problem of evil. But it leaves something crucial unresolved. Notice the stark difference between two answers to the same question:

The Philosophical Answer (Leibniz): Why does suffering exist? Because this is the best possible world. God could have created a world without free will, without suffering. But such a world would be inferior. Evil is a necessary feature of the optimal world.

The Incarnational Answer (Jesus): Jesus does not explain suffering. He does not argue: "Your pain is necessary for the best possible world." Instead, he enters suffering. He enters it as a human being. At Lazarus's tomb, he weeps. He does not defend God's justice philosophically. He demonstrates God's presence practically—by entering death, by suffering with us, by transfiguring suffering from within.

These are profoundly different responses. One is intellectual. One is personal. One tries to reconcile suffering with divine perfection through reason. One treats suffering as so real, so terrible, that the only adequate response is presence and solidarity and love.

Which is more true? Which is more adequate? That is one of the deepest questions a human can ask. And Leibniz, for all his brilliance, did not—could not—answer it. Because it requires not a philosopher, but a person. Not an argument, but a presence.

Location 36: Leibniz and the Best of All Possible Worlds

You arrive at location 36. Behind you: Spinoza and the harmonious nature of being. Ahead: Hume, Rousseau, Kant—and the great age of Enlightenment. This location holds Leibniz, the last great medieval mind trying to reconcile reason with faith, logic with life, monads with God.

Place This Lesson’s Content in Location 36

The 5 Images for Location 36

1. Millions of tiny mirrors, glittering and silent, each reflecting the entire cosmos—but none with windows, none touching each other — The monads. Perfect isolation. Perfect reflection. The strange isolation-within-unity that defines reality.

2. A divine watchmaker (God) setting thousands of clocks, adjusting them with infinite precision—all synchronized perfectly though they never touch — Pre-established harmony. The mechanism of cosmic coordination without causation.

3. An angel or divine figure examining rows of glowing snow globes on shelves, holding each up to light—choosing the brightest one — Theodicy. God examining infinite possible worlds and selecting the best.

4. Leibniz and Newton pulling a mathematical formula from opposite ends, both claiming victory, tears of frustration streaming down their faces — The calculus priority dispute. Two geniuses. One great idea. Bitter competition.

5. Dr. Pangloss (a caricature of Leibniz) standing in the rubble of Candide after an earthquake, smiling serenely, saying "This is the best of all possible worlds" — Voltaire's satire. The idea that made Leibniz famous for the wrong reason.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 questions to reflect on deeply. Write honest answers. There are no wrong answers.

The Problem of Evil

Does Leibniz's answer comfort you? Does it make sense? Or does it seem to excuse suffering in a way that feels false when you confront real pain?

Windowless Monads

Reality is made of things that never touch each other. Yet somehow the universe works. Does this idea make sense? How? Or is it just clever philosophy without substance?

Candide and Voltaire

Voltaire's satire of Leibniz became more famous than Leibniz himself. Why did the satire work so well? Did Voltaire refute Leibniz, or just mock him?

The Principle of Sufficient Reason

Everything has a reason. Does this principle comfort or unsettle you? Does it leave room for freedom, chance, grace? Or does it close the universe into necessity?

Mind and Body in Monads

How does a soul (a monad) govern a body (made of other monads) if they don't causally interact? What is Leibniz really trying to explain about the mind-body problem?

The Universal Language

Leibniz dreamed of a characteristica universalis—a language in which all knowledge could be expressed and all disputes solved by calculation. Is this dream alive in computers? Should it be?

Two Answers to Suffering

Leibniz answers "why evil?" with a philosophical argument. Jesus answers by entering suffering and death. Which response is more adequate? Can both be true?
