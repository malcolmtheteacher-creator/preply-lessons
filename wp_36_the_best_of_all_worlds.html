The Best of All Possible Worlds

Leibniz: Monads, pre-established harmony, and theodicy

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) was perhaps the last great universal mind. He believed that God created the best of all possible worlds, where every detail is harmonized by God's infinite wisdom. But Leibniz left a wound in philosophy: if this is the best of all possible worlds, why do innocent children suffer? Why do earthquakes kill thousands? This is the problem of evil—and it will haunt philosophy until the end of time. You will place Leibniz and optimism in your palace, and carry forward a question that Jesus himself wrestled with on the cross.

I. Before We Begin

If God is good and all-powerful, why does evil exist?

Think about suffering. Think about injustice. How does this fit with a perfect God?

♦ ♦ ♦

The deepest question

Leibniz's answer: this is the best of all possible worlds. But can we believe that? Can we say that a child's cancer serves a greater good? That an earthquake serves divine purposes?

II. The Story — The Optimist's Wound

Leibniz (1646–1716) was a diplomat, mathematician, philosopher, inventor. He saw more of the world than most philosophers. And he tried to make sense of it all through the lens of God's infinite wisdom.

Monads and Harmony

Leibniz proposed that reality is made of monads—spiritual atoms, each reflecting the entire universe from its own perspective. God created an infinite number of monads and harmonized them perfectly. Everything that happens follows from this pre-established harmony.

And as the same city viewed from different sides looks entirely different and is, as it were, multiplied perspectively, just so, from the infinite number of substances, there are, as it were, just as many different universes, which are, nevertheless, only the perspectives of a single one.— Leibniz, Monadology

Theodicy: Defending God

The question Leibniz had to answer: if God is all-powerful and all-good, why do bad things happen?

His answer: God created the best of all possible worlds. This world contains evil, yes. But removing any evil would require removing something good. Every detail is necessary to the whole. God is not constrained by divine power to create a perfect world, because this is the most perfect world—the one that balances freedom, goodness, and necessity most perfectly.

The wound he left: Voltaire, living in the 18th century, could not accept this. When an earthquake destroyed Lisbon in 1755 and killed tens of thousands, Voltaire wrote Candide, mocking Leibniz's optimism. The protagonist meets Dr. Pangloss, who keeps saying "all is for the best in this best of all possible worlds" even as catastrophe unfolds. The irony was devastating. And it has echoed through philosophy ever since.
♦ ♦ ♦

The Question Leibniz Could Not Solve

Is there any way to reconcile an all-powerful, all-good God with the existence of innocent suffering? Leibniz tried. Augustine tried. Job cried out against it. And Jesus on the cross may have experienced it as abandonment. The wound remains open.

III. The Ideas — Harmony and the Cost of Existence

Idea 1: Monads (Windowless Spiritual Atoms)

Reality is made of infinite monads, each a complete perspective on the universe, each reflecting the whole.

This solved Descartes's mind-body problem in a radical way: there is no interaction between monads. Each unfolds according to its own nature. They appear to interact, but this is illusion—they are simply pre-harmonized by God to seem to interact.

Idea 2: Pre-Established Harmony

God has arranged all things so perfectly that they harmonize without interaction.

Your mind and body seem to interact, but they don't really. They are like two clocks set to the same time by a master clockmaker. They keep perfect time together, not because one influences the other, but because they were set the same way from the beginning.

Idea 3: Theodicy

This is the best of all possible worlds because God chose it from infinite alternatives.

God did not create a world constrained by necessity. God freely chose to create this world, knowing all possible worlds. And God chose this one because, taking everything into account—freedom, goodness, beauty, harmony—this is the best. Evil exists because removing it would require removing something good, or constraining freedom.

VI. Your Turn

Is this really the best world?

Leibniz says yes. But can you imagine a better world? One without suffering? Why would a perfect God choose this one?

Monads and interaction

Leibniz says monads don't interact, only appear to. Does this make sense? Or is it a way of avoiding the real problem?

The problem of evil

This is the deepest wound in theology and philosophy. Is there any answer? Or must we accept the mystery?

Voltaire's mockery

Voltaire used Candide to mock Leibniz's optimism. Was Voltaire right? Or did he miss something about faith?

The tetrapharmakos and Leibniz

The Stoics said: accept necessity, accept suffering. Leibniz says: this suffering is necessary. Is acceptance enough?

Jesus on the cross

Jesus cried out "My God, why have you forsaken me?" Is this the cry of someone in the best of all possible worlds?

VII. Final Retrieval

1. Monads

What are they, and how do they work?

Spiritual atoms that are each complete perspectives on the universe. Each monad reflects the infinite universe from its own unique angle. God harmonized all monads so that they work together perfectly without actually interacting.

2. Pre-Established Harmony

What does this mean?

God set everything up from the beginning so that it all works together perfectly, like two clocks set to the same time by a master clockmaker. Mind and body appear to interact, but they don't—they're simply synchronized.

3. Theodicy and the Problem of Evil

What is Leibniz's answer to why evil exists?

This is the best of all possible worlds. God could have created any possible world, but freely chose this one because it achieves the perfect balance of freedom, goodness, and harmony. Evil exists because removing it would require removing something good or constraining freedom.

MILESTONE: The Modern Turn is Complete

You have completed 36 lessons. The medieval synthesis is gone. Descartes has given us the isolated mind. Hobbes has stripped away God and left naked power. Spinoza has made God into Nature. Locke has made the mind a blank slate. And Leibniz has tried one last time to defend the God of tradition—and created a wound that will not heal. Ten lessons remain. The 19th and 20th centuries will push philosophy to its breaking point. And then, in the final two lessons, everything converges.
