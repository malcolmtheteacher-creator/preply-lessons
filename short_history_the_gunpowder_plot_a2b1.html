The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — Angry men and a secret plan

In England in 1605, your religion could change your whole life. The country was Protestant. But some people were Catholic — they followed an older form of the Christian religion, led from Rome. The Catholics lived under hard laws. They had to pay money if they did not go to the Protestant church. Some were sent to prison.

In 1603, a new king came to the throne: King James I. The Catholics hoped that life would get better for them. But it did not. The king said he did not like the Catholic religion, and he sent Catholic priests away.

A small group of angry young Catholic men decided to act. Their leader was Robert Catesby. He made a shocking plan: they would kill the king and blow up Parliament, the building where the king and the leaders of England met. The men needed someone who knew about explosives. They found a soldier called Guy Fawkes. He had fought in many wars, and he knew all about gunpowder.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why were the Catholics unhappy when King James I became king?

2. Who was Guy Fawkes, and why did the group want him?

1. They hoped life would get better, but the king said he did not like their religion and sent Catholic priests away.

2. He was a soldier who had fought in many wars and knew all about gunpowder, so he could look after the explosives.

Talk about it:
  • The men felt that no one would help them. Is it ever right to use violence? When is it always wrong?

Part 2 — Thirty-six barrels

The plan was simple and terrible. On 5 November 1605, the king would open Parliament. On that day, the king, his son, and all the important leaders of England would be in one building together. If the men could blow up that building at that moment, they would destroy the whole government at once.

The men rented a cellar — a room under Parliament. Through the year, they quietly carried in barrels of gunpowder and hid them under wood and coal. In the end there were thirty-six barrels, more than enough to destroy the building. Guy Fawkes used a false name and pretended to be a servant. His job was to guard the gunpowder. When Parliament opened and the room was full of people, he would light the gunpowder and run.

But the secret was too big. Some of the men worried about the Catholic leaders who would also die. Then someone sent a secret letter to a man called Lord Monteagle. The letter told him to stay away from Parliament, because something terrible was going to happen there. Monteagle was afraid, and he took the letter straight to the king's men. Now the king's men were looking.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was 5 November the perfect day for the attack?

2. What was in the secret letter, and what did Lord Monteagle do?

1. On that day the king, his son and all the important leaders of England would be in one building together, so one explosion could destroy the whole government.

2. It told Lord Monteagle to stay away from Parliament because something terrible would happen. He took the letter straight to the king's men.

Talk about it:
  • One small letter stopped the whole plan. Do you think big secrets are always discovered in the end? Can you think of an example?

Part 3 — Caught in the cellar

The king's men searched the rooms under Parliament. Just after midnight, in the early morning of 5 November 1605, they went down into the cellar. There they found a tall man standing next to a big pile of wood. He gave a false name: "John Johnson." When the men moved the wood, they found the thirty-six barrels of gunpowder. The man had matches with him, ready to light the fuse. They arrested him at once. The plan was stopped with only hours to spare.

At first, the man would not talk. But the king ordered his men to hurt him until he spoke. After days of pain, he told them the truth: his real name was Guy Fawkes, and he gave the names of the other men. By then, the other men had run away to the countryside. They hoped people would join them and fight, but no one came. Some of the men were killed by soldiers. The rest were taken back to London. In January 1606, Guy Fawkes and the others were put to death for their crime.

The plan failed completely. But the night was never forgotten. To celebrate that the king was safe, people lit bonfires across London — and they have lit them every year since. More than four hundred years later, on the evening of 5 November, people in Britain still light bonfires and fireworks. This is called Bonfire Night. A few men wanted to change history with gunpowder. Instead, they gave their country a night of fire that people still remember today.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How was Guy Fawkes caught in the cellar?

2. The plan failed — so why do people still remember 5 November every year?

1. The king's men searched the cellar just after midnight, moved the wood, and found him with the barrels and matches, ready to light the fuse. They arrested him.

2. People lit bonfires to celebrate that the king was safe, and the tradition continued. Today it is called Bonfire Night, with bonfires and fireworks.

Talk about it:
  • People in Britain still celebrate this night after more than four hundred years. Why do you think this story is so famous?

Grammar: the first conditional

This story is full of plans: if we do this, that will happen. We use the first conditional to talk about a real future possibility — something that may really happen, and its result.

The pattern:
If + present simple, will + base verb.
• "If they light the gunpowder, Parliament will explode."
• "If one man talks, they all will die."
• "If the king reads the letter, the plan will fail."
The if-part shows the possibility. The will-part shows the result.
One important rule: we do not use will in the if-part. We use the present, even though we mean the future.
✓ If they find the barrels, they will arrest him.
✗ If they will find the barrels…
You can also turn it around: "They will arrest him if they find the barrels." The rule is the same.

1. Put the verbs in

One verb goes in the present (the if-part). The other takes will + base verb.

1. If the guards (search) the cellar, they (find) the barrels.

2. If Guy Fawkes (light) the gunpowder, the building (explode).

3. If one man (betray) the group, they all (die).

4. The plan (fail) if the king (read) the letter.

1. search / will find (if + present, will + base verb)

2. lights / will explode

3. betrays / will die

4. will fail / reads (the if-part stays in the present, even when it comes second)

2. Finish the sentence — your own way

Say it out loud first, then write. Keep the rule: if + present, will in the other part.

1. If the plan works, …

2. If the king dies, …

3. If someone sends a letter, …

1. … the men will be very happy.

2. … the country will have no leader.

3. … the king's men will find the plot. (Your answers will be different — just keep the present after "if" and "will" in the other part.)
