Warm-up — What Makes a Poem a Poem?

Most people meet poetry in school and either fall in love with it or run away from it. Today we start writing it — so first, let's strip away some of the mystery.

Quick discussion
1. Have you ever read a poem you loved? What was it about?
2. Have you ever read a poem you hated? Why?
3. Have you ever written a poem? If yes, what was it about? If no, what stopped you?

Brendan Behan at Oxford — a true story

The Irish playwright Brendan Behan was once invited to Oxford to debate the difference between prose and poetry. His opponent spoke for nearly two hours. Behan stood up, promised to be brief, recited this old Dublin rhyme, and sat down again:

There was a young fellow named Rollocks
Who worked for Ferrier Pollocks.
As he walked on the strand
With a girl by the hand,
The water came up to his ankles.

"That," Behan said, "is prose. If the tide had been in, it would have been poetry."

Then he sat down.

The joke — and why it matters for writing poetry

Read the first four lines of the rhyme aloud. Listen to the rhyme pattern:

  • Rollocks
  • Pollocks
  • strand
  • hand
  • ankles ←

The first four lines set up a tight rhyme scheme. Your ear EXPECTS line 5 to rhyme with Rollocks and Pollocks.

What word does the rhyme want? (Mild warning — slightly rude)

The expected rhyme is bollocks — a mild British/Irish slang word for testicles. Your ear is set up for it by the Rollocks / Pollocks pairing. It's the only word that completes the rhyme.

But Behan says "ankles" instead. That breaks the rhyme deliberately. As a sentence, "the water came up to his ankles" is fine. As a limerick, it's broken — the music doesn't land.

Behan's joke: had the tide been HIGHER — up to his bollocks — the rhyme would have completed and the limerick would have worked. With the tide low, it's just a sentence. The whole gag is built on the missing rhyme word.

What this teaches us

Behan's two-line lesson — delivered in about 30 seconds against a two-hour opponent — contains three of the most important ideas in poetry:

  1. Sound matters as much as sense. Rhyme, rhythm and repetition aren't decoration — they're what makes poetry different from prose. Take the rhyme away from Behan's limerick and you've just got a sentence about a wet man.
  2. Expectation is a tool. A poem can use the rhyme you expected, OR deliberately break it to make a joke or a point. Behan does the second. Both moves are available to you.
  3. Compression. Behan answered a two-hour speech in 30 seconds. That's what poetry does — say a lot in very few words.

Every poem you ever write or read will be working some combination of compression, image and music. Most other "rules" of poetry are just techniques for getting those effects.

Vocabulary — Eight Words That Open the Door

Tap each card to reveal the definition and an example. These are the eight words you'll meet in today's reading — and the eight tools you'll use when you start writing.

Line
Tap for definition
The basic unit of a poem. Where a line ends matters — it creates a tiny pause and puts weight on the last word.
e.g. each row of words you see in a poem is one line.
Stanza
Tap for definition
A group of lines, like a paragraph in prose. Stanzas are separated by blank lines.
e.g. a poem with 4 groups of 4 lines has 4 stanzas.
Metaphor
Tap for definition
Saying one thing IS another, with no "like" or "as". A direct, unmarked comparison.
e.g. "she is a small country" — she isn't literally a country.
Simile
Tap for definition
A comparison that uses "like" or "as". Softer than a metaphor — it admits it's a comparison.
e.g. "her hands like brown paper bags".
Image
Tap for definition
A picture made of words. Strong poems give the reader something to SEE, not just something to feel.
e.g. "the kitchen light is yellow" — you can picture it.
Rhythm
Tap for definition
The music of the words — the pattern of stress, pause, and pace when you read aloud.
e.g. short choppy lines feel different from long flowing ones.
Free verse
Tap for definition
Poetry without a fixed rhyme scheme or meter. Most modern poetry is free verse. You still have rhythm — it just isn't measured.
e.g. the reading on the next tab is free verse.
Repetition
Tap for definition
A word or phrase that comes back deliberately. One of the simplest and most powerful tools in poetry.
e.g. saying the same line twice in a row makes it land harder.

You don't need to memorise these. You'll see them all in the reading on the next tab — and you'll find yourself using them naturally when you start writing.

Reading — "The Kitchen Window"

Read the poem aloud, slowly. After you've read it once, read it again silently. The highlighted words are the vocabulary terms from the last tab — hover to see which is which.

The Kitchen Window
I watch my grandmother
at the kitchen window,
her hands like brown paper bags,
folding and folding the same cloth.
She is a small country
that has forgotten its borders.
The radio hums beside her,
the same low hymn.
She doesn't see me.
She doesn't see me.
She sees something far,
something that was once a garden.
I think she is a clock
running backwards.
I think she is a letter
nobody answered.
The kitchen light is yellow.
The kitchen smell is bread.
The kitchen window holds her
like a hand holding a moth.

What's in this poem?

The eight vocabulary words — where they appear
  • Line — every row of words. Notice how line breaks create small pauses.
  • Stanza — there are five, each separated by a blank line.
  • Metaphor — "She is a small country", "I think she is a clock running backwards", "I think she is a letter nobody answered". She isn't literally any of these — but each one tells you something true about her.
  • Simile — "her hands like brown paper bags", "like a hand holding a moth". Both use "like" to compare.
  • Image — paper bags, the radio, the garden, the yellow kitchen light, the smell of bread, the moth. The whole poem is built on pictures.
  • Rhythm — read it aloud and you'll feel it: short lines, slow pace, gentle pauses. No fixed beat, but clear music.
  • Free verse — there's no rhyme scheme, no fixed meter. Just lines and stanzas.
  • Repetition — "She doesn't see me. / She doesn't see me." The repeat makes you feel the speaker's hurt.

Discussion

  • Who do you think the grandmother is? What's happening to her?
  • Why does the poet repeat "She doesn't see me"? What would change if it was said only once?
  • Which of the metaphors lands hardest for you — the country, the clock, or the letter? Why?
  • The last simile compares the window to "a hand holding a moth". Why a moth? What does that suggest?

Your Turn — Write Using the Frame

The example poem follows a clear shape. We're going to use the same shape — and you fill in your own content. Pick a person in your life that you watch, often, but don't always talk to. A relative. A neighbour. A stranger you see on your commute. Anyone.

Writing timer
10 minutes. Don't edit while you write — just fill the slots.
10:00
Stanza 1 — A person you watch
"I watch ____ at the ____ / [something they do, repeated]"
Stanza 2 — Two things they are (metaphor)
No "like" or "as" — they ARE these things. Don't try to be clever, just true.
Stanza 3 — A repeated line
Say one short sentence about them. Then say it again. Then say what they DO see / hear / feel instead.
Stanza 4 — Two more metaphors
Use the phrase "I think they are..." twice. Try things that shouldn't logically work — those are often the best.
Stanza 5 — A setting word repeated three times + a closing simile
Pick ONE word (a place, a colour, a body part) and start three lines with it. Then end on a simile.

When you're done, read it aloud. The frame is just a starting point — you can change anything that doesn't feel true. The frame is there to stop you staring at a blank page, not to lock you in.

Free Write — Without the Frame

Now you've used a frame to write a poem. The frame did some of the thinking for you. The next step is to write without one — but you'll find the techniques are still in your head.

Your prompt
Write a poem about a place that mattered to you when you were younger but you no longer live in or visit. It can be a room, a street, a town, a country. Try to include:

• At least one metaphor
• At least one simile
• At least one moment of repetition
• Strong concrete images — things the reader can see, smell, or touch

Free verse. No need to rhyme. Aim for 4–6 stanzas.
Free-write timer
15 minutes. Don't stop writing. You can fix it after.
15:00

After you've written

Once your timer goes off — read what you've got aloud. Three quick checks:

  1. Did you include the four ingredients? One metaphor, one simile, one repetition, strong images. Tick them off.
  2. Read it aloud — where did you stumble? Wherever you tripped, that line probably needs adjusting.
  3. What's your strongest line? The one you'd put on a postcard. Underline it.
