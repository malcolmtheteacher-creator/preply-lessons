At the Doctor's

A cough that won't shift, a tiredness that sleep won't fix, and a small worry you've not quite said aloud. Time to describe it clearly — and hear that you're going to be fine.

The Scene 🎬

You've had a cough for the best part of a fortnight now — the kind that won't quite shift — and you're tired in a way that a good night's sleep doesn't seem to fix. On its own, nothing alarming. Together, just enough to make you finally book the appointment you'd been putting off.

Sitting in the waiting room, you half-rehearse what to say. There's an art to this: describe too little and you're waved off with "take some paracetamol and rest"; describe too much and you sound like you've been reading things you shouldn't at two in the morning. What you're after is to be clear, calm and honest — including about the small fear you haven't quite admitted to anyone.

Across the desk: a doctor who has heard ten thousand coughs described, and can tell in about ninety seconds the worried-but-fine from the genuinely unwell. Warm, unhurried, and very good at the one thing you came for — telling you the truth, kindly.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When you feel unwell, are you a "wait and see if it clears up on its own" person or a "book the appointment straight away" person — and where do you think that habit came from?
  2. Think of a time someone — a doctor, a parent, a friend — reassured you about something you'd been quietly dreading. What did they actually say, and why did it work?

The other voice

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Listen for how the patient turns a vague "I feel rough" into something a doctor can actually work with.

DoctorCome in, take a seat. Now then — what seems to be the trouble?
YouWell, I've not been feeling myself for a couple of weeks, to be honest. It's probably nothing, but I thought I'd better get it checked.
DoctorYou did the right thing coming in. Talk me through it — what have you actually noticed?
YouMainly a cough that just won't shift, and I'm shattered all the time. It's more of a dry, tickly cough than a chesty one — it comes and goes, but it's worse at night, and it's been keeping me awake.
DoctorI see. And when did all this start, roughly — any fever or aches to go with it?
YouIt came on quite gradually, maybe two or three weeks ago, and if anything it's slowly got worse rather than better. I was achy and a bit feverish for the first few days, though that's settled now.
DoctorRight. And any shortness of breath, or chest pain when you cough?
YouNo chest pain… though now that you mention it, I do get a little out of breath going up the stairs. I hadn't really connected the two, actually.
DoctorGood that you've told me. And has anyone at home been unwell recently?
YouMy daughter had a nasty flu a few weeks back, come to think of it — I probably caught it from her.
DoctorThat would fit. Let me have a quick listen… (she listens to your chest) …Well, your chest sounds nice and clear, and everything else looks reassuring. My honest feeling is that this is a viral infection that's rather outstayed its welcome — these things can drag on for weeks, unfortunately.
YouThat's a relief to hear. Just to be clear, though — is there anything I should actually be doing, or is it a case of waiting it out?
DoctorMostly rest, fluids and patience, I'm afraid, with paracetamol for the aches. I would like to run a quick blood test, though — just to rule anything else out and put both our minds at rest.
YouThat makes sense. I have to admit, I'd started to worry it might be something more serious.
DoctorI understand the worry entirely — but this really is a world away from that. If the cough's no better in a fortnight, or that breathlessness gets any worse, I want you straight back here. Deal?
YouDeal. Thank you — that's put my mind at rest more than you know.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The patient only mentions being "a little out of breath on the stairs" once the doctor asks about it — and adds "I hadn't really connected the two." Why is that small detail worth having, and what does it show about the way people report their own symptoms?

2. The doctor gives a clear diagnosis AND orders a blood test. Two different reasons are given for that test — what are they?

3. The patient's last line thanks the doctor for putting their mind at rest "more than you know." What was really worrying them underneath the cough — and how did the doctor answer the fear, not just the symptom?

1. Breathlessness on exertion is exactly the kind of thing a doctor needs to know, but patients often don't volunteer it — they mention only the symptom that bothers them most (here, the cough). "I hadn't connected the two" shows how we tend to report symptoms as separate, unrelated complaints rather than as a picture; part of the doctor's job is drawing the picture out with questions.
2. First, to rule anything else out — to make sure nothing more serious is being missed behind an ordinary-looking virus. Second, to "put both our minds at rest" — the reassurance of a normal result matters for the patient as much as the medicine.
3. Underneath, the patient feared it might be "something more serious." The doctor didn't just dismiss it — she acknowledged the worry ("I understand the worry entirely"), reassured honestly ("a world away from that"), and set a clear safety net ("straight back here" if things change). She treated the fear as well as the cough.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1918, a flu swept the whole world and killed more people than the war it followed — tens of millions of them. Sufferers described almost exactly what you described today: a cough, an ache in the bones, a bottomless tiredness. The difference is that they had no antibiotics, no blood tests, often no doctor to see at all — only a crowded room and a great deal of hope.

A century on, you walked into a warm room, listed your symptoms, and walked out with a plan and your mind at rest. It's easy to forget what a quiet miracle that is: to be listened to carefully, and told the truth kindly.

Your great-grandparents feared the flu for very good reason. You get to be reassured — so take the reassurance, and don't take it for granted.

Two bigger questions

  1. A hundred years ago, most people with your symptoms would never have seen a doctor at all. Now we can book an appointment over a cough. Have we become better at looking after ourselves — or just quicker to worry?
  2. The doctor spent almost as much time calming your fear as treating your cough. How much of "feeling better" is really about being reassured? Can words themselves be a kind of medicine?
