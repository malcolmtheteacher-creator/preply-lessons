Should This Study Be Allowed?

People were told to give electric shocks to a stranger. Many did. What does this teach us about ethics?

The words for today's reading

Eight words, three short activities. Say each answer out loud before you reveal.

A · What does the word mean?

Read the sentence and say what the bold word means. Then reveal.

1. Every participant must give consent before the study starts.
consent (n) — permission that someone gives after they understand what will happen. Informed consent.
2. At the end, the researcher explained the real aim in a debrief.
debrief (n/v) — a talk after the study where you explain what really happened.
3. The participant was told that he could withdraw at any time.
withdraw (v) — to stop taking part and leave the study.

B · Which word is it?

Say the word. The first letter helps you. Then reveal.

4. Which word means 'to tell someone something that is not true'? (d_______)
deceive (v) — The noun is deception. Example: "The study used deception."
5. Which word means 'to do what a person in charge tells you'? (o___)
obey (v) — The noun is obedience. Example: "Many people obeyed the researcher."
6. Which word means 'a committee that checks research plans before a study'? (e_____ b____)
ethics board (n) — also ethics committee or IRB. Example: "The ethics board must approve the plan."

C · Complete the phrase

Which word completes the phrase?

7. The study caused a lot of ______ to some participants. (s_____)
stress (n) — also harm, distress. Example: "Researchers must avoid causing harm."

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The shock machine

In 1961, a psychologist called Stanley Milgram started a famous study at Yale University. Men were invited to take part in a study about learning. Each man was told he was the 'teacher'. Another man, the 'learner', sat in a different room.

The teacher had to give the learner an electric shock every time the learner made a mistake. After each mistake the shocks became stronger, from 15 volts up to 450 volts. In fact, the shocks were not real, and the learner was an actor. But the teacher did not know this.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the study about, in the story told to the men?

2. Were the shocks real?

1. Learning.

2. No. The learner was an actor and there were no real shocks.

Discuss:
  • What would you do if you were the teacher?

Part 2 — The shocking result

When the learner cried out, many teachers wanted to stop. But a man in a grey coat, the researcher, said, 'Please continue.' 'The experiment requires that you continue.' In the first study, 26 of 40 men, or 65%, went all the way to 450 volts.

Many men were very worried, they laughed nervously and sweated. Milgram said the study showed how far ordinary people can obey an authority. But some people asked: was it right to do this to the participants?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many men went to 450 volts?

2. What did the researcher say?

1. 26 of 40, or 65%.

2. 'Please continue' / 'The experiment requires that you continue.'

Discuss:
  • Is the result important enough to justify the stress?

Part 3 — New rules

Critics said Milgram deceived the men and caused them stress. Milgram said that most men were glad to have taken part. Later researchers, however, said that some men did not believe the shocks were real, and that the studies were less simple than Milgram said.

Today, rules are much stricter. Every study must be checked by an ethics board. Participants must give informed consent, can withdraw at any time and must be debriefed. A study like Milgram's would be very difficult to run today. But some people say that without such studies we would know less about obedience.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name two rules from today.

2. Why did critics say Milgram's study was wrong?

1. Ethics board, informed consent, the right to withdraw, debrief.

2. It deceived the men and caused them stress.

Discuss:
  • Do you think the old studies should still be taught? Why or why not?

Grammar — must, mustn't, can, be allowed to

Use these words and phrases to talk about rules and permission.

Rules:
• must + verb — it is necessary (a rule): Researchers must get consent.
• mustn't + verb — it is forbidden: You mustn't hide the risks.
• have to / has to — similar to must: Participants have to sign a form.
Permission:
• can + verb — it is allowed: Participants can leave at any time.
• can't + verb — not allowed: You can't use real shocks.
• be allowed to + verb: Researchers are allowed to use deception only in some cases.
• don't have to — it is not necessary (but it is allowed): You don't have to explain the aim before the study.

1. Choose the word

Type the missing word or words.

1. Researchers get consent before the study. (it is a rule; one word)

2. You hurt the participants. (it is forbidden; one word with n't)

3. Participants leave at any time. (it is allowed; one word)

1. must (or "have to")

2. mustn't

3. can

2. Fix the form

Each sentence needs one small change. Type the correct word.

1. Participants are allowed leave. (one word)

2. She must (sign) the form.

3. He doesn't have to (explain) the aim before the study.

1. to

2. sign

3. explain

3. Your rules

Finish each sentence with your own idea, then say it aloud.

1. In my country, researchers must .

2. Researchers mustn't .

2. a verb (for example: share private information.)

Remember: must / mustn't + base verb. Not 'must to'. But: be allowed TO.

Allowed or not?

Five situations. Say whether the study is allowed and why. Then reveal an idea.

1. A researcher wants to watch people in a public park without telling them. Is this allowed?
Idea. It can be allowed if no private information is collected and no harm is done, but an ethics board must decide.
2. A study uses a false story about its aim. Is it allowed?
Idea. Sometimes, but only if there is no other way and the researcher debriefs the participants.
3. A researcher wants to use data from a clinic. Can she do it without asking the patients?
Idea. Often not. Data are private. She needs permission or must remove all names.
4. A participant says she wants to stop. The researcher says 'Please continue.' Is this allowed?
Idea. No. Participants must be free to leave at any time.
5. A study on children. Who must give consent?
Idea. Parents or guardians must give consent. The child should also agree if she is old enough.
