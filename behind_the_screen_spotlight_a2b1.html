The film, in three parts

Tom McCarthy's Spotlight (2015). Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one. (There are spoilers — that is fine, this is real history and we want to talk about it. This is a serious subject, so the film — and this lesson — focus on the reporters and the cover-up, not on the abuse itself.)

Part 1 — A new boss asks a question

In 2001, a newspaper in Boston gets a new boss. His name is Marty. He is new to the city, and he is not Catholic — that is unusual in Boston. One day, Marty reads a small story in another newspaper. A lawyer says that a priest hurt children for many years, and that Church leaders knew about it. Marty asks a small team of reporters, called Spotlight, to look into this properly.

The team is not sure at first. They think it is a sad story about one bad priest. But Marty also asks them to do something bigger: to go to court and ask for secret Church documents. Slowly, the reporters start to talk to grown-ups who were hurt as children. They hear the same story again and again: a priest, a quiet complaint, and then — instead of trouble for the priest — a move to a new church, where no one is told anything.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is Marty, and what does he ask the reporters to do?

2. What same story do the reporters hear again and again?

1. He's the newspaper's new boss; he asks the Spotlight team to properly investigate a lawyer's claim that a priest abused children and that Church leaders knew.

2. A priest hurts a child, someone complains quietly, and the priest is simply moved to a new church, with no warning to anyone there.

Talk about it:
  • Marty is new to the city and doesn't feel the same loyalty to the Church as people who grew up there. Do you think it's easier for an outsider to ask hard questions? Why?

Part 2 — Finding the pattern

The reporters look through old Church lists and files. One reporter, Matt, builds a list of priests who were often marked "away" or "sick" for strange periods of time. Slowly, the list of names grows — not two or three priests, but many more. This makes the reporters feel sick, too.

The Church does not want to help. Its lawyers fight to keep the important documents secret. Some people in the city do not want to believe the story and don't want to talk. Then, on the very morning the team is ready to push harder, a terrible thing happens: the attacks of September 11, 2001. The whole newspaper must stop and cover that instead, for months.

When the team comes back to the story later, they realise something hard: their own newspaper had small pieces of this story years before — and didn't follow them. Now, they must do it properly.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Matt help the team see how big the problem really is?

2. What makes the reporters stop working on the story for months?

1. He builds a list from Church records, finding priests often marked "away" in ways that match the years of the alleged abuse — showing this is a pattern, not one case.

2. The September 11 attacks; the whole newspaper has to cover that story instead.

Talk about it:
  • The reporters learn that their own newspaper missed chances to tell this story years earlier. Why do you think even good people and good organisations can be slow to face a hard truth?

Part 3 — The true story is printed

In January 2002, the newspaper is ready. It prints the true story — not about one priest, but about a whole system that protected many priests for years. The story names the leader of the Boston Church, Cardinal Law, directly, showing that he knew and did nothing.

After the story, many more people call the newspaper. Many of them have never told anyone about what happened to them before. Every week, the newspaper prints new stories, and the number of priests involved grows — from a few, to dozens, and then to many more.

By the end of 2002, Cardinal Law resigns. The story does not stay only in Boston: other cities, and other countries, start asking the same hard questions about their own churches.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the newspaper's first story, in January 2002, prove?

2. What happens after the story is printed?

1. It proves that Cardinal Law and Church leaders knew about the abuse and moved priests instead of stopping them.

2. Many more people come forward, the number of priests involved grows, and Cardinal Law resigns before the end of the year.

Talk about it:
  • The story that started in Boston ends up affecting many other countries too. Does that make it a bigger, more important story — or does it also make it easier to forget who was really responsible?

Grammar: the present perfect

This film is full of things that happened in the past but still matter now. English uses the present perfect for this, with some useful little words: ever, never, just, already and yet.

have/has + past participle for life experience: "Have you ever read a shocking news story?" "I have never met a real reporter."
just = a short time ago: "The reporter has just found an important paper."
already = sooner than you expected: "The team has already spoken to ten people."
yet = not happened, used in negatives and questions: "The newspaper hasn't printed the story yet." "Have they finished the report yet?"

Use ever in questions about experience, never for "at no time," already when something happened sooner than expected, and yet in negatives and questions about something that hasn't happened.

The Real Story — is any of this true?

Here is the important part: this is not just a film. It really happened, almost exactly like this.

📰 A real newspaper team did this

A real newspaper, the Boston Globe, had a real new boss called Marty Baron in 2001. He really did ask a small team of reporters, called Spotlight, to investigate. The lawyer in the story, Mitchell Garabedian, was real too, and he had spent years trying to make people listen.

The reporters did not guess — they checked

The reporters did not just believe one story. They used old Church records to find a pattern: many priests were often marked "away" in the same strange way. They also fought in court to see secret documents. Only when many different people, who had never met each other, told the same story did the newspaper feel sure enough to print it.

What really happened next

The Boston Globe printed its first story on 6 January 2002. Over that year, the number of priests involved in Boston grew to about 249, and more than 1,000 people said they had been abused. Cardinal Law resigned in December 2002. In 2003, the Spotlight team won the Pulitzer Prize — the biggest prize in journalism — for this work. Other cities and countries then started asking the same questions about their own churches.

Talk about it:
  • Before this lesson, did you know the number of people affected was so big?
  • The film does not show the abuse itself — it shows the reporters working. Do you think that is the right way to tell this story?
  • Does it matter that a film changes small details, if the big facts — the cover-up, the numbers, the resignation — are all true?
