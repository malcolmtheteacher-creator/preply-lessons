Meet the poem

Read it once, straight through, without stopping to analyse. Line numbers are every fifth line.

Ozymandias · Percy Bysshe Shelley (1818)

1I met a traveller from an antique land,
Who said—"Two vast and trunkless legs of stone
Stand in the desert. . . . Near them, on the sand,
Half sunk a shattered visage lies, whose frown,
5And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command,
Tell that its sculptor well those passions read
Which yet survive, stamped on these lifeless things,
The hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed;
And on the pedestal, these words appear:
10My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings;
Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!
Nothing beside remains. Round the decay
Of that colossal Wreck, boundless and bare
The lone and level sands stretch far away."

SPEAK: Finish these sentences aloud, then jot a few words.

  1. The statue looks… (three adjectives)
  2. The king’s words sound… but the scene around them is…

Who is speaking?

The poet meets a traveller, who tells the poet what he saw. The traveller describes a statue. The statue carries the king’s own words.

SPEAK: Why might Shelley place so many voices between the reader and the king?

The layers (poet, traveller, sculptor, king) create distance in time and space. The king’s boast reaches us third-hand, so his power has already faded into a story, and the distance itself suggests how far his authority has drifted.

Your analysis toolkit

For each one: name it, point to it, explain the effect. Say the effect before you reveal it.

Imagery of decay

Words for broken, empty and ruined things.

SPEAK: Find three images of damage. What do they have in common?

“Trunkless legs”, “shattered visage”, “colossal Wreck”: the body of power is in pieces. Everything that was meant to look permanent is fragmented.

Irony

A gap between what is said and what is true.

SPEAK: What is ironic about “Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!”?

The king meant the Mighty to despair at his greatness. The reader looks and sees nothing left, so the despair now belongs to anyone who believes power lasts.

Structure and sound

Sonnet form, caesura, alliteration, the final line.

SPEAK: Read the last two lines aloud. What do the sounds and the line length do?

The long, open vowels and the alliteration of “lone and level” stretch the line across empty sand. The form of a sonnet is usually compact, but the ending opens out into emptiness, which mirrors the idea.

Tone and word choice

“Sneer of cold command”, “frown”, “wrinkled lip”.

SPEAK: What attitude does the sculptor capture in the face? What is Shelley’s own attitude?

The face shows contempt and arrogance, and the sculptor read these “passions” accurately. Shelley’s tone toward the king is mocking, but not angry: the poem lets time do the criticism.

Check: which tool?

“The king’s boast now describes the opposite of what he intended.”

“Shattered visage”, “trunkless legs” and “colossal Wreck” all show broken things.

“The long final line spreads across empty space, like the desert itself.”

Close reading

Round 1: Model, then you. Spot the difference before you read on.

Weak: The poem shows that the statue is broken and so the king is not powerful.

Strong: By juxtaposing the king’s inscription, “Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!”, with the “boundless and bare” sand that surrounds it, Shelley exposes the irony of human pride: the monument meant to prove permanence becomes the proof of decay.

SPEAK: Tell me three things the strong version does better.

It quotes precisely, names the technique (juxtaposition, irony), explains the effect, and links to the larger idea about pride and time, with precise verbs (exposes, proves).

Four key moments

A. “Half sunk a shattered visage lies, whose frown, / And wrinkled lip, and sneer of cold command…”
B. “…the hand that mocked them, and the heart that fed”
C. “My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings; / Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!”
D. “Nothing beside remains.”

SPEAK: Pick one. Name the technique, explain the effect, and connect it to power.

A: The piled-up facial features (“frown”, “wrinkled lip”, “sneer”) show arrogance frozen in stone. B: “Mocked” and “fed” are ambiguous: the sculptor mocked the king’s passions, or the king mocked his subjects; “fed” hints that the ruler’s heart fed on its own pride. Either way, the artist outlasts the tyrant. C: The capital letters and commanding imperative show total self-assurance, which makes the collapse more ironic. D: A short, flat sentence delivers the verdict bluntly after the grand boast.

Round 3: Challenge your reading.

SPEAK: Does the poem say all power is doomed, or only arrogant power? And what does last: the king, the sculptor, or the poem?

From poem to argument

Point: Shelley suggests that power is…
Evidence: This is shown when… (a short quotation)
Technique: The use of… creates…
Effect: This makes the reader…
Link: Therefore the poem warns that…

SPEAK: Rank from weakest to strongest and defend your order.

  1. The poem is about a broken statue in a desert.
  2. Shelley uses irony and decay imagery to argue that pride in power is foolish because time destroys all human monuments.
  3. Shelley hates kings.
Number 2 is strongest: arguable, specific and linked to technique. Number 1 only describes. Number 3 is an unsupported claim about the poet’s opinion.

Key words

irony (noun) — a gap between what is said or expected and what is true. Example: "The irony is that the boast describes the opposite of the scene."
sonnet (noun) — a poem of fourteen lines with a fixed rhyme pattern. Example: "The sonnet form is compact, but the last line opens out."
visage (noun) — a person's face, especially in a literary style. Example: "The shattered visage still shows contempt."
colossal (adjective) — extremely large. Example: "The colossal statue is now a ruin."
hubris (noun) — excessive pride that leads to downfall. Example: "The poem is a study of hubris."
transience (noun) — the quality of lasting only a short time. Example: "The poem explores the transience of power."
juxtaposition (noun) — placing two different things side by side for contrast. Example: "The juxtaposition of the boast and the desert is striking."
undermine (verb) — to weaken something gradually. Example: "The setting undermines the king's claim."
