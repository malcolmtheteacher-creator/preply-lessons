The Cuban Missile Crisis

13 days when the world nearly went to war.

Today: tell the story of 13 days when the world nearly had a big war, and talk about war and peace. Grammar: "if" sentences with would.

Key words — match them first

Before we read, let's learn the important words. Match each word (1–8) to its meaning (A–H). Say your answers out loud — "I think 1 is D" — then check.

Words

  1. a war
  2. a missile (a weapon)
  3. an enemy
  4. dangerous
  5. a leader / a president
  6. a deal (an agreement)
  7. to stop
  8. afraid

Meanings

  • A. the most important person in a country
  • B. when two countries fight each other
  • C. a person or country that wants to hurt you
  • D. a flying weapon that can destroy a city
  • E. scared; you feel that something bad may happen
  • F. not safe; it can hurt or kill people
  • G. to make something not happen, or not continue
  • H. when two sides say yes and agree to do something

1 = B (a war) · 2 = D (a missile) · 3 = C (an enemy) · 4 = F (dangerous)

5 = A (a leader / a president) · 6 = H (a deal) · 7 = G (to stop) · 8 = E (afraid)

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — A dangerous secret

In 1962, two very powerful countries almost started a terrible war. They were the United States (USA) and the Soviet Union (Russia). At that time, these two countries were not friends. They were enemies.

This story is called the Cuban Missile Crisis. It lasted 13 days in October 1962.

American spy planes flew high in the sky and took photos. The photos showed something frightening. The Soviet Union was secretly building places for nuclear missiles on a small island called Cuba. Cuba is very close to America. From Cuba, a missile could hit an American city very quickly. This was very dangerous.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which two countries were in this story? Were they friends or enemies?

2. What did the American spy planes find in Cuba?

1. The United States (USA) and the Soviet Union (Russia). They were enemies — they were not friends.

2. They found that the Soviet Union was secretly building places for nuclear missiles on Cuba, very close to America.

Talk about it:
  • America found the missiles with photos from spy planes. Do you think it is OK for a country to take secret photos of another country? Why?

Part 2 — The world is afraid

The American president was a man called John F. Kennedy. When he saw the photos, he was very worried.

President Kennedy wanted to stop more weapons from coming to Cuba. So he used American ships. The ships made a line around the island. They were there to stop Soviet ships with more missiles.

For many days, the whole world was very afraid. People thought there could be a nuclear war. A nuclear war would kill millions of people. Nobody knew what would happen next.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was the American president? How did he feel?

2. What did Kennedy do with the American ships?

1. The president was John F. Kennedy. He was very worried.

2. He used the ships to make a line around Cuba, to stop more weapons from arriving.

Talk about it:
  • For many days, people all over the world were afraid. How do you feel when you hear frightening news on TV or online?

Part 3 — A deal, and peace

The most dangerous day was 27 October. On this day, many people were afraid the war would start.

But the leaders of both countries did not want a war. They knew a war would be terrible for everybody. So they talked, and they tried to find an answer.

On 28 October, they made a deal. The Soviet Union agreed to take the missiles away from Cuba. America agreed not to attack Cuba. Both sides gave something, and both sides got something. There was no war. The world was safe again.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the leaders not want a war?

2. What was the deal on 28 October?

1. They knew a war would be terrible for everybody.

2. The Soviet Union took the missiles away from Cuba, and America promised not to attack Cuba. So there was no war.

Talk about it:
  • The leaders chose to talk, not to fight. In your life, is it sometimes better to talk than to fight? When?

Grammar: "if" sentences with would

In this story, people were afraid of things that did not really happen. We can talk about an imagined situation with "if" and would.

"If" + would = imagine a situation.
We use: If + past simple, … would + verb.
"If there was a war, many people would die." (There is no war now — we only imagine it.)
"If you were the president, what would you do?" (You are not the president — we just imagine.)

1. Complete the sentences

Put the verbs in the right form: if + past simple, and would + verb.

1. If there (be) a nuclear war, it (be) terrible.

2. If the leaders (fight), millions of people (die).

3. If America (attack) Cuba, the Soviet Union (be) very angry.

1. was / would be

2. fought / would die

3. attacked / would be

2. Your turn — imagine and speak

Read each question and answer it out loud. Start your answer with "If…, I would…".

1. If you were a leader, what would you do? (Example: "If I were a leader, I would talk to the other side.")

2. If you had a big problem with a friend, what would you do?

3. If there was a big problem in your town, who would you ask for help?

1. "If I were a leader, I would try to make a deal." / "If I were a leader, I would not start a war."

2. "If I had a big problem with a friend, I would talk to them quietly."

3. "If there was a big problem in my town, I would ask the police (or my family) for help."

Remember: if + past simple, then would + verb.

Task B · Talk or fight? (3 minutes)

The leaders chose to talk, not to fight. Was that the right choice? Why do you think so? Is it always better to talk?

Task C · A big problem, no fighting (3 minutes)

Tell me about a time when you, or someone you know, solved a big problem without fighting. What was the problem? What did they do?

Not sure what to talk about? Choose one:

  • An argument with a friend or family member that you talked through and fixed.
  • A problem with a neighbour — noise, parking, rubbish — that you solved by talking.
  • A disagreement at work or school that you stopped before it got worse.
  • A time you and a friend both wanted different things, so you found a middle way.
  • Nelson Mandela — he chose to talk, not to fight, to bring peace to his country.
