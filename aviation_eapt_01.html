What the examiner is scoring, from your first word

Pronunciation
An accent is fine — the pass question is only: can a listener understand you without effort?
Structure & vocabulary
Basic grammar under control, and the right word most of the time — with a paraphrase ready when it isn't.
Fluency
A steady flow. Not fast — steady. Long silences cost more than small mistakes.
Comprehension & interaction
Understanding what was actually asked, answering that, and checking when unsure instead of guessing.
The single most common failure is not vocabulary and not grammar. It is the ten-word answer: “I check the weather at the airport. That's my job.” — followed by forty seconds of silence while the examiner writes. Sixty seconds is the space you are given to show your level; an empty half-minute is an empty half of your mark.
Warm up — feel the length of a minute

Talk about anything at all — your morning, the weather outside — and do not stop until the timer ends. Notice how long sixty seconds really is. That is the container every interview answer has to fill.

The 60-second answer — a shape you can trust under pressure

You cannot memorise answers — examiners hear recitation instantly. What you can memorise is a shape that turns any question into a full minute of organised English. Four moves, always in the same order.
1 · Answer the question
First sentence, directly: the fact, the date, the choice. “My job is to observe and report the weather at an international airport.”
2 · Give the reason or the story
One or two sentences of why or how: “Pilots and controllers depend on those reports, because…”
3 · Land one concrete example
A real morning, a real storm, a real report. Details are what fluency sounds like: “Last winter, for instance…”
4 · Leave a door open
End with something the examiner can pick up: “…and that's still the part of the job I like most.” You are feeding the conversation — that is the interaction mark.
The tense map that runs underneath: your job now takes the present simple (“I observe, I report, a shift starts at…”). The moment you chose it takes the past simple (“I decided in 2012, I started as…”). Everything from then until today takes the present perfect (“I have worked there for ten years / since 2014”). Choose the time zone first, and the grammar chooses itself.
1. The question is “When did you decide to choose a career in aviation?” Which opening earns marks?
2. Halfway through your answer, your mind goes blank on the word “binoculars”. The Level 5 move?
Build one answer with the shape (90 seconds)

Question: “What do you like about your job?” Say the four moves out loud — direct answer, reason, one concrete example, open door — and keep talking until the timer ends.

You & your job — the general question bank

These are the shapes the general questions take — every one of them is really a door into the same story: your work, your choice, your place, your plans. Answer each aloud for the full minute, then open the phrase box and steal anything better than what you said.
1. How would you describe your job?

Reach for: “I'm responsible for…” · “My main task is to…” · “In practice, that means…” · “The part other people depend on most is…” — and always one concrete example.
2. When did you decide to choose a career in the field of aviation?

Reach for: “It goes back to…” · “What first attracted me was…” · “I'd always been interested in…, so when the chance came…” · “…and I've been doing it ever since.” A moment, a reason, a result.
3. What kind of responsibilities do you have?
01:00
Reach for: “Above all, I'm accountable for…” · “Part of my role is also to…” · “If I get it wrong, the consequence is…” — responsibility language is consequence language.
4. Can you describe your workplace? What kind of place is your work environment?
01:00
Reach for: “Physically, it's…” · “What makes it unusual is…” · “The atmosphere changes completely when…” — describe the place, then describe the feeling of working in it.
5. Why is teamwork important in your job?
01:00
Reach for: “No single person can…” · “My information only matters if…” · “We rely on each other to…” — and one story of a day teamwork actually mattered.
6. How do you see yourself in ten years' time?
01:00
Reach for: “I'm hoping to…” · “I'd like to move towards…” · “Whatever happens, I want to keep…” — honest and modest beats ambitious and vague.
Prepare the story, not the speeches. All six questions enter the same territory through different doors: what you do, when and why you chose it, where you do it, who you do it with, and where it is going. Know that story flexibly — able to start from any door — and no general question can surprise you.

Upgrade the answer — judge it like the examiner

Same question, weak and strong answers. Your job: find exactly why the weak ones lose marks — because the fault you can name in someone else's answer is the fault you can fix in your own. Question throughout: “What advice would you give to a young person who wanted to do this job?”
1. Answer A: “Study hard and be serious.” — What is its real problem?
2. Answer B: “I am telling him he must to study the meteorology since many years before he can working here.” — Which repair fixes the most at once?
Now say the Level 5 version (60 seconds)

Give the advice answer properly: the direct advice, the reason, one example from your own path, and a closing door. Then compare with one strong version.

Something like: “I'd tell them two things. First, learn the science properly, because everything in this job rests on it — I spent three years studying before I ever wrote a real report. But second, and people forget this: work on your English early. The day something goes wrong, the science is only useful if you can communicate it clearly — and that's a skill you build long before you need it.” — advice, reasons, a personal detail, and a theme the examiner can follow up.

Mock section — six questions, test conditions

A full Section 1 run: six questions, 60 seconds each, no restarts. Read each aloud in the examiner's voice, answer in your own. If you need a repeat, ask for it properly — once: “Could you repeat the question, please?”
The interview (run all six on one 8-minute clock, or question by question)
  1. What attracted you to work in the field of aviation?
  2. What do you like most about your job?
  3. What is interesting about your workplace?
  4. How is your work organised?
  5. What are the disadvantages of doing this job?
  6. If you had the opportunity to try a new activity, what would you choose?
