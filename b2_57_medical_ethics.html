Medical Ethics: Where Do We Draw the Line?

B2 Level · Health & Society

Ethical Questions

Medical progress raises profound questions: When does innovation become intervention? Who decides what's acceptable? How do we balance individual choice with collective responsibility?

The Tension Between Innovation and Safety

Medicine advances through research, but research requires testing. We need human trials to understand if treatments work and whether they're safe. Yet testing on humans carries risks. Historically, vulnerable populations—prisoners, the poor, the institutionalised—have been exploited in the name of science. Today, ethical oversight exists, but questions remain about access, equity, and consent.

Consider: Should people have the right to take experimental treatments if they're dying and conventional medicine has failed? What about the risks to others if the treatment isn't fully tested?

When Science Goes Too Far

New technologies push boundaries. Gene editing can prevent hereditary diseases but could enable "designer babies." AI diagnoses diseases faster than human doctors but may lack contextual understanding. Extending human lifespan indefinitely raises questions about resource distribution and social inequality. Creating chimera organisms—mixing human and animal cells—challenges our definitions of what's human.

Access and Inequality

Advanced medical treatments are expensive. Who gets access? Should genetic treatments be available only to the wealthy? When breakthrough drugs are developed, should they be made available immediately or after longer testing? How do we balance individual benefit with collective good? These aren't merely ethical questions—they're deeply political.

Reflection: What ethical concern in medicine worries you most? Why?

The Role of Consent

Informed consent—understanding risks and benefits before agreeing to treatment—is fundamental to medical ethics. But true informed consent is difficult. How much information is enough? Can people truly understand complex medical scenarios? What about unconscious patients? What about patients with cognitive impairments? When does consent from parents or guardians supersede individual autonomy?

Think About It

Should parents be able to make permanent medical decisions for children, like gene editing? At what age should young people have decision-making power over their own medical care?

Medical Language

Key vocabulary for discussing medical ethics:

Organ Donation The voluntary transfer of organs or tissues from a donor to a person in need of transplantation
Clinical Trial A research study conducted with human participants to test safety and effectiveness of medical treatments
Informed Consent Permission given by a patient after understanding the benefits, risks, and alternatives of a treatment
Euthanasia Intentionally ending the life of a patient to end their suffering, typically in terminal illness
Genetic Screening Testing DNA to identify inherited diseases or predispositions to certain conditions
Artificial Intelligence in Medicine Computer systems that diagnose diseases, predict outcomes, or recommend treatments using algorithms
Confidentiality The obligation to keep a patient's medical information private and protected
Malpractice Failure by a healthcare professional to provide adequate care, resulting in harm to the patient

Phrases for Discussing Ethical Positions

  • "To weigh the pros and cons" — to compare advantages and disadvantages
  • "On one hand... on the other hand" — presenting both sides of an argument
  • "The ethical dilemma" — a difficult choice with moral implications
  • "To prioritise..." — to consider something as more important
  • "The greater good" — what benefits society overall, not just individuals
  • "Autonomy" — the right to make decisions about yourself
  • "To compromise on..." — to accept a solution that's partially acceptable
  • "The slippery slope" — the idea that one decision could lead to unwanted consequences

Language in Context

Example discussion:

"I understand the argument for genetic screening, but I worry about the slippery slope. Once we start selecting for traits, where does it end? On the other hand, preventing hereditary diseases clearly benefits individuals and families. The ethical dilemma is balancing individual autonomy with concerns about inequality and how we define a 'worthy' life."

Case Studies

Case 1: Choosing Your Baby's Genes

The Situation: A couple has a history of cystic fibrosis in their family. New genetic editing technology could eliminate this disease from their child's genes. It's expensive, not yet completely risk-free, and not legal in all countries. Should they do it?

Discussion Prompts:

  • Is this fundamentally different from other parental decisions like education or nutrition?
  • What if the technology were also offered for traits like intelligence or appearance?
  • Who should make this decision—parents, doctors, society?
  • What about fairness to those who can't afford the technology?
Ethical Considerations:
  • Beneficence (benefit): Preventing suffering is good
  • Autonomy: Parents should decide for their children, but what about the child's future autonomy?
  • Justice: Access is unequal; might widen gap between wealthy and poor
  • The "enhancement vs. therapy" line: Disease prevention is acceptable to most; enhancement is controversial

Case 2: The Transplant List

The Situation: There's only one donor heart available. Two patients need it: a 40-year-old alcoholic who damaged their heart through drinking, or a 60-year-old doctor who worked to help others their whole life. Hospital guidelines say allocate based on medical urgency and likelihood of survival. The alcoholic is more urgent and has better survival chances. Who gets the organ?

Discussion Prompts:

  • Should people's past behaviour affect medical treatment?
  • Is it fair to judge someone's "worthiness" of treatment?
  • How do we make scarce resource decisions ethically?
  • Should social contribution matter in medical allocation?
Ethical Considerations:
  • Utilitarianism: Choose who survives longest and adds most good
  • Fairness: Should past behaviour determine current treatment?
  • Medical ethics: Clinical criteria only, not moral judgment
  • Real-world practice: Most systems use medical factors exclusively to avoid bias

Case 3: Experimental Treatment for a Dying Child

The Situation: A 10-year-old child has a rare cancer. Standard treatments have failed. Parents want to try an experimental drug not yet approved, tested only in early trials with unknown effects. The child is too young to consent independently. There's a small chance it works and a significant risk of serious harm. What should doctors do?

Discussion Prompts:

  • Should "hope" influence medical decisions?
  • How much risk is acceptable when the alternative is death?
  • Who decides—parents, doctors, or the child?
  • How do we balance individual cases with the need for reliable clinical evidence?
Ethical Considerations:
  • Beneficence vs. non-maleficence: Helping vs. not causing harm—which takes priority?
  • Autonomy: Child's inability to consent; parental authority limits
  • Research ethics: Need evidence for treatments; individual cases can't guide policy
  • Hope vs. realism: Emotional hope doesn't equal medical probability

Your View

Healthcare Systems & Innovation

Medical ethics doesn't exist in a vacuum. Healthcare systems vary globally—some emphasise individual choice, others collective welfare. Some nations lead in innovation; others struggle with access to basic care. Ethical decisions differ based on context and resources.

Discussion: How does your country's healthcare system approach these ethical questions? Is it different from systems in other countries you know about?

The Rise of Medical AI

Artificial intelligence now diagnoses diseases, predicts patient outcomes, and recommends treatments. AI can process vast data and may outperform human doctors in specific tasks. But AI lacks contextual understanding, may embed historical biases in data, and raises questions about accountability. If an AI makes a wrong diagnosis, who's responsible?

Reflection

Would you trust a diagnosis from AI? What would you need to feel confident in an AI-based medical recommendation?

Balancing Innovation with Ethics

We can't stop medical progress, nor should we. But progress without ethical guardrails causes harm. The challenge is fostering innovation whilst protecting vulnerable people, ensuring access and equity, and maintaining public trust in medicine.

Final Discussion Points

  • Medical ethics ultimately reflect broader societal values—who we are, what we care about, what kind of world we want to build
  • There are rarely perfect answers; often we must choose between imperfect options
  • Your voice matters—as a patient, citizen, and person thinking about the future
  • The best ethical frameworks combine principles (beneficence, autonomy, justice) with humility about complexity
