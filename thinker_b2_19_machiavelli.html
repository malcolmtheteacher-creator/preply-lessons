Can a good result ever justify bad methods?

A new mayor promises to clean up a city full of crime. She keeps her promise, and the streets become safe. But later you learn how she did it: she lied to the public, broke rules and quietly pushed out honest officials who disagreed with her. Was she a good leader? Five hundred years ago, a diplomat from Florence wrote a small book that still makes people argue about questions like this.

Today: You'll meet Niccolò Machiavelli and his book The Prince, so you can talk about ends and means and argue whether a good result can justify a bad method.

Meet Machiavelli

Read one part at a time. After each part, close your eyes and answer the question before you check.

🧭

A diplomat in Florence

Niccolò Machiavelli was born in Florence in 1469, during the Renaissance. In 1498 he became a senior official of the Florentine Republic. For fourteen years he travelled as a diplomat, meeting kings, popes and generals. He watched closely how they won and lost power. One of them, the ruthless Cesare Borgia, impressed him deeply: he was clever, fast and willing to do almost anything to win.

What did Machiavelli learn as a diplomat?

He watched closely how rulers really won and lost power, for example the ruthless Cesare Borgia.
⛓️

Prison and the farm

Then things changed. In 1512 the powerful Medici family returned to rule Florence, and the Republic fell. Machiavelli lost his job. In 1513 he was suspected of a plot against the Medici, arrested and tortured. After his release, he moved to his small farm outside the city. In the evenings, he wrote, he put on his best clothes before reading the great writers of the past. There he wrote The Prince.

Where and when did Machiavelli write The Prince?

On his small farm outside Florence, around 1513, after he lost his job and was arrested and tortured.
📕

A shocking little book

The Prince is a short guide for rulers. Earlier books told princes to be honest, generous and kind. Machiavelli described what he had actually seen. A ruler who is always good, he warned, will be ruined among so many who are not. Sometimes a prince must lie or be cruel to keep the state safe. It is better to be feared than loved, he wrote, if you cannot be both. Today 'Machiavellian' means cunning and ruthless, although in other books he praised free republics.

What did Machiavelli say about being feared or loved?

That it is better for a ruler to be feared than loved, if he cannot be both.
Talk: Machiavelli dedicated The Prince to a member of the Medici family, probably hoping to get a job again. Does knowing that change how you read his advice?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. In the film, the villain is completely ruthless: he betrays even his oldest friends.
ruthless (adj) — willing to hurt others or be cruel in order to get what you want. Example: "The company's ruthless new boss fired half the staff in one day."
2. She had a cunning plan: she let her rival speak first, then used his own words against him.
cunning (adj) — clever at getting what you want, often by tricking people. Example: "In the old story, the cunning fox fools all the other animals."
3. Instead of arguing about the ideal plan, the pragmatic manager chose the one they could finish this year.
pragmatic (adj) — dealing with problems in a practical way, based on what really works rather than on ideals. Example: "We need a pragmatic solution, not a perfect one."
4. The government tried to justify the new tax by saying it would pay for hospitals.
justify (v) — to give a good reason for something, to show that it is right. Example: "How can you justify spending so much on a phone?"
5. He manipulated his friends by making each one feel guilty until they did what he wanted.
manipulate (v) — to control or influence people in a clever, unfair way, for your own advantage. Example: "Some adverts manipulate our fears to sell us things."
6. Driven by ambition, the young politician worked eighteen hours a day to reach the top.
ambition (n) — a strong wish to be successful, powerful or rich. Example: "Her ambition is to run her own company by the age of thirty."
7. A judge with integrity will never accept money to change a decision.
integrity (n) — the quality of being honest and having strong moral principles. Example: "Even her opponents respected her integrity."

The idea: Ends and means

🎯
The endDecide the goal: a safe, stable state.
🛠️
The meansLook at every method that could reach it.
⚖️
The costAsk what each method costs, and who pays.
🏁
The resultMachiavelli: rulers are judged mainly by results.

See it in action

Here is the idea in action. A football coach wants to win the league: that is the end. One way is to train harder and buy better players. Another way is to lie to young players about their contracts so they work harder, and to drop a loyal captain without warning. A Machiavellian coach asks only: what works? A critic asks: even if we win, what have we lost? Machiavelli made people face that question honestly, instead of pretending that good leaders never get their hands dirty.

Thought experiment: The lie that saves a city

A leader learns that a dangerous storm will hit the city in three days. If she tells the truth now, there may be panic, and people could be hurt in the chaos. If she says it's only a 'routine safety exercise', the city can be emptied calmly, but she will have deceived everyone.

Give two reasons for your choice. Then argue for the other side for thirty seconds. What would Machiavelli say? Would you trust her afterwards?

Useful language: Ends and means

in order to...Machiavelli said a ruler may have to be cruel in order to keep the state safe.
at the cost of...The coach won the league at the cost of his players' trust.
It's worth it if...Some people think a small lie is worth it if it saves lives.
The end doesn't justify...For many people, the end doesn't justify the means: you can't do wrong to achieve good.

Connect

Compare

Plato and Machiavelli: Plato imagined an ideal city ruled by wise philosopher-kings. Machiavelli said he would write about how things really are, not about imaginary republics. Which is more useful for a leader: an ideal to aim at, or a clear picture of reality?
Plato's ideal gives leaders something to aim for and a standard to judge them by. Machiavelli's realism helps them survive in a world where others don't play fair. Perhaps a good leader needs both: an ideal for the direction, and realism for the road.
Marcus Aurelius and Machiavelli: Marcus Aurelius was a Roman emperor who tried to rule as a good, calm Stoic. What would Machiavelli say about him, and what would Marcus say about Machiavelli?
In The Prince, Machiavelli actually praises Marcus as a just and kind emperor who was respected all his life, but he notes that Marcus inherited his throne, which made things easier. Marcus would probably reply that a ruler who gives up his integrity has lost the one thing truly in his control, even if he keeps his throne.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From William of Ockham

What does Ockham's razor say?

Don't assume more things than you need: if two explanations fit the facts, choose the simpler one.
From Averroes (Ibn Rushd)

What did Averroes believe about faith and reason?

That truth cannot contradict truth: real faith and careful reasoning must agree, and if they seem to clash, the words of scripture should be interpreted more deeply.
From Pyrrho

What did Pyrrho think we should do, since we can't be sure of anything?

Suspend judgement: don't insist that anything is true or false. He believed this brings peace of mind.
From Socrates

Why did the oracle call Socrates the wisest man in Athens?

Because he knew that he knew nothing, while others believed they knew things they didn't.
