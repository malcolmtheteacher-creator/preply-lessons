Formal Language, Tone, and Style in Scholarly Writing

Register: The Language of Context

Register refers to the variety of language appropriate to particular situations. Academic register is the formal, objective, evidence-based language expected in scholarly writing. It differs markedly from conversational, journalistic, or creative writing.

Characteristics of Academic Register

Formal Tone: Impersonal, objective, serious. Avoids colloquialisms, slang, and casual expressions.

Precision: Vocabulary chosen for exactness. Terms defined explicitly, especially technical or field-specific language.

Nominalisation: Converting verbs and adjectives to nouns (analyse > analysis; important > importance) to create dense, formal expression.

Passive Voice: Used strategically for objectivity and to foreground actions/findings rather than agents.

Hedging and Qualification: Language indicating tentativeness (may, suggests, appears) and appropriate caution about claims.

Complex Syntax: Multi-clause sentences reflecting complex ideas. Subordinate clauses and parenthetical information common.

Third Person: Preference for "the research shows" over "I found," though first person increasingly acceptable in some fields.

Why Register Matters

Register signals disciplinary membership. Mastering academic register demonstrates you understand scholarly conventions and are participating in academic conversations seriously. It also serves functional purposes: objectivity signals impartiality; precision reduces ambiguity; formal tone signals importance.

Register Across Disciplines

While general academic register principles apply broadly, disciplinary variation exists. STEM writing may emphasise passive voice and nominalisation more; humanities writing may accept more first-person perspective; social sciences occupy middle ground.

Contrasting Registers

Informal/Conversational

"A lot of studies have shown that stress can mess with your memory."

Issues: "a lot of," "mess with" are colloquial; vague agent; no attribution.

Academic Register

"Extensive empirical evidence demonstrates that psychological stress impairs mnemonic function."

Qualities: Specific ("extensive empirical evidence"), formal vocabulary, nominalisation ("mnemonic function").

Informal/Conversational

"I think the reason why climate change is such a big deal is because it" + "'" + "s going to affect where people can live."

Issues: First person unnecessary; hedging too casual; vague ("big deal," "affect"); "is" linking verb weak.

Academic Register

"Anthropogenic climate change poses substantial threats to human habitation patterns by altering precipitation and temperature regimes across diverse geographic regions."

Qualities: Nominalisation ("anthropogenic climate change," "habitation patterns"), passive construction, specific vocabulary, complex syntax.

Informal/Conversational

"Women" + "'" + "s lack of progress in science is pretty obvious when you look at how few female professors there are."

Issues: "pretty obvious" is colloquial; prescriptive assumption; weak evidence framing.

Academic Register

"Persistent underrepresentation of women in academic science is evident in disparities in professorial rank, with women comprising approximately 15% of full professors in STEM disciplines."

Qualities: Specific data, formal nominalisation, objective presentation, evidence-based.

Key Register Shifts

Avoiding Contractions

Informal: "It" + "'"s clear that..." Academic: "It is evident that..."

Informal: "They" + "'"re showing..." Academic: "They demonstrate..." or "Evidence shows..."

Eliminating Informal Intensifiers

Informal: "really important," "so significant," "quite interesting"

Academic: "significant," "substantial," "noteworthy" (let adjectives carry weight without intensifiers)

Using Nominalisation

Informal: "We analyse the data to understand patterns."

Academic: "Analysis of data elucidates patterns." or "Data analysis reveals patterns."

Strategic Passive Voice

Informal: "We conducted interviews with 30 participants."

Academic: "Semi-structured interviews were conducted with 30 participants." (focuses on method, not agent)

Precise Verbs

Weak: "showed," "seems," "is," "gets"

Strong: "demonstrates," "suggests," "indicates," "acquires," "deteriorates," "exhibits"

Register Revision Practice

Example 1: Casual Language

Original: "It" + "'"s really important to understand how social media affects teenagers" + "'" + " mental health because it" + "'"s a huge problem that" + "'"s getting worse every day."

Revised: "Understanding the impact of social media on adolescent mental health is crucial, given escalating evidence of psychological harm and the ubiquity of digital platforms in youth populations."

Changes: Eliminated contractions ("It" + "'"s"); replaced "really" and "huge" with specific words ("crucial," "escalating," "ubiquity"); converted "affects" to nominalisation "impact."

Example 2: First-Person Informality

Original: "I did a study and found that people" + "'" + "s attitudes change when they" + "'"re exposed to different opinions."

Revised: "This investigation examined attitude change in response to exposure to diverse viewpoints."

Changes: Removed first-person agent ("I"); nominalised action ("did a study" > "This investigation"); formalised result ("found" > "examined"); nominalised the mechanism ("attitudes change" > "attitude change").

Example 3: Weak Verb and Vague Expression

Original: "The results show that climate change is really bad for the environment and it makes lots of different species go extinct."

Revised: "Findings demonstrate that anthropogenic climate change precipitates biodiversity loss through multiple mechanisms, including habitat destruction and phenological disruption."

Changes: Upgraded verb ("show" > "demonstrate"); replaced "really bad" with specific terminology; added precision ("lots of species" > "biodiversity loss"); nominalised effects.

Academic Register Without Losing Your Voice

An important misconception: mastering academic register means becoming verbose and losing your voice. Actually, academic register is precise, clear, and efficient. It demands clarity.

Principles of Clear Academic Writing

Clarity Over Complexity: A clear, well-structured sentence is better than a convoluted one. Use subordination strategically, not to obfuscate.

Precision Over Pretension: Use sophisticated vocabulary when it adds precision, not to impress. "Utilise" isn" + "'"t superior to "use" if "use" is clearer.

Active Voice When Appropriate: While academic writing uses more passive voice, active voice can be clearer. "The researcher interviewed participants" is sometimes better than "Interviews were conducted with participants."

First Person Where Appropriate: Many disciplines now accept "I examined..." or "My analysis suggests..." when discussing your own work. Check disciplinary norms.

Developing Your Academic Voice

Voice vs. Register: Register is the formal code. Voice is your individual expression within that code. Two scholars writing in academic register will sound different based on their vocabulary choices, sentence patterns, and emphasis. Develop your voice by reading extensively in your field and letting scholarly language become natural.

Analysis: Formal vs. Authentic

Sample A (Overly Formal): "The aforementioned phenomenological investigation endeavoured to elucidate the multifaceted dimensions of pedagogical praxis within post-secondary educational milieus."

Sample B (Appropriately Formal): "This study examined how instructors" + "'" + " pedagogical choices shape learning outcomes in university classrooms."

Assessment: Sample A uses unnecessarily complex vocabulary. Sample B achieves formality through structure, vocabulary, and tone without excess.
