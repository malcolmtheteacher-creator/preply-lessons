The Upgrade

Take everyday language and make it academically precise. Spoken, not written.

Watch what changes when a sentence shifts from colloquial to academic English:

Example 1

Colloquial: "Loads of studies show that people who don't sleep enough do worse at work."
Academic: "Extensive research demonstrates that sleep deprivation correlates with diminished occupational performance."

Example 2

Colloquial: "Social media is pretty bad for young people's mental health."
Academic: "Emerging evidence suggests a potential negative association between social media engagement and adolescent psychological wellbeing."

Example 3

Colloquial: "Companies need to change the way they do things or they'll fail."
Academic: "Organisational adaptation to environmental change is increasingly critical for sustained competitive viability."

Here are the six key moves to shift from informal to academic register:

1Nominalisation ▼

What it means: Turn verbs and adjectives into nouns. It sounds more formal and abstract.

analyse → we analyse
the analysis of / the analytical framework
important → this is important
the importance of / of particular significance
2Impersonal Constructions ▼

What it means: Remove the person (I, you, we) from the sentence. Academic writing avoids personal opinion and centres evidence instead.

I think social media is bad.
It can be argued that social media presents concerns for adolescent wellbeing.
We found that students improved.
The data indicates that student performance improved.
3Precise Vocabulary ▼

What it means: Replace vague words with precise academic terms. Each word should carry exact meaning.

a lot of → a significant proportion of / a substantial increase in
shows → demonstrates / indicates / corroborates / suggests
affects → influences / modulates / correlates with
4Hedging Language ▼

What it means: Academic writing qualifies claims. Use words that soften absolute statements and match the strength of your evidence.

Social media causes depression.
Social media tends to / may / appears to / could contribute to depressive symptoms in some populations.

Common hedges: tends to, appears to, may suggest, to some extent, arguably, provisionally, under certain conditions

5Formal Connectives ▼

What it means: Connect ideas with formal transition words. This creates logical flow and sophistication.

but → Nevertheless, Conversely, By contrast
and → Furthermore, Additionally, In addition
so → Consequently, Therefore, As a result
because → Due to, Given that, In light of
6Citation Language ▼

What it means: Academic English integrates citations smoothly. Show whose idea you're referencing.

People say that remote work is better.
According to recent research (Smith, 2023), remote work arrangements correlate with increased productivity.

Patterns: According to X (year)... / As X demonstrates... / This finding aligns with X's argument that... / X's research suggests that...

Reflection

Academic register is not pretentious—it's precise. Every shift you made (nominalisation, formal connectives, hedging) serves a purpose: it makes your meaning clearer and more defensible. This is how experts communicate with each other.

Complete Register Reference

Nominalisation Patterns

analyse → the analysis of / analytical consideration
determine → the determination of / determinative factors
improve → improvement / enhancement
fail → failure / inadequacy
develop → development / the development of
significant → the significance of / of particular import
important → the importance of / critically, centrally

Impersonal Constructions

It can be argued that...
The evidence suggests that...
There is compelling reason to believe that...
It is widely acknowledged that...
The data indicate that...
Research has demonstrated that...

Precise Verbs (Replacing "shows/says")

demonstrates / corroborates / substantiates / validates
indicates / suggests / implies / proposes
argues / contends / posits / maintains
establishes / determines / ascertains
reveals / exposes / uncovers / elucidates

Hedging Language (Uncertainty/Qualifications)

tends to / may / might / could / appears to
arguably / to some extent / in certain contexts
potentially / provisionally / tentatively
the evidence suggests rather than proves / would seem / would appear
under specific conditions / in limited populations / within this framework

Formal Connectives

Contrast: Nevertheless, However, Conversely, By contrast, In opposition
Addition: Furthermore, Moreover, In addition, Additionally, Notably
Causation: Consequently, Therefore, As a result, Due to, Given that
Timing: Subsequently, Thereafter, Earlier, Previously, Initially
Emphasis: Indeed, Certainly, Notably, Significantly, Importantly

Citation Language

According to [Author, Year]...
As [Author] demonstrates...
This finding aligns with [Author]'s argument that...
[Author]'s research suggests that...
The literature indicates that / Research has established that...
