The Samurai

Japan's warrior class — and the code of honour that outlived their swords.

Today: you'll tell the story of a warrior class that rose, ruled and vanished — and discuss what a code of honour really costs — using relative and purpose clauses and the language of war and loyalty.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The rise of the warrior class

For most of Japan's early history, real power lay with the imperial court and its nobles. But from around the tenth and eleventh centuries, provincial families began raising private armies of skilled fighters to protect their land and enforce their will. These were the first samurai — a warrior class that would rise, over the next few centuries, from hired muscle to the true masters of Japan.

Their moment came in the late twelfth century. After years of war between rival clans, a victorious general established a new kind of government, led not by the emperor but by a supreme military commander: the shogun. The emperor remained on his throne and was honoured as a sacred figure — but it was the shogun who actually ruled. Below him stood the great lords, the daimyo, each controlling his own territory; and below them, the samurai who served as their warriors. Loyalty bound the whole pyramid together, flowing upward from samurai to lord, and from lord to shogun.

What set the samurai apart was not only the sword but the way they were expected to live. Over time their values hardened into an unwritten code known as bushido — "the way of the warrior" — built on loyalty, courage, honour, discipline and self-control. A samurai was meant to serve his lord absolutely, to face death without flinching, and to guard his good name above everything. The most famous symbol of all this was the katana, the elegant curved sword that a samurai was said to carry as if it held a piece of his own soul.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did real power lie once the shogun's government was established — and what was the emperor's role?

2. What was bushido, and which values did it ask of a samurai?

1. Real power lay with the shogun, the supreme military commander, supported by the daimyo lords and their samurai. The emperor kept his throne and was honoured as a sacred figure, but did not actually rule.

2. Bushido, "the way of the warrior," was the samurai's unwritten code of values — loyalty, courage, honour, discipline and self-control — including serving one's lord absolutely and facing death without fear.

Discuss:
  • The emperor was honoured but the shogun held the power. Why might a society keep a powerless figurehead at the top while real authority sits elsewhere?

Part 2 — War, peace, and the masterless warrior

For long stretches, the samurai's world was one of almost constant war. The most violent era came in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries — the Sengoku period, the "age of warring states" — when central authority collapsed and dozens of daimyo fought one another for survival and supremacy. It was a brutal, unstable time, but also the age that produced the most legendary warlords and the sharpest test of a samurai's skill and nerve.

Out of that chaos came unity. By the early seventeenth century, one family — the Tokugawa — had defeated its rivals and seized the shogunate, ushering in the Edo period: more than two centuries of strict, enforced peace. And here lay a strange irony. The samurai were a warrior class, yet now there were few wars to fight. Many became administrators, officials and teachers, fighting with brushes and ledgers rather than swords. Their code, bushido, was studied and written down more carefully than ever, precisely as the fighting that had created it faded away.

Honour, in this world, could be merciless. A samurai who failed his lord, suffered a deep disgrace, or wished to avoid capture might be expected to take his own life through a painful, formal ritual called seppuku — a way of restoring honour through unflinching self-discipline. And a samurai whose lord died or fell from power could become a ronin: a masterless warrior, adrift in a society built on service, with no place and no purpose. The figure of the lone, wandering ronin — proud, dangerous and free — became one of the most haunting images in all of Japanese culture.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the Sengoku period, and how did the Edo period that followed it change the samurai's role?

2. What was a ronin, and why was such a figure so troubling in samurai society?

1. The Sengoku period was the "age of warring states" (15th–16th centuries), when central power collapsed and the daimyo fought constantly. The Edo period that followed brought over two centuries of enforced peace, so many samurai became officials, administrators and teachers rather than fighters.

2. A ronin was a masterless samurai — one whose lord had died or lost power. In a society built entirely on loyalty and service, a warrior with no master had no clear place or purpose, which made the figure both pitiable and dangerous.

Discuss:
  • A warrior class kept its code most carefully once it had almost no wars left to fight. Why do people sometimes hold tightest to traditions just as the world that needed them disappears?

Part 3 — The end of the sword

The peace could not last forever. In the mid-nineteenth century, foreign warships arrived and forced Japan, after centuries of near-isolation, to open itself to the outside world. The shock exposed how far the country had fallen behind the industrial powers, and it shattered confidence in the old order. In 1868, in the upheaval known as the Meiji Restoration, the shogunate was swept away and power was returned, at least in name, to a young emperor and the modernising reformers around him.

The new government was determined to build a modern nation-state, and the samurai did not fit into it. A country with a Western-style conscript army had no need for a hereditary warrior caste — and, just as importantly, paying the samurai their traditional incomes was draining the treasury. Within a few years, their special privileges were stripped away: their stipends were ended, and they were even forbidden to carry their swords in public. The warrior class that had ruled Japan for seven hundred years was, quite deliberately, abolished.

Some samurai rose in rebellion against the loss of their world, but they could not stop it; the most famous revolt was crushed in 1877. And yet the samurai never truly vanished. Many former warriors became the soldiers, officials, teachers and businessmen who built modern Japan, carrying their discipline into a new age. More than that, the ideal of bushido — loyalty, honour, self-mastery — lived on in the national imagination and travelled around the world through films, books and legend. The men were gone; the image of the samurai has proved far harder to bury.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the modernising Meiji government decide it no longer needed a samurai class?

2. In what sense did the samurai not really disappear after their class was abolished?

1. A modern nation with a Western-style conscript army had no need for a hereditary warrior caste, and paying the samurai's traditional stipends was draining the treasury — so their privileges were ended and they were forbidden to carry swords in public.

2. Many former samurai became the soldiers, officials, teachers and businessmen who built modern Japan, and the ideal of bushido — loyalty, honour, self-mastery — lived on in the national imagination and spread worldwide through films, books and legend.

Discuss:
  • The samurai class was abolished, yet its image grew more powerful than ever afterwards. Why do we so often romanticise things only once they are safely in the past?

The grammar this story runs on: relative & purpose clauses

A story like this is full of people and things that need defining ("a warrior who lived by a code") and actions done for a reason ("they trained for years in order to master the sword"). Two kinds of clause do this work — get them right and your sentences carry far more in one breath.

The two tools:
• Relative clauses — they add information about a noun, using who (people), which (things), that (people or things) and whose (possession): "the sword which he carried," "the lord whose land they defended."
• Purpose clauses — they explain why something is done, using to, in order to, so as to (+ verb) or so that (+ clause): "they trained hard in order to master the katana," "a samurai obeyed his lord so that his honour stayed intact."

1. Relative clauses — who / which / that / whose

Fill each gap with the right relative pronoun, then combine the idea in your head as one smooth sentence.

1. The samurai were warriors served the daimyo lords.

2. The katana, was a curved sword, became the symbol of the whole class.

3. A ronin was a samurai lord had died or lost power.

4. The Sengoku period was an age central authority collapsed. (when / in which)

1. who (or that) — defining people

2. which — extra, non-essential information about a thing (note the commas)

3. whose — possession: the samurai's lord

4. when / in which — relating to a time

2. Purpose clauses — to / in order to / so that

Complete each sentence so it explains the reason for the action. Use to / in order to + verb, or so that + a full clause.

1. Samurai trained for years master the sword.

2. A samurai took his own life through seppuku restore his honour.

3. The Meiji government ended the samurai's stipends the treasury would not be drained. (so that…)

1. to / in order to / so as to master the sword

2. to / in order to restore his honour

3. so that the treasury would not be drained (use "so that" + clause when the subject changes)

3. Put them together — combine the sentences

Join each pair into one sentence using a relative clause, a purpose clause, or both. Read your answer aloud.

1. The shogun was a military commander. He ruled in the emperor's name. →

2. The samurai studied bushido carefully. They wanted to preserve their values in peacetime. →

1. The shogun was a military commander who ruled in the emperor's name.

2. The samurai studied bushido carefully in order to / so as to preserve their values in peacetime. (or: …so that their values would survive in peacetime.)

Both answers pack two ideas into one clean sentence — exactly what you want when you retell the story.
