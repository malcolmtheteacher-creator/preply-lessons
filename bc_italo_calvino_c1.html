MINDS WORTH MEETING · C1 · 50 min

Italo Calvino

The Italian writer who believed that lightness, play and pure invention could carry the weight of the world more truthfully than grim realism ever could.

You talk first — before you read anything

This is a talking lesson, not a reading one. So before a single fact, the floor is yours.

Start here · 2 minutes

Look at the title of this lesson: “lightness, play and pure invention”. What kind of writer do you imagine he was? What might it mean to carry the weight of the world through lightness? And have you read anything by him — what pulled you in, or kept you away? Just talk.

Now meet him — one beat at a time

Three short facts. After each one, you do the talking.

Calvino was born in 1923 — not in Italy at all, but in Cuba, where his Italian parents were working as botanists. The family soon returned to San Remo, and he grew up surrounded by his parents' gardens and laboratories, steeped in the precise, observant habits of science.

Over to you:
  • A writer raised among botanists and laboratories — what might a scientific eye give a storyteller that other writers lack? Do you notice the world more like a scientist or like a dreamer?

The war pulled him out of any quiet life. As a young man he joined the Italian partisans fighting the Fascists and the German occupation in the mountains above his home. That experience fed his first novel, a hard, neorealist book — but Calvino quickly grew restless inside realism. He felt that simply reporting the grim facts of the world somehow missed the truth of it.

Over to you:
  • He lived through real horror, then decided that reporting grim facts missed the truth. Can you understand that? When has a plain, factual account of something failed to capture what it was really like?

So he turned to fable, fantasy and elaborate games of structure — knights, climbing barons, talking particles at the dawn of the universe, impossible cities. Late in life he joined the Oulipo, a Paris group of writers and mathematicians who set themselves strict, playful constraints, convinced that limits set the imagination free. By his death in 1985 he was one of the most translated Italian writers of the century.

Over to you:
  • Knights, barons in trees, cosmic particles — do you trust fantasy to tell the truth about real life, or does it feel like an escape from it? Where is the line for you?

Lightness — and you do the thinking out loud

The doorway into Calvino is a single, slippery word: lightness. I will hand it to you in small pieces. After each piece, you talk — I will mostly listen.

Near the end of his life, Calvino was invited to give a famous series of lectures at Harvard, one for each value he thought literature should carry into the next century. The first, and the one closest to his heart, was Lightness. By it he did not mean shallowness. He believed the world is heavy — full of suffering and dull mechanical reality that turns people to stone — and that the writer's job is not to pile on more weight but to rise above it, finding the image or the leap of invention that lets us see the heaviness clearly without being crushed by it.

Put yourself there

So lightness is meant to be a way of taking the world more seriously, not less. Does that make sense to you, or does it sound like a contradiction? Think of something heavy in your own life — how might “rising above it” reveal it more clearly than staring straight at it?

This is why Calvino loved structure and games. A novel could be built like a deck of tarot cards, or as a catalogue of impossible cities. In Invisible Cities, the explorer Marco Polo describes city after city to the emperor Kublai Khan — and slowly you realise each city is not a place at all but a single idea given a shape you can walk through: a city of memory, a city of desire, a city of the dead.

Over to you:
  • If each city is really one feeling made into a place — and you had to turn memory, fear or desire into a city — which would you choose, and what would its streets be like?
  • Is there a danger here? Could turning real things into elegant games become a way of avoiding what should genuinely weigh on us?

For Calvino, the constraint was the freedom. A strict structure, far from boxing the imagination in, gave it walls to push against and surprising rooms to discover. Fantasy was not an escape from reality — it was a precise instrument for examining it, the way a scientist builds a clean model to see how something really works.

Take away the weight, he suggested, and you do not lose the truth — you finally see it floating clearly in front of you.

Take a side · 3–4 minutes

“A fairy tale can say something truer about life than a documentary can.” Argue for it or against it — and bring real examples: a film, a book, a story you were told as a child. Build your case, then I will push back and you defend it.

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that a fairy tale tells us more about reality than a documentary does.
  • It might well be that a strict form sets the imagination free rather than caging it.
  • I suspect the truth is somewhere in between.
  • You could argue that lightness is just a graceful way of avoiding what should weigh on us.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say that structure is the truest kind of freedom.
  • What strikes me most is how playful his most serious books are.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, his case for lightness reads as more urgent now than in 1985.

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure I fully buy that.
  • I take the point, but doesn't that assume…?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…

A · Build an Invisible City (3–4 min)

Calvino turned single ideas — memory, desire, fear — into cities you could walk through. Take one feeling that runs through your own life right now and build it into a city for me.

Describe it the way Marco Polo would: what are its streets like, what do people do there, what is strange about it? Then land the idea underneath in one sentence — what is this city really about?

B · Lightness vs. Weight (3–4 min)

Calvino argued that lightness — invention, humour, imagination — tells the truth about a heavy world better than grim realism does. But you live in an age of very serious problems.

Take one heavy issue from your own world — your work, your country, the news. Would you reach it better through Calvino's lightness, or does some weight demand to be met head-on? Make the case with a concrete example, then answer the strongest objection to your view.

C · The Power of the Rule (4–5 min)

The Oulipo writers believed a strict constraint sets the imagination free — write a story with no letter "e", and watch what happens. Invent your own constraint and apply it to something you do: cooking, work, a hobby, a relationship.

Describe the rule, walk me through what it would force you to discover, then defend it: is the limit really a doorway, or just a cage? I will push back, and you hold your ground.

Open discussion

  3. He left realism behind for fantasy and games. Do you trust a writer more when they stay close to "real life", or less?
