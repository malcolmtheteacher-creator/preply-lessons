Take Off Your Engineering Hat

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — The night before

On the evening of 27 January 1986, NASA planned to launch the space shuttle Challenger the next morning. The forecast was for an unusually cold night at the launch site in Florida, with temperatures falling well below freezing. The boosters that helped lift the shuttle were built by a company called Morton Thiokol, in Utah.

Some of Thiokol's engineers were worried. Rubber O-rings sealed the joints of the boosters, and on earlier flights the rings had been damaged — more often when the weather was colder. They feared that in the cold the rubber would stiffen and fail to seal. Among them were Roger Boisjoly and Arnie Thompson.

That evening, engineers and managers from Thiokol and NASA held a long telephone conference. The company's first recommendation, presented to NASA, was not to launch at temperatures below 53 degrees Fahrenheit, about 12 degrees Celsius — the temperature of the coldest launch the shuttle had made so far.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the forecast, and why did it matter?

2. What were the engineers afraid of?

3. What did Thiokol recommend at first?

1. A very cold night, well below freezing. Cold might stiffen the rubber O-rings that sealed the booster joints.

2. That the rubber would stiffen and fail to seal the joints in the cold, as had been seen in damage on earlier flights.

3. Not to launch below 53°F (about 12°C) — the coldest temperature at which the shuttle had launched so far.

Discuss:
  • The first recommendation was clear and gave a number. What makes a recommendation easier to defend — a number, a reason, or a story?

Part 2 — "Next April?"

NASA's managers did not welcome the recommendation. One of them is recorded as saying he was appalled at the reasoning. Another, Lawrence Mulloy, is quoted by several accounts as asking Thiokol: "When do you want me to launch — next April?" He later said he did not believe he was putting pressure on anyone that night.

The engineers' case had a weakness. The data showed damage and cold weather together on some flights, but not all, and the charts they presented did not make the pattern easy to see. Later analysts, including the statistician Edward Tufte, argued that a clearer chart of the whole record would have made the argument much stronger.

Thiokol's managers asked for a few minutes offline. The break, called a caucus, was meant to last five minutes. It took about half an hour.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did NASA's managers react to the first recommendation?

2. What was the weakness in the engineers' case?

3. What did Thiokol ask for, and how long did it last?

1. With resistance. One said he was appalled; another is recorded asking whether Thiokol wanted to launch "next April".

2. The data was not clear and their charts did not show the whole pattern; the case was easy to challenge.

3. A private caucus offline, planned for five minutes, which lasted about half an hour.

Discuss:
  • "When do you want me to launch — next April?" is not a question; it is a push. How could you answer a line like that calmly?

Part 3 — The hat

In the caucus, according to later testimony, a senior Thiokol executive told the company's vice-president for engineering to "take off your engineering hat and put on your management hat". The managers then voted among themselves. When the call resumed, Thiokol reversed its position and recommended that the launch go ahead. NASA accepted.

The next morning, 28 January, Challenger lifted off in very cold weather. Seventy-three seconds later it broke apart and all seven crew members were killed. The presidential commission that investigated found that the joint seal had failed and criticised the decision-making that led to the launch.

People still read the evening in two ways. In one reading, sound engineering advice was overruled by pressure from above and from the customer. In the other, the engineers did not have the clear, convincing data that would have held the room — and silence from those who still had doubts mattered too. Both readings have evidence. The question for anyone who has ever held an unpopular view is what would have made the position hold.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the executive tell the vice-president for engineering?

2. What happened after the call resumed?

3. What are the two ways people read the evening?

1. To take off his engineering hat and put on his management hat.

2. Thiokol reversed its position and recommended launching; NASA accepted. Challenger launched next morning and broke apart after 73 seconds, killing all seven crew.

3. Either that engineering advice was overruled by pressure — or that the engineers' data was not clear enough to hold the room. Both have evidence behind them.

Discuss:
  • What would you have needed to hold the position that night — a better chart, a calmer sentence, an ally, or something else?

How the tool works

Pushback tests two things at once: your position and your temperature. People tend to fail in one of two ways — they give way too fast, or they get sharp. The tool has four moves that keep you in the top-right corner of the grid: check the challenge, hold the position, bridge back to your point, and own the gap when you do not know.

Using it, step by step

  1. Check it. Slow the exchange and make sure you are answering the real question: If I understand correctly, you're asking whether…
  2. Hold it. Acknowledge the challenge, then restate your position with one reason: I hear that, and my recommendation stays the same, because…
  3. Bridge it. If the conversation drifts, bring it back to the point that matters: What matters most here is…
  4. Own the gap. If you do not know, say so and give a date: I don't have that figure to hand. I'll confirm it by Thursday.

Try it yourself

Someone has just pushed back on your recommendation. Is each reply too soft, firm and calm, or too sharp? Choose, then check.

1. I hear the concern, and my recommendation stands — the risk is small and the saving is real.
2. You're probably right. Maybe I haven't thought it through enough.
3. If you had read the report, you wouldn't be asking that.
4. Fine. We'll do it your way, then — and let's see how that goes.
5. That's a fair challenge. My view hasn't changed, and here is the evidence.
6. Oh, sorry — I didn't mean to push. Let's forget it.
7. I don't have that figure to hand. I'll confirm it by Thursday.

1. Firm and calm — it acknowledges the challenge, restates the position and gives one reason.

2. Too soft — it gives the position away before any argument has been made.

3. Too sharp — it attacks the person instead of answering the question.

4. Too sharp — it sounds like acceptance but it is an accusation; the tone is cold and the position is lost.

5. Firm and calm — it respects the challenge and answers it with substance.

6. Too soft — apologising for having a view closes the discussion.

7. Firm and calm — it admits the gap without losing authority, and it commits to a date.

Where the tool breaks

You may be wrong. Holding a position is not the same as refusing to listen. The aim is to change your mind for a good argument, not for a loud one: I'd change my view if I saw…

Weak evidence. Calm tone cannot rescue a thin case. In the story above, a clearer chart might have done more than a firmer voice.

Real danger. When safety, law or ethics are at stake, "firm and calm" may not be enough. You may need to put the concern in writing and take it higher.

Talk it through:
  • Which of the four moves would be hardest for you under real pressure, and why?
  • What would make you change your position in a meeting? Say it as a sentence beginning I'd change my view if…

Language — Conceding without giving way

To hold a position calmly you acknowledge the other view in one clause and keep your own in the next. The grammar of concession does exactly that — and each connector needs a different kind of structure after it.

although / even though + clause: Although the risk is real, it is smaller than the cost of waiting. After although you need a full clause with a subject and verb.
despite / in spite of + noun or -ing: Despite the risk, we recommend going ahead. Despite having doubts, she agreed. Never despite that the…
however / that said + a new sentence: The risk is real. That said, it is smaller than the cost of waiting. These connectors begin a second sentence and are followed by a comma.
unless = if … not: I'd keep my recommendation unless I saw new data. It is a way to show you are open to evidence without giving way to pressure.
