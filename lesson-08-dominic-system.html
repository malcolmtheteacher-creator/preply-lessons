People With Numbers

A British champion saw a man learn a pack of cards on television and went on to win the world title eight times. His system turns every pair of digits into a famous person.

In this lesson: you will learn the Dominic System letter code, turn number pairs into people, and use the rule that one person plus another person's action can hold four digits.

Vowels, w, h and y carry no digit, so you can add them to make real words.

The man who learned a pack of cards on television

In 1987 a young Englishman called Dominic O'Brien was watching television when a man appeared who could memorise the order of a shuffled pack of cards. The presenter shuffled, the man looked at each card once, and then he named the whole pack in order, without a mistake.

O'Brien did not think of himself as someone with a special memory. That evening he did something unusual. He picked up a pack of cards and tried to find out how the trick was done.

What happened in 1987 to change O'Brien's mind?
He saw someone memorise a shuffled pack of cards on television, and decided to find out how it was done.
Talk about it. Have you ever seen someone do something that made you say 'I could learn that'? What was it?

He read about the old methods: the places, the pictures, the stories. He trained, and he made up his own ways of speeding it up. In 1991 he won the first World Memory Championship, in London. He went on to win it eight times in all.

In 2002 he memorised fifty-four decks of cards, in a single viewing of each, a record that made the newspapers: 2,808 cards in total. He did it with the same two tools you have already learned, a long route of places and strong pictures. He also built a way of turning numbers into people.

What did O'Brien win?
The first World Memory Championship in 1991, and eight world titles in all.
Talk about it. What do you think was the hardest part of his training: making the pictures, or learning the places?

His idea is that a person is the best picture there is. A word like toes is a picture, but you meet it and forget it. A person has a face, a voice, a way of moving, and things they do. If every pair of digits is a famous person doing something, then a long number becomes a short film, and a film is far easier to remember than a row of digits.

This system is named for him. It is the system behind the PAO method of the next lesson, and it is the link between the simple systems of lesson 6 and the champion's methods of lessons 21 and 23.

Why does O'Brien use famous people for numbers?
A person is rich in detail: face, voice, movement and action, so a person is a stronger picture than a plain word.
Talk about it. Which famous person do you find easiest to picture, and what is the first thing you see them doing?

Digits to letters, letters to people

Every digit becomes a letter. A pair of digits becomes a pair of initials. The initials belong to a person you can see, and that person has one famous action.

DigitLetterDigitLetter

5E0O

Why these letters? 1 to 5 simply follow the alphabet. After that: an S looks like a 6, a G looks a little like a 7, the letter H says aitch, which sounds like eight, N is the first letter of nine, and O looks like a zero.

  1. Split the number into pairs. 1066 → 10 and 66.
  2. Turn each digit into a letter. 10 → A, O. 66 → S, S.
  3. Find a person with those initials. A. O. = Al Oerter (an Olympic discus champion). S. S. = Steven Spielberg.
  4. Give each person one action. Oerter: throwing a discus. Spielberg: directing a film.
  5. Four digits = one person + another's action. Take the person of the first pair and the action of the second pair.
