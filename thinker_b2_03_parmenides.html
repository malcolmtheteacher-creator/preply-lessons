When your reasoning and your eyes disagree, which should you trust?

Every day your eyes tell you that the sun moves across the sky. But reasoning and science tell you that the Earth is turning, and you believe them. So sometimes we trust thinking over seeing. Around 2,500 years ago, a poet in southern Italy pushed this to the limit: he used pure logic to argue that change itself is impossible.

Today: You'll meet Parmenides and his argument that change is impossible, so you can build an argument step by step and decide when to trust reason over your senses.

Meet Parmenides

Read one part at a time. After each part, close your eyes and answer the question before you check.

📜

A poet from Elea

Parmenides was born around 515 BC in Elea, a Greek city in southern Italy. He wrote his philosophy not as a list of arguments but as a poem. In it, a young man rides in a chariot to the gates of Day and Night, where a goddess is waiting for him. She shows him two paths: the way of truth, which follows reason, and the way of opinion, which follows what only seems to be true.

What two paths does the goddess show the young man?

The way of truth, which follows reason, and the way of opinion, which follows what only seems to be true.
🕳️

Nothing comes from nothing

The goddess's argument starts with something very simple: what is, is, and what is not, is not. So nothing can come from nothing. And if something exists, it cannot turn into nothing. But change means that something new appears and something old disappears. Therefore, Parmenides concluded, real change is impossible. Reality is one complete, unchanging whole, and all the movement we see is only appearance.

How does Parmenides get from 'nothing comes from nothing' to 'change is impossible'?

Change means something new appears and something old disappears. But nothing can come from nothing or become nothing, so real change cannot happen.
⚔️

The opposite of Heraclitus

At around the same time, Heraclitus was saying that everything flows. Parmenides said the opposite: nothing truly moves or changes. Most people found his conclusion crazy, but nobody could easily find the mistake in his reasoning. According to Plato, the old Parmenides once visited Athens and met the young Socrates. Plato admired him so much that he named one of his books after him.

Why was Parmenides' argument so hard to reject?

Because, even though the conclusion seemed crazy, every step looked logical, and nobody could easily find the mistake.
Talk: Parmenides trusted his logic even when it told him something that seemed impossible. Is that brave, or foolish?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. The detective used pure logic to work out, step by step, who had taken the money.
logic (n) — a way of thinking in which each idea follows clearly and correctly from the one before. Example: "There's no logic in buying a car you can't afford to drive."
2. If the first premise of your argument is false, everything that follows from it is weak.
premise (n) — an idea that you accept as true and use as the starting point of an argument. Example: "The whole plan rests on the premise that customers want cheaper tickets."
3. The researchers studied the data for months before they reached a conclusion.
conclusion (n) — the decision or opinion you reach after thinking about something carefully. Example: "After reading all the reviews, I came to the conclusion that the cheaper phone was better."
4. The magician made it look as if the woman was floating, but of course it was an illusion.
illusion (n) — something that seems real or true but is not. Example: "On hot days the road ahead looks wet, but it's just an illusion."
5. People who are colour-blind perceive red and green differently from most people.
perceive (v) — to notice or become aware of something through your senses or your mind. Example: "Dogs can perceive sounds that humans cannot hear."
6. He saw the empty plates and the dirty pans and deduced that the children had already eaten.
deduce (v) — to reach an answer by careful reasoning from the facts you already have. Example: "From the muddy boots by the door, she deduced that her son was home."
7. The argument sounded strong at first, but she quickly found a flaw in it.
flaw (n) — a mistake or weakness that makes something less good or less correct. Example: "There's a flaw in your plan: the shop is closed on Mondays."

See it in action

Here is a step-by-step argument about something everyday. If the bakery is closed on Sundays, then you can't buy bread there on a Sunday. Today is Sunday. So the bakery is closed. Therefore, you'll have to buy bread somewhere else. Each step follows from the one before. Parmenides built the same kind of chain with huge ideas: being, nothing and change. If you accept his first step, his conclusion is very hard to escape.

Thought experiment: The perfect argument

A friend gives you an argument. Every step looks correct, and you can't find a single flaw. But the conclusion is that time does not really exist and nothing ever changes. You have checked it three times.

Now give two reasons for your choice. Then argue for the other side for thirty seconds. Which do you trust more in the end: your reasoning or your experience?

Compare

Heraclitus and Parmenides: Heraclitus said everything flows; Parmenides said nothing truly changes. How can two intelligent people look at the same world and reach opposite conclusions?
They started from different places. Heraclitus trusted what he observed: rivers flowing, seasons turning. Parmenides trusted pure reasoning, even when it went against the senses. Different starting points led them to opposite conclusions.

Remember earlier thinkers

From Heraclitus

What did Heraclitus mean by 'You cannot step into the same river twice'?

That everything is always changing. New water is always flowing, so the river, like the whole world, is never exactly the same twice.
