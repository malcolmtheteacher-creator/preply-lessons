The Masque of the Red Death

Edgar Allan Poe (1842) — a prince walls a thousand nobles inside an abbey to outwit a plague, and throws a party while the world dies outside

Read in three parts

Poe gives us a fable with the surface of a fairy tale: a prince, an abbey, a masked ball. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. Watch the colours, and watch the great black clock at the far end of the rooms. Everything in the story is moving in one direction, and the last paragraph is where it arrives; do not skip ahead.

Part 1 — The plague and the sealed abbey

The "Red Death" had long devastated the country. No pestilence had ever been so fatal, or so hideous. Blood was its Avatar and its seal — the redness and the horror of blood. There were sharp pains, and sudden dizziness, and then profuse bleeding at the pores, with dissolution. The scarlet stains upon the body and especially upon the face of the victim were the pest ban which shut him out from the aid and from the sympathy of his fellow-men. And the whole seizure, progress, and termination of the disease were the incidents of half an hour.

But the Prince Prospero was happy and dauntless and sagacious. When his dominions were half depopulated, he summoned to his presence a thousand hale and light-hearted friends from among the knights and dames of his court, and with these retired to the deep seclusion of one of his castellated abbeys. This was an extensive and magnificent structure, the creation of the prince's own eccentric yet august taste. A strong and lofty wall girdled it in. This wall had gates of iron. The courtiers, having entered, brought furnaces and massy hammers and welded the bolts. They resolved to leave means neither of ingress nor egress to the sudden impulses of despair or of frenzy from within. The abbey was amply provisioned. With such precautions the courtiers might bid defiance to contagion. The external world could take care of itself. In the meantime it was folly to grieve, or to think. The prince had provided all the appliances of pleasure. There were buffoons, there were improvisatori, there were ballet-dancers, there were musicians, there was Beauty, there was wine. All these and security were within. Without was the "Red Death."

Pause and discuss:
  • The plague kills in "half an hour" and marks its victims with scarlet stains on the face. Why does Poe make the disease so visible — so impossible to hide?
  • Prospero's answer to the plague is to wall himself in with a thousand friends and weld the gates shut. What does the welding of the bolts — sealing people in, as well as out — quietly suggest?
  • "It was folly to grieve, or to think." Is refusing to think about death a kind of wisdom here, or a kind of madness?

Part 2 — The seven rooms and the ebony clock

It was towards the close of the fifth or sixth month of his seclusion, and while the pestilence raged most furiously abroad, that the Prince Prospero entertained his thousand friends at a masked ball of the most unusual magnificence. It was a voluptuous scene, that masquerade. But first let me tell of the rooms in which it was held. There were seven — an imperial suite. The apartments were so irregularly disposed that the vision embraced but little more than one at a time. There was a sharp turn at every twenty or thirty yards, and at each turn a novel effect.

That at the eastern extremity was hung, for example, in blue — and vividly blue were its windows. The second chamber was purple in its ornaments and tapestries, and here the panes were purple. The third was green throughout, and so were the casements. The fourth was furnished and lighted with orange — the fifth with white — the sixth with violet. The seventh apartment was closely shrouded in black velvet tapestries that hung all over the ceiling and down the walls. But in this chamber only, the colour of the windows failed to correspond with the decorations. The panes here were scarlet — a deep blood colour. In no one of the seven apartments was there any lamp or candelabrum; but in the corridors that followed the suite, there stood opposite to each window a heavy tripod bearing a brazier of fire, that projected its rays through the tinted glass and so glaringly illumined the room. And thus were produced a multitude of gaudy and fantastic appearances. But in the western or black chamber the effect of the fire-light upon the blood-tinted panes was ghastly in the extreme, and produced so wild a look upon the countenances of those who entered, that there were few of the company bold enough to set foot within its precincts at all.

It was in this apartment, also, that there stood against the western wall a gigantic clock of ebony. Its pendulum swung to and fro with a dull, heavy, monotonous clang; and when the minute-hand made the circuit of the face, and the hour was to be stricken, there came from the brazen lungs of the clock a sound which was clear and loud and deep and exceedingly musical, but of so peculiar a note and emphasis that, at each lapse of an hour, the musicians of the orchestra were constrained to pause in their performance to hearken to the sound; and thus the waltzers perforce ceased their evolutions; and there was a brief disconcert of the whole gay company; and, while the chimes of the clock yet rang, it was observed that the giddiest grew pale, and the more aged and sedate passed their hands over their brows as if in confused revery or meditation. But when the echoes had fully ceased, a light laughter at once pervaded the assembly; and they smiled as if at their own nervousness, and made whispering vows, each to the other, that the next chiming of the clock should produce in them no similar emotion. And then, after the lapse of sixty minutes, there came yet another chiming of the clock, and then were the same disconcert and tremulousness and meditation as before.

Pause and discuss:
  • The rooms run from blue in the east to black in the west — the direction of sunrise to sunset. If the seven rooms are a life, where in that life is the scarlet-windowed black room?
  • Why are the revellers afraid to enter the seventh, black room at all? They came to a party — what are they really avoiding?
  • Every hour the ebony clock stops the music and the dancing, and everyone goes briefly pale before laughing it off. What is the clock doing to them, and why can't they ignore it?

Part 3 — The masked figure and the ending

And now was acknowledged the presence of the Red Death. He had come like a thief in the night. And one by one dropped the revellers in the blood-bedewed halls of their revel, and died each in the despairing posture of his fall. But before that, there had been a masked figure moving among the dancers — a figure tall and gaunt, and shrouded from head to foot in the habiliments of the grave. The mask which concealed the visage was made so nearly to resemble the countenance of a stiffened corpse that the closest scrutiny must have had difficulty in detecting the cheat. And yet all this might have been endured, if not approved, by the mad revellers around. But the mummer had gone so far as to assume the type of the Red Death. His vesture was dabbled in blood — and his broad brow, with all the features of the face, was besprinkled with the scarlet horror.

When the eyes of Prince Prospero fell upon this spectral image, he was seen to be convulsed, in the first moment with a strong shudder either of terror or distaste; but, in the next, his brow reddened with rage.

"Who dares," he demanded hoarsely of the courtiers who stood near him — "who dares insult us with this blasphemous mockery? Seize him and unmask him — that we may know whom we have to hang at sunrise from the battlements!"

It was in the blue room that the Prince Prospero stood when he uttered these words. But from the masked intruder, who at this moment was also within the blue chamber, there came a slow and solemn movement. He made his way uninterruptedly through the blue chamber to the purple — through the purple to the green — through the green to the orange — through this again to the white — and even thence to the violet, ere a decided movement had been made to arrest him. It was then, however, that the Prince Prospero, maddening with rage and the shame of his own momentary cowardice, rushed hurriedly through the six chambers, while none followed him on account of a deadly terror that had seized upon all. He bore aloft a drawn dagger, and had approached, in rapid impetuosity, to within three or four feet of the retreating figure, when the latter, having attained the extremity of the velvet apartment, turned suddenly and confronted his pursuer. There was a sharp cry — and the dagger dropped gleaming upon the sable carpet, upon which, instantly afterwards, fell prostrate in death the Prince Prospero.

Then, summoning the wild courage of despair, a throng of the revellers at once threw themselves into the black apartment, and, seizing the mummer, whose tall figure stood erect and motionless within the shadow of the ebony clock, gasped in unutterable horror at finding the grave-cerements and corpse-like mask which they handled with so violent a rudeness untenanted by any tangible form.

And now was acknowledged the presence of the Red Death. He had come like a thief in the night. And one by one dropped the revellers in the blood-bedewed halls of their revel, and died each in the despairing posture of his fall. And the life of the ebony clock went out with that of the last of the gay. And the flames of the tripods expired. And Darkness and Decay and the Red Death held illimitable dominion over all.

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • The figure passes through all seven rooms, east to west, and only in the black room does Prospero die. Why does Poe make the chase follow the exact order of the rooms?
  • When the revellers finally seize the figure, there is nothing inside the shroud — "untenanted by any tangible form." What does it change about the story that the Red Death has no body?
  • "And Darkness and Decay and the Red Death held illimitable dominion over all." After a thousand guests, a sealed abbey, and welded gates — what, in the end, was all that wealth and walling worth?
  • Your turn: Prospero tries to buy his way out of death with walls and a party. In what ways do people today still try to wall themselves off from things they cannot ultimately escape — and does it ever work?

Grammar — Inversion & Fronting for Emphasis

Poe does not write "The revellers dropped one by one." He writes "And one by one dropped the revellers." He moves a word to the front and turns the sentence around — and the sentence tolls like the clock. This is fronting (putting a phrase first for effect) and inversion (swapping the normal subject–verb order). Master both and your English gains a register Poe lived in.

1. Negative & restrictive adverbial inversion

When a sentence begins with a negative or restrictive adverbial — Never, Not until, No sooner… than, Only then, Scarcely… when, Little, Nowhere, Not only — English inverts the subject and the (auxiliary) verb, as in a question: Never had he seen… / Only then did they understand… / No sooner had the clock struck than the music stopped.

1. Rewrite, starting with the given adverbial. "No plague had ever been so fatal." →
Never a plague so fatal. (use an auxiliary + subject + main verb)

2. "They understood the danger only when the figure turned." →
Only when the figure turned they the danger. (auxiliary + subject + bare verb)

3. "The clock had struck. The dancing stopped immediately." → join with No sooner:
No sooner the clock struck the dancing stopped. (auxiliary + subject; then the linking word)

4. "He had scarcely entered the black room when the cry came." →
Scarcely he entered the black room the cry came. (auxiliary + subject; then the linking word)

1. Never had a plague been so fatal.

2. Only when the figure turned did they understand the danger.

3. No sooner had the clock struck than the dancing stopped.

4. Scarcely had he entered the black room when the cry came.

2. Fronting for dramatic effect

Beyond the fixed negative triggers, Poe simply lifts a vivid phrase to the front and inverts the verb in front of the subject: "And one by one dropped the revellers." / "Down came the darkness." The effect is theatrical — the action arrives before we are told who it happened to. Rewrite each plain sentence by fronting the phrase in brackets and inverting.

1. "The revellers dropped one by one." (front one by one) →
One by one the revellers. (verb before the subject)

2. "The darkness came down." (front down) →
Down the darkness. (verb before the subject)

3. "A gigantic ebony clock stood against the western wall." (front against the western wall) →
Against the western wall a gigantic ebony clock. (verb before the subject)

1. One by one dropped the revellers.

2. Down came the darkness.

3. Against the western wall stood a gigantic ebony clock.

3. Inversion or normal order? — choosing for effect

Inversion is a tool, not a default. Use it when you front a negative/restrictive adverbial or want a dramatic flourish; keep normal order otherwise. Complete each sentence with the structure cued in brackets.

1. (negative inversion) Begin the idea "Prospero did not only seal the gates; he also welded them" with Not only:
Not only Prospero the gates, but he welded them shut. (auxiliary + subject + bare verb)

2. (restrictive inversion) "They felt safe only inside the walls." →
Only inside the walls they safe. (auxiliary + subject + bare verb)

3. (dramatic fronting) "A masked figure moved among the dancers." Front Among the dancers:
Among the dancers a masked figure. (verb before the subject)

2. Only inside the walls did they feel safe.

What Poe does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are why a story published in 1842 still reads as one long, slow tightening of the chest.

1. Allegory — the whole tale stands for one idea

Read literally, it is a prince and a party. Read as Poe intends, every element allegorises a single truth: no one escapes death, however rich, however walled-in. Prospero's wealth, his thousand friends, his welded gates and amply provisioned abbey are precisely the defences that fail. The story universalises the lesson — the abbey is anyone's fortress, the plague is anyone's mortality — so that the fairy-tale surface encodes a sermon we cannot refuse.

2. Symbolism — the rooms, the colours, the clock

The seven rooms run east (blue, the colour of dawn and birth) to west (black, the colour of night and death), so the suite symbolises the seven stages of a life. The final room is doubly marked: black velvet with scarlet windows, where decay meets the blood of the Red Death. And against its wall the ebony clock tolls every hour and freezes the dancers — Poe emblematises mortality itself in a single object, so that time literally interrupts the pleasure. The party-goers' fear of the seventh room is the human fear of the end the room stands for.

3. Personification — the plague becomes a guest

Poe does not merely describe a disease; he gives it a body, a costume and a will. The Red Death "had come like a thief in the night"; it walks the rooms as a masked figure, "tall and gaunt," shrouded "in the habiliments of the grave." By personifying the plague as an uninvited guest who out-walks the prince room by room, Poe animates an abstraction — and then pulls the shroud back to reveal nothing inside, so the personified Death dissolves back into a force rather than a man.

4. Inevitability and structure — the march from blue to black

The architecture is the plot. Because the rooms are fixed in order and the figure moves through them one by one — blue, purple, green, orange, white, violet, black — the ending feels foreordained the moment the chase begins. Poe structures the climax as a procession we already know the destination of; the relentless single direction enacts the story's argument that death is not an event you can dodge but a direction you are already travelling.

Task A · Retell the story in your own words (3 minutes)

Before we discuss it, tell the story back to me as if I had never read it — in your own words, not line by line. Aim for about eight to ten sentences.

As you go, use at least five words from the Vocabulary tab and weave in at least two inversion or fronting structures from the Grammar tab ("Never had…", "No sooner… than…", "One by one dropped…"). These beats will help you: a Red Death pestilence devastates the country · Prince Prospero seals a thousand friends inside a walled abbey, welding the gates · he throws a masquerade through seven coloured rooms · an ebony clock freezes the dancers every hour · a blood-spattered masked figure appears and walks room by room to the black chamber · Prospero dies, the figure is empty, and Death holds dominion over all.

Task B · The denial of mortality (4 minutes)

Prospero's whole plan is a refusal to think about death — "it was folly to grieve, or to think." Describe a time you watched someone (or yourself) refuse to face something inevitable — an illness, an ageing parent, a failing business, a relationship clearly ending. Take it in three steps:

  1. what the inevitable thing was, and how the person walled it out;
  2. what "party" they threw instead — the distraction that kept the thought away;
  3. what happened when reality finally walked through the door anyway.

Use at least one negative-inversion sentence ("Never did they admit…", "Only when it was too late did…") and two effect verbs from the Technique tab.

Task C · Wealth versus safety (3 minutes)

Prospero has every resource — an abbey, a thousand friends, provisions, iron gates — and none of it saves him. Talk to me about money and safety. Where, in real life, can wealth genuinely buy protection (better healthcare, safer housing, a bodyguard), and where does it only buy the illusion of it?

Think of a real example — a gated community, private medicine, a "secure" investment, an exclusive club. Was the safety real, or a masquerade? Use at least one restrictive-inversion structure ("Only with money can you…", "Not until disaster strikes do people realise…").

Task D · A place that "feels like" something (4 minutes)

Poe's seven rooms are not just rooms — they are an allegory; the décor means something. Describe a real place you know — an office, a hospital, a childhood house, a city at night — but describe it the way Poe describes the abbey: so that the physical details quietly stand for a feeling or an idea (control, decay, ambition, dread, hope).

Choose your colours, your light, your one recurring object (Poe had his ebony clock). Use fronting for drama at least twice ("Down the corridor came…", "In the corner stood…") and tell me at the end what the place is really an allegory of.

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. Poe gives the prince the name "Prospero" — the magician-duke of Shakespeare's The Tempest, a man who controls an island. What is the joke in naming a man who controls nothing after a man who controls everything?
  2. The story was written in 1842, long before modern medicine. Reading it after a pandemic, does the sealed abbey feel like fantasy — or like something you recognise?
  3. The figure turns out to be empty: a shroud and a mask "untenanted by any tangible form." Is an enemy you cannot fight or unmask more frightening than one you can — and why?
  4. If you had Prospero's resources and a plague at the gates, what would you actually do — and would it be any wiser than what he did?
