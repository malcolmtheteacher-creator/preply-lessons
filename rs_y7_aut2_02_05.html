2. Encounter

Let's meet three worldviews that don't include God.

• • •

Three Non-Religious Positions

These words get confused all the time. Here's what they actually mean:

KEY TERM

Atheism

The belief that there is no God or gods. An atheist actively believes God does not exist.

Example: "I have examined the arguments for God's existence and found them unconvincing. I conclude that God does not exist."

KEY TERM

Agnosticism

The position that we cannot know whether God exists or not. The evidence is insufficient.

Example: "Maybe God exists, maybe not. We don't have enough information to decide."

KEY TERM

Humanism

A complete worldview that doesn't require belief in God. It emphasises human reason, evidence, compassion, and building meaning through human relationships and actions.

Important: Humanism is NOT just "not believing in God." It's a positive philosophy with values, ethics, and meaning.

The key difference: Atheism answers ONE question: "Does God exist?" (No.) Humanism answers ALL the questions: "How should I live? What's right and wrong? What gives life meaning?" (Through reason, compassion, and human connection.)

• • •

What Humanists Actually Believe

Humanism is positive, not negative. It's not just "no God." Here are the core principles:

Reason and Evidence

Beliefs should be based on evidence and logic, not faith or revelation. Science and reason are trusted guides.

Human Dignity

All humans have value and deserve respect. We don't need God to establish human worth.

The Golden Rule

"Treat others as you'd want to be treated." Morality comes from empathy and reciprocity, not divine commands.

This Life Matters Most

There is likely no afterlife. So this life — right now — is what counts. Make it meaningful and good.

Secularism

Religion is a private choice. Public life (government, schools, laws) should be secular — based on reason, not religious belief.

Community and Compassion

Meaning comes from relationships, helping others, and contributing to society. That's enough.

The Problem of Evil: The Strongest Argument Against God

This is a logical puzzle that many atheists use:

The Argument

If God is omnipotent (all-powerful) → God CAN stop suffering
If God is omnibenevolent (all-good) → God WANTS to stop suffering
If both are true → Suffering should not exist
But suffering DOES exist → Therefore, either God is not all-powerful, not all-good, or does not exist at all.

This isn't a proof that God doesn't exist. But it's a serious challenge that religious people must answer.

Humanist Ceremonies: Meaning Without God

Humanists don't pray or worship. But they do mark important moments in life. Here's how:

Humanist Naming Ceremony

Similar to a christening, but without religious elements. The community gathers to celebrate the birth of a child. There are readings, personal stories, and commitment to support the family.

Humanist Wedding

Two people publicly commit to each other through personal vows. The ceremony reflects their values, not religious doctrine. Love and partnership are sacred to Humanists — without needing God.

Humanist Funeral

Life is celebrated, not as a gateway to the afterlife, but as something complete and meaningful. The focus is on the impact the person had on others and their lasting legacy.

Bertrand Russell, mathematician and atheist philosopher: "What is wanted is not the will to believe, but the wish to find out, which is the exact opposite of the will to believe."

— Bertrand Russell

In the UK, about 37% of people identify as non-religious (2021 Census). Globally, about 15% of the world's population identifies as non-religious. The fastest-growing "religion" in many countries is "no religion."

3. Explore

Activity 1: Humanist or Religious?

Read these statements. For each one, decide: Would a HUMANIST agree? Would a RELIGIOUS person agree? Or would BOTH agree?

1. "Treat others as you'd want to be treated."

BOTH. This is the Golden Rule. It appears in Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Buddhism, Confucianism — and it's also core to Humanist ethics. Different reasons, same conclusion.
2. "The universe was created for a purpose."

RELIGIOUS (mostly). Many religious worldviews teach that the universe has divine purpose. Humanists would say the universe has no inherent purpose — we create meaning through our actions.
3. "We should base our beliefs on evidence."

HUMANIST (strongly). This is core to Humanism. Religious people may also value evidence, but they also accept faith. Humanists trust reason above all.
4. "Life has meaning because of my relationships."

BOTH. Religious people see relationships as part of God's design. Humanists see relationships as the source of all meaning. Same practice, different framework.
5. "God revealed the truth through scripture."

RELIGIOUS (only). Humanists reject revelation as a source of knowledge. They trust reason and evidence instead.

Activity 2: Responding to the Problem of Evil

The Problem of Evil challenges religious worldviews. How might different traditions respond? Let's explore three answers:

Challenge: A child gets a terrible disease. They suffer greatly. Then they die.

How does each tradition make sense of this?

Christian Response

God allows suffering for reasons we may not understand. This is a fallen world corrupted by sin and human free will. God's plan includes both suffering and redemption. We trust that God will bring good from evil, and that the child's soul is at peace with God.

Islamic Response

Everything is part of Allah's will (qadr). The test of faith is how we respond to suffering with patience (sabr). The child's suffering is temporary; the eternal reward in the afterlife is infinite. This life is just one chapter.

Buddhist Response

Suffering (dukkha) is part of existence. It arises from attachment and desire. The child's death is part of the cycle of rebirth. Our response should be compassion (karuna) for all who suffer. We work to reduce suffering, but accept that it's part of the nature of existence.

The Humanist challenge remains: If God is all-powerful and all-good, why does this child suffer? Why does innocent suffering exist at all?

These questions jump around across all five lessons. No looking back!

1. What is the difference between atheism and Humanism? (Hint: one is about belief, one is about worldview.)

2. State the Problem of Evil argument in one sentence.

3. Name one of the 99 Names of Allah from Lesson 3.

4. What is the Hindu concept of Brahman? How is it different from the Christian Trinity?

5. What would a Humanist say is the source of morality?

1. Atheism is the belief that God does not exist (one claim). Humanism is a complete worldview based on reason, human dignity, and the Golden Rule (a full philosophy).

2. If God is all-powerful and all-good, then evil should not exist; but it does, so either God isn't all-powerful, isn't all-good, or doesn't exist.

3. Any of: Al-Rahman (The Merciful), Al-Wadud (The Loving), As-Salaam (The Peace), Al-Adl (The Just), Al-Alim (The Knowing), etc.

4. Brahman is the ultimate reality, the divine force behind all existence (often called "Brahman with no attributes"). The Trinity is God as three persons in one being (Father, Son, Holy Spirit). Hindus often see Brahman as transcendent and beyond form; Christians see the Trinity as personal and relational.

5. Humanists say morality comes from human reason, empathy, and reciprocity — not from God or divine commands. We follow the Golden Rule because it makes sense and helps society function.
