Predestination (Al-Qadr) in Islamic Belief

What Is Al-Qadr (Predestination)?

Al-Qadr (القَدَر) literally means "power" or "measure," but in Islamic theology it refers to divine predestination--the belief that God knows and has predetermined all events in the universe. It is the sixth article of faith in Sunni Islam.

Key aspects of Al-Qadr:

  • God knows all past, present, and future events
  • God's knowledge is eternal and unchanging
  • Nothing happens without God's permission
  • God's will is the ultimate power in creation
  • Yet humans retain moral responsibility for their choices

Central paradox: How can God predestine all events AND humans have free will to choose?

Qur'anic Foundation: Al-Qadr

The Qur'an repeatedly affirms divine predestination:

Surah 54:49
"Surely We have created all things with measure and power (predestination)."

Surah 3:145
"No soul can die except by God's permission and at the appointed term."

Surah 87:2-3
"Who has created and fashioned all things in perfect proportion. And Who has determined destinies."

Significance: These verses emphasise that all events--birth, death, success, failure--occur according to God's foreknowledge and will.

The Tension: Predestination vs. Free Will

Islam holds two seemingly contradictory truths:

1. God's Omniscience (All-Knowing):

  • God knows all human choices before they happen
  • God's knowledge does not change; it is eternal
  • Therefore, God knows who will be righteous and who will be sinful

2. Human Moral Responsibility:

  • Humans are accountable for their choices on the Day of Judgement
  • If God forced all choices, humans could not be justly punished for sin
  • The Qur'an commands obedience, implying humans have choice

Theological Resolution: Islamic scholars propose that God's knowledge exists outside of time. God sees all moments simultaneously--past, present, future--as "now." God's foreknowledge does not cause events; rather, God knows what will happen because God exists beyond the temporal sequence. Humans remain free to choose, even though God's eternal knowledge encompasses that choice.

Sunni Understanding

  • God knows all: Nothing surprises God
  • Humans choose: Free will is real
  • Tension held: Both truths accepted, though not fully explained
  • Trust in God: Accept divine wisdom even when incomprehensible

Shi'a Understanding

  • Adalat (Justice): Divine justice demands free will
  • God is Just: Cannot punish for actions not freely chosen
  • Emphasis on choice: Humans must freely choose right over wrong
  • Lesser role for predestination: Stress human responsibility

Predestination, Judgement & Muslim Life

Al-Qadr and the Day of Judgement

Belief in predestination is inseparable from belief in the Day of Judgement (Yawm al-Qiyama). The sixth article of faith encompasses both:

  • God predestines all: Every human is born destined for paradise or hell (Qadr)
  • Humans choose: Yet each person's choices determine their eternal fate (Moral accountability)
  • Day of Judgement: Each person accounts for their freely made choices before God
  • Justice preserved: No one is punished for predetermined actions; all are accountable for chosen actions

Qur'anic synthesis: Surah 76:2-3 states: "We have created humans from a mix of sperm and egg-cells, testing them. We have given them hearing, sight, intellect--perhaps they will be grateful." Humans are tested; their responses are their choices.

How Belief in Al-Qadr Affects Muslim Daily Life

Acceptance of divine predestination shapes Muslim attitudes toward suffering, failure, and success:

  • In hardship: "Innalillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un" ("To God we belong, and to God we return"). Suffering is accepted as part of God's plan, often as a test or purification.
  • In loss: "Qadar Allah wa ma sha'a fa'ala" ("It is the decree of God, and God does what God wills"). This brings peace, knowing God's wisdom transcends human understanding.
  • In success: Gratitude is directed to God, not pride in oneself. Success comes by God's will.
  • In moral choice: While accepting God's will, Muslims actively strive to choose righteousness, knowing God values conscious obedience.

Practical impact: A Muslim diagnosed with illness does not despair but seeks medical treatment (using human effort) while trusting in God's healing (submission to divine will). This balance--effort plus trust--characterises Islamic approach to life.

The Philosophical Schools: How Different Muslims Understand Qadr

Islamic jurisprudence has produced several schools interpreting Al-Qadr:

  • Jabariyyah (Determinism): Humans have no real choice; all actions are predetermined by God. (Minority view, considered extreme)
  • Mu'tazilah (Free Will): Humans have absolute free will; God's knowledge must conform to human freedom. (Historical school, now uncommon)
  • Ash'arite (Mainstream Sunni): God predestines, but humans "acquire" (kasb) their actions through conscious choice. Both truths held in tension.
  • Maturidite (Central Asian Sunni): Similar to Ash'arites; emphasises human responsibility while affirming divine predestination.
  • Shi'a approach: Emphasises Adalat (justice): God's predestination cannot undermine human free will, which is essential to divine justice.

Consensus: Despite nuances, all mainstream Islamic schools affirm both divine knowledge/will and human moral responsibility.
