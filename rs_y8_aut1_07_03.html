What Do You Remember?

Before we look at Christianity, let's revisit what you learned about Abraham and the Jewish covenant. Try to answer each question from memory, then click to check.

Question 1: Who was Abraham and why is he important in Judaism?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Abraham (Ibrahim in Arabic) is the founder of monotheism — the belief in one God. God made a covenant (sacred agreement) with him, promising land, descendants, and a special relationship with God. This covenant forms the foundation of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

Question 2: What is the Torah and why does it matter to Jewish people?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: The Torah is the first five books of the Hebrew Bible. It contains God's laws and teachings, given to Moses on Mount Sinai. For Jewish people, living by the Torah's commandments (mitzvot) is a way of keeping their covenant with God.

Question 3: What is a covenant?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: A covenant is a sacred, binding agreement between God and a person or people. It's more than a contract — it's a relationship. God promises blessings and protection; the people promise obedience to God's laws. The Jewish covenant with God through Abraham and Moses is at the heart of Judaism.

If these answers feel new or confusing, that's okay. You'll see how Christianity built on these Jewish ideas in the Encounter section.

Jesus and the Idea of a New Covenant

Why Christianity Grew Out of Judaism

Christianity didn't start as a separate religion. It began as a Jewish movement — Jesus was Jewish, his followers were Jewish, and they believed they were staying true to the Torah and the covenant with God. But a radical claim eventually set Christianity apart: Jesus IS God.

The Core Question: If Jesus is God in human form, what does that mean for the covenant? For the Torah? For what it means to follow God?

Jesus as a Jewish Teacher

Jesus was born around 4 BCE in Bethlehem (though he grew up in Nazareth). He was raised in the Jewish tradition, studied the Torah, and spent his early life as a Jewish teacher. He taught using parables (stories) and drew crowds who believed he was performing miracles — healings that seemed impossible.

Many Jewish people of the time were expecting the Messiah — a savior figure promised in the Torah who would free the Jewish people and bring God's kingdom to earth. When Jesus began his ministry around age 30, some people believed he was the Messiah.

Messiah — From Hebrew "mashiach," meaning "anointed one." The Jewish hope for a savior who would restore Israel and God's kingdom. Christians believe Jesus fulfilled this prophecy; most Jewish people did not.

The "New Covenant" Claim

Jesus taught that a new era was beginning. At his last meal with his disciples before his execution, he broke bread and poured wine, saying:

"This is my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins."
Matthew 26:28

This echoed the covenant language of the Torah, but with a shocking difference: Jesus was saying his own death would create a new agreement between God and humanity. Instead of following the detailed laws of the Torah, faith in Jesus would be the path to God's forgiveness and salvation.

Incarnation: God in Human Form

Christians believe that Jesus wasn't just a teacher or prophet — he was God become human. This is the doctrine of the Incarnation: God entered into human flesh in the person of Jesus.

Incarnation — From Latin "in carne," meaning "in the flesh." The Christian belief that Jesus was fully divine and fully human; God in human form.

This idea was shocking to Jews (and remains so today). In Judaism, God is one and utterly beyond human form. The idea that God could become human violated the core Jewish belief in God's uniqueness. But Christians saw it as the ultimate act of love: God suffering and dying for humanity's salvation.

Resurrection and "New Life"

Christians believe Jesus was executed by crucifixion (a Roman execution method), but rose from the dead three days later. This resurrection is the foundation of Christian faith. It proved to Jesus's followers that he was indeed God's son, and that death itself could be overcome.

Because of Jesus's resurrection, Christians believe death no longer has power over those who have faith in him. This is the hope Christianity offers: eternal life through trust in Jesus.

New Covenant — Christianity's belief that Jesus's death and resurrection created a new agreement between God and humanity, replacing the Torah-based covenant of Judaism. Entry into this covenant comes through faith in Jesus, not through obedience to Jewish laws.
"I will put my law in their minds and write it on their hearts. I will be their God, and they will be my people... For I will forgive their wickedness and will remember their sins no more."
Jeremiah 31:33-34 (quoted by Christian theologians as prophecy of the new covenant)

The Gospel Message

The word "gospel" means "good news." The Christian gospel is the message that through Jesus's death and resurrection, humanity can be forgiven and reconciled with God. This good news was preached by Jesus's followers (his apostles) across the Roman Empire, eventually converting non-Jews (Gentiles) to faith in Jesus.

Gospel — The "good news" of Jesus's life, death, and resurrection. The first four books of the New Testament (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John) are called the Gospels because they tell this story.

Why This Caused Division

For most Jews, Jesus's claims were blasphemy (insulting to God). They believed:

  • God cannot become human
  • The Torah cannot be replaced
  • The Messiah would be a political leader who would free Jews from Roman rule, not a crucified teacher
  • Salvation comes through obedience to God's law, not faith in a person

By around 70-100 CE, Christianity and Judaism had become separate faiths. This separation marks one of the most important splits in Western religious history.

A Word on Historical Context

When Christianity split from Judaism (around 70-150 CE), it was a painful separation. For centuries, Christians and Jews were separate communities. It's important to understand that this wasn't about Christianity being "better" or "replacing" Judaism — it was about two communities arriving at different understandings of God's revelation and the role of Jesus.

Today, both faiths have coexisted for nearly 2,000 years. Most Jews do not believe Jesus was the Messiah, while Christians do. This is a genuine, deep difference — not a misunderstanding that can be resolved. Respecting both traditions means understanding what each believes and why those beliefs matter.

Weighing the Evidence: Was Christianity a Continuation or a Break?

This is a genuine historical and theological question. Some scholars argue Christianity continued Judaism; others argue it broke from it. Both views have evidence.

The "Continuation" View

Christians often argue that Jesus didn't reject Judaism — he fulfilled it. They point to:

Evidence for Continuation
  • Jesus kept Jewish law, celebrated Jewish festivals, and taught in synagogues
  • Early Christians saw Jesus as the answer to Jewish prophecies about the Messiah
  • The New Testament quotes the Hebrew Bible hundreds of times, showing continuity
  • Early Christian leaders like Peter and James were Jewish and saw themselves as continuing Judaism

The "Break" View

Other scholars argue that Christianity fundamentally broke from Judaism because:

Evidence for a Break
  • Christians claim Jesus is God in human form — an idea Judaism explicitly rejects
  • Christians eventually said followers didn't need to obey Torah commandments (like dietary laws or circumcision)
  • By 100 CE, Christians and Jews were separate communities with different practices
  • Jesus's crucifixion and resurrection claim was seen as blasphemous by Jewish authorities of the time

The Balanced View

Most historians today see it as both: Christianity began as a Jewish movement that eventually became something fundamentally new. Think of it like a child who shares a parent's DNA but grows up to be a completely different person — related, but distinct.
