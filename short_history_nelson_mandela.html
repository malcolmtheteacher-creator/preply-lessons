1. Under the system of apartheid, the law separated people entirely by race, deciding where they could live, work, study and travel.

2. The government treated the protest movement as a threat and tried to suppress it with arrests, bans and force.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The lawyer who chose the fight

He was born in 1918 in a small village in the Eastern Cape of South Africa, and given the name Rolihlahla; a teacher later added the English name "Nelson". He belonged to the Thembu people, and grew up hearing the elders tell of a time before their land was taken. He was clever, ambitious, and drawn to the law — but he came of age in a country that was tightening its grip on its Black majority year by year.

In 1948, the government made segregation the official policy of the state and called it apartheid. Under these laws, the white minority held nearly all the power and wealth, while millions of Black South Africans were told where they could live, whom they could marry, and what work they could do. They could not vote. Mandela, by then a lawyer in Johannesburg, joined the African National Congress and helped lead a campaign of protests, strikes and defiance against a system he considered a profound injustice.

For years he pushed for peaceful change. But the state answered protest with bans, arrests and, in 1960, with gunfire against unarmed demonstrators. Concluding that peaceful protest alone had failed, Mandela helped found an armed wing of the movement aimed at sabotage — striking at property and power stations, not people. He went underground, moving in secret, until the police finally caught him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was apartheid, and how did it affect the lives of Black South Africans?

2. Why did Mandela eventually turn away from purely peaceful protest?

1. Apartheid was the official system of racial segregation from 1948; the white minority held power, while the Black majority could not vote and were told where to live, work and marry.

2. Because the state met peaceful protest with bans, arrests and violence, including firing on unarmed demonstrators; he concluded peaceful means alone had failed and helped found an armed wing aimed at sabotage.

Discuss:
  • Mandela trained as a lawyer — someone who works within the law — yet he came to break the laws of his own country. When, if ever, is it right to disobey the law?

Part 2 — Twenty-seven years

In 1964, Mandela and several others stood trial for sabotage, facing a possible death sentence. From the dock he spoke for hours, ending with words that would echo for decades: he was prepared, he said, to die for the ideal of a free and equal society — but it was an ideal he hoped to live for and to achieve. The verdict was life imprisonment. He was sent to Robben Island, a windswept prison island off Cape Town.

There he spent eighteen of his twenty-seven years behind bars. He was given a tiny cell, forced to do hard labour in a blinding limestone quarry, and allowed one visitor and one letter every six months. The years took a heavy toll: his mother and his eldest son died while he was inside, and he was refused permission to attend their funerals. Yet the prisoners turned the island into what some later called a university, teaching one another, debating, and keeping their cause alive. Mandela's discipline and quiet authority made him a leader even in captivity.

Outside, he became a symbol the government could not erase. For years his photograph was banned and his words could not be printed, yet the call to "Free Nelson Mandela" spread across the world. Governments, students, musicians and ordinary people joined an international boycott of apartheid South Africa, refusing to trade with it or play sport against it. Slowly, the pressure of that isolation began to tell on the regime.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What sentence did Mandela receive in 1964, and where was he sent?

2. How did the outside world put pressure on the apartheid government while he was in prison?

1. He was sentenced to life imprisonment for sabotage and sent to Robben Island, where he spent eighteen of his twenty-seven years, doing hard labour in a quarry.

2. Through a growing international boycott — many countries, organisations and individuals refused to trade with, invest in or play sport against apartheid South Africa, and campaigned to free him.

Discuss:
  • The government banned his image and his words, yet he became more powerful in prison than out of it. How can silencing someone sometimes make them stronger?

Part 3 — The walk to freedom, and the choice to forgive

By the late 1980s, apartheid was cracking under protest at home and isolation abroad. In secret, the government began talking to the prisoner it had jailed for life. On 11 February 1990, after twenty-seven years, Nelson Mandela walked out of prison a free man, raising his fist to an enormous crowd. Millions watched on television as the man behind the banned photograph stepped, at last, into daylight.

What he did next surprised the world. He could have called for revenge; instead he called for reconciliation. Working with President F. W. de Klerk, he negotiated an end to apartheid, and the two men shared the Nobel Peace Prize. In 1994, South Africa held its first election in which every adult, of every race, could vote. People queued for hours in lines that stretched for miles. Mandela won, becoming the country's first Black president.

As president, he set out to hold one nation together rather than settle old scores. He backed a Truth and Reconciliation Commission, where victims and perpetrators of apartheid-era crimes could speak openly, so that the country might face its past without tearing itself apart. He served a single term and then, remarkably for a man of such power, stepped down. When he died in 2013, aged 95, the world mourned not a warrior but a peacemaker — proof that a person can suffer profound injustice and still choose, in the end, to build rather than to burn.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Mandela choose to do after his release, instead of seeking revenge?

2. What was historic about the 1994 election, and what did Mandela do as president?

1. He chose reconciliation over revenge, negotiating an end to apartheid with President de Klerk (sharing the Nobel Peace Prize) and working to unite the country.

2. It was the first election in which every adult of every race could vote; Mandela won and became South Africa's first Black president, backing a Truth and Reconciliation Commission and serving only one term.

Discuss:
  • After twenty-seven years in prison, Mandela chose forgiveness over revenge. Is forgiveness a sign of strength or of weakness — and could you have made the same choice?

Grammar — -ever words & no matter

Mandela's story is a study in unbreakable resolve: no matter how long it took, whatever they did to him, he would not give up the ideal. English packs that "I don't care what the obstacle is" feeling into a small family of -ever words — and their close cousin, no matter.

The words, side by side:
• however = in whatever way / to whatever degree: "However hard they tried to break him, he held on." (= it didn't matter how hard)
• whatever = any thing that / it doesn't matter what: "Whatever the regime did, the movement kept going."
• whenever = at any time that / every time: "Whenever he was allowed a visitor, he stood tall."
• wherever / whoever follow the same pattern: any place that / any person who.
• no matter (how / what / when / who) = a very common spoken alternative: "No matter how long it took, he refused to give up."
Two key points of form:
• After these words we use a normal clause: however + adjective/adverb + subject + verb ("however long it took"); whatever + (noun) + subject + verb ("whatever they did").
• no matter how = however, and no matter what = whatever — they are interchangeable in meaning. "No matter what happened" = "Whatever happened".
• Careful: however also means "but" as a linking word ("He was tired; however, he continued"). Here we mean the "no matter how" sense.

1. Choose the -ever word

Complete each sentence with however, whatever, whenever, wherever or whoever.

1. long it took, Mandela was determined to see apartheid end.

2. the government banned, the movement found another way to be heard.

3. he was moved, in Cape Town or on the island, his resolve stayed the same.

2. Whatever (= any thing that / it didn't matter what)

2. Rewrite with no matter

Rewrite each sentence, replacing the -ever word with no matter how / what / when (the meaning stays the same). Write the new opening.

1. However hard the years were, he did not lose hope. → hard the years were, he did not lose hope.

2. Whatever they took from him, they could not take his dignity. → they took from him, they could not take his dignity.

3. Whenever he spoke, people listened. → he spoke, people listened.

1. No matter how (no matter how = however)

2. No matter what (no matter what = whatever)

3. No matter when (no matter when = whenever)
