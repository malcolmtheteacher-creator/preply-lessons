The literature that thinks about the future

Science fiction is often dismissed as escapism — rockets, robots, ray-guns. Yet at its most serious it may be the most philosophical writing we have: a laboratory of the imagination in which a culture tests its hopes and fears about knowledge, power and what it means to be human. Over these two lessons we'll trace how it grew from a single Gothic novel into a global literature — and, just as importantly, examine how it is written.

Two families of fiction — and where SF sits

One bigger map first. All fiction falls, roughly, into two families:

Realist (mimetic) fiction shows the world as it is, or could really be — no law of reality is broken.

Speculative fiction changes one or more rules of reality and explores the result. It has several close neighbours, and the differences matter:

  • Science fiction — the strange is made rationally explicable: a technology, discovery or future that does not exist yet, but that the story asks you to accept as scientifically possible.
  • Fantasy — the impossible is worked by magic or the supernatural, with no scientific explanation offered or expected (wizards, dragons, spells).
  • Horror — whatever the mechanism, the primary aim is to frighten.
  • Alternate history / dystopia — “what if the past had gone differently?” / “what if a present-day danger won?”

The borders are porous, and many great works straddle them — which is exactly what makes the next question worth arguing.

Argue it

Using those definitions: is Star Wars science fiction or fantasy? (Think about “the Force,” and whether its technology is ever really explained.) And where would you put The Handmaid's Tale? Defend each placement out loud with the “rationally explicable vs magic” test.

Origins: From the Gothic to the Scientific Romance

SF has no single birthday, but most critics start with a teenager's ghost-story wager. In 1818, aged just twenty, Mary Shelley published Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus. Crucially, her monster is created not by magic but by science — the anxieties of the industrial, electrical age given flesh. The critic Brian Aldiss called it the first true SF novel for exactly this reason: its horror is rational, extrapolated from real science.

Later in the century two very different giants professionalised the “scientific romance.” Compare them — the contrast is a gift for a comparative essay.

React first

Our founding science-fiction story is a warning — about a scientist who creates life and then refuses responsibility for it. Written by a nineteen-year-old, on a dare. What does that tell you about what the genre is for? Say it before you read on.

Theme: the Promethean danger of knowledge without responsibility.

1828–1905

Jules Verne

Journey to the Center of the Earth (1864); Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Sea (1870).
Mode: optimistic adventure, engineering, exploration — the romance of the machine.

1866–1946

H. G. Wells

The Time Machine (1895); The War of the Worlds (1898); The Island of Doctor Moreau (1896).
Mode: social critique — evolution, class, empire, human smallness.

Verne vs Wells — the founding contrast

Verne asked “what can we build?” and delighted in the answer. Wells asked “what will it do to us?” and often shuddered. Which of the two, do you think, is the truer ancestor of modern SF? Defend your view aloud before revealing a critic's take.

A critical view
Most critics see Wells as the deeper influence: he turned the genre from a celebration of gadgets into a vehicle for ideas and social criticism — the tradition that runs through Orwell, Le Guin and beyond. But the strongest answer resists a flat choice: it argues that SF has always oscillated between Verne's wonder and Wells's warning, and that the tension between them is the genre.

Pulp, Professionalism & the Golden Age

In 1926 the inventor-editor Hugo Gernsback founded Amazing Stories, the first magazine devoted entirely to the form. He coined the label — first the clumsy “scientifiction,” then “science fiction.” (The genre's top honour, the Hugo Award, is named after him.) The pulp magazines were cheap, garish and wildly popular — and they gave SF a home and an audience.

The so-called Golden Age (roughly 1938 onward) began when editor John W. Campbell took over Astounding and demanded two things: plausible science and competent, thinking characters. Out of that discipline came the towering figures of “hard SF.”

How SF Works — The Machinery of the Genre

What makes a text science-fictional is not the presence of a spaceship but a set of techniques. Learn to name them and you can analyse any SF text at exam depth. (We'll add the prose-level devices — neologism, tone, immersion — in Part 2.)

Keep one in mind

Choose a science-fiction story, film or game you love and hold it in your head. As we meet each of the four techniques below, test it against your example — and be ready to tell me how it uses each one.

1. The novum, rationally grounded

One new element is introduced and treated as scientifically explicable, however fantastic. The reader is invited to reason about it, not simply to marvel.

e.g. Wells's time machine is a device with levers, not a spell — so the story becomes an argument about time, evolution and class.

2. Extrapolation — “if this goes on…”

Take a real trend — automation, surveillance, climate change — and push it to its logical extreme. The future becomes a controlled experiment on the present.

e.g. Bradbury's Fahrenheit 451 extrapolates mass media and censorship into a world where books are burned.

3. Cognitive estrangement

By setting the story elsewhere/elsewhen, SF strips the familiar of its invisibility. We judge our world more clearly by seeing it reflected in a strange one.

e.g. An alien society with different rules exposes which of “our” rules are natural and which are merely habit.

4. The thought experiment

Many SF stories are a single question dramatised: What if we could live forever? What if machines could think? Character and plot exist to test the idea to its limit.

e.g. Asimov's Three Laws are a premise the stories then probe for loopholes — fiction as logic puzzle.

Spot the device

Take a science-fiction story, film or series you know well. Identify its novum, the trend it extrapolates, and the present-day anxiety it estranges. Talk me through all three.

Close Analysis — Reading Like an Examiner

Advanced analysis prizes how a text creates meaning. Here is the celebrated opening idea of Wells's The War of the Worlds (1898, public domain). Read it, then work through the guided analysis — the method transfers to any unseen passage in an exam.

“… intellects vast and cool and unsympathetic, regarded this earth with envious eyes, and slowly and surely drew their plans against us.” H. G. Wells, The War of the Worlds, 1898

1. What is the novum, and why is it frightening?

Model analysis
The novum is an intelligent, superior alien species studying humanity as prey. Its terror lies in the reversal of the imperial gaze: the Victorian reader, used to seeing Britain as the civilising observer of “lesser” peoples, is suddenly the specimen under glass.

2. How does the language create that effect?

Model analysis
The triad “vast and cool and unsympathetic” strips the aliens of emotion, making them the more chilling; the polysyndeton (“and…and”) enacts their slow, inexorable method; “envious eyes” and “drew their plans” grant them cold intention. The estrangement is doing political work: this is a critique of empire disguised as an invasion story.

3. Your turn

In two or three spoken sentences, argue what present-day anxiety The War of the Worlds would estrange if it were written today. Justify with the idea of the reversed gaze.

The Big Debate

Choose two of these and argue them at length. Take a position, support it with the writers and terms from today, and be ready to concede a point to the other side — that concession is exactly what raises an argument from good to excellent.

Is Frankenstein really “the first science fiction” — or is that claim a Western, literary bias that ignores older imaginative traditions?

“Science fiction is the literature of ideas at the expense of character.” Fair criticism, or a misunderstanding of what the genre is for?

Does early SF ultimately celebrate science or fear it? Can a single work do both?

If SF rarely predicts accurately, why do we keep praising it for being “prescient”? What are we really valuing?
