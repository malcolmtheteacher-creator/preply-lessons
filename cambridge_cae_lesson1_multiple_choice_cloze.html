Multiple Choice Cloze

Reading & Use of English — Part 1

C1 Advanced (CAE)

Part 1 at a Glance

This is the very first thing you see when you open the exam paper. It sets the tone for everything that follows.

FormatA text with 8 gaps. Each gap has 4 options (A, B, C, D).
TimeAbout 10-12 minutes (of your 90-minute paper)
Marks8 marks — 1 per correct answer
What it testsVocabulary: collocations, phrasal verbs, fixed expressions, easily confused words
Why this part matters: It looks easy — four choices, pick one. But the options are designed to trick you. Three of the four words will "almost" fit. Only one is the natural, correct combination in English. This is about knowing which words go together.

A Quick Example

Look at this sentence:

The company decided to (1) a new policy on remote working.
A make
B do
C introduce
D put
C — introduce is the answer. We "introduce a policy" in English. You can "make a decision" or "do research" or "put forward an idea" — but policies are introduced. This is a collocation question: the gap tests which verb naturally pairs with "policy."

Before we go further: Have you done multiple choice cloze exercises before? What do you find hardest — the vocabulary, the time pressure, or something else?

Know What They Are Testing

Every gap in Part 1 falls into one of four categories. When you can identify the type, you already know where to look for the answer.

Type 1: Collocations

Words that naturally go together. You cannot explain WHY — they just do.

Example: "She ___ a complaint." → made (not did, not put, not took)

Common patterns: make/do, take/have, heavy/strong, wide/broad

Type 2: Phrasal Verbs

Verb + preposition/particle combinations that change the meaning completely.

Example: "The meeting was ___ off until next week." → put (put off = postpone)

You need to know the whole phrase, not just the individual words.

Type 3: Easily Confused Words

Words with similar meanings but different usage or nuance.

Example: "The film had a deep ___ on audiences." → effect (not affect, not impact [wrong grammar], not result)

Classic pairs: affect/effect, rise/raise, avoid/prevent, remind/remember

Type 4: Fixed Expressions & Linking Words

Set phrases, idioms, and discourse markers that only work one way.

Example: "She passed the exam with flying ___." → colours (fixed expression)

Also tests: in spite of, as a result, on the other hand, by all means

Can You Spot the Type?

For each gap below, decide if it tests a collocation, phrasal verb, confused word, or fixed expression.

1. "I need to ___ up with a solution quickly." (A) come (B) think (C) bring (D) make

A — come. "Come up with" = phrasal verb meaning to produce/think of. This is a phrasal verb question.

2. "The new employee made a very good ___ on her first day." (A) effect (B) impact (C) impression (D) mark

C — impression. "Make a good impression" is the fixed expression. You can "have an impact" or "have an effect" but you "make an impression."

3. "The government needs to ___ measures to reduce pollution." (A) make (B) take (C) do (D) give

B — take. We "take measures" — this is a collocation. You make decisions, do research, take measures.

4. "She tried to ___ making the same mistake again." (A) prevent (B) avoid (C) stop (D) refuse

B — avoid. "Avoid + -ing" is the correct pattern. "Prevent" needs an object + from + -ing. This is an easily confused words question — both mean "not do" but the grammar is different.

Your 5-Step Approach

Use this method every time. It takes practice, but it becomes automatic.

Step 1: Read the Whole Text First

Ignore the gaps. Read for meaning. What is the text about? What is the tone? You need the big picture before you can fill the details.

Step 2: Read the Sentence Around the Gap

Look at the words BEFORE and AFTER the gap. They are your biggest clue. The answer almost always depends on what comes next.

Key question: What word MUST go with the word after/before the gap? "Pay attention ___" → to. "Keen ___" → on. The surrounding words dictate the answer.

Step 3: Try Each Option in the Gap

Read the full sentence with each option. Does it sound natural? Does it make grammatical sense? Trust your ear — if you have read widely in English, your instinct is often right.

Step 4: Eliminate Wrong Answers

Even if you are not 100% sure of the right answer, you can often remove 1 or 2 wrong ones. This dramatically improves your odds.

From 25% (random guess) → 50% (2 eliminated) → near certain (1 eliminated)

Step 5: Never Leave a Gap Empty

There is no penalty for wrong answers in CAE. If you are stuck, eliminate what you can and guess from what remains. A blank is always 0 marks.

Common Traps to Watch For

The "sounds right in my language" trap — Some options are direct translations from other languages. English collocations are unique. "Make a photo" feels logical but English says "take a photo."

The "all four words mean the same thing" trap — The options often have very similar meanings. The difference is grammar or collocation, not meaning. "Big/large/great/wide" all mean "not small" but they pair with different nouns.

The "I know this phrasal verb... but it is not the right one" trap — All four options might form real phrasal verbs. "Look up / look into / look over / look through" are all real. Only one fits the context.

Which of these traps do you think you fall into most often? Can you think of a collocation from your language that does not translate directly into English?

Practice Text 1: The Rise of Remote Working

Read the text below. For questions 1-8, decide which answer (A, B, C, or D) best fits each gap.

Time: 10:00

The shift to remote working, which was dramatically (1) by the global pandemic, has fundamentally changed the way millions of people (2) out their daily tasks. Many employees have discovered that working from home allows them to (3) a better balance between their professional and personal lives.

However, the transition has not been without its challenges. Some workers have found it difficult to (4) boundaries between work and leisure time, often finding themselves answering emails late into the evening. Companies have also had to (5) with the challenge of maintaining team spirit when colleagues rarely meet face to face.

Research (6) out by several universities suggests that productivity has actually increased for many remote workers, (7) some employers had initially feared the opposite. Nevertheless, experts warn that the long-term (8) on mental health remain unclear, and that a hybrid model may ultimately prove to be the most sustainable approach.

Question 7

C however
D whereas

Question 8

B results

Answer Explanations

1. B — accelerated. "Accelerated by" is the correct collocation for describing something that was speeded up. "Hurried" and "rushed" describe people, not processes.

2. C — carry. "Carry out tasks" is a fixed collocation. You cannot "bring out" or "pull out" tasks.

3. D — strike. "Strike a balance" is a fixed expression. "Find a balance" is possible in informal English, but "strike" is the strong collocation tested at C1.

4. A — set. "Set boundaries" is the standard collocation. "Draw a line" exists but "draw boundaries" is not standard.

5. B — deal. "Deal with" fits the grammar (deal with the challenge). "Cope" needs "with" too but "cope with the challenge of" is less natural here. "Face" and "handle" do not take "with."

6. C — carried. "Research carried out by" is the fixed expression. "Taken out" means something different. "Brought out" means to release/publish.

7. A — although. "Although" introduces a contrast clause with a subject + verb. "Despite" needs a noun/gerund. "However" needs a new sentence or semicolon. "Whereas" implies comparison, not concession.

8. D — effects. "Effects on" is the correct collocation. "Consequences" takes "of" or "for." "Results" and "outcomes" do not collocate naturally with "on mental health."

Practice Text 2: The Art of Persuasion

Same format — 8 gaps, 4 options each. Try to use the 5-step strategy from earlier.

Time: 10:00

The ability to persuade others is a skill that has been (1) valued throughout human history. From ancient Greek orators to modern-day advertisers, those who can (2) people around to their point of view have always held considerable influence.

Psychologists have long been (3) to understand the mechanisms behind persuasion. One key finding is that people are more likely to be (4) by arguments that appeal to their emotions rather than their logic. This is why charity campaigns often (5) use of powerful images rather than statistics alone.

In the digital age, the art of persuasion has taken (6) new dimensions. Social media influencers can (7) opinions on a massive scale, often without their audiences being fully (8) of how their views are being shaped.

Question 7

C shape
D mould

Question 8

Answer Explanations

1. C — highly. "Highly valued" is the standard collocation. "Deeply" pairs with emotions (deeply moved), "strongly" with opinions (strongly believe), "greatly" with verbs (greatly appreciated).

2. B — bring. "Bring people around to (a point of view)" is a phrasal verb meaning to persuade. "Win over" exists but "win around to" is less standard.

3. A — seeking. "Seeking to understand" is formal and correct. "Looking" needs a different preposition (looking to is informal). "Wanting" and "hoping" change the meaning.

4. D — swayed. "Swayed by arguments" is the natural collocation. "Persuaded" and "convinced" take different constructions (persuaded to do, convinced that). "Influenced by" is possible but less precise here.

5. B — make. "Make use of" is a fixed expression. Not "do use of" or "take use of."

6. A — on. "Taken on new dimensions" = phrasal verb meaning to acquire/develop. "Taken up" means to start a hobby. "Taken in" means to deceive. "Taken over" means to seize control.

7. C — shape. "Shape opinions" is the strong collocation. "Form opinions" means to develop your own. "Build" and "mould" are less natural with "opinions on a massive scale."

8. B — aware. "Aware of" is the correct pattern. "Conscious of" is possible but less common in this context. "Known" does not work grammatically here. "Familiar with" changes the meaning.

Reflection: Look at the questions you got wrong. Can you identify which type each one was — collocation, phrasal verb, confused word, or fixed expression? Knowing your weak area tells you what to study.
