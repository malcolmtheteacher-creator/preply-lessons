Fifteen minutes before writing feels frightening. It's the highest-scoring quarter of an hour in the exam: answers fail from poor selection far more often than from poor sentences.

The 10-minute annotation method

First: circle the question's FOCUS words and write them at the top of the data. Then sweep the transcript four times, one lens per sweep — fast:

SweepHunting for
1 · Soundseverything in /slashes/ — name the pattern beside each
2 · Words & grammarvirtuous errors, missing little words, stretchy meanings, expanded noun phrases, stage evidence
3 · The adultthe six moves; count the questions
4 · The child's skills + contextinitiating, repair, topic moves; shared-knowledge moments; setting

Then build the plan: group your findings into 4–5 clusters, each answering the question focus from a different angle — NOT "paragraph 1: phonology, paragraph 2: grammar…" A levels-tour reads mechanical; a question-led plan reads like an argument.

Which plan is stronger — and why?

Plan A: 1. Phonology 2. Lexis 3. Grammar 4. The mother 5. Conclusion
Plan B: 1. The child's developing forms still succeed as conversation (sounds + grammar evidence) 2. What the child initiates and steers (discourse evidence) 3. How the adult's scaffolding powers it all (the six moves) 4. Why shared context makes it work (context evidence) 5. What the theories make of it (woven throughout, gathered briefly at the end)
SPEAK Say precisely what Plan B does that Plan A doesn't. Then the harder question: where does Plan B put phonology — and why is that clever?
Plan B's paragraphs are ANSWERS to the question, with language levels as evidence inside them; Plan A's are just categories. Phonology lives inside paragraph 1 as evidence for a claim, not as a topic of its own. Same knowledge, argument-shaped.

The paragraph engine

Every body paragraph runs the same five-part engine:

1 · Claim tied to the question focus 2 · Quoted evidence 3 · Named feature + stage 4 · Theory woven in 5 · Land back on the focus

"[1] The child's still-developing grammar never blocks the interaction — it fuels it. [2] When she announces 'teddy falled in the bath', [3] the overgeneralised past tense is a classic virtuous error of the late telegraphic stage, [4] evidence of the rule-building nativists describe rather than imitation — no adult models 'falled'. Her father's recast, 'he FELL in, did he?', returns the standard form inside the game, Bruner's scaffolding in miniature. [5] The 'error' thus becomes the very turn that keeps the joint story going — her developing system engaging him, not failing him."

Notice the engine's secret: parts 3 and 4 are where terminology and theory live, but parts 1 and 5 — the question focus — are the bread of the sandwich. Examiners taste the bread first.

Engine practice

Data: "want more nana /ˈnænə/" (child 2;3, holding out an empty bowl to her grandmother, who refills it saying "more banana coming up"). Build the five parts aloud, in order — then compare.

"[1] Even at the two-word stage the child's language gets real things done. [2] 'Want more nana', with [3] its weak-syllable-deleted /ˈnænə/ and telegraphic frame, [4] performs Halliday's instrumental function — language as a tool for needs — and its instant success ('more banana coming up') supplies exactly the reward behaviourists say stamps forms in. [5] Two words plus a held-out bowl: a complete, successful transaction — engagement achieved with minimal grammar."

Weak v strong — same data, same knowledge

Both writers below know the same terms. Read both answers on this data:

Child (3;0): where my wellies gone? (.) it raining
M: where HAVE your wellies gone (.) it's raining hard isn't it (.) look by the door
Answer 1: "The child makes two mistakes. She misses out 'have' which is auxiliary omission and she misses out 'is' which is copula omission. The mother corrects her by saying the right sentence. This shows child-directed speech and supports Bruner."
Answer 2: "The child's 'where my wellies gone?' omits the auxiliary 'have', and 'it raining' the copula — both typical of a child around 3;0 whose content words outrun her grammatical ones. Yet the utterances function perfectly as interaction: a question that secures help and a comment that shares an observation. Her mother's expansion — 'where HAVE your wellies gone' — models the missing auxiliary with contrastive stress, and her tag question 'isn't it' hands the turn straight back: scaffolding, in Bruner's terms, that converts each developing form into a successful conversational move."
SPEAK List every difference you can find — there are at least five. Then check the examiner-eye view.
Answer 1: deficit framing ("mistakes", "corrects"), features listed not analysed, no stage placement, theory bolted on, never touches the question focus. Answer 2: development framing, quotes worked hard (even the stress!), stage placed, theory woven, and both sentences land on interaction. Same knowledge — different level entirely.

Upgrade drill

Rewrite Answer 1's final sentence ("The mother corrects her…Bruner") into top-level style. Type it, then compare.

"Her mother's expansion returns the standard forms inside the flow of help — no correction announced, none needed — the real-time support Bruner calls scaffolding, which lets the child's developing grammar keep doing its conversational job."

A full answer's skeleton

The intro and close matter less than paragraphs — keep both short. Model intro (three sentences, job done):

"At 2;11, the child in this data sits late in the telegraphic stage: content words flow freely while auxiliaries, determiners and some consonant clusters are still stabilising. None of this limits the interaction — across the transcript her developing forms initiate topics, secure help and sustain a shared game, with her father's child-directed speech converting every turn into support. This response examines how that partnership works across her sound system, her grammar, and the discourse the two build together."

Why it works: stage placed immediately · thesis answers the question focus · a map of what's coming · not a word wasted. The close mirrors it in two sentences — restate what the child's language achieved, name the strongest single piece of evidence.

11:00

When the timer ends: run the Tab 5 checklist on what you wrote, then open the feature key below and count what you found versus what was there.

Sounds: /dʌ_dʌk/ reduplication fading into the full form mid-utterance — variability gold · /bwed/ substitution (w for r) inside a reduced cluster.
Grammar: "throwed" virtuous error (recast as "threw") · "he my favourite" copula omission · "the big brown one with the funny foot" — a glorious expanded noun phrase · "all gone after" — emerging time reference.
Adult moves: expansions ("you threw it really far"), tag/echo questions, reformulation, the mother adopting HIS phrase ("the one with the funny foot") — scaffolding running both directions.
Child's skills: initiates ("more bread mummy"), addresses the ducks directly (Halliday: interactional + imaginative), negotiates ("LAST piece" with stress — echoing her limit back), topic-manages toward his favourite duck.
Context: the pond setting decodes everything; pointing + shared gaze ("that one") does grammatical work words don't have to.
Theories available: Bruner everywhere; nativism via "throwed"; behaviourism via the echoed "LAST piece"; Halliday via the duck-directed talk; usage-based via "dinnertime" as a household chunk.
