A Different Idea of Kingship

In early Ireland there were many kings at once — a different idea of what a king was for.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Many kings

Early medieval Ireland had no single kingdom. Historians estimate there were around 100–150 small kingdoms, called túatha. Each had a rí, a king. Small kings used to pay tribute and give hostages to an overking, and overkings in turn would answer to a provincial king.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was a túath?

2. What did small kings give overkings?

1. A small kingdom.

2. Tribute and hostages.

Discuss:
  • Is it better to have one big ruler or many small ones?

Part 2 — A king for the land

Old Irish texts describe a sacred bond between the king and the land. A good, truthful king was supposed to bring good harvests and peace; a false one would bring bad weather and hunger. These ideas are partly literary and partly religious, so we should not read them as everyday fact.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did a good king bring, according to the texts?

2. Should we read these texts as fact?

1. Good harvests and peace.

2. Not simply — they are partly literary ideals.

Discuss:
  • Do we still blame leaders for things they can't control?

Part 3 — Chosen from a family

A king did not have to be the eldest son. Any man whose father or grandfather had been king (the derbfine group) could be a candidate. This meant there were often several rivals, and succession could be violent. The law tracts even said a physically blemished king could be unfit to rule.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who could become king?

2. What was a disadvantage of this system?

1. A man from the royal kin group whose father or grandfather had been king.

2. Many rivals and violent succession.

Discuss:
  • Is a wider choice of leaders good or bad?

Grammar — Used to & Would — Past Habits

To describe how life used to be — the habits and customs of a reign — English uses used to and would.

How it works:
• used to + verb for past habits and states: "People used to believe the king could heal."
• would + verb for repeated actions only (not states): "Every Christmas he would hold court."
• Negative: didn't use to.

Going deeper

The famous title "High King of Ireland" (ard rí Érenn) was more an ambition than a reality for most of this period. No king ruled the whole island the way an English king ruled England.

Worth noticing: "Used to" works for states and habits; "would" only for repeated actions — we don't say "Ireland would have many kings".
Learning from the past — for today: Leadership is a relationship, not just a title. Irish kings were judged by results — harvests, peace, fairness. Ask yourself: what would people say about your leadership if they judged by results?
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Why so many kingdoms?
  • Is leadership a contract?
  • What results show good leadership at work?
