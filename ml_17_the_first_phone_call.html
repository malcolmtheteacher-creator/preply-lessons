The First Phone Call

Three weeks of messages, and you still don't know what she sounds like. Ten minutes on the phone will tell you more than another fortnight of typing — if you can get through the first thirty seconds.

The Scene 🎬

Three weeks. Something like four hundred messages. You know she has a brother in Leeds, that she can't watch films with subtitles, and that she once queued two hours for a doughnut and regrets nothing. What you don't know is what she sounds like — whether she laughs easily, whether she's warm, whether the person doing the typing and the person on the other end are recognisably the same human being.

The messages have got very good. That's the problem. They've got so good that they've started to feel like homework — drafted, re-read, deleted, sent at a sensible hour. Meanwhile the actual question sits there untouched: are you ever going to meet, or is this just a nice pen-friendship with a photo attached?

So you suggested a phone call first. She said yes, which was either a very good sign or the beginning of something excruciating. It's ringing. Ten minutes, and you'll both know something the messages were never going to tell you.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. How long could you message someone before you'd need to hear their voice? Is there a point where all that typing starts working against you?
  2. When have you built a picture of someone in your head before meeting them — and how far off was it? What had you got wrong, and what had you got right?

Who's who

You
The One Who Rang 📞

You'd rather know now than in six weeks' time. You want to find out who she actually is, say the sensible safety bit out loud without sounding like you're accusing her of anything, and — if it goes well — get something small and low-stakes in the diary.

The other voice
Priya 📱

Three weeks of messaging, and neither of you has heard the other's voice. She said yes to the call, then spent the afternoon slightly regretting it. Funny in writing; not at all sure she's funny out loud.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's meant to sound a bit clumsy; that's what a first call sounds like. Listen for how the clumsiness gets used rather than covered up.

PriyaHello? Hi. Sorry — hang on, I'm just — right. Hi.
YouHi. Hiya. Right — this is a bit odd, isn't it, after all that typing.
PriyaMm. Massively. Three weeks of, what, essays, and now I've got absolutely no — sorry, you go.
YouNo, no. Although — I should probably warn you. I'll be honest, I'm rubbish on the phone. I do this thing where I fill every gap with noise. So if I start talking about the weather, just stop me.
PriyaYou're doing fine, actually. Better than my— mm. Anyway. Go on.
YouActually — can I ask you something slightly nosy?
PriyaDepends how nosy. Go on then.
YouWhereabouts are you, roughly? You don't have to say. I only ask because you've mentioned the park about nine times and I've got no idea if that's twenty minutes from me or the other end of the country.
PriyaNorth. Sort of north-east, near the park, that kind of thing. So — forty minutes? Something like that, if the trains behave.
YouThat's nothing. That's fine. Can I — what made you say yes to this, out of interest? The ringing, I mean. Most people don't.
PriyaHonestly? Because the typing had started to feel like a job. I was drafting things. I drafted a message about a sandwich once — I re-wrote it, I'm not proud of that — and I thought, right, this has gone somewhere silly. What was the— sorry, what did you ask?
YouThat, more or less. And — well. I've had a couple of these that went nowhere, if I'm honest. Lovely chat, weeks of it, and then it sort of evaporates and nobody says why.
PriyaMm. Yeah. Same, to be fair. It's not — I don't know. Anyway.

PriyaYeah. Go on then. When were you thinking?

PriyaThat's not weird, that's just— God, no, I do exactly the same. My flatmate gets a name and a photo and a time. Right. Saturday. I'll probably be early and pretend I'm not.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Priya explains why she agreed to the call. What had the messaging started to feel like to her, and what small, slightly embarrassing thing did she admit she'd been doing?

2. Two of the caller's questions are nosy ones. Each time, something small is attached to the question. What gets attached, and what does it do to the person being asked?

3. By the end, both of them turn out to have the same habit before meeting a stranger. What is it — and why doesn't it insult either of them?

1. It had started to feel like a job — something she was working at rather than enjoying. She admitted she'd been drafting her messages, including re-writing one about a sandwich, which is the moment she realised the typing had gone somewhere silly.
2. A way out. One question comes with permission not to answer, and both come with a reason for asking, so Priya can hear that she's being asked out of interest rather than interrogated — which is why she answers rather than dodges.
3. They both tell someone where they're going: the caller tells a sister, Priya tells a flatmate. It doesn't insult either of them because the caller says it first, about himself, and offers it as his own habit rather than as a condition he's imposing on her.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for testing the water with someone you've only ever read — naming the awkwardness, asking the real questions, and getting something in the diary without either of you having to promise anything. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. The whole thing turns on tempo. Push these lines out fast and bright and you sound like you're selling something; leave a small gap before the nosy ones, let your voice go up rather than down at the end, and they land as curiosity instead of interrogation. Nobody minds a personal question that arrives slowly.

😬 Naming the awkwardness

Phrase 1

The first ten seconds are strange for both of you, and pretending otherwise makes it stranger. Which opener says so out loud and hands her half of it?

A. "Hi! So, how are you? How's your day been?"
B. "Right — this is a bit odd, isn't it, after all that typing."
C. "Sorry if this is awkward. I'm quite an awkward person generally."
B — "Right — this is a bit odd, isn't it, after all that typing."
A pretends nothing unusual is happening, which leaves her carrying the strangeness on her own, and C loads it all onto you before she's even spoken. B does the useful thing: it says the awkward part out loud, and the tag question "isn't it" invites her to agree, so within one line the two of you are on the same side of it rather than suffering separately.
Phrase 2

You'd rather warn her that you're not great at this than have her decide, halfway through, that you're strange. Put the pieces in order:

on the phone.I'll be honest,rubbishI'm
"I'll be honest, I'm rubbish on the phone."
"Rubbish at" or "rubbish on" something is ordinary British English for being bad at it, and it's cheerfully unbothered rather than self-pitying, which is why it works here. Admitting a small weakness early buys you a lot of room afterwards, because every pause you leave now reads as the thing you warned her about rather than as boredom.

🔍 Asking the real questions

Phrase 3

You're about to ask something more personal than the messages have covered, and you'd rather flag it than sneak it in. Put the pieces in order:

slightly nosy?ask youCan Isomething
"Can I ask you something slightly nosy?"
Someone "nosy" wants to know about other people's private business, so by calling your own question nosy before you ask it you admit the cheek of it and take the sting out at the same time. The word "slightly" keeps it light, and asking permission means she gets to say no, which is usually the reason she says yes.
Phrase 4

You want to know where she lives — mostly because it decides whether any of this is realistic — without it sounding like you're collecting her address. Which version does that?

A. "Whereabouts are you, roughly? You don't have to say."
B. "Where do you live? What part of town, exactly?"
C. "You wouldn't want to tell me where you live, would you?"
A — "Whereabouts are you, roughly? You don't have to say."
"Whereabouts" already asks for a rough area rather than a precise spot, and "roughly" doubles down on that, so the question only wants as much as she's comfortable giving. Then "you don't have to say" hands the decision back to her. B asks for exactly the detail a stranger shouldn't be asking for, and C is so apologetic that it makes the whole subject sound shifty.
Phrase 5

You're curious about why she agreed to a call at all, and you want it to sound like genuine interest rather than a test. Complete the chunk — one word: "What ___ you say yes to this, out of interest?"

"What made you say yes to this, out of interest?"
"What made you…" asks about the reason behind a decision without any suggestion that the decision was wrong, which "why did you…" can sometimes carry. Tagging "out of interest" onto the end marks the question as idle curiosity rather than something she has to account for, so she can answer as fully or as briefly as she likes.
Phrase 6

You've done this before and it hasn't always worked, and saying so gives her permission to admit the same. Put the pieces in order:

if I'm honest.a couple of theseI've hadthat went nowhere,
"I've had a couple of these that went nowhere, if I'm honest."
If something "went nowhere" it carried on for a while and then simply stopped without arriving at anything, which is exactly what these conversations do. Saying it about yourself first, rather than asking her about her history, means you've taken the risk and she can match it — and "a couple" keeps the number vague and undramatic.

☕ Getting something in the diary

Phrase 7

Three weeks of messages had you expecting someone rather more intimidating than this. Complete the chunk — two words: "You're much easier to talk to than I'd ___ ___ in my head."

"You're much easier to talk to than I'd built up in my head."
To "build someone up in your head" is to construct a version of them out of very little and then treat it as real, and admitting you did it is a compliment twice over: she's better than the version, and you were nervous enough to have made one. It's warmer than "you're nice", because it tells her something true about you as well.
Phrase 8

You want to meet, but you don't want the meeting to carry the weight of three weeks' expectation. Which suggestion keeps it small?

A. "We should meet properly and give this a real chance, I think."
B. "Do you want to meet up sometime? No pressure, whenever suits."
C. "Shall we just do a coffee and see how we go?"
C — "Shall we just do a coffee and see how we go?"
Everything in C is doing the same job: "just" and "a coffee" make it small and cheap and easy to leave, while "see how we go" refuses to decide in advance what it means. A makes the coffee into a verdict, and B is so vague and so unbothered that "sometime" will quietly turn into never.

🛟 The sensible bit, said out loud

Phrase 9

You're meeting a stranger, and so is she. You want the sensible precautions on the table without either of you sounding suspicious of the other. Which line manages that?

A. "Somewhere public, obviously — you can't be too careful these days."
B. "Somewhere public, and I'll tell my sister where I am — I hope that's not weird."
C. "Is it alright if we meet somewhere public? Sorry, I know how that sounds."
B — "Somewhere public, and I'll tell my sister where I am — I hope that's not weird."
The trick is that it's about you: you are the one telling someone where you'll be, so nothing is being demanded of her and nothing is being implied about her. Then "I hope that's not weird" is said lightly enough that she can wave it away, which is usually the moment she says she does the same. A hints darkly at what strangers might do, and C apologises so hard that it turns a normal habit into a confession.
Phrase 10

One thing left. You want to build in a proper escape route — for both of you — so that neither is trapped in a bad coffee out of politeness, and so that leaving early wouldn't be a disaster. Say it out loud in your own words first. What would you offer her?

e.g. "If either of us wants to make an excuse and go after twenty minutes, that's completely fine."
Notice that it's "either of us", so it isn't you generously letting her off — it's a rule you're both under, which is what makes it comfortable rather than patronising. Naming a specific length of time and saying plainly that an excuse would be acceptable takes away the one thing people dread about meeting a stranger, which is being stuck there.

🔑 Make them yours

Testing the water is not only a dating skill — it's what you do before any first meeting where you know the words and not the person. Pin three of these to something real in your own life, so you'll actually reach for them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"Can I ask you something slightly nosy?" — a question you've been wanting to ask someone but haven't. What is it, and who's it for?

"You're much easier to talk to than I'd built up in my head." — someone you'd built a version of before you met them. What was the version, and what were they really like?

"Shall we just do a coffee and see how we go?" — something you've been putting off because it feels too big. How would you make it small?

The Twist

🍻 She'd rather skip the coffee

Your neat little plan is gone. Priya says a coffee sounds a bit like a job interview, and instead — her mates are having drinks on Saturday, come to that, you can meet everyone at once, it'll be far less awkward. She's warm, she means it, and she's not wrong that it might be easier. But it's her ground, her friends, and no way out after twenty minutes, and you've never even met her. Start the timer. Decide, out loud, in real time: do you take it, refuse it, or find a third thing — and can you do it without making her feel she's suggested something sinister? One rule: you may not say you'll think about it and let her go.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • Every personal question I asked came with a reason, a way out, or both.
  • I admitted something true about myself before asking her to.
  • I said the safety bit as my own habit, not as a condition for her.
  • The plan I made was small, specific, and easy for either of us to leave.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Typing lets you be your best self, slowly. You get to draft, and delete, and arrive at the funny version twenty minutes late — and so does she, which means three weeks of messages can produce two people who don't quite exist.

A voice ruins that, usefully. It comes with pauses in it, and a laugh you can't edit, and the small hesitation before an honest answer. Ten minutes of it will tell you what a month of writing wouldn't.

Which is why the call is worth the awkwardness. Not because it's romantic — mostly it isn't — but because it's the first thing either of you has said that wasn't drafted first.

Two bigger questions

  1. When you write, you get to edit yourself. When you speak, you don't. Which version of you is the more honest one — and which would you rather someone met first?
  2. We now do a great deal of getting-to-know-someone in writing, with strangers, before any voice is involved. Is that a kinder way to meet people, or just a slower way to find out the same things?
