The Psychology of Colour

Why does a blue room feel calm, a red sign feel urgent, and warm light feel like home?

We feel colour before we think about it

We rarely choose colours by accident. The walls of a hospital, the lighting in a restaurant, the red of a sale sign, the blue of a bank's logo — someone decided on each one, hoping it would make us feel a certain way. Let us start with you.

Let's talk:
  • What is your favourite colour — and has it changed over your life?
  • Is there a colour you would never paint a room, or never wear? Why?
  • Do you prefer warm light or cool, bright light at home? Does it depend on the time of day?
  • Do you think colours genuinely change how we feel, or is it mostly in our heads?

The hidden language of colour

Here is a short overview of how colour affects us. Read it, then we will talk about it.

Long before we read a word or hear a sound, we react to colour. Researchers who study this believe that some of our reactions are physical and almost universal, while others are learned from our culture — and telling the two apart is not always easy.

Warm colours — red, orange, yellow — tend to feel energetic and attention-grabbing. Red, in particular, raises the heart rate slightly and is linked to urgency and appetite. That is why so many sale signs, warning labels and fast-food brands are red: it makes us feel that we must act now. Restaurants often use it because it is thought to make people hungrier.

Cool colours — blue, green, purple — usually feel calm and reassuring. Blue is the world's most popular favourite colour, and it is everywhere in banking and technology because it makes customers feel that a company is trustworthy and stable. Green, the colour of nature, tends to make people feel balanced and relaxed, which is why it appears in pharmacies and hospitals.

Lighting matters just as much as colour. The same room can feel completely different under warm, golden light and under cold, blue-white light. Warm light makes a space feel cosy and intimate; cooler light makes it feel alert and businesslike. Offices, shops and homes are increasingly designed around this idea.

But colour is not the same everywhere. White, for example, suggests purity and weddings in much of the West, yet in parts of Asia it is the colour of mourning. So while some reactions may be wired into us, many are taught — which means the “meaning” of a colour can change completely as you cross a border.

A colour is never just a colour. It is a quiet message — and we receive it before we even notice.

Grammar focus — make someone feel something

Notice how the reading keeps saying what colours do to us: “Red makes us feel that we must act”, “Warm light makes a space feel cosy”, “Green makes people feel relaxed.” This pattern — make + someone/something + adjective — is one of the most natural ways in English to describe an effect.

1. The pattern

We use make to say that one thing causes a feeling or state in another. Crucially, there is no “to” and the adjective has no extra words.

make + object + adjective
Blue makes the room calm.  ·  Red makes people hungry.

We often add feel or look for feelings and appearance:

feel: Warm light makes me feel relaxed.

look: Pale colours make a small room look bigger.

You can also use make + object + base verb (no “to”): “The red sign makes you want to buy now.”

2. Common mistakes to avoid

✗ Wrong✓ Right
It makes me to feel calm.It makes me feel calm.
It makes me feeling calm.It makes me feel calm.
It makes the room to look bigger.It makes the room look bigger.

Note: after the passive, “to” does return — “I was made to wait” — but in the active effect pattern, never use “to”.
