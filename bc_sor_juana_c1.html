Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz

The seventeenth-century Mexican nun who built a library inside a convent and wrote one of the boldest defences ever made of a woman's right to think.

You talk first — before you read anything

This is a talking lesson, not a reading one. So before a single fact, the floor is yours.

Start here · 2 minutes

Just her name and one line she wrote: “If Aristotle had cooked, he would have written much more.” What does that line make you think about her — before you know anything else? Have you heard of her? Just talk.

Now meet her — one beat at a time

Three short beats. After each one, you do the talking.

Juana Inés de Asbaje was born near Mexico City, in what was then New Spain — in 1648 or 1651; two different baptism records exist, and scholars still debate which is hers. As a child she hid in the chapel of her grandfather's estate to read the books in his library, something forbidden to girls. Later she wrote that, on hearing that Mexico City had a university, she begged her mother to dress her in boys' clothes so she could study there. Her mother said no.

Over to you:
  • A girl so desperate to learn that she wanted to disguise herself as a boy. What would the equivalent be today — and where in the world is it still literally true?

In 1664 she was sent to Mexico City and became a lady-in-waiting at the viceroy's court. To test her, the viceroy gathered theologians, jurists, philosophers and poets to question the 17-year-old without warning; her answers astonished them. Then, in 1669, she entered the Hieronymite convent of San Jerónimo, and stayed there until her death. In her own words, she chose the convent because of her “total rejection of marriage” (our translation). In her convent rooms she collected a large library, received visitors — including two vicereines — and wrote poetry, plays and music. Her admirers called her “the Tenth Muse”.

Over to you:
  • She chose a life of strict religious rules because it was the only place she could think freely. Is that freedom, compromise, or both?

In November 1690 the Bishop of Puebla published one of her letters — a critique of a famous sermon — without her permission, under the pen name “Sor Filotea”, and added his own letter telling her to focus on religious rather than worldly studies. Her answer, the Respuesta a Sor Filotea, dated 1 March 1691, is a defence of women's right to learn. By 1693 she seems to have stopped writing. A penitential document from 1694 bears her name, signed “I, the worst of all”, though some question whether its tone is really hers. She died on 17 April 1695 after nursing sister nuns during an epidemic.

Over to you:
  • She went silent at the height of her powers. Do you read that as defeat, as a strategic retreat, or as something we simply cannot know?

Philosophy in the kitchen — and you do the thinking out loud

Sor Juana's big idea is that the mind cannot be fenced in — not by sex, not by walls, not by an official's letter. I will hand it to you in small pieces. After each piece, you talk — I will mostly listen.

In the Respuesta, she recalls that a superior once ordered her not to study. For about three months she obeyed and did not touch a book — but, she says, she could not stop thinking. Then she lists the “natural secrets” she has discovered while cooking: an egg holds together and fries in butter or oil but falls apart in syrup; the yolk and the white of the same egg behave in opposite ways. With heavy irony she asks: “what can we women know, but kitchen philosophies?” And then her punchline: “Si Aristóteles hubiera guisado, mucho más hubiera escrito” — “If Aristotle had cooked, he would have written much more.” (Translations ours.)

Put yourself there

Before I go on: what everyday place in your life — a kitchen, a bus, a workplace, a game — has quietly taught you something real about how the world works? Why do we not count that as “knowledge”?

The Respuesta argues that women have a right to formal education, and to act as intellectual authorities — not only by writing but by publishing. She suggested that learned older women could teach girls. She also described punishing herself as a girl: she would cut off four to six fingers' width of her hair and set herself something to learn before it grew back; if it grew back first, she cut it again — because, she wrote, a head so bare of knowledge did not deserve to be dressed in hair (our paraphrase).

Over to you:
  • Cutting her own hair as a penalty for learning too slowly — is that admirable discipline or something more troubling?
  • Her claim is not just “women can learn” but “women can be authorities”. Why is the second claim so much more threatening to people in power?

Her poetry carries the same argument with sharper teeth. In the 1680s she wrote “Hombres necios”: “Hombres necios que acusáis / a la mujer sin razón, / sin ver que sois la ocasión / de lo mismo que culpáis” — in Muriel Kittel's translation, “You foolish men, who accuse / Women without good reason, / You are the cause of what you blame.” And in one sonnet she asks the world why it persecutes her, when all she wants is “to fix beauty in my intellect, and never my intellect fix on beauty” (Edith Grossman's translation).

Her wit was a weapon: she argued from inside the rules of her world, and used them so precisely that the rules themselves began to look ridiculous.

Vocabulary — click each card to reveal

Ten words you need to discuss Sor Juana properly — and that work just as well for any serious conversation about education, power and freedom of thought. Tap to reveal the meaning and an example.

erudite / erudition
adjective / noun
Having or showing great knowledge gained from study.
"Visitors came to the convent to argue with the most erudite woman in New Spain."
cloistered
adjective
Living a life apart from the outside world, especially in a convent or monastery; also, sheltered.
"She was cloistered behind convent walls, yet her poems travelled to Spain."
patron / patronage
noun
A powerful person who supports an artist or scholar; that support.
"Without the patronage of the vicereine, her first book might never have been printed."
to censure / censure
verb / noun
To criticise someone formally and severely, especially from a position of authority.
"By 1693 she seems to have stopped writing to avoid the risk of official censure."
double standard
noun phrase
A rule applied more strictly to one group than to another.
"“Hombres necios” attacks the double standard that blamed women for men's behaviour."
intellectual autonomy
noun phrase
The freedom to think, study and reach conclusions for yourself.
"Her whole life was a fight for intellectual autonomy inside a system built to limit it."
irony / ironic
noun / adjective
Saying the opposite of what you mean, often to criticise.
"“What can women know but kitchen philosophy?” is pure irony — she clearly knows a great deal."
misogyny / misogynistic
noun / adjective
Dislike of, contempt for, or prejudice against women.
"Her poems expose the misogyny of a society that praised her and tried to silence her at the same time."
to recant
verb
To publicly withdraw a belief or statement, often under pressure.
"Some readers see the 1694 document as a forced recantation; others doubt it reflects her voice at all."
to silence
verb
To stop someone from speaking or expressing their views.
"The bishop's letter was polite, but its purpose was to silence her."

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that the convent gave her more freedom than marriage ever could have.
  • It might well be that her silence was a strategy rather than a surrender.
  • I suspect the bishop agreed with her more than he admitted.
  • You could argue that she was a feminist before the word existed.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say the Aristotle line is one of the best arguments ever made in one sentence.
  • What strikes me most is how she used humour as armour.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, her argument is still unfinished business in many places.

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure we should read modern feminism back into the seventeenth century.
  • I take the point, but doesn't calling the convent “freedom” ignore how strict it was?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…

A · The Kitchen Test (3–4 min)

Sor Juana found physics and chemistry in an egg and a pan of syrup. Think of an everyday activity that is usually seen as “not intellectual” — cooking, caring for children, fixing cars, playing a video game.

Argue one side: is the knowledge in that activity as real and valuable as knowledge from books — or is there an important difference? Build a real case — give your example, raise the strongest objection to your own view, then answer it.

B · The Bargain (3–4 min)

To get time, books and freedom to think, Sor Juana accepted a life of strict religious rules and gave up marriage.

What freedom would you give up to gain another — and is there a modern version of her bargain (a job, a city, a relationship)? Make the case with one concrete example from your real life.

C · Build Your Own (4–5 min)

Your turn to write a Respuesta. Think of a time someone told you — or someone like you — to stop studying, doing or saying something, “for your own good”.

Answer them the way she did: politely, with humour, and with arguments they cannot easily reject. I will play the bishop and push back, and you defend your case.

Your take — the conversation that matters most

Open discussion

  1. After today, has anything shifted in how you see Sor Juana — or in how you see the history of women and learning?
  2. She was celebrated and silenced by the same society. Can you think of anyone today who is both praised and punished for the same talent?
  3. Is there knowledge in your own life — from a kitchen, a workshop, a family — that has never been counted as “real” learning? Should it be?
  4. Her last years remain a mystery: did she give up, or was she forced? Why do you think people care so much about how this story ends?
  5. If you could ask Sor Juana one question, what would it be? And what do you think she would say back?
  6. Who is the next writer or thinker you would put in front of me — and what is the one idea of theirs I need to understand?
