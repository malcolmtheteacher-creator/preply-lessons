Why This Matters

Interdisciplinary research is increasingly valued but increasingly risky. You can be too specialized for humanists and not rigorous enough for scientists. You must signal credibility to both groups without oversimplifying either. The language you choose will make or break your reception.

Move 1: Jargon Translation

1 When to use field-specific terms, when to translate +
The key is signaling: use jargon to show you know the field, then translate immediately for outsiders. This signals competence AND accessibility. Never avoid technical terms—that signals you don't understand them.
Not This

"We analysed the text in a really simple way that everyone can understand."

This

"Using computational stylometry—quantitative analysis of word frequency, syntactic patterns, and semantic fields—we identified three distinct narrative voices in the manuscript."

Move 2: Epistemic Humility

2 Knowing what you don't know +
Acknowledge the limits of your approach in each discipline. Show you respect the deep expertise in both fields. Say: "While I'm not a literary theorist, I observe that..." or "From a neuroscience perspective, what's striking is..."
The Move

"I approach this as a neuroscientist, but I've learned from literary scholars that interpretation requires historical and cultural context that neurobiological data alone cannot provide."

Move 3: Explaining Your Methodology to Skeptics

3 Defending your choices to audiences trained in different rigor standards +
Scientists want replicability and n-size. Humanists want contextual depth and interpretive nuance. You must justify your choices in both vocabularies. Explain WHY you chose this methodology, not just WHAT you did.
For Both Audiences

"We selected a corpus of 50 autobiographical narratives for two reasons: large enough to identify statistical patterns, small enough to conduct close reading of individual rhetorical choices."

Move 4: Finding Shared Language

4 Using metaphors and frameworks that work across disciplines +
Concepts like "pattern," "structure," "function," "change over time," and "causation" exist across all fields. Use these bridge concepts to move between disciplinary languages without sounding shallow in either.
Bridge Language

"Both neuroscience and narrative analysis ask: How do we move from surface observation to underlying meaning? How do we trace causation when the system is complex?"

Move 5: Defending Interdisciplinarity Itself

5 Why you need both disciplines (not just applying one to another) +
The risk is seeming like you're just "neuroscience applied to literature" or vice versa. Show that the disciplines genuinely reshape each other. Neither could answer your question alone.
The Genuine Claim

"Neuroscience alone cannot explain why certain metaphors persist across centuries. Literary analysis alone cannot explain the brain states readers experience. Together, they reveal something neither field sees in isolation."

Move 6: Framing Novelty

6 How to position your work as new without dismissing prior work +
You must show that specialists in both fields have overlooked something. But do this respectfully. Say: "Previous neuroscientific work on language has not examined..." and "Literary theory has rightly focused on... but hasn't considered..."
Respectful Novelty

"Neuroscience has asked: What brain systems process narrative? Literary scholarship has asked: What do narratives mean? We ask: How do the meanings narratives have shape the brain systems that process them?"

Scaffold: Key Framing Moves

  • "This research sits at an intersection that neither discipline has adequately explored..."
  • "Both neuroscience and literary study grapple with how X happens. But they ask different questions."
  • "I'm a [discipline] by training. What surprised me when I engaged with [other discipline] is..."
  • "To specialists in Y: your tools are essential for understanding Z. Here's why."
