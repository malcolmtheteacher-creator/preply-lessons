Communicating Across Disciplines

Making your research intelligible to scholars from other fields

10:00

Disciplinary Languages and Literacies

Disciplines constitute interpretive communities with distinctive vocabularies, methodologies, evidentiary standards, and research questions. This specialization creates epistemic depth—scholars can communicate precisely about nuanced theoretical and methodological issues. However, it simultaneously creates barriers: disciplinary-specific terminology becomes opaque to outsiders.

What Makes Something "Jargon"?

Jargon is not inherently problematic. It reflects specialized knowledge and enables efficient communication among experts. "Jargon" becomes a problem only when it impedes understanding in contexts where clarity is needed. In disciplinary writing, jargon is appropriate. In interdisciplinary contexts, the same terminology constitutes unnecessary obfuscation. The skilled writer adjusts terminology strategically.

The Paradox of Simplification

When explaining disciplinary concepts to outsiders, you face a fundamental tension: simplifying terminology may lose technical precision, yet maintaining terminology creates comprehension barriers. The solution lies not in choosing one pole but in layering explanation: introduce the technical term, define it accessibly, provide examples, then use the term precisely. This enables both clarity and accuracy.

Disciplinary Assumptions as Hidden Barriers

Beyond vocabulary, disciplines embed specific assumptions about what counts as evidence, how research should be conducted, what explanations are satisfying. Economists accustomed to rational-choice models may find qualitative narrative analysis unconvincing. Humanists steeped in close reading may find statistical generalization insufficient. Cross-disciplinary communication requires making these assumptions explicit.

Disciplinary Communication Vocabulary

Evidentiary standards What types of evidence count as proof within a discipline; varies across fields.
Methodological paradigm Accepted approaches and frameworks for conducting research within a discipline.
Theoretical framework Conceptual structure organizing how a discipline understands its phenomena.
Epistemic community Group sharing common understandings about valid knowledge and how it is created.
Translation (across disciplines) Reformulation of disciplinary concepts in language comprehensible to other fields.
Commensurability Degree to which different disciplinary frameworks can be meaningfully compared.
Methodological pluralism Recognition that multiple methodological approaches can generate valid knowledge.
Boundary work Efforts to establish and maintain disciplinary distinctiveness; can impede collaboration.

Making Your Research Intelligible Across Boundaries

Effective cross-disciplinary communication requires strategic translation: retaining disciplinary specificity while building bridges of understanding.

The Translation Strategy

When introducing disciplinary concepts to mixed audiences, use a three-part structure: (1) define the concept using non-disciplinary language; (2) explain why your discipline considers it important; (3) connect it to concerns shared across disciplines. Example: "In our field, we use 'habitus'—the ingrained habits and dispositions people develop through their social experiences—because we've found it explains why people from different social backgrounds make different choices even when facing identical options. Economists might say this captures 'preferences'; psychologists might call it 'personality'; we see it as socially constructed and culturally variable."

Explaining Methodological Choices

Different disciplines trust different methodologies. Rather than asserting your methodology's superiority, explain its rationale. "We used ethnographic observation rather than surveys because our research question concerned the meaning people construct through daily practice—something difficult to access through questionnaires but visible through sustained participation." This demonstrates methodological sophistication while remaining accessible.

Effective translation: "We analyzed how linguistic patterns (word choice, syntax) in interviews reflect and reinforce social hierarchies—a concern central to sociolinguistics, though other disciplines might describe this as 'ideology' or 'discourse.'"

Excluding translation: "We conducted a critical discourse analysis following Fairclough's framework." (Assumes disciplinary literacy)

Language Choices for Cross-Disciplinary Audiences

Anticipate unfamiliar terminology: "We collected data through phenomenological interviews—open-ended conversations where we asked participants to describe their lived experience of [phenomenon]—rather than structured surveys."

Explain theoretical positioning: "We approached this question from an interpretive perspective, assuming that understanding human behavior requires grasping how people construct meaning, rather than assuming fixed causal mechanisms."

Connect to universal concerns: "This research contributes to understanding how organizations balance efficiency (concern across business, health, and policy contexts) with employee wellbeing."

Managing Interdisciplinary Collaborations

Interdisciplinary research multiplies perspectives but requires deliberate communication practices to bridge methodological and epistemological differences.

Establishing Common Ground

Successful collaborations begin by making assumptions explicit. Meet early to discuss: What counts as evidence in your respective fields? What would constitute a definitive answer to our research question? What are you skeptical about regarding other approaches? This conversation often reveals non-negotiable differences but also identifies surprising overlaps. Proceed by honoring these differences rather than attempting homogenization.

The Contribution Question

In collaborative projects, clarify each person's contribution and the value of interdisciplinary integration. Poor collaboration produces weak work where different perspectives sit uncomfortably alongside each other. Strong collaboration integrates insights such that the whole exceeds the sum of parts. This requires deliberate effort to identify where disciplinary differences generate novel understanding rather than mere redundancy.

Collaboration Reflection

If you have interdisciplinary collaborators, describe: What does each discipline contribute uniquely to this project? Where do methodological or evidentiary differences create tension? How are you resolving these tensions?

Your Disciplinary Position

Interdisciplinary work requires understanding your own disciplinary location. What are your field's distinctive concerns, methods, and evidentiary standards? What assumptions might outsiders not share? What can other disciplines learn from your perspective?

Reflect: What aspects of your disciplinary perspective feel so natural you sometimes forget others don't share them? What methodology or evidentiary standard would be controversial outside your field? What distinctive value does your discipline bring to interdisciplinary collaboration?

The Broader Purpose of Cross-Disciplinary Work

Interdisciplinary communication ultimately serves intellectual progress. Complex problems often resist single-disciplinary analysis. Climate change requires integration of climate physics, ecology, economics, political science, and ethics. Pandemic response requires epidemiology, immunology, public health, economics, and philosophy. Developing these integrative capacities—learning to communicate across disciplinary boundaries while respecting disciplinary expertise—constitutes an essential academic skill for addressing contemporary challenges.
