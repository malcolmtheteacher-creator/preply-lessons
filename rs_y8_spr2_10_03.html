Encounter: What Does Jihad Really Mean?

A Misunderstood Word

In English-language news, "jihad" is often used to mean "holy war" or terrorism. But this is a serious misunderstanding. The Arabic word "jihad" simply means "struggle" or "effort." It comes from the root word jahada, meaning to strive or exert oneself. For most Muslims, jihad is primarily about the personal, inner struggle to live a good life.

Greater Jihad (Al-Jihad Al-Akbar)

This is the more important form of jihad. It means the personal, spiritual struggle within yourself — the daily battle against selfishness, temptation, laziness, and wrongdoing. A famous hadith (saying of Prophet Muhammad) states: "The greatest jihad is the struggle of the soul against its own desires."

Examples of Greater Jihad

Choosing honesty when lying would be easier. Controlling your temper when someone angers you. Studying hard instead of giving up. Standing up for someone being bullied. Giving to charity when you want to keep your money.

Why It's Called "Greater"

Islamic scholars call this the "greater" jihad because the internal battle against your own weaknesses never ends. It requires constant effort, every single day. A military battle eventually finishes, but the struggle to be a good person lasts a lifetime.

Lesser Jihad (Al-Jihad Al-Asghar)

This refers to physical struggle, including military action. But Islam sets very strict rules about when this is ever allowed.

Must Be Defensive

Military jihad is only permitted in self-defence — to protect Muslim communities from attack. Wars of conquest or aggression are not jihad.

Legitimate Authority

Only a proper Islamic leader or government can declare lesser jihad. Individuals or small groups cannot decide for themselves.

Strict Rules of Conduct

Civilians must not be harmed. Trees and crops must not be destroyed. Prisoners must be treated with dignity. Places of worship must be protected.

"There is no compulsion in religion." — Quran 2:256
Key point: The rules for lesser jihad in Islam are remarkably similar to Christian Just War Theory. Both traditions try to set moral limits on when and how violence can be used.

Apply: Why Do Terrorists Misuse "Jihad"?

The Misuse of a Sacred Word

Groups like ISIS and Al-Qaeda call their violence "jihad." But the vast majority of Muslim scholars and communities worldwide reject this claim completely. According to Islamic teaching, what these groups do is not jihad — it is murder.

Why It's Not Jihad

They deliberately target and kill civilians. They kill other Muslims. They have no legitimate authority — they are self-appointed groups. They attack rather than defend. They cause maximum suffering, which Islamic law forbids.

Why They Use the Word

Calling terrorism "jihad" gives it a sense of religious authority and makes it easier to recruit followers. It makes their political violence seem like a religious duty. But using a religious word doesn't make an action religious — just as a Christian terrorist claiming to act "in Jesus's name" doesn't make their actions Christian.

Evaluate: Is the Concept of Jihad Helpful or Harmful?

Jihad is a positive and helpful concept

Greater jihad encourages personal responsibility and self-improvement. It motivates Muslims to be honest, generous, and compassionate. The rules for lesser jihad set clear moral limits on violence — similar to international law. The concept has inspired education, healthcare, social justice, and charitable work throughout Islamic history.

Jihad is a dangerous concept that causes harm

The word is easily misused by extremists to recruit and justify terrorism. Even though mainstream scholars reject this, the word's association with violence creates fear and Islamophobia. Having any religious concept that can be linked to military action is risky because people can interpret it to suit their political goals.

The concept itself is not the problem — misuse is

Every religion has concepts that have been misused. Christians used "holy war" to justify the Crusades. Buddhists in Myanmar used their faith to justify violence. The problem is not the concept of jihad but the human tendency to twist religious ideas for political purposes. Blaming the concept is like blaming a tool for how someone misuses it.

Key Terms

Jihad
Arabic word meaning "struggle" or "effort." Refers to both spiritual self-improvement and, in limited cases, physical defence.
Greater Jihad
The inner spiritual struggle against temptation, selfishness, and wrongdoing. Considered more important than lesser jihad.
Lesser Jihad
Physical or military struggle, only permitted in self-defence under strict conditions set by Islamic law.
Hadith
The recorded sayings and actions of Prophet Muhammad, used alongside the Quran as a guide for Muslim life.
Quran
The holy book of Islam, believed by Muslims to be the direct word of Allah revealed to Prophet Muhammad.
Legitimate Authority
The idea that only a proper government or Islamic leader can declare lesser jihad — not individuals or private groups.
Islamophobia
Prejudice, fear, or hatred directed against Muslims, often fuelled by misunderstandings about Islamic teachings like jihad.
Extremism
Holding extreme political or religious views, especially those that reject mainstream interpretations and promote violence.
