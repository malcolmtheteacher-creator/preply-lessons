Jihad — Greater and Lesser

What does Islam really teach about struggle, conflict, and warfare?

What You Think You Know

The word "jihad" often appears in the news, usually in connection with violence and terrorism. But this is a serious misunderstanding. Jihad is a core Islamic concept, and what it really means might surprise you.

Question 1: What does the word "jihad" mean in Arabic?

🔒Think about "struggle"!

Answer: Jihad literally means "struggle" or "effort." It comes from the Arabic word jahada, meaning to strive or exert oneself.

Question 2: Can you think of struggles that aren't military?

🔒Think broadly!

Answer: Yes! Struggling to overcome temptation, fighting injustice through law, battling disease through medicine, competing in sport—all these are forms of struggle.

Meeting the Islamic Teaching

The Two Forms of Jihad

Islamic scholars distinguish between two types of jihad, based on teachings in the Quran and Hadith (the recorded sayings of Prophet Muhammad).

Greater Jihad (Al-Jihad Al-Akbar)

This is the personal, spiritual struggle within oneself. It's the internal battle against ego, sin, desire, and temptation. A hadith (quote from the Prophet) says: "The greatest jihad is the struggle of the soul against inclination to evil."

Examples of Greater Jihad:
  • Struggling to be honest when dishonesty would benefit you
  • Overcoming anger and responding with patience
  • Fighting prejudice and learning to accept others
  • Resisting temptation and living ethically
  • Learning and educating yourself
  • Speaking truth even when it's risky

For most Muslims, this inner jihad is the most important. It's about becoming a better person and drawing closer to Allah (God). Many Muslims see greater jihad as the true meaning of the faith.

Lesser Jihad (Al-Jihad Al-Asghar)

This refers to physical, military struggle. It can include defending Islam and Muslims against aggression, or fighting to protect the vulnerable. But Islam sets very strict rules for when this is justified.

Rules for Military Jihad in Islam:
  • It must be defensive, not aggressive conquest
  • It requires permission from a legitimate Islamic authority (not individuals)
  • It must have a just cause (self-defence, protecting the oppressed)
  • Civilians must not be deliberately harmed
  • Sacred places must not be desecrated
  • Weapons causing unnecessary suffering are forbidden
  • Prisoners of war must be treated with dignity
  • Trees and crops must not be destroyed

These rules are remarkably similar to Christian Just War theory and modern laws of war. They show that Islam also tries to set moral limits on violence.

Understanding the Misunderstanding

Why Do Terrorists Call Themselves "Jihadists"?

Groups like ISIS claim to wage "jihad," but Islamic scholars worldwide reject this. Why?

How Terrorists Misuse Jihad:
  • They target civilians deliberately, which Islamic law forbids
  • They kill other Muslims, which the Quran explicitly forbids
  • They have no legitimate authority—they're just groups deciding for themselves
  • They're not fighting defensively; they're attacking
  • They cause maximum suffering, against Islamic teaching

Most Muslim leaders and scholars say these terrorists are misusing jihad. They're using the word as a label for terrorism, not following Islamic teaching. It's like if a Christian terrorist claimed to act in Jesus's name—that wouldn't make their actions Christian.

Important fact: The vast majority of Muslims condemn terrorism. Polls show that Muslims are often the first victims of terrorist violence, and Muslim communities work hard to counter extremism.

Jihad in Practice Throughout History

Muslim leaders have interpreted jihad differently at different times. Some examples:

  • Defensive wars: Early Muslim armies fought to defend Islamic territories from Byzantine and Persian empires
  • Spiritual jihad: Muslim scholars spent lifetimes in intellectual jihad—studying, writing, preserving knowledge
  • Medical jihad: Muslim doctors saw healthcare as a form of jihad (serving humanity)
  • Anti-colonialism: Muslims in the 20th century used the language of jihad when fighting European colonialism
  • Social justice: Modern Islamic activists use jihad to describe the struggle for justice and equality

Applying Islamic Teaching

Scenario 1: Personal Temptation

A Muslim teenager is pressured by friends to lie to their parents about where they're going. They want to go, but they know it's wrong. This is greater jihad—an internal struggle against the desire to do wrong.

Islamic response: The struggle to be honest is jihad. Choosing integrity over peer pressure is a spiritual victory—even though no physical battle is fought.

Scenario 2: Military Attack

A country attacks another country's territory, killing civilians. Could the attacked country defend itself through lesser jihad (military means)?

Islamic response: Yes, defensive military action is permitted. But it must follow Islamic rules: don't target civilians, treat prisoners well, don't cause unnecessary suffering. If those rules are broken, it's not jihad by Islamic definition.

Question 3: Is all military action "jihad" in Islam?

🔒Think about the conditions!

Answer: No. Military action is only jihad if it meets Islamic criteria: legitimate authority, just cause (defence), protection of civilians, and following the rules of warfare. A war of conquest or aggression isn't jihad.

Evaluating the Teaching

Strengths of the Islamic Approach

  • Greater jihad emphasises personal responsibility and spiritual development
  • Rules for military jihad set clear moral limits on violence
  • Protects civilians and prisoners, similar to modern laws of war
  • Allows for diverse interpretations and non-violent responses
  • Emphasises that struggle takes many forms beyond combat

Challenges

  • Throughout history, some Muslim leaders have interpreted jihad more broadly
  • The concept can be misused by groups with political ambitions
  • Defining "legitimate authority" and "just cause" is contested
  • Modern warfare makes protecting civilians increasingly difficult

Question 4: Why do mainstream Muslims reject terrorist claims to jihad?

🔒Think about Islamic rules!

Answer: Because terrorists deliberately kill civilians, attack without legitimate authority, and violate the rules that Islamic law sets for any military action. By Islamic standards, they're not waging jihad—they're committing murder.

Consolidating Your Understanding

Key Ideas

  • Jihad means struggle, not necessarily war. The internal spiritual struggle is considered greater than military struggle.
  • Islamic teaching limits military action. Only defensive wars with just cause are permitted, and even then, civilians must be protected.
  • Terrorists misuse the term. Mainstream Muslims reject the claim that terrorism is jihad because it violates Islamic law.
  • Jihad is personal responsibility. Each Muslim is called to struggle against evil and temptation in their own life.

Key Vocabulary

  • Jihad: Struggle or effort, particularly spiritual struggle
  • Greater Jihad: The internal struggle against ego and temptation
  • Lesser Jihad: Military struggle in defense of Islam
  • Hadith: Recorded sayings and actions of Prophet Muhammad
  • Quran: The holy book of Islam
