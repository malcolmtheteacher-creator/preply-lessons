The Red Room

A sceptic spends one night in a supposedly haunted room. The darkness does not need a ghost.

Adapted for B2/C1 from H. G. Wells's 1896 story

Memory first

Have a go without notes; uncertainty is useful here.

  1. Complete: “The corridor was so dark that…”

    One possible answer: “The corridor was so dark that I could not see the door.” So + adjective + that connects intensity to a result.

  2. Change “The room was extremely strange” using such + noun.

    “It was such a strange room.” Use such + a/an + adjective + singular countable noun.

  3. Explain the difference between too frightened to move and frightened enough to move.

    Too frightened to move means fear prevents movement. Frightened enough to move means the amount of fear is sufficient to cause movement.

The story in three movements

1 · Three warnings

The three old caretakers watched the young man from the far side of the fire. One had a withered arm, one walked with a stick, and the oldest repeated the same warning whenever the room fell quiet.

“It is your own choosing.”

The visitor had come to Lorraine Castle to prove that its famous Red Room was not haunted. Their warnings strengthened his determination. He took a candle and followed their directions through passages that seemed to turn away from the inhabited part of the house.

Armour stood in the shadows. A bronze statue stretched a hand across the corridor and touched his shoulder as he passed. He laughed aloud, though there was no one to hear him.

The Red Room was large, cold and furnished with dark objects whose shapes were difficult to recognise. He inspected the walls and windows, then lit every candle he could find. Soon flames stood on the table, the mantelpiece, the shelves and the floor.

The room changed. Corners became ordinary corners; shadows retreated behind furniture.

He walked around the room once more, naming what he saw: a wardrobe, a mirror, a curtained bed, a fireplace. Naming each object made it manageable. Superstition, he decided, depended on distance and poor light.

“Now,” he said, “if anything wishes to frighten me, it must do so in full light.”

His voice returned from the high ceiling. He sat beside the fire and waited for midnight.

Answer from memory

Why does the narrator light so many candles if he claims not to be afraid?

His behaviour reveals fear before his language admits it. The candles are evidence that his scepticism already needs support.

2 · The first light dies

The first candle went out at twelve.

It was the candle beside the fireplace. The visitor crossed the room and relit it. Before he had replaced the matchbox, a flame on the table disappeared.

There was no wind. The curtains did not move.

He relit the second candle. A third died behind him.

For several minutes he moved calmly from light to light, determined to treat the matter as a practical problem. But the extinctions came faster. A flame vanished on the mantelpiece; two more disappeared near the door. Darkness began to gather in separate pools.

He checked a window and found it firmly fastened. He held one flame close to the wall and watched it burn straight and steady. Then a candle behind him went out. The darkness was not moving with the air; it appeared to be moving with his attention.

He ran with a candle in one hand and matches in the other. The room was so large that every journey allowed another flame to die. Soon he could no longer remember which candles he had relit and which had never been touched.

The darkness was no longer an absence. It appeared to move ahead of him, choosing each light.

Only the fire remained. He threw candles into it, but the flames sank as if covered by an invisible hand. Something struck his knee. He turned, collided with a table and heard glass break.

The last light went out.

He ran towards where he believed the door should be. Furniture caught his legs. A heavy object struck his forehead, and the darkness closed over him completely.

Answer from memory

How does a practical task—relighting candles—turn into panic?

The repeated failures remove his sense of control. Speed replaces thought, and his own movement creates further confusion.

3 · The name of the ghost

He woke in daylight in another room. His head was bandaged, and the three caretakers stood around the bed.

The oldest leaned towards him. “Did you see it?”

“There is something in that room,” the young man answered.

They began offering names. Was it the old earl? The woman who had died on the stairs? The child whose death no one discussed?

“No,” he said. “It was none of them.”

He remembered the first small flame dying and the speed with which reason had followed it. The room had not shown him a face or touched him with a hand. It had allowed him to supply every terror himself.

His bruises had come from tables and walls, not claws. His blood was on the corner of the bed, not on the hand of a ghost. The evidence supported him—and yet the memory of those deliberate extinctions made certainty impossible.

“The ghost is fear. Fear lives in that room. It follows you through the darkness and takes away your judgement. It makes a shadow seem alive and turns your own movement against you.”

The caretakers were silent. His explanation named what had attacked him, but it did not explain why the candles had gone out one after another in a sealed room.

The oldest caretaker looked towards the passage.

“Yes,” he said at last. “The room has always been full of fear.”

Answer from memory

Does naming Fear explain the event completely, or leave room for a supernatural reading?

It provides a psychological explanation, but the unexplained pattern of extinguished candles preserves ambiguity.

Grammar — so / such / too / enough

so + adjective/adverb + that

It was so dark that he lost direction.

Intensity leading to a result.

such + (a/an) + adjective + noun

It was such a strange room.

Intensity expressed through a noun phrase.

too / enough + infinitive

Too frightened to think; calm enough to laugh.

A limit or sufficient degree.

Mixed practice

Complete all six before revealing. Explain the choice, not only the missing words.

The caretaker's warning was ___ vague that it increased his curiosity.

It was ___ unsettling silence that every sound felt deliberate.

The corridor was too narrow for two people ___ side by side. (walk)

There were ___ many candles that he felt safe.

The matches were not dry enough ___. (light)

It was such ___ old furniture that every collision hurt.

  1. so — So + adjective + that links the degree of vagueness to its result.
  2. such an — Silence is a singular countable noun here, so use such an + adjective + noun.
  3. to walk — Too + adjective + to-infinitive expresses a limit that prevents the action.
  4. so — Use so many before a plural countable noun.
  5. to light — Enough is followed by a to-infinitive when it enables an action.
  6. — / no article before uncountable furniture — Furniture is uncountable, so no article follows such.

A short analysis

Give your answer first. Then reveal a model and identify one idea you handled differently.

A supernatural figure could be fought or escaped, but fear converts the narrator's own mind into the threat. Wells psychologises the haunting without fully solving the candles. That remaining uncertainty allows rational explanation and Gothic possibility to occupy the room together.
