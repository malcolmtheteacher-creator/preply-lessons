Branding Vocabulary & Strategy

When discussing how a brand is promoted in a country, consider these key elements:

Product & Quality

What it is: The physical item or service the brand offers

In branding: Quality, innovation, reliability—is the product high-end, value, or premium?

Example: Apple positions iPhones as premium, innovative products. Xiaomi positions phones as high-quality at lower cost.

Target Audience

What it is: Who the brand tries to reach

In branding: Age, income, values, lifestyle—does the brand target young professionals, families, luxury seekers?

Example: Nike targets athletes and fitness enthusiasts. Hermès targets wealthy, status-conscious consumers.

Brand Image & Values

What it is: The personality and values the brand projects

In branding: Is the brand cool, traditional, innovative, sustainable, family-oriented, rebellious?

Example: Tesla projects innovation and environmental consciousness. Coca-Cola projects happiness and universality.

Advertising & Marketing

What it is: How the brand communicates with its audience

In branding: TV, social media, influencers, events—does it adapt for local markets or use global campaigns?

Example: McDonald's uses global "I'm lovin' it" but adapts menu and cultural references locally. Uniqlo uses minimalist, lifestyle-based advertising globally.

Name & Logo

What it is: The visual and linguistic identity

In branding: Does the name translate well? Does the logo have cultural meaning? Is it instantly recognisable?

Example: Apple's logo is simple and universal. Coca-Cola's script is iconic worldwide. Some brands change names for different markets (e.g., Esso → Exxon in some regions).

Understanding Brand Strategy

Global vs. Local

The key tension in global branding:

  • Standardise: Keep the same brand globally (consistency, efficiency)
  • Adapt: Modify for local cultures (relevance, acceptance)

Most successful global brands do both—core identity stays the same, but execution adapts to local tastes and values.

Grammar: Inversion for Emphasis

Inversion is a powerful technique to emphasise negative or limiting statements. It places the negative word at the beginning of the sentence, which gives it more impact and formality.

Basic Pattern

Normal: Companies never bring out different TV advertisements for different countries.

Inverted: Never do companies bring out different TV advertisements for different countries.

The inverted version sounds more emphatic and formal. Perfect for presentations where you want to make a strong point.

Common Inversion Patterns

Never + do/does/did + subject + verb

Normal: Young people rarely identify with long-established local values.

Inverted: Rarely do young people identify with long-established local values.

Effect: Makes the statement sound more conclusive and dramatic.

Only + can/could + subject + verb

Normal: We can only occasionally consider Europe as a single homogenous area.

Inverted: Only occasionally can we consider Europe as a single homogenous area.

Effect: Emphasises the limitation or restriction.

Never + should/could + subject + verb

Normal: Lancôme Paris should never be considered a low-quality brand.

Inverted: Never should Lancôme Paris be considered a low-quality brand.

Effect: Very strong, emphatic statement against a misconception.

Seldom + do/does + subject + verb

Normal: We seldom associate 'Made in Germany' with low quality.

Inverted: Seldom do we associate 'Made in Germany' with low quality.

Effect: Emphasises the rarity and makes the statement more formal.

Hardly ever + will/would + subject + verb

Normal: You will hardly ever find Scottish-made haggis in America.

Inverted: Hardly ever will you find Scottish-made haggis in America.

Effect: Emphasises how rare or impossible something is.

Negative Words Used in Inversion

These words can be inverted for emphasis:

  • Never, seldom, rarely, hardly, scarcely — for very negative statements
  • Only, only then, not only — for limitations or conditions
  • Not, no, neither — for straightforward negation
  • At no time, under no circumstances — for absolute negation

Presentation Task: Globalising a National Brand

You will prepare and deliver a 2-minute presentation about how a successful national brand in your country could be globalised.

Step 1: Choose Your Brand

Select a successful national brand from your country

Step 2: Prepare Your Content

The Brand: What is it like? Why is it successful?

How could it be globalised?

  • Target audience: Who would buy this globally? (age, income, values)
  • Adaptations: What would need to change to appeal to international markets?
  • Advertising: How would you market it globally? What platforms? What message?
  • Name and logo: Does the name work in other languages? Would the logo resonate globally?

Timed Speaking Task

10:00

Instructions: Deliver your 2-minute presentation. You can use the full 10 minutes to practice multiple times and refine your delivery.

What to Include in Your Presentation

  • Introduction: Name the brand and briefly say why it's successful at home
  • Global potential: Describe how it could appeal to international audiences
  • Strategy: Explain your approach to target audience, adaptations, and marketing
  • Use inversion: Try to use at least one inverted sentence for emphasis (e.g., "Rarely do consumers find this level of quality at this price," or "Never should we abandon the core identity.")
  • Conclusion: Which brand would most likely be successful globally and why?
