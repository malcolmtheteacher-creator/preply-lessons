Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The travellers stood in silence before the towering stone monolith — a single figure hacked from one vast block, three times the height of a man, staring out over the valley with blank, heavy eyes.
a monolith (n) — a single great block of stone, especially one shaped into a pillar or figure. On Rapa Nui these carved monoliths are called moai.
2. High on the hillside lay the quarry, where for centuries workers had cut blocks of stone straight out of the rock face and dragged them down to be shaped.
a quarry (n) — a place where stone is dug or cut out of the ground for building or carving.
3. With nothing but stone tools and patience, the craftsman began to hew the figure, striking the same seam hour after hour until a face slowly emerged from the rock.
to hew (v) — to cut or shape something hard, such as stone or wood, with heavy blows from a tool.
4. The valley was divided between several clans, each one a large family group with its own land, its own leaders, and its own fierce pride.
a clan (n) — a group of families who share a common ancestor and act together as one community.
5. Where a thick forest had once covered the hills, there was now only bare grass; years of deforestation had stripped the land of almost every tree.
deforestation (n) — the clearing or destruction of forests, so that trees no longer cover the land.
6. The village lay in perfect seclusion: the nearest neighbours were a thousand miles of empty ocean away, and for generations no stranger ever appeared on the horizon.
seclusion (n) — the condition of being screened off and kept apart from other people or the outside world; sheltered privacy.
7. The old chief died content, certain that his children's children — the descendants who would carry his blood and his name for centuries after him — would still be tending these same fields.
a descendant (n) — a person related to someone who lived earlier; a member of a later generation of the same family.
8. In their fury the attackers heaved against the great pillar until, with a groan, it began to lean — and then to topple, crashing face-down into the dust.
to topple (v) — to fall over, or to push something tall over so that it falls.
9. No one could prove what had really happened, so each expert put forward a different conjecture, then spent as much effort attacking a rival's as defending his own.
a conjecture (n) — an opinion or conclusion formed on incomplete evidence; a reasoned guess that has not been proven.
10. Against all the odds the little community continued to thrive: the gardens were full, the children healthy, and the people went on building and growing year after year.
to thrive (v) — to grow well, stay healthy and be successful, especially in difficult conditions.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The most remote place on Earth

In the South Pacific, more than 2,000 miles from the coast of South America and over 1,000 miles from the nearest inhabited island, sits a tiny triangle of land just fifteen miles across. Its people call it Rapa Nui; the wider world calls it Easter Island. There is almost nothing else for thousands of miles in any direction. Few places on the planet are so completely defined by their isolation.

Around the year 1200 AD, give or take a century, a small group of Polynesian voyagers found this speck of rock at the end of an extraordinary sea journey, navigating thousands of miles of open ocean by the stars, the swell and the flight of birds. They stepped ashore onto an island then covered in a vast palm forest, and they stayed. Cut off from the rest of humanity, they built a society all their own.

Over the following centuries that society did something astonishing. The islanders divided into clans, and each clan honoured its ancestors by carving enormous human figures from stone — the moai. There are close to 900 of them. Most were quarried at a single volcanic crater, Rano Raraku, where the soft rock could be cut with stone tools. The largest finished moai stands ten metres tall and weighs as much as eighty tonnes. Then, somehow, these giants were moved miles across the island and raised upright on stone platforms, their backs to the sea, their faces watching over the living.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What makes the island's location so extraordinary?

2. Who were the moai built to honour, and roughly how many are there?

1. It is one of the most remote inhabited places on Earth — over 1,000 miles from the nearest island and more than 2,000 from a continent, defined by total isolation.

2. They honoured the clans' ancestors; there are close to 900 moai.

Discuss:
  • A small population on a tiny island poured generations of effort into carving and moving hundreds of stone giants. What might drive a community to invest so much in something like that?

Part 2 — The vanished forest

When Europeans finally arrived — the Dutch explorer Jacob Roggeveen sighted the island on Easter Sunday, 1722, which is how it got its European name — they found something that puzzled them. The island was almost bare. The great palm forest that had once covered it was gone. To outsiders it seemed impossible: how could people raise such monuments and yet leave themselves a treeless, windswept rock?

For a long time, the most popular answer was a grim one, and it became famous through books read all over the world. In this version, the islanders were the authors of their own ruin. They are said to have cut down their forest recklessly — to clear fields, to burn as fuel, and, above all, to move and raise their ever-larger moai. As the trees disappeared, the soil washed away, the fishing canoes could no longer be built, and food ran short. Some researchers argue that the islanders destroyed the very environment that kept them alive, in a slow self-inflicted disaster often called an "ecocide." In this telling, starvation drove the clans to war, and in their rage rival groups toppled one another's moai, pulling down the ancestors of their enemies. By the time outsiders arrived, the story goes, a once-great society had already collapsed.

It is a powerful, even haunting story — a warning, many said, for our own world. But over the past twenty years a growing number of scholars have begun to deny that it is true.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What surprised the first European visitors about the island in 1722?

2. According to the older "ecocide" story, what caused the society's collapse?

1. The island was almost bare — the great palm forest that had once covered it was gone, even though the people had raised huge monuments.

2. That the islanders themselves cut down their forest recklessly (partly to move the moai), wrecking their environment until soil loss and hunger triggered war and a self-inflicted collapse.

Discuss:
  • The "ecocide" story spread because it works as a warning for the modern world. Does a story being a useful lesson make it more likely to be believed — even if the evidence is thin?

Part 3 — Rewriting the warning

The newer view does not deny that the forest disappeared — it clearly did. But it challenges almost everything else. For one thing, the trees may not have been felled by people alone. Polynesian rats, which arrived with the first settlers, bred in their millions and ate the palm nuts, so that for every tree cut down, few or none grew back. Recent research suggests rats played a major part in stripping the island of its forest. And losing the trees, these scholars argue, did not doom the islanders: they adapted, farming cleverly with stone gardens, and their numbers stayed modest and steady. Many now deny that any great pre-European collapse ever happened at all.

So what did destroy the Rapa Nui world? The newer answer points not inward but outward — to the visitors. European ships brought diseases to which the islanders had no resistance. Then, in 1862 and 1863, slave raiders from Peru fell upon the island and seized perhaps 1,500 people — a huge share of the population — to work and die in mines and plantations far away. Most never returned; the few who did brought smallpox home with them. Within a few years a community of several thousand had crashed to barely a hundred souls. The carving of moai had already ended, and the toppling of the great statues — every standing moai was eventually pulled down — went on through these terrible decades.

Today both stories are still argued over. One side insists the islanders wrecked their own home; the other insists the real catastrophe was inflicted from outside. The truth is probably tangled and partly lost. What is certain is that the people of Rapa Nui did not vanish: their descendants live on the island still, and in recent years they have raised some of the fallen moai back onto their feet. The stone giants have gone from a warning about how a people destroyed themselves to a warning about what was done to them — and the argument over which is right has itself become part of the island's mystery.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Give two ways the newer view challenges the older "ecocide" story.

2. What happened to the island's people in 1862–63, and what was the result?

1. Any two of: rats (not just people) may have destroyed the forest by eating palm nuts; losing the trees did not doom the islanders, who adapted; the population stayed modest and steady; there may have been no great pre-European collapse; the real catastrophe came from outside (disease and slave raids).

2. Peruvian slave raiders seized around 1,500 people; most never returned, and the few who did brought smallpox — within years the population crashed from several thousand to about a hundred.

Discuss:
  • One story blames the islanders; the other blames outsiders. Why might it matter — politically and morally — which version of Rapa Nui's history people choose to believe?

The grammar this story runs on: reporting verbs & patterns (suggest / claim / deny)

This whole story is a clash of theories — and to retell it, you need the verbs English uses to report what people say, think and claim. Reporting verbs are how you stand back from a debate: instead of stating "the islanders destroyed their forest" as fact, you say "some researchers argue that they did," and you stay in control of whose claim is whose. The catch is that each verb takes its own pattern, and getting the pattern wrong is one of the clearest marks of a non-native speaker.

The four patterns to master:
• verb + (that) + clause — most reporting verbs: argue, claim, insist, deny, suggest, admit. "Some researchers argue that the islanders destroyed their own forests." "Others deny that the collapse was self-inflicted."
• suggest + (that) + clause — note: not "suggest to do" and not "suggest him to go." "One theory suggests that rats played a part."
• suggest / deny / admit + -ing — these can take an -ing form instead of a clause: "She admitted making a mistake." "He denied cutting down the trees." "I suggest waiting for more evidence."
• urge / warn + somebody + to + verb — these need a person, then to: "Scientists urge us to question the old story." "Historians warn readers not to trust a single source."
Two traps to avoid:
• suggest never takes "to + person + to do": write "I suggest that he leave" or "I suggest leaving," never "I suggest him to leave."
• After deny and admit you can use -ing ("denied taking it") or that ("denied that he took it") — but never a plain to-infinitive (not "denied to take it").

3. Report the debate — your own sentences

Use the reporting verb given to report each idea about Rapa Nui. Keep the right pattern.

2. (insist) Others that outsiders were to blame.
