Presenting at Conferences

Sharing Research with the Academic Community

10:00

Academic Conferences: Overview

Conferences are the spaces where researchers share ongoing work, present findings, and build intellectual networks. Understanding conference culture is essential for effective participation.

Types of Conference Presentations

Paper Presentation (20-30 minutes): Presenter delivers a prepared talk, followed by Q&A. Typically 15-20 minute talk with 5-10 minutes for questions.

Panel Discussion (60-90 minutes): Multiple presenters address a common theme. May include formal presentations, moderated discussion, or audience interaction.

Poster Presentation: Visual presentation of research on a large board, with the researcher available for one-on-one conversations.

Lightning Talks/Flash Sessions: Brief presentations (5-10 minutes) allowing many researchers to share emerging ideas.

Conference Submission Strategy

Most conferences require abstract submission 2-4 months before the event. Abstracts (150-250 words) must clearly articulate your research question, methods, and significance.

Audience and Networking

Conference audiences include peers, competitors, potential collaborators, and senior scholars. Present your work as a contribution to ongoing scholarly conversations, not as a finished product. Be generous with other presenters and receptive to feedback.

Preparing Your Presentation

Slide Design Principles

Minimalism: Each slide should contain one main idea. Avoid dense text; use visuals when possible.

Legibility: Fonts must be readable from the back of the room (minimum 24pt). High contrast between text and background.

Consistency: Maintain consistent fonts, colour schemes, and formatting across all slides.

Emphasis: Use formatting (bold, colour, size) to highlight key points. Avoid multiple emphatic techniques on the same slide.

Content Structure

Slides should follow your talk, not replace it. Each slide prompts audience attention toward key points while you elaborate verbally.

Vocabulary: Conference Terms

Keynote A major address by a prominent scholar opening or closing a conference
Concurrent Session Multiple presentations happening simultaneously; audience chooses which to attend
Peer Review Expert evaluation of abstracts or papers for quality and relevance
Call for Papers Announcement inviting submission of papers for conference consideration
Session Chair Scholar who moderates presentations in a session and manages time
Proceedings Published collection of papers or abstracts presented at a conference
Respondent Scholar invited to formally respond to or critique a presentation
Abstract Brief summary of research project, typically 150-250 words

Opening Your Presentation

Attention-Grabbing Openers

• "Research on X typically assumes that... but recent evidence suggests otherwise."

• "Imagine a scenario where... This is precisely what our study explores."

• "Despite decades of research, a crucial question remains unanswered: How does X influence Y?"

• "The conventional wisdom suggests that X. I" + "'"m here to complicate that understanding."

Establishing Relevance

• "This work matters because..."

• "Understanding X has implications for policy, practice, and theory..."

• "The gaps in current knowledge that we address are significant because..."

Guiding Your Audience

Signposting Your Structure

• "I have three main points to cover today. First... Second... Third..."

• "We" + "'"ve established X. Now I want to examine Y as a test case."

• "Having outlined the theoretical framework, I" + "'"ll now present our methodology."

• "So where does this leave us? The implications fall into three categories..."

Transitions Between Sections

• "That addresses the problem. Now let" + "'"s consider how we investigated it."

• "The background is clear. The question is: what did we find?"

• "These findings raise an important question: what accounts for this pattern?"

Presenting Findings and Implications

Discussing Results

• "The data reveal three key patterns... First... Second... Third..."

• "Perhaps counterintuitively, our analysis shows..."

• "This finding aligns with Smith" + "'"s theoretical prediction but contradicts Chen" + "'"s empirical results."

• "The magnitude of this effect is significant. To put it in perspective..."

Articulating Significance

• "Why should this matter to you? These findings suggest..."

• "The theoretical implications are substantial. This revises our understanding of..."

• "Practically, this research has applications for..."

• "The broader significance lies in its challenge to the assumption that..."

Closing Effectively

• "In conclusion, this research demonstrates that..."

• "To return to our opening question: the answer appears to be..."

• "Future research should investigate whether these patterns extend to..."

• "I" + "'"d like to thank [collaborators] and welcome your questions."

Developing Your Abstract

Step 1: Research Question (2-3 sentences)

State your research problem and question clearly.

Step 2: Methods (2-3 sentences)

Briefly explain your approach and why it" + "'"s appropriate.

Step 3: Findings (2-3 sentences)

Highlight your key findings or arguments.

Step 4: Significance (1-2 sentences)

Explain why your work matters to the field.

Pro Tip: Read your abstract aloud. It should flow smoothly and be understandable to someone with general knowledge of your field but not your specific expertise.

Crafting Your Talk

Time Budget for a 20-Minute Presentation

Introduction: 2 minutes | Background/Literature: 4 minutes | Methods: 3 minutes | Findings: 8 minutes | Implications/Conclusion: 3 minutes

Allocate your time strategically. Most of your time should go to your novel contribution (findings/analysis), not to background others may already know.

Practice Recommendations

Practice your talk at least 3 times aloud. Time yourself. Adjust for clarity and pacing. Practice in front of colleagues for feedback.

Handling Questions with Confidence

Preparing for Q&A

Anticipate likely questions. What assumptions might audience members challenge? What limitations might people ask about? What extensions might people suggest? Have thoughtful answers ready.

Listening to Questions

Pay careful attention. Repeat or paraphrase the question if it" + "'"s unclear. This gives you processing time and ensures you understand what" + "'"s being asked.

Strategic Response Techniques

Acknowledging Valid Points

• "That" + "'"s an excellent point. You" + "'"re right that we didn" + "'"t address X directly, but our framework suggests..."

• "I hadn" + "'"t considered that angle. That would be a valuable direction for future research."

• "Thank you for highlighting that limitation. It" + "'"s true that our sample is limited to..."

Defending Your Position

• "I understand that concern, but our methodology accounts for that by..."

• "While that interpretation is possible, the weight of evidence points toward..."

• "That" + "'"s a fair question. Here" + "'"s why we made that methodological choice..."

Graceful Admission of Uncertainty

• "I haven" + "'"t investigated that question, but it" + "'"s an important one that future research should address."

• "That" + "'"s beyond the scope of this project, but I suspect..."

• "I don" + "'"t have a definitive answer, but I can share my preliminary thinking..."

Difficult Question Scenarios

The Hostile Questioner

Stay calm and professional. Address the substantive concern beneath the hostility. "I appreciate your skepticism. Let me address your core concern..." Never match their tone or defensiveness.

The Rambling Question

Politely clarify. "I want to make sure I understand. Are you asking about X or Y?" This helps you respond precisely and demonstrates good listening.

The Self-Promotional Question

Acknowledge their work respectfully while maintaining focus on your research. "Yes, your work on X is important. Our findings complement that by showing..."

The Question You" + "'" + "re Not Prepared For

Honest acknowledgment. "That" + "'"s a terrific question I hadn" + "'"t considered. My instinct is that..., but I" + "'"d want to think more carefully about that." Follow up afterward if interested.

Networking Beyond the Talk

Maximising Your Conference Experience: Attend other presentations in your area. Ask thoughtful questions. Engage in hallway conversations. Exchange contact information. Attend social events. Conferences are about building intellectual community, not just presenting.
