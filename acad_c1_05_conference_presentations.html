What Makes a Strong Opening

Start with the problem, not the method. Bad openings: "Today I'll present a mixed-methods study..." Good openings: "One in three people in this room will experience chronic pain that medication can't fix. I've found a way that works."

Then signal: the gap, the solution, the roadmap. So the room knows: (1) why this matters (2) what you'll say (3) what to expect.

  • Hook: Start with a problem, stat, or question that makes the audience care
  • Gap: What we don't yet know or can't yet do
  • Your contribution: What you've discovered or developed
  • Roadmap: "I'll structure this in three parts..."

Presentation Moves: Six Core Techniques

Click each to see how to use it in your conference talk.

1. The Opening Hook▼

What it is: Start with the problem, not the solution. Make the audience care before you tell them your findings.

Weak opening

"This study uses machine learning to predict patient outcomes."

Strong opening

"Every year, 100,000 patients arrive at hospitals with symptoms we can't yet interpret. Doctors have to guess — and 30% of the time, they guess wrong. The cost of these mistakes isn't just financial; it's human. I'm here to show you how we can reduce that error rate from 30% to 8%."

Why it works

You've stated the problem (doctors can't predict), the human cost (people suffer), and the promise (here's the solution). The audience now wants to know how.

2. The Research Gap▼

What it is: Explicitly state what we don't yet know. This justifies your research in the eyes of the audience.

Real example

"We know that exercise improves depression outcomes. What we don't know is whether the type of exercise matters. Do people benefit equally from running, swimming, and yoga? Or are there specific mechanisms where one modality is superior? That's the question I set out to answer."

Why it works

You've positioned your research as answering a genuine gap. The audience thinks "oh, that's actually a good question" rather than "so what?"

3. Signposting▼

What it is: Tell the audience where you're going. Explicit transitions keep them with you.

Real example

"I'll structure this talk in three parts. First, the problem — why current methods fail. Second, my approach and findings. Third, practical implications for your clinics. Let's start with the problem."

Transitions between ideas

"Now that we've established the problem, let me shift to the methodology."
"What I've just shown is that X. Turning now to Y..."
"These findings have three implications. The first is..."

Why it works

The audience isn't reading your slides — they're listening. Explicit signposting keeps them oriented. They know where they are and where you're taking them.

4. Presenting Findings with Confidence▼

What it is: Use language that matches your evidence strength. Don't overstate or understate.

Real examples

"The data shows..." (when evidence is clear)
"Our findings indicate..." (when pattern is clear but not definitive)
"This suggests..." (when the link is plausible but not proven)
"What's striking here is..." (when something unexpected emerged)
"Contrary to our expectations..." (when findings contradicted predictions)

Why it matters

Using appropriate hedging makes you credible. If you overstate ("this proves...") when evidence only suggests, you lose expert credibility. If you understate ("might perhaps possibly suggest..."), you sound uncertain.

5. Handling Q&A Gracefully▼

What it is: You don't know every answer. How do you respond authentically without losing credibility?

Buying time

Question: "What about X?" You: "That's a great question. Let me think through that..."
This pause lets you collect your thoughts without filling silence with um/uh.

Partial answers

"What I can say is [related point you have thought about]. What I can't answer is [the specific point they asked] — but that's actually a great direction for future research."

Clarifying

"Let me make sure I understood your question correctly... Are you asking about [interpretation]?"
This buys time, shows respect, and often clarifies what they actually want to know.

Redirecting

"That's beyond the scope of this study, but it speaks to a broader question about [related topic]. In my research, what I focused on was [your topic]."

6. The Bridge▼

What it is: Connect your findings to the bigger picture. So what? Who cares? Why does this matter beyond your specific study?

Real example

"I've shown that peer feedback is more effective than instructor feedback for writing confidence. But why does that matter? Because universities spend millions on writing centres staffed by experts. My findings suggest those resources could be reallocated toward building peer feedback communities — which would reach more students at lower cost. This research doesn't just answer an academic question; it transforms how we invest in student success."

Why it works

You've taken your specific finding (peer feedback > instructor feedback) and connected it to real-world implications (resource allocation, student reach, cost). The audience thinks "this actually matters" rather than "interesting, but so what?"

The Audience

Six questions from different audience types. Each reveals as you progress. 45 seconds to respond to each.

1
(The Expert) Your methodology assumes X. Isn't that assumption questionable given Y?

Someone who knows this field well has spotted a potential flaw in your logic. They're not hostile, but they're testing whether you've thought this through.

0:45
2
(The Naive) Could you explain what you mean by that technical term?

You've used jargon that's standard in your field but not for everyone. They're not stupid — they're legitimate. Can you translate without being condescending?

0:45
3
(The Hostile) This contradicts [major paper/finding]. How do you account for that?

Someone is challenging your findings head-on. They know a paper that seems to contradict you. How do you respond without getting defensive?

0:45
4
(The Scoper) Isn't this just a specific case of the broader problem of Z?

Someone is questioning the scope of your work. They think you're addressing a symptom, not the disease. How do you argue that your specific focus matters?

0:45
5
(The Practical) This is interesting theoretically, but what are the practical implications?

They want to know: so what? How does the world change if you're right? Who implements this? What action follows?

0:45
6
(The Curator) What do you see as the next step for this research?

The most generous question. They're asking: what does your finding open up? What question comes next? This is where you show vision.

After the Applause: Reflection & Language Reference

Reflection Questions

Which presentation move felt most natural to you?▼

Was it the hook? Signposting? The bridge to implications? The move that felt natural is probably one you're already doing intuitively — build on that strength.

Which question in the audience gauntlet was hardest to answer?▼

Was it the expert pushing on methodology? The naïve question requiring translation? The hostile challenge to your findings? Or the practical "so what?" Knowing which is hard tells you where you need to think more deeply.

What would you do differently in your next conference presentation?▼

Would you spend more time on the hook? Practice signposting more? Prepare responses to predictable hostile questions? Would you strengthen your bridge to real-world implications? Use these reflections to improve your next talk.

Conference Presentation Language Reference

Opening Hook

"Every year, [problem]. This costs [implication]. Today I'll show you [solution or approach]."

"We thought [assumption], but our findings suggest [reality]."

Signposting

"I'll structure this talk in three parts: first, [part 1]; second, [part 2]; third, [part 3]."
"Turning now to the methodology..."
"What this tells us is [interpretation]. Let me shift to the implications..."

Presenting Findings

"The data shows..." (strong evidence)

"Our findings indicate..." (clear pattern)

"This suggests..." (plausible but not proven)

"What's striking is..." (unexpected emergence)

"Contrary to expectations..." (finding contradicted predictions)

Handling Questions

"That's a great question. Let me think through that..."
"What I can say is [related point]. What I can't answer is [specific point] — which is actually a great direction for future research."
"Can I clarify what you're asking...?"
"That's beyond the scope of this study, but it speaks to a broader question about [related area]. What I focused on was [your topic]."

Defending Your Findings Against Challenge

"That's a fair point. The study you're referencing used [different method/context], which explains why [interpretation]."
"I'm aware of that finding. My research suggests that [your nuance], which complements rather than contradicts [their research]."

The Bridge to Implications

"I've shown that [finding]. But why does this matter? Because [practical implication]. This means [who acts on it and how]."
"The significance of this is that it challenges current practice, which assumes [current assumption]. We should instead [recommendation]."

Acknowledging Limitations

"This study is limited by [limitation], though this doesn't affect [central finding] because [reasoning]."
"I'm aware that [challenge], and that's something future research should address."

Closing Strong

"So: [restate key finding]. The implication is [so what]. The next question is [opens up future work]."
