Reading the Room

Body language tells you what your audience is really thinking. Match these physical cues to audience states.

Body Language Cues

Crossed arms, minimal eye contact
State: Bored or defensive. The audience may feel disconnected or skeptical. Consider changing pace, adding interaction, or inviting questions.
Leaning forward, nodding, making notes
State: Engaged and interested. Continue your current approach. These audience members are absorbing information and want to retain it.
Furrowed brows, confused looks, fidgeting
State: Confused. Clarify your point with examples or simpler language. Invite questions to surface misunderstandings.
Checking phones, looking at watches, glazed eyes
State: Disengaged or restless. The audience may be tired, hungry, or losing interest. Try a brief break, energizer, or shift to interactive content.

Last-Minute Changes

Unexpected situations happen. Here's how adaptable presenters handle them using hedging and conditional language.

Scenario 1: New Data Arrives

The Situation
Five minutes before you present, someone sends you important new market data that contradicts your prepared slides.
How would you adapt?

We might want to consider opening with a caveat: "I've just received data that may potentially shift our earlier conclusions." This would probably be wise to do before presenting the original data, so the audience isn't misled.

Scenario 2: Key Slide Fails

The Situation
Your critical visual—a graph, animation, or video—won't load. You have 30 seconds to decide.
How would you adapt?

You could try skipping the slide and describing the data verbally: "The graph would show a 25% increase over Q2, which suggests that our approach is working." Move on confidently rather than dwelling on the technical issue.

Scenario 3: VIP Joins Unexpectedly

The Situation
Mid-presentation, a senior stakeholder or decision-maker arrives. The tone and expectations shift instantly.
How would you adapt?

Acknowledge them naturally: "I should probably give a quick recap for context." You might want to pivot toward strategic points rather than operational details. It would be smart to invite their questions to ensure relevance.

Scenario 4: Time Gets Cut in Half

The Situation
Just before you start, you're told you have 15 minutes instead of 30. Your 20-slide deck is now impossible.
How would you adapt?

You could prioritize your top 3 points and might opt to skip the deep dives. It may be best to offer follow-up conversations: "We could potentially dive deeper in a follow-up session." This keeps momentum while acknowledging the constraint.
