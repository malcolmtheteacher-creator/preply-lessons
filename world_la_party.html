Always Dancing? Carnival, Samba and Tango

Latin Americans are imagined dancing all night — samba at carnival, tango in Buenos Aires. Music really is a powerful part of the culture. But not everyone dances, and the stories behind the dances are deeper than the stereotype.

The stereotype: "All Latin Americans love to party and are born dancers." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The roots of samba

Samba developed in the late 19th and early 20th centuries from Afro-Brazilian music and dance, especially among communities from Bahia who had moved to Rio de Janeiro. "Pelo Telefone", recorded in 1917, is often called the first recorded samba. Samba was at first looked down on by many in the elite, but it was later adopted as a national symbol of Brazil.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did samba develop?

2. What is "Pelo Telefone"?

1. From Afro-Brazilian music, especially in Rio among communities from Bahia.

2. Often called the first recorded samba (1917).

Discuss:
  • Why might a music be looked down on before becoming a national symbol?

Part 2 — Carnival and tango

In Rio's carnival, samba schools — community organisations from neighbourhoods — prepare all year for parades judged in the Sambadrome. In Buenos Aires and Montevideo, tango was born in the late 19th century in working-class and immigrant neighbourhoods; in 2009 it was recognised by UNESCO as intangible heritage of Argentina and Uruguay. Colombia's Barranquilla carnival is also recognised by UNESCO.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What are samba schools?

2. When was tango recognised by UNESCO?

1. Community organisations that prepare carnival parades.

2. In 2009.

Discuss:
  • Can a festival be both tourism and tradition?

Part 3 — Not everyone dances

For many people, carnival is a holiday to escape the noise, and plenty of Latin Americans say they can't dance at all. The region's music is also far wider than samba and tango: Andean folk music, Mexican mariachi, Cuban son, Colombian cumbia and vallenato, Brazilian bossa nova and modern reggaeton, which has been made global by artists from Puerto Rico and Colombia.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name three musical styles beyond samba and tango.

2. Does everyone enjoy carnival?

1. Mariachi, son, cumbia, bossa nova, reggaeton (any).

2. No — many people escape it.

Discuss:
  • Is "loving to party" a compliment or a stereotype?

Grammar — The Passive & Reporting Passives

When we talk about beliefs and reputations, we often don't know or care who says them. That is the job of the passive.

How it works:
• be + past participle: "The survey is carried out every year."
• Reporting passives: "Finns are said to be shy." / "It is often claimed that…"
• Add by only when the doer matters.

Going deeper

The party stereotype grows from the global fame of carnival, tango and Latin pop, and from tourism images. It ignores the history of these traditions — often created by poor, Black or immigrant communities — and the everyday reality of work, study and family. A "fun-loving" image can also be used to suggest people are not serious, which is unfair.

Worth noticing: The passive is formed with be + past participle; use it when the action matters more than who did it.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Join in, but don't assume. If you're invited to dance, try — enthusiasm matters more than skill. Ask about a country's music and its history, and don't expect every Latin American to dance or to love carnival.
