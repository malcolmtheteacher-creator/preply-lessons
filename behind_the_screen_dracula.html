Bram Stoker's Dracula

A real Romanian prince, a novelist who never went to Romania, and a love story invented a century after the book. This is how a legend gets built.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Bram Stoker's Dracula — a creature who cannot enter a house unless he is invited, and who believes he has found a woman he lost four hundred years ago — discover how much of it comes from real history and how much was manufactured, and master the English of habits and characteristic behaviour: will for what someone typically does, tend to, be inclined to, keep + -ing and be forever + -ing.
Before we begin — talk it through: Is there a story everybody in your country "knows" that probably isn't true? Where do you think such stories come from — and who gains from them? And what makes a monster frightening: what it does, or what it wants?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word sits in a situation that gives its meaning away — you don't need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. He stopped on the threshold with one foot still on the step outside, waiting for someone in the hallway to wave him in.
a threshold (n) — the strip of floor in a doorway; the line you cross to go from outside to inside. Figuratively, the point of entry into something new.
2. The cliff loomed over the little harbour, a huge dark mass that blocked out half the evening sky and made the boats look like toys.
to loom (v) — to appear as a large, dark, threatening shape; also used of an event that is approaching and worrying people.
3. Each time he retold the night at sea, he embellished it a little further: the wave grew higher, the crew grew braver, and by the tenth telling there was a shark in it.
to embellish (v) — to add invented or exaggerated details to a story to make it more impressive. The noun is embellishment.
4. His notoriety travelled faster than he did: in villages two hundred miles away, people who had never met him could tell you exactly what he was supposed to have done.
notoriety (n) — fame of a bad kind; being widely known for something shocking or wicked. The adjective is notorious.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. lore is closest in meaning to: (a) a written law   (b) traditional stories and beliefs passed down   (c) a heavy load
(b) — lore (n) is the body of knowledge, stories and customs a community hands down, usually by word of mouth rather than in books.
6. spurious is closest in meaning to: (a) false   (b) sacred   (c) generous
(a) false — spurious (adj) means not genuine: a spurious document, a spurious claim, a spurious connection that doesn't really hold.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. The newspapers conflated the two brothers so thoroughly that within a month nobody could say which of them had actually done what.
To conflate two things is: (a) to keep them carefully separate; (b) to merge them and treat them as one; (c) to compare their prices.
(b) — to conflate (v) — to combine two distinct things into one, usually by mistake, so that the difference between them disappears.
8. She held out for three nights without sleep, and on the fourth she finally succumbed, sat down on the stairs and closed her eyes.
To succumb is: (a) to resist successfully; (b) to complain loudly; (c) to stop resisting and give way.
(c) — to succumb (v) — to give in to a force, a temptation or an illness that you have been fighting. Often succumb to something.

D. Collocation choice — which word completes the phrase?

9. He knocked at midnight on the ______ of returning a borrowed umbrella, but what he really wanted was a look inside the house.
Choose: (a) prospect   (b) pretext   (c) precedent
(b) — a pretext (n) — a false reason you give in order to hide your real one. We say on the pretext of doing something, or under the pretext of.
10. For four centuries the family ______ sway over the whole valley, and nothing was decided there without their word.
Choose: (a) took   (b) made   (c) held
(c) held — to hold sway (over something) — to have controlling power or dominant influence. Also used of ideas: a theory that still holds sway today.

The film, in three parts

Francis Ford Coppola's Bram Stoker's Dracula (1992), written for the screen by James V. Hart. Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — The soldier who renounces God

The film opens in 1462, on the eastern edge of Christian Europe. A Romanian prince, Vlad Dracula, rides away to fight the armies of the Ottoman Empire and wins. But his enemies send false word back to his castle that he has been killed, and his wife Elisabeta, believing it, throws herself into the river below. When he returns in triumph, a priest tells him that because she took her own life her soul is damned. Something in him breaks. He renounces the God he has been defending, drives his sword into the stone cross of his own chapel, and drinks the blood that runs from it. He becomes what he will be for the next four hundred years.

Jump to 1897. A young London solicitor, Jonathan Harker, is sent to Transylvania to complete a property purchase for a client named Count Dracula — the job having passed to him after his colleague Renfield returned from the castle out of his mind and was shut in an asylum. Harker crosses the threshold of the castle and finds an ancient, courteous, unsettling host who keeps him talking late and is never seen in daylight.

Then the Count catches sight of a photograph of Harker's fiancée, Mina Murray — and stops. The face is Elisabeta's. Harker's usefulness ends there. He is left imprisoned in the castle, and the Count takes ship for England, carrying boxes of his own Transylvanian earth.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happens in 1462 that turns Vlad Dracula into a vampire?

2. Why does the Count lose interest in Jonathan Harker?

1. He wins a battle against the Ottomans, but a false report of his death drives his wife Elisabeta to suicide; told her soul is damned, he renounces God and drinks blood from the chapel cross.

2. He sees a photograph of Harker's fiancée Mina and believes she is Elisabeta returned. Harker is imprisoned in the castle while the Count sails for England.

Discuss:
  • The film gives its monster a reason: grief, and a Church that condemned the woman he loved. Does explaining a monster make it more frightening or less?

Part 2 — London, and the rules of the thing

In London the Count takes possession of Carfax Abbey and, by night, hunts. Mina's wealthy, high-spirited friend Lucy Westenra — courted by three men at once — begins to sicken. She sleepwalks; she loses blood; no doctor can explain it. Dr Seward sends for his old teacher, Professor Abraham Van Helsing, who examines her and says the unsayable word. There is a creature at work, and it obeys rules: it casts no reflection, it recoils from the sacred, it will not enter a house unless someone inside invites it. Lucy dies, rises as a vampire, and the men who loved her have to destroy her themselves.

By day, meanwhile, the Count has made himself young. He appears in London as an elegant foreign gentleman and meets Mina by apparent chance. He is charming, sad, oddly familiar. She is engaged to a man who has vanished somewhere in Eastern Europe, and she finds she cannot stay away. It is a courtship, filmed as a courtship — and this is the film's boldest decision: the audience is asked to want it, at least a little.

Then news arrives that Harker has escaped the castle and is being cared for in a convent in Romania. Mina goes to him and marries him there. The Count, left behind, does the cruellest thing available to him, and Lucy's death is the result.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What rules does Van Helsing say the creature obeys?

2. How does Mina come to know the Count, and what interrupts it?

1. It shows no reflection, it recoils from sacred objects, and it cannot enter a house unless it is invited in.

2. He appears in London by day as a young, elegant stranger and courts her; the news that Jonathan has escaped and is in a convent in Romania takes her away to marry him.

Discuss:
  • Nearly every vampire rule in the film is about consent and boundaries — he cannot come in unless he is asked. Why do you think that rule has survived for over a century?

Part 3 — The pursuit, and the woman who ends it

Mina returns to London married. Van Helsing's group hunts down the Count's boxes of earth and destroys them, cutting off his refuges one by one. But the Count comes to Mina, confesses everything he has done, and she — furious, grieving for Lucy — tells him she loves him anyway, and remembers, or believes she remembers, a life as Elisabeta. At her own insistence he begins to turn her. He stops before it is finished; he will not damn her as she was damned before. When the hunters break in, he escapes.

The chase runs east: by sea to Varna, then overland to the Borgo Pass as winter closes in. Van Helsing takes Mina — now half-changed, and dangerous — to the castle itself and destroys the Count's three brides. The others intercept the cart carrying his coffin at sunset, in the snow below the castle walls. In the fight Quincey Morris, one of Lucy's three suitors, is stabbed and dies. The Count is mortally wounded.

Mina carries him into the chapel where, four centuries earlier, he cursed God. There, dying, he becomes a young man again and asks her for peace. She gives it to him: she drives the blade through his heart, and then, so that it is finished properly, takes his head. The mark on her forehead fades. Above them the chapel's painted ceiling shows the two of them at rest. Whether that is redemption or merely a beautiful ending is left for the audience to decide.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does the Count stop transforming Mina?

2. Who kills him, and where?

1. He refuses to condemn her to the state he chose for himself — he stops before the change is complete, even though she asked for it.

2. Mina does, in the chapel where he renounced God in 1462, after he is mortally wounded in the fight below the castle.

Discuss:
  • The film ends with the monster forgiven and released by the woman he pursued. Is that a moving resolution — or does it let him off too easily?

Grammar — habits & characteristic behaviour

This is a story built entirely out of what things typically do. Vampires have rules. Villagers have customs. Characters have compulsions they cannot stop. English has a whole set of structures for exactly this — for saying what is in someone's nature, not just what happened once.

1. will for typical, predictable behaviour
This is not the future. It is a statement about character: this is what he does, reliably, every time.
• "He will only enter a house if he is invited."
• "He will not cross running water, and a mirror will show nothing where he stands."
• "Show a villager the castle on a map and they will change the subject."
Stress it (he WILL do that) and it becomes a complaint. Unstressed, it is simply a rule of nature.
2. tend to and be inclined to — softer, more cautious
• "The villagers tend to lock their doors before sunset." (a general pattern, most of the time)
• "Harker is inclined to trust his host at first." (a leaning, a mild disposition — often about attitudes and opinions)
Both are useful when you don't want to sound absolute: tend to = usually; be inclined to = has a slight pull towards.
3. keep + -ing and be forever + -ing — repetition, often exasperating
• "Renfield keeps catching flies." (again and again, he won't stop)
• "Lucy is forever sleepwalking towards the churchyard." (so often that it has become a complaint — also always, constantly: "he's always arriving after dark")
Note the form: keep + -ing, and be + forever/always + -ing.
4. One backward glance: would for past habit
Move the whole idea into the past and will becomes would:
• Present: "As the Count, he will sleep through the daylight hours."
• Past: "As a young prince, he would ride out at dawn against the Ottoman armies."
Same meaning — characteristic behaviour — just no longer true now. Everything else in this lesson stays in the present.

The Real Story — how a prince became a vampire

Everyone "knows" that Dracula was based on Vlad the Impaler. It is one of the most confidently repeated facts in popular culture. It is also, on the evidence, largely wrong — and the story of how it became a fact is more interesting than the fact itself.

🦇 The prince was entirely real

Vlad III of Wallachia — Vlad Țepeș, "the Impaler" — lived from around 1428–31 until the winter of 1476–77. He ruled the principality of Wallachia, in what is now southern Romania, three separate times: briefly in 1448, then from 1456 to 1462, and finally for a few weeks in November 1476 before he was killed in battle. His name came from his father, Vlad II, who joined the Order of the Dragon, a knightly society founded to resist Ottoman expansion: Dracul, the dragon, and Dracula, "son of Dracul". In modern Romanian, awkwardly, dracul also means "the devil".

In 1462 he crossed the Danube and attacked Ottoman territory, and Sultan Mehmed II invaded Wallachia in reply. On the night of 16–17 June 1462 Vlad rode into the Ottoman camp near Târgoviște and tried to kill the Sultan himself. He failed, lost the war, and spent years imprisoned in Hungary before his last brief return to power. His punishments were savage even by the standards of his century, and German-language pamphlets printed after his death — with woodcuts of him dining among the stakes — turned that savagery into one of the earliest bestsellers of the printing press. In Romania the memory ran the other way: a harsh ruler, yes, but one who defended his country and imposed order. Both versions have held sway ever since.

What Bram Stoker actually took

Here is where the legend was manufactured. Stoker's working notes for the novel survive — they are held at the Rosenbach Museum & Library in Philadelphia — and they show what he read. On 8 August 1890, on holiday in Whitby, he borrowed from the public library a book published in 1820 by a British consul, William Wilkinson: An Account of the Principalities of Wallachia and Moldavia. From it he copied a few lines about a "Voivode Dracula" who crossed the Danube and attacked Turkish troops — and one footnote, which he wrote out in capitals: DRACULA in the Wallachian language means DEVIL.

That footnote is the whole of it. Wilkinson never gives the man a first name. There is no Vlad in the book, no Țepeș, no Impaler, no atrocity, no castle and certainly no vampire. Up to that moment Stoker had been calling his creature Count Wampyr; in the notes he crosses that out and writes Dracula instead. He took a name because it sounded like the devil. Vlad III is not mentioned anywhere in the notes.

Be fair to the other side, though: the novel does hand its Count a warlike ancestry. Dracula boasts of a man of his own race who as Voivode crossed the Danube and beat the Turk on his own ground — Wilkinson's sentence, almost word for word — and Van Helsing later says the Count must have been that same Voivode Dracula. So Stoker's vampire is loosely attached to a real warlord. But it is a thread, not a portrait, and no reader in 1897 could have identified the man.

Where the rest of it came from — and who assembled the myth

Stoker never set foot in Romania. His Transylvania was built in libraries: the atmosphere and the word nosferatu came largely from Emily Gerard's essay "Transylvanian Superstitions" (1885) and her book on the region. Whitby gave him the rest of the machinery — the abbey on the cliff, and a Russian ship called the Dmitry that had been driven aground there in 1885, which he rewrote as the Demeter. Elizabeth Báthory, the Hungarian countess usually named as another source, appears to be a later addition too: Stoker took notes from Sabine Baring-Gould's The Book of Were-Wolves, but not one of them concerns her.

The Vlad identification arrived much later. In 1972 two Boston College historians, Raymond McNally and Radu Florescu, published In Search of Dracula, which fused the novel with the prince and sold enormously — written, crucially, before Stoker's notes had been properly studied. Both authors later softened the claim. But it was out. A 1974 television version already gave its Count a lost wife whose double he pursues, and closed by pointing at Vlad the Impaler. And then Coppola's 1992 film made the fusion definitive and beautiful: a prologue in 1462, a wife named Elisabeta, a reincarnated love. None of that is in Stoker's novel. Not the wife, not the reincarnation, not Vlad, not the sword in the cross. It was invented for the screen — and it is now what most of the world means by "Dracula". Even the castle is a stand-in: Bran Castle in Romania draws the tourists, though its connection to both Vlad and the novel is essentially an invention of the tourist trade.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Which part of tonight's "true story" most surprised you — and where had you first heard the version you believed?
  • A tourist industry, a 1972 bestseller and a 1992 film built a connection the author never made. Who, if anyone, is responsible for correcting it? Does it matter?
  • Romania inherited a national ruler who is world-famous for something he never did. Is that a burden or a windfall — and would you feel differently if it were your country's history?
  • Stoker took a name from a footnote. Coppola took a love story from nowhere at all. Is a work of fiction ever obliged to be fair to the real people it borrows from?

Task B · The film's central argument (4 minutes)

Coppola's film asks the audience to sympathise with its monster — to see a grieving husband rather than a predator, and to accept a heroine who says she loves him. Build an argument. Does giving a monster a tragic backstory deepen the story, or does it quietly excuse what he does to Lucy, to Harker and to Mina? Take a side and defend it, then argue the other side as well as you can.

Task C · How legends get made (4 minutes)

A footnote in 1820, a bestseller in 1972, a film in 1992 — and a "fact" that hundreds of millions of people now believe. How does something like that happen, and can it be undone? Make your case.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • A famous historical "fact" from your own country that turns out to be shakier than people think.
  • Whether films and television should carry any duty of accuracy when they use real people.
  • How a place profits from a story about it — tourism built on legend, and what that does to the place.
  • Why a good story tends to travel further and faster than a correct one.
  • Whether it is even possible to correct something most people already believe.
  • A monster or villain from your own culture's folklore, and what it says about the fears of the people who invented it.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The film's Dracula is a man who could not accept a death and spent four hundred years refusing it. Is that a monster, or simply grief with no limit on it?
  2. Stoker took a name because of a footnote about the devil. If he had opened a different book, we might have no Dracula at all. How much of what we think of as culture is that accidental?
  3. Romania is famous worldwide for a vampire it did not invent, attached to a ruler who was not one. If a false story brings a country attention and money, is there any reason to fight it?
