The Art of the Impression

What the science really says about being unforgettable

The Uncomfortable Truth About First Impressions

How long do you think you have to make a first impression? 30 seconds? 7 seconds? Think again.

0.1 sec Time to form an impression of someone's face Princeton University - Willis and Todorov, 2006
82% Of judgments based on just two traits: warmth and competence Princeton University Research

That's one-tenth of a second. Before you've even opened your mouth, people have already decided whether you seem trustworthy, competent, and likeable. Longer exposure doesn't significantly change these snap judgments - it just makes people more confident in them.

But Here's What's Really Fascinating...

The Liking Gap We systematically underestimate how much people like us after conversations Yale University - Boothby et al., 2018

Psychologists at Yale discovered something remarkable: after meeting someone new, we almost always think they liked us less than they actually did. This "liking gap" persists for months - even college roommates living together underestimated how much their roommates liked them.

Why does this happen? We're so busy analysing our own performance - "Did I talk too much? Was that joke stupid? Why did I say that?" - that we miss the signals showing the other person is genuinely enjoying the conversation.

Dr. Erica Boothby

Lead researcher, Yale University

"We suspected there might be small differences between how we see ourselves and how others see us, but we were struck by the size and consistency of the liking gap. This pattern held true across various contexts, cultures and age groups."

The Two Questions Everyone Is Silently Asking

When you walk into a room full of important people, every single person is unconsciously evaluating you on exactly two dimensions:

Can I trust you? WARMTH: Are you friendly, likeable, genuine?
Can I rely on you? COMPETENCE: Are you capable, credible, knowledgeable?

Research from Princeton University found that highly charismatic people aren't necessarily more attractive or extroverted - they've simply mastered signalling both warmth AND competence simultaneously. Too much warmth without competence? You seem nice but not serious. Too much competence without warmth? You seem cold and arrogant.

James, 45

Partner, International Law Firm in Singapore

"For years I focused only on appearing knowledgeable at client events. I prepared facts, case studies, talking points. But I noticed colleagues who seemed far less prepared were building stronger relationships. When I learned about the warmth-competence balance, everything changed. I started asking more questions, showing genuine curiosity. My network transformed."

The Harvard Discovery That Changes Everything

A 2017 study from Harvard Business School analysed thousands of conversations and found something counterintuitive:

People who ask more questions - particularly follow-up questions - are significantly more likeable than people who talk about themselves.

The researchers found that asking just one additional follow-up question during a speed date increased the likelihood of getting a second date. The top third of question-askers received the most "yes, I want to see you again" responses.

Mei Lin, 38

Senior Director, International Finance Corporation

"At a World Bank reception, I met someone who asked me, 'What brought you to development finance?' When I answered, instead of jumping in with their own story, they asked, 'What keeps you passionate about it after all these years?' I ended up talking for fifteen minutes. Afterwards, I realised I knew almost nothing about them - but I thought they were the most interesting person I'd met all evening."

Language Focus A

Expressions for creating warmth and connection

1. "I'd love to hear more about..." Click to reveal
Meaning

A warm, inviting way to show genuine interest and encourage someone to elaborate on a topic they've mentioned. Much stronger than "Tell me more" because it signals personal investment.

Form

I'd love to hear more about + noun/gerund

"I'd love to hear more about your work in sustainable development."
"I'd love to hear more about how you approached that challenge."
Register Note

Appropriate in both formal and semi-formal contexts. The use of "love" adds warmth without being unprofessional. Can soften to "I'd be very interested to hear..." in extremely formal settings.

NOW YOU TRY

Someone at a conference mentions they recently relocated to open their company's new Asia office. Use this expression to invite them to share more.

2. "What struck me most was..." Click to reveal
Meaning

Used to share your genuine reaction to something - an event, a speech, a situation - while signalling that you've been paying attention and thinking deeply. Creates immediate common ground.

Form

What struck me (most) was + noun clause / noun

"What struck me most was how passionate the keynote speaker was about inclusion."
"What struck me was the incredible diversity of perspectives in that panel."
Register Note

Sophisticated and slightly intellectual. Works beautifully in professional and formal contexts. Shows you're not just making small talk - you're engaging thoughtfully with content. Alternative: "What really stood out to me..."

NOW YOU TRY

You've just listened to a keynote speech about leadership in times of uncertainty. Use this expression to start a conversation with someone nearby.

3. "That sounds like a really demanding role." Click to reveal
Meaning

A validation statement that acknowledges the difficulty or complexity of someone's work. Creates instant rapport because everyone feels their work is challenging, and having that recognised feels good.

Form

That sounds like a (really) + adjective + noun

"That sounds like a really complex project to manage."
"That sounds like an incredibly rewarding position."
"That sounds like quite a responsibility."
Register Note

This is technically not a question, but research shows it works better than questions at getting people to open up. It validates before it probes. Choose adjectives carefully: "demanding", "complex", "challenging", "rewarding", "fascinating" all work well.

NOW YOU TRY

Someone tells you they manage a portfolio of projects across multiple time zones with stakeholders in five different countries. Respond using this pattern.

Language Focus B

Expressions for demonstrating competence while staying warm

4. "From my experience in [X], I've found that..." Click to reveal
Meaning

A sophisticated way to share your expertise without sounding arrogant. Frames your knowledge as personal experience rather than absolute fact, which comes across as both competent AND humble.

Form

From my experience in + field/role, I've found that + observation

"From my experience in change management, I've found that the initial resistance often becomes the catalyst for the most meaningful transformation."
"From my time working with cross-functional teams, I've found that patience is really the key variable."
Register Note

Perfect balance of confidence and humility. The "I've found that" softens what could sound like lecturing. Much better than "In my expert opinion..." or "As someone who knows about this..." Alternatives: "What I've learned from..." / "In my work with X, I've noticed..."

NOW YOU TRY

Someone asks what you think is the biggest challenge in your field. Use this pattern to share an insight without sounding like you're giving a lecture.

5. "I'd be curious to know your perspective on..." Click to reveal
Meaning

A deeply respectful way to invite someone's opinion, especially someone senior or more experienced. Signals intellectual humility while positioning yourself as someone who values nuanced thinking.

Form

I'd be curious to know/hear your perspective/take on + topic

"I'd be curious to know your perspective on how the industry has evolved since you started in this role."
"I'd be curious to hear your take on the recent shifts in the market."
Register Note

Excellent for speaking with VIPs, senior officials, or experts. "Curious" is warmer than "interested" and more intellectual than "want to know". "Perspective" elevates the question beyond simple opinion-asking. This signals: "I respect your expertise and I think for myself."

NOW YOU TRY

You're speaking with a senior executive who has decades of experience in your industry. You want to ask about changes they've observed over their career. Use this expression.

Key Insights to Remember

The Liking Gap: People almost always like you more than you think they do. Your self-critical inner voice is not an accurate measure of how others perceive you.

Warmth + Competence: The most charismatic people signal both. Too much of one without the other creates imbalance.

The Power of Questions: Asking follow-up questions makes you more likeable than talking about yourself. Listen, then ask more.
