Listen in on this conversation

A: How did the call with the landlord go?

B: Interesting. He claimed he'd already sent someone to look at the leak. Then, when I pushed, he admitted no one had actually been. I pointed out that we'd reported it three times. He just insisted it wasn't urgent.

Notice four different reporting verbs in there: claimed, admitted, pointed out, insisted. They all mean roughly "said" — but read the conversation again. Do they feel identical, or does each one hint at something different about the landlord, and about how B feels about him?

Check your idea CLICK
They're not interchangeable. Claimed signals B doesn't quite believe him; admitted shows the landlord reluctantly confirmed something unflattering; pointed out presents B's own words as a plain fact; insisted shows the landlord refusing to budge. Choosing the right reporting verb lets you show your attitude without ever saying "I don't believe him" out loud.

Claiming, Admitting, Denying

Verbs that do more than "say" — they reveal whether you believe the speaker.

Spot the pattern

Three ways of reporting what someone said about themselves:

  • "My sister admitted forgetting my birthday." (a reluctant confession)
  • "He claimed to have sent the invoice twice." (you're not fully convinced)
  • "The company denied that it had leaked the data." (rejecting an accusation)

Before you check: what comes straight after each of these three verbs — and is it always the same kind of word?

Check the pattern CLICK
Admit and deny take a "that"-clause or the -ing form directly ("admitted forgetting", "denied leaking"). Claim is different — it takes a "that"-clause or to have + past participle ("claimed to have sent"), never a plain -ing form.
admit / deny + (that) ...  or  + -ing
claim + (that) ...  or  + to have + past participle
"I forgot to invite them," he said.
→
He admitted forgetting to invite them.
A few more reporting verbs work the same way as "admit" — they take + that and reveal your attitude to what was said: concede (agree, reluctantly, after resisting), argue (push a position, expecting disagreement), point out (draw attention to something as plain fact). For example: "In the end, my dad conceded that the new road probably was safer." "The neighbours argued that the fence was on their land." "I pointed out that the shop had already closed early twice that month."

Telling People What To Do

Advice, warnings and insistence — reported so the structure matches the force behind them.

Spot the pattern

  • "The doctor advised him to rest for a week."
  • "She warned us not to be late."
  • "My friend urged me to apply for the job."

What always sits between the reporting verb and "to" here?

Check the pattern CLICK
The person. Advise, warn and urge all need an object (the person being told) directly before "to + infinitive". For a negative, "not" goes right before "to": "warned us not to be late."
warn / advise / urge + someone + (not) to + infinitive

A different pattern: insisting and suggesting

  • "He insisted that she take the money."
  • "The doctor suggested that he cut down on salt."
  • "The doctor suggested cutting down on salt."

Look at "take" and "cut" in the first two. Why isn't it "takes" or "he cuts", given the subjects are "she" and "he"?

Check the pattern CLICK
After insist and suggest + "that", the verb stays in its base form — no -s, no backshift to the past. This holds even for "he" and "she". You can also add "should" before the base form ("insisted that she should take the money") without changing the meaning much. Suggest has a second option too: drop "that" and use -ing instead ("suggested cutting down on salt").
insist / suggest + that + subject + base form  (or  + should + base form)
suggest + -ing

Reporting Questions & Commands

And deciding whether to shift the tense back at all.

Spot the pattern

  • "Where do you live?" → She asked me where I lived.
  • "Are you coming tonight?" → He asked if I was coming that night.
  • "Turn off the lights before you leave." → The manager told us to turn off the lights before we left.

Look at the first two. What happens to the word order, and to "do/are"?

Check the pattern CLICK
Reported questions use statement word order (subject before verb) with no question mark and no "do/does/did". A wh-question keeps its wh-word ("where I lived"); a yes/no question gets if or whether instead. Commands drop the imperative and become told/asked + someone + (not) to + infinitive.
asked (+ wh-word / if / whether) + subject + verb
told / asked + someone + (not) to + infinitive
"Don't forget to call the plumber," she said.
→
She told me not to forget to call the plumber.
One more thing: backshift isn't always compulsory. If what was said is still true right now, you can choose to keep the original tense. Said this morning: "The 9 o'clock train is delayed." Still delayed now? Either "She said the train is delayed" or "She said the train was delayed" works. But said three years ago, "I live in Manchester" — if they've since moved, you must backshift: "She said she lived in Manchester", not "lives", because that's no longer true.
