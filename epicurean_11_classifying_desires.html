Classifying Desires

The practical framework for the Epicurean life

Before We Begin...

🧠 What Do You Already Know?

Think about your own desires for a moment. Can you categorize them? Which are essential to your wellbeing? Which are optional? Which are just wishes that never really satisfy? Write your own categories.

Today: We're learning Epicurus's most practical contribution to ethics — his threefold classification of desires. This isn't abstract philosophy; it's a practical tool for simplifying your life and increasing your pleasure (in the Epicurean sense).

Why Classification Matters

In Lesson 10, we learned that pleasure (ataraxia and aponia) is the highest good. But how do you achieve it? You have limited time, energy, and resources. You can't satisfy every desire.

Epicurus's answer: Be ruthlessly selective about which desires to pursue. But on what basis? He provides a clear framework: classify every desire and decide accordingly.

We must reckon that of desires some are natural and necessary, some are natural but not necessary, and others are neither natural nor necessary but are due to empty opinion.

— Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus

The Threefold Classification

Epicurus divides all desires into three categories. Let's start with an overview, then explore each in depth.

Category 1: Natural and Necessary

Essential for survival and basic wellbeing. These desires should be satisfied.

Examples: Food (basic nutrition), water, shelter, sleep, clothing, health care
Characteristic: You suffer without them. They're universal and stable — everyone needs food.
Epicurean strategy: Satisfy them simply and reliably. Bread and water meet these needs perfectly well.

Category 2: Natural but Not Necessary

These desires arise naturally but aren't essential. They can be satisfied or not without harm. Choose carefully.

Examples: Varied food, pleasant company, sex, beautiful surroundings, entertainment
Characteristic: They feel good when satisfied, but you don't suffer without them. They enhance life but aren't necessary for wellbeing.
Epicurean strategy: Enjoy some of these, but only when they don't create pain or complications. A little variety in food is pleasant and causes no harm.

Category 3: Neither Natural nor Necessary

Desires based on empty opinion and false beliefs. These should be avoided because they create endless, unsatisfiable wanting.

Examples: Wealth beyond what you need, fame, power, luxury status symbols, endless accumulation
Characteristic: These desires have no natural limit. The wealthy want more wealth. The famous want more fame. There's always a higher rung on the ladder. They're driven by opinion (what others think, social comparison) not by nature.
Epicurean strategy: Actively suppress these. They're traps. Once you start pursuing them, you're on a hedonic treadmill with no end.
🎯
The Strategic Insight:
Categories 1 and 2 are saturable — you can satisfy them completely and feel full. Category 3 is insatiable — no amount of wealth, fame, or power ever satisfies. The Epicurean strategy is to focus on saturable desires and avoid insatiable ones.

Natural and Necessary Desires

The Foundation of Pleasure

These are the base of the pyramid. Satisfy them first, satisfactorily, and everything else becomes easier.

Food

The most discussed of Epicurus's examples. Remember: he lived on bread, water, and occasional cheese. This wasn't deprivation — it was the rational satisfaction of a necessary desire.

The pleasure isn't in luxury food; it's in eating when hungry. Once you've eaten enough, additional fancy food doesn't add pleasure — it risks creating pain (bloating, sluggishness) and complications (cost, effort, guilt).

When we are hungry, water and plain bread are the most pleasant food. But to enjoy fine food, one must not have had it before.

— Epicurus (reported by Seneca)

Your turn: Epicurus suggests that enjoying fine food requires not having had it before. What do you think he means? Is this realistic?

The Adaptation Problem:

Epicurus is describing what modern psychology calls adaptation or hedonic adaptation. Once you regularly eat fine food, you adapt to it. It becomes your baseline. Then:

  • You need even finer food to get the same pleasure
  • Regular food now seems unsatisfying
  • You're less happy than you were eating bread contentedly

Epicurus's insight: Better to keep your desires simple so that simple pleasures remain genuinely pleasurable. This is why he thought extreme self-discipline could increase overall pleasure.

Other Natural/Necessary Desires

Shelter: A modest dwelling is all you need. It protects you from the elements. A mansion doesn't protect you better; it just creates more maintenance, expense, and complexity.

Clothing: Adequate clothing keeps you warm and covered. Designer brands serve the same function at much greater cost and complication.

Friendship: The desire for genuine human connection is natural and necessary for wellbeing. But this means quality friendships, not a large social network or social status. Paradoxically, pursuing the natural/necessary desire for friendship may be more satisfying than pursuing status (category 3).

Natural but Not Necessary Desires

The Middle Ground

These desires are legitimate — they're natural, not based on false opinion. But they're not essential for wellbeing. Epicurus doesn't say to suppress them entirely. Instead, he provides a careful framework for engaging with them.

The Principle: Cost vs. Benefit

For natural but unnecessary desires, ask:

  • Will satisfying this desire create complications or pain? If yes, avoid it.
  • Is it worth the effort, time, or expense? If no, avoid it.
  • Can I satisfy it simply without great effort? If yes, it may be worth doing.
Your turn: Consider these natural but unnecessary desires: varied food, wine, sex, entertainment, pleasant conversation, music. For each, work through Epicurus's questions. Which pass his cost-benefit test?

Varied food: A little variety from plain bread is pleasant and causes no harm. Occasional cheese, figs, or vegetables add flavor without great expense. This passes the test — enjoy it.

Wine: Epicurus actually drank wine! But water was usually preferable — cheaper, easier, less likely to create hangovers or loss of reason. Occasional wine at a friend's gathering? Sure. But constant drinking? No — it creates health problems and complications.

Sex: A natural desire. Epicurus was notably permissive about this. But he warned: don't pursue it in ways that create entanglement, jealousy, or reputation damage. Simple, mutual satisfaction is fine. Expensive courtesans or elaborate schemes? No.

Entertainment: Music, games, conversation — these can be genuinely pleasant and cost little. Enjoy them. But if you're constantly seeking new entertainment to avoid boredom, you're treating it as an insatiable desire (category 3), not a natural one.

The Pattern: Epicurus's advice is nuanced, not ascetic. Enjoy natural pleasures when they're available and simple. Avoid them when they create complications. Never become dependent on them.

Sex: A Case Study

Epicurus has a surprisingly progressive view here. He doesn't condemn sexual desire or pleasure — it's natural. But he's practical:

Sexual intercourse has never harmed anyone, but neither has it ever benefited anyone either.

— Epicurus (reported by Seneca)

Your turn: What does Epicurus mean? Is he saying sex is morally neutral? That it's not worth pursuing? That it shouldn't be the focus of life?

He's saying it's neither essentially good nor bad.

Sex doesn't harm you if pursued simply and without complications. But it doesn't contribute to the highest pleasure (ataraxia and aponia) either. You won't achieve tranquility or freedom from pain through sex.

The danger isn't sex itself but dependency on it. If you think you can't be happy without it, you're treating it as a necessity (category 1) when it's only natural but unnecessary (category 2). If you're constantly seeking new sexual partners or elaborate sexual scenarios, you're pursuing it as an insatiable desire (category 3).

The healthy view: Sexual desire is fine. Satisfy it simply when possible. Don't make it central to your pursuit of the good life. Don't become dependent on it. And don't let it create complications (jealousy, disease, social damage).

Three Categories:

  • Natural and Necessary: Food, water, shelter, sleep, health — essential for survival and wellbeing. Satisfy them.
  • Natural but Not Necessary: Varied food, sex, entertainment, pleasant company — natural but not essential. Enjoy selectively; avoid complications.
  • Neither Natural nor Necessary: Wealth, fame, power, luxury, status — based on empty opinion. Avoid these — they're insatiable.

Why Category 3 is Dangerous:

  • They have no natural limit — more wealth, more fame, more power never fully satisfies
  • They're based on social comparison and opinion, not genuine nature
  • Once you start pursuing them, you're on a hedonic treadmill with no end
  • The pursuit itself creates anxiety and pain

Natural but Unnecessary Desires:

  • Ask: Will this create complications? Is it worth the effort? Can I satisfy it simply?
  • If complications arise, avoid it
  • If it can be satisfied simply, it may be worth enjoying
  • The key is moderation and avoiding dependency

Saturable vs. Insatiable:

  • Saturable: Categories 1 and 2 — you can satisfy them completely and feel full
  • Insatiable: Category 3 — no amount ever fully satisfies; always wanting more
  • The Epicurean strategy focuses on saturable desires to achieve stable pleasure (ataraxia)
