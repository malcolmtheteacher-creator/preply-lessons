The Body

Bill Bryson is a travel writer, not a scientist. In 2019 he published a guide to the one place he had lived all his life and never explored — and found that the experts kept giving him the same answer: nobody knows.

Today: you'll take Bill Bryson's tour of the human body — what you are made of, what your brain does in the dark, and how much is still a mystery — and learn the language scientists use for what they have not yet worked out: no one knows why, it remains unclear, is thought to, have yet to explain.

The story, in three parts

A real book in three parts — the shopping list, the dark room, and the things nobody knows. Read each part; after it, try the quick check from memory, then talk about the question.

Part 1 — The shopping list

Bill Bryson was born in Iowa in 1951 and has spent most of his adult life in Britain. He made his name with funny, grumbling travel books, and then, in 2003, surprised everyone with A Short History of Nearly Everything, a history of science written by a layman who admitted that he had started out knowing almost none of it. In 2019 he turned the same method on a smaller territory. The Body carries the subtitle A Guide for Occupants, and the joke is the point: each of us has lived in one of these all our lives, and most of us could not say where the spleen is or what it does.

He begins with a bill. In 2013 the Royal Society of Chemistry worked out what it would cost to buy the elements needed to build one human being, taking the actor Benedict Cumberbatch as their model. A body, it turns out, needs fifty-nine elements. Six of them — carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, calcium and phosphorus — account for just over ninety-nine per cent of you; the rest are present only as traces. Oxygen makes up most of your weight and costs almost nothing. Purified carbon is the expensive item. The total came to a little under £97,000. An American television programme that had done the same sum a year earlier put the figure at about $168.

Bryson's point is that the argument about the price does not matter. Spend what you like, hire whichever scientists you please, and you still could not assemble a person from that pile. The ingredients are entirely mundane: the same atoms that sit in a lump of coal or a glass of water. There are roughly seven billion billion billion of them in you, none of them is alive, and none of them knows that you exist. Nobody is in charge. And yet, for the length of a human life, they hold together and do everything that you call being you.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is Bill Bryson, and what is unusual about him as the author of a book on the body?

2. What did the Royal Society of Chemistry calculate, and what does Bryson conclude from it?

1. An American-born writer, long resident in Britain, known for travel books and for A Short History of Nearly Everything. He is a layman, not a scientist or a doctor: he writes as an ordinary occupant trying to understand the place he lives in.

2. The cost of the fifty-nine elements needed to build a human being: a little under £97,000, although another estimate was about $168. His conclusion is that the price is beside the point. The ingredients are mundane and nobody could assemble a person from them; the wonder is that these lifeless atoms make a living you.

Discuss:
  • Was your guess before reading closer to £97,000 or to $168? Does it change how you feel about your body to hear that its ingredients are completely ordinary — or does that make it more impressive?

Part 2 — The dark room

Of all the organs Bryson visits, the one that astonishes him most is the brain. To look at, it is unpromising: a little under a kilo and a half of soft, pale matter, three-quarters or more of it water. It accounts for about two per cent of your weight and takes about twenty per cent of your energy. Even so, it is thrifty. It runs on roughly four hundred calories a day, which Bryson compares to the energy in a blueberry muffin, and on that it does more than any machine yet built.

What really stops him is where it works. The brain sits in silence and total darkness inside the skull. It has never seen light. It has never heard a sound. It has no feeling of its own; a surgeon can touch it and the patient feels nothing. All that ever reaches it is a stream of electrical pulses, like the taps of a telegraph key. From these, and from nothing else, it conjures a whole world: the blue of the sky, the smell of coffee, the voice of someone you love. The colour is not out there. Light has wavelengths; it is the brain that turns them into blue.

It even has to cheat with time. Signals from the eyes take a moment to arrive and be processed — by some estimates about a fifth of a second. If the brain simply showed you what it received, you would always be living slightly in the past, and you would never catch a ball. So it guesses. What you experience as 'now' is the brain's forecast of what the world will be like by the time the news gets through. You have spent your whole life, Bryson suggests, in a world that your brain is making up as it goes along — and it does it so well that you have never noticed.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Give three facts about the brain's size, make-up or energy use.

2. Why does Bryson describe the brain as working in a dark room, and how does it 'cheat with time'?

1. It weighs a little under a kilo and a half; it is three-quarters or more water; it is about two per cent of body weight and uses about twenty per cent of the body's energy, roughly four hundred calories a day.

2. It is shut inside the skull in silence and darkness and receives only electrical pulses, from which it conjures everything we see, hear and smell. Because signals take about a fifth of a second to arrive and be processed, it predicts what the world will be like a moment ahead, so that what we experience as the present is a forecast.

Discuss:
  • 'The colour is not out there.' If blue, music and the smell of coffee are made inside the brain, in what sense do two people ever experience the same thing? Does it matter that we can never check?

Part 3 — What nobody knows

Bryson spent years on the book, reading the research and questioning surgeons, anatomists and biologists. The surprise is how often the answer to his questions was a shrug. Take the yawn. Everybody does it, it is famously catching, and nobody can say for certain what it is for; it does not even seem to be closely connected with tiredness. Or the chin: human beings are the only apes that have one, and no explanation of it has ever been generally accepted. We are, as far as anyone can tell, the only creatures that cry because of what we feel, and why emotional tears should have evolved at all remains baffling.

The list goes on. About nine people in ten are right-handed, in every society that has been studied, and the reason is unknown. We spend something like a third of our lives asleep, and although it is clear that we cannot do without it, there is still no agreement on what sleep is fundamentally for. A full explanation of most of these things has proved elusive, not because nobody has looked, but because the body turns out to be far stranger and far more complicated than a diagram in a textbook suggests.

None of this makes Bryson gloomy. What strikes him is how much goes right. The heart is relentless: about a hundred thousand beats a day, something like three billion in a long life, without your ever having to remember to do it. You blink some fourteen thousand times between waking and sleeping. Every second, without consulting you, the body is making and repairing and defending itself. We occupy the most sophisticated thing we will ever come across, we did nothing to earn it, and most of us, Bryson gently points out, repay it with crisps and a sofa. His book is an invitation to look at the place where we live — and to be astonished that it works at all.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name three things about the body that, according to this part, nobody can fully explain.

2. What attitude does Bryson take to all this ignorance — and what, in the end, impresses him most?

1. Any three of: what yawning is for; why humans alone have a chin; why we cry emotional tears; why about nine in ten people are right-handed; what sleep is fundamentally for.

2. He is delighted rather than gloomy. What impresses him most is how much goes right without any effort from us: a relentless heart, about a hundred thousand beats a day; some fourteen thousand blinks; constant making, repairing and defending. The book invites us to be astonished that the body works at all.

Discuss:
  • Does it reassure you or worry you that science cannot explain something as ordinary as a yawn? Would you rather have a doctor who says 'nobody knows' or one who always has an answer?

The grammar this story runs on: the language of not knowing

Part 3 is almost entirely about ignorance, and yet it never sounds vague. English has a precise grammar for saying exactly how much is not known. At C1 you are expected to use it, in science, in business and in any honest report.

1. No one knows / It is not known + question word.
• "No one knows why human beings have chins."
• "It is not fully understood how the brain stores a memory."
• After the question word, use statement word order: why we yawn, not why do we yawn.
2. remain · have yet to.
• remain + noun / adjective: "The purpose of the yawn remains a mystery / remains unclear."
• have yet to + verb = have not done it so far (but may): "Researchers have yet to agree on what sleep is for."
3. is thought / believed / said + to-infinitive.
• For something true now: "The appendix is thought to store helpful bacteria."
• For something earlier: to have + past participle: "Our ancestors are believed to have lost their body hair more than a million years ago."
• It reports what experts think without claiming it as certain fact.

Why this book stays with you

There were already hundreds of books about the human body, most of them by people far better qualified. What Bryson brings is the position of the reader. He does not know the answer either, so he asks the question you would have asked, and when a professor tells him something astonishing he is allowed to be astonished. His method is always the same: a number too large to feel, then a comparison from the kitchen or the high street, then a dry remark that lets you breathe out. The four hundred calories become a muffin. The elements become a shopping bill.

The deeper effect is a change of attitude towards ignorance. School science tends to present the body as a finished diagram with labels. Bryson's version is a place that is still being explored, where the people who know most are the quickest to say 'we don't know'. For anyone learning a language, or anything else, that is a useful model: being precise about the edge of your knowledge is a sign of expertise, not a lack of it.

Look again at the subtitle. A Guide for Occupants treats the body as a building and you as the tenant. A tenant does not own the place, did not build it, cannot see the plans, and will one day have to leave. How does that picture differ from the usual way we talk about 'my body'?
