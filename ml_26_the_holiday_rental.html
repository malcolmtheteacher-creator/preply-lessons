The Holiday Rental

Four hours of travel, one flight of stairs, and the key finally turns — on a flat that is not the flat in the photos. It's 9pm, your family is standing in the hallway, and the host is one phone call away. Make it count.

The Scene 🎬

Somewhere between the photos and the front door, one bedroom and the air conditioning went missing.

The listing was lovely: sunny two-bedroom apartment, air conditioning, glimpse of the sea. The reality, at 9pm with the bags still on your shoulders: one bedroom, a sofa bed in the lounge, an air-conditioning unit with a hand-written "broken — sorry!" note taped to it, and a sea view available only from the bathroom window, standing on tiptoe, in a specific mood.

Here's what matters about the next ten minutes: you have maximum leverage tonight. Report it now and you're a guest with a problem; report it Thursday and you're a guest who stayed four nights and then wanted money. And the host, Marco, is probably not a villain — most listing gaps are half wishful photography, half things breaking between guests. Treat him as someone who'd like to fix it until he proves otherwise, and keep every option open while you do.

The call has a shape: the problem, the evidence, the question what can you do tonight? — and, held politely behind your back, the platform's complaints process, which both of you know about and neither of you has mentioned. Yet.

  1. Why does complaining on day one feel rude, when it's actually fairer to everyone than complaining on day four?
  2. Photos, menus, job adverts, dating profiles — where's the line between presenting well and lying?
YouTired, paid-up, and standing in the gap between the listing and the flat. Wants a working holiday more than a refund.
MarcoThe host. Friendly, defensive in waves, juggling three properties and a broken AC unit he's been meaning to deal with since June.
First: read the conversation and watch the order: problem stated kindly → evidence offered → his solution invited → conditions attached → agreement repeated back. The anger never arrives — and notice it's never needed.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for when what you got isn't what you were promised — rentals, hotels, deliveries, upgrades. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The tone of this whole call is reasonable person, unbudging on facts. You can be warm everywhere except the two load-bearing points — what was promised, and what happens tonight. Practise saying "What can you do for us tonight?" without either apology or aggression in it; that's the note the call lives on.

📞 Opening the problem

Phrase 1

The job: raising it immediately, without declaring war. Which opening?

A. "We've just got in, and I'm afraid it's not quite what we booked."
B. "Hi Marco. There are some serious problems with your so-called apartment."
C. "Hi! Everything's lovely, thanks! Just one or two tiny things, no rush at all…"
A — "We've just got in, and I'm afraid it's not quite what we booked."
B starts a fight, and people in fights defend instead of fix. C buries the problem so politely it may never be found — "tiny things, no rush" is how people end up paying full price for half a flat. A is calm and unmistakable: something is wrong, it's being raised now, and the phrase "what we booked" quietly puts the contract at the centre of the call.

Take it anywhere: "I'm afraid it's not quite what we booked/ordered/agreed." The civilised alarm bell.
Phrase 2

The job: the gap, stated as two facts side by side. Complete it:

"The listing ___ two bedrooms and air conditioning. What we've ___ got is one bedroom and a broken unit."

"The listing says two bedrooms. What we've actually got is one bedroom and a broken unit."
The strongest complaint shape in English: promise, then reality, no adjectives. Nothing to argue with — every word is checkable. The moment you add "disgraceful" or "unbelievable", the conversation becomes about your reaction; keep it promise-versus-reality and the conversation stays about his listing.

Take it anywhere: "It says ___, but what we've actually got is ___." Menus, contracts, job offers, product pages.
Phrase 3

The job: ending the "technically…" argument with evidence. Complete it:

"Let me send you photos so we're talking about the ___ ___."

"Let me send you photos so we're talking about the same thing."
Said kindly, this is checkmate on every "if you look closely at the listing…" dodge. You're not calling him a liar — you're proposing that both of you look at the same evidence, which is impossible to refuse and ends the version-war instantly. Take the photos before you call: listing on one side, room on the other.

Take it anywhere: "Let me send you a photo so we're talking about the same thing." Landlords, garages, deliveries, insurance.

🔧 Steering to the fix

Phrase 4

The job: declaring what kind of caller you are. Complete it:

"I'm not trying to make ___ — I just want it put ___."

"I'm not trying to make trouble — I just want it put right."
Hosts brace for two kinds of caller: the refund-hunter and the review-writer. This sentence tells him you're neither, which is what lets him stop defending and start fixing. Put right is the perfect request word — it doesn't specify money or repairs, so every kind of solution stays on the table.

Take it anywhere: "I'm not trying to make trouble — I just want it put right." Disarms first, negotiates second.
Phrase 5

The job: handing him the problem, with a deadline built in. Put the pieces in order:

for usWhat cantonight?you do
"What can you do for us tonight?"
Six words doing three jobs: it assumes he can do something (hard to answer with "nothing"), it makes the solution his idea (people deliver their own ideas), and "tonight" quietly rules out "I'll look into it next week". Ask it and then — this is the discipline — stop talking and let him answer.

Take it anywhere: "What can you do for me today?" The question that turns a complaint into a plan.
Phrase 6

The job: accepting his offer — with a condition riveted on. Complete it:

"That would work — ___ you can guarantee them by ten."

"That would work — if you can guarantee them by ten."
The yes-with-a-bolt-through-it. "That would work" rewards the offer; "if… by ten" converts it from a friendly intention into a commitment with a clock on it. Vague acceptances get vague deliveries — "forty minutes, more or less" becomes eleven-thirty unless someone says ten out loud.

Take it anywhere: "That would work, if ___ by ___." Accept the offer, attach the condition, name the time.

💷 Money, leverage, and sealing it

Phrase 7

The job: introducing compensation — for what can't be fixed. Complete it:

"If it can't be fixed, we'll need to talk about ___ ___."

"If it can't be fixed, we'll need to talk about money off."
The order matters enormously: fix first, money second. Opening with a refund demand marks you as a refund-hunter and poisons the call; arriving at money only after the fixable things are fixed makes it self-evidently fair — you're not seeking profit, you're declining to pay for a bedroom that doesn't exist. "We'll need to" is firm without being fierce.

Take it anywhere: "If it can't be fixed, we'll need to talk about money off." The fair sequence, in one sentence.
Phrase 8

The job: using your biggest lever without ever swinging it. Complete it:

"I'd rather sort this ___ us than ___ the platform."

"I'd rather sort this between us than through the platform."
The masterstroke of the call. It mentions the formal complaints machinery exactly once, framed as the thing you're declining to use — which means he hears the lever without being threatened by it. A threat makes people dig in; a preference makes them grateful. Note his reaction: relief, and a refund "through the app tonight so it's official".

Take it anywhere: "I'd rather sort this between us than ___." …than go through head office, than involve the lawyers, than put it in writing. The velvet lever.
Phrase 9

The job: sealing the deal so it exists outside both your memories. Complete it:

"So we've ___: two AC units by ten, and twenty percent through the app tonight."

"So we've agreed: two AC units by ten, and twenty percent through the app tonight."
Every phone agreement needs this — the playback, with numbers and deadlines, while you're both still on the line. It catches misunderstandings at the cheapest possible moment and turns a warm feeling into a checkable list. Follow it with one line in the app's chat and it's evidence, too.

Take it anywhere: "So we've agreed: ___ by ___, and ___." End every negotiation by saying the deal out loud.
Phrase 10

The job: closing warm — the relationship survives the complaint. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Thanks for taking it seriously — it's a lovely place apart from this."
Both halves are doing work. "Taking it seriously" praises exactly the behaviour you want more of over the next six nights. "Lovely place apart from this" restores proportion — the complaint was about the gap, not the whole holiday — and leaves the relationship intact with a man who still holds your deposit and your door codes.

Take it anywhere: "Thanks for taking it seriously — it's ___ apart from this." Complain about the part; keep the whole warm.

⚡ The twist

Round 1 again — and Marco, apologetic, makes a different offer: "Honestly? The AC part won't come this week, and I can't magic a second bedroom. I'll refund you the whole week, tonight — full amount. But I've got guests arriving Saturday, so I'd need you out by ten tomorrow morning."

Full money back — and nowhere to sleep tomorrow in high season, kids and all. It's 9pm. Do you take it, refuse it, or turn it into a third option nobody's mentioned yet? Negotiate.

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you raise a problem in the first hour instead of the last day?

  • Can you ask "what can you do tonight?" and then stay silent?
  • Can you accept an offer and rivet a deadline to it?
  • Can you mention your biggest lever exactly once, as a preference?

The Echo 🕰️

The listing was spectacular: a city of gold, somewhere inland, where a king powdered himself with gold dust and treasure lay in heaps. El Dorado. On the strength of that promise, thousands of Spanish conquistadors marched into jungles and mountains — through fever, starvation and years of ruin — chasing an advert nobody had fact-checked.

The gap between promise and reality has rarely been wider. There was no golden city; there was a lake ceremony, exaggerated in the retelling, polished by every teller because polishing it served every teller. Expeditions kept setting out anyway — because they'd already paid so much to believe it, and the more a man has invested in a promise, the harder he'll fight anyone who checks it.

You, at least, can check. The photos are on your phone, the listing is on the app, and the gap between them is a ten-minute phone call — made tonight, before you've invested a single night more.
