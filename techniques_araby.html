Araby

James Joyce's story of a Dublin boy, a promised gift, and a bazaar closing its lights.

Today: you'll tell the story of a boy's first infatuation and the evening it ended — and you'll master the adjective + preposition pairs English uses for feelings: keen on, ashamed of, disappointed with, nervous about.
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers — the point is to start thinking about the ideas the story plays with.
Discuss:
  • Do you remember your first crush — or the first time you admired someone from a distance? What small things suddenly became enormously important?
  • Why do faraway places — a foreign market, an exotic name — seem magical until we actually arrive?
  • Is disappointment a good teacher? Think of something that taught you more by failing than it would have by succeeding.

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four short activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. Every morning he lay on the floor of the front parlour watching her door — a boy completely infatuated, though he had barely spoken ten words to her.
infatuated (adj) — filled with an intense, short-lived, often unrealistic passion for someone. Noun: infatuation.
2. The word "Araby" — strange, Eastern, musical — cast an enchantment over him: everything ordinary seemed to fade beside it.
an enchantment (n) — a magical spell; a feeling of being under magic. Verb: to enchant.
3. He couldn't concentrate at school; the teacher's voice became a distant murmur while his mind was somewhere else entirely — he had never been so distracted.
distracted (adj) — unable to concentrate because your mind is on something else.

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: bazaar · errand · vanity. Match each definition to the right word — out loud, then reveal.

1. Which word means a market, especially one with an Eastern or exotic character?
a bazaar (n) — "Araby" is the name of a charity bazaar visiting Dublin — stalls, lights, music.
2. Which word means a short trip to do a small task for someone?
an errand (n) — "He carried parcels on Saturday errands with his aunt." Collocation: to run an errand.
3. Which word means excessive pride in yourself; caring too much how you appear?
vanity (n) — the story's famous last line calls the boy "a creature driven and derided by vanity".

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. "His uncle came home late, having completely forgotten the boy's request, and fumbled for his keys at the door." To fumble is to…
  (a) knock loudly   (b) handle something clumsily, without skill   (c) whistle
(b) — to fumble = to use your hands clumsily; also "to fumble for words".
2. "By the time he arrived, most of the stalls were closed and half the hall was in darkness; the visit was in vain." In vain means…
  (a) too early   (b) very expensive   (c) without success; for nothing
(c) — in vain = uselessly, achieving nothing. Related to vanity — both from Latin vanus, "empty".

D · Word building & collocations

1. You know to admire. Make the noun ("he watched her with silent ___") and the person ("her secret ___").
admiration · admirer. "A secret admirer" — exactly what the boy is.
2. You know to promise. Complete both patterns: "He ___ to bring her something" and "He made her a ___." Then the adjective for a young person with a bright future: "a ___ student".
promised to bring · made her a promise · a promising student. The whole story hangs on one small promise.
3. Collocation check: which verbs fit? "___ the train" (be too late for it), "___ your temper" (become angry), "___ your way" (become lost)."
miss the train · lose your temper · lose your way. The boy nearly misses everything — the train is late, the evening drains away.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The girl on North Richmond Street

North Richmond Street was a quiet, blind street — a dead end — where the houses gazed at one another with brown imperturbable faces. The boy lived there with his aunt and uncle in a house whose former tenant, a priest, had died in the back drawing-room, leaving behind old yellowed books and a rusty bicycle-pump under a bush in the wild garden. In winter the boys played in the dark lanes until their bodies glowed, and their shouts echoed in the silent street.

And every evening, from the shadows, he watched for Mangan's sister. She lived across the way; she would come out on the doorstep to call her brother in to tea, and the light from the half-opened door caught the white curve of her neck and the soft rope of her hair. Her name sprang to his lips at moments in strange prayers and praises which he himself did not understand. His eyes were often full of tears, and he could not say why. He had never spoken to her, beyond a few casual words — and yet her name was like a summons to all his foolish blood.

Her image accompanied him even in places the most hostile to romance. On Saturday evenings, carrying parcels through the flaring streets for his aunt — among the drunken men and bargaining women, the shrill litanies of shop-boys, the nasal chanting of street-singers — he imagined that he bore his chalice safely through a throng of foes. He did not know whether he would ever speak to her at all, or, if he spoke, how he could tell her of his confused adoration. But his body was like a harp, and her words and gestures were like fingers running upon the wires.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What kind of street does the boy live on, and with whom?

2. How well does the boy actually know Mangan's sister — and how does he feel about her?

1. A quiet dead-end street in Dublin ("blind" street), in a house where a priest once lived and died. He lives with his aunt and uncle.

2. Hardly at all — they have exchanged only a few casual words. Yet he is completely infatuated: he watches her door every morning, her name comes to him like a prayer, and his eyes fill with tears he cannot explain.

Discuss:
  • The boy imagines himself carrying a "chalice" — a holy cup — through a crowd of enemies. What does it tell us that he pictures his crush in the language of religion and knighthood?

Part 2 — The promise

At last she spoke to him. She asked him was he going to Araby. He forgot whether he answered yes or no. It would be a splendid bazaar, she said; she would love to go. But she could not go, she told him, turning a silver bracelet round and round her wrist — there was a retreat that week in her convent school. The light caught her as she stood at the railing, and he stood alone at the bars, seeing only the white curve of her neck and the border of her petticoat. "If I go," he said, "I will bring you something."

What innumerable follies laid waste his waking and sleeping thoughts after that evening! He wished to annihilate the tedious intervening days. He chafed against the work of school; the word Araby was called to him through the silence in which his soul luxuriated and cast an Eastern enchantment over him. His schoolmaster, noticing his idleness, hoped he was not beginning to idle; but he could not call his wandering thoughts together. He had hardly any patience with the serious work of life which, now that it stood between him and his desire, seemed to him child's play — ugly, monotonous child's play.

On Saturday morning he reminded his uncle that he wished to go to the bazaar in the evening. The uncle, fussing at the hallstand with his hat-brush, answered curtly: "Yes, boy, I know." All day the boy waited. At dinner-time the uncle had not yet come home. The boy sat staring at the clock, then marched upstairs through the high, cold, empty rooms of the house, and looked down at the street, and over at the dark house where she lived. An hour passed. At nine o'clock — at last — he heard his uncle's latchkey in the door, heard him talking to himself, heard the hallstand rocking under the weight of his overcoat. His uncle had forgotten. "The people are in bed and after their first sleep now," the uncle said. Then, when the boy asked again for the money: "Do you know The Arab's Farewell to his Steed?" — and the boy left the kitchen while his uncle was still reciting the opening lines to his aunt.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why can't Mangan's sister go to the bazaar, and what does the boy promise?

2. What goes wrong on Saturday, and how late is the boy delayed?

1. There is a retreat at her convent school that week. The boy promises: "If I go, I will bring you something."

2. His uncle — who holds the money — forgets all about the bazaar and comes home at nine o'clock. Instead of hurrying, he jokes and begins reciting a poem, while the boy finally escapes with the coin, hours too late.

Discuss:
  • Between the promise and the bazaar, school and ordinary life become "ugly, monotonous child's play" to the boy. Is that what strong longing always does — and is it a gift or a distortion?

Part 3 — The lights going out

He held a florin tightly in his hand as he strode down Buckingham Street towards the station. The sight of streets thronged with buyers and glaring with gas recalled to him the purpose of his journey. He took his seat in a third-class carriage of a deserted train. After an intolerable delay the train moved out of the station slowly, crept onward among ruinous houses and over the twinkling river. In a few minutes, the train drew up beside an improvised wooden platform. It was ten minutes to ten.

He could not find any sixpenny entrance and, fearing the bazaar would be closed, passed in quickly through a turnstile, handing a shilling — a whole shilling — to a weary-looking man. He found himself in a big hall girdled at half its height by a gallery. Nearly all the stalls were closed and the greater part of the hall was in darkness. He recognised a silence like that which pervades a church after a service. He walked into the centre of the bazaar timidly. A few people were gathered about the stalls which were still open. Before a curtain, over which the words Café Chantant were written in coloured lamps, two men were counting money on a tray. He listened to the fall of the coins.

Remembering with difficulty why he had come, he went over to one of the stalls and examined porcelain vases and flowered tea-sets. At the door of the stall a young lady was talking and laughing with two young gentlemen — about nothing at all. Observing him, the young lady came over and asked him did he wish to buy anything. The tone of her voice was not encouraging; she seemed to have spoken to him out of a sense of duty. He looked humbly at the great jars that stood like eastern guards at either side of the dark entrance to the stall and murmured: "No, thank you." He lingered a moment, then turned slowly away. He allowed the two pennies to fall against the sixpence in his pocket. A voice called from one end of the gallery that the light was out. The upper part of the hall was now completely dark. Gazing up into the darkness, he saw himself as a creature driven and derided by vanity; and his eyes burned with anguish and anger.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the boy find when he finally reaches Araby?

2. What happens at the stall — and what does he understand in the final moment?

1. A bazaar that is closing: most stalls shut, half the hall dark, a church-like silence, and two men counting the evening's money. The magic name turns out to be an ordinary charity sale at closing time.

2. A saleswoman, chatting idly with two men, asks him out of pure duty if he wants anything; he says "No, thank you" and buys nothing. As the lights go out, he sees himself clearly for the first time — "a creature driven and derided by vanity" — and burns with anguish and anger.

Discuss:
  • Nothing dramatic happens at the bazaar — a dull stall, an indifferent saleswoman, coins on a tray. Why does this ordinary scene destroy the boy's dream so completely?

Grammar — adjectives + prepositions for feelings

This is a story made of feelings: the boy is infatuated with a girl, obsessed with a word, ashamed of himself by the end. In English, many feeling-adjectives take a fixed preposition — and choosing the right one is what makes you sound natural.

The core pairs — learn them as single units:
• of: ashamed of, proud of, afraid of, tired of, fond of
• with / by: infatuated with, obsessed with, disappointed with/by (a thing), bored with, impressed by
• about: nervous about, excited about, worried about, serious about
• on / in / at: keen on, interested in, good/bad at
How they work in sentences:
• After the preposition use a noun or an -ing form: "She's keen on photography" / "He's nervous about speaking in public."
• People vs things: disappointed in a person ("I'm disappointed in you") but disappointed with/by a thing ("disappointed with the result").
• Everyday examples: "I'm proud of my sister." "He's terrible at names." "We're excited about the trip."

Going deeper

"Araby" appears in Dubliners (1914), Joyce's book of stories about his home city — each one ending in what he called an epiphany: a sudden moment when a character sees their life as it really is. It draws on Joyce's own boyhood; there really was an "Araby" bazaar in Dublin in 1894.

The heart of it. The story runs on a gap between two languages: the boy narrates his crush in words borrowed from religion and romance — chalice, litanies, enchantment — while around him Joyce shows us drab, ordinary Dublin. The bazaar is where the two languages finally meet: "Araby" the magic word turns out to be a half-dark hall, a bored saleswoman, and men counting coins. Nothing bad happens; that is the point. The boy's epiphany is not about the girl, or the bazaar — it is about himself: he has been "driven and derided by vanity", in love with his own dream of being in love. Joyce ends on the burning eyes and refuses to tell us what the boy does next — because the seeing itself is the story.

Talk it through:
  • The boy's feelings are described in the language of worship — prayers, a chalice, adoration. Is his infatuation really about Mangan's sister at all? What is it about?
  • An "epiphany" is a sudden true sight of yourself. Do such moments really change people, or does the feeling fade by morning? What do you imagine the boy is like at forty?
  • Blame is spread thin here: the forgetful uncle, the slow train, the closing time — and the boy's own illusions. Who or what really defeats him?
  • Joyce called Dublin the "centre of paralysis" and filled Dubliners with people who cannot move or change. Where do you see that stillness in this story — the dead-end street, the waiting, the empty rooms?

Task C · The magic that wore off (4 minutes)

"Araby" is about the distance between how we imagine things and how they turn out. Tell a story — true or invented — about anticipation and arrival.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • A place you dreamed about visiting — and what it was actually like when you got there.
  • A first day (job, school, city) that was nothing like you had imagined — better or worse.
  • Something you saved up for or waited months for. Did it live up to the wait?
  • A childhood crush or hero, seen with adult eyes.
  • An event that everyone said would be magical. Was it?
  • The reverse: something you expected to be dull that turned out wonderful.
