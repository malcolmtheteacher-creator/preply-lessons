The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — From poverty to the stage

He was born Erik Weisz in Budapest in 1874, the son of a Jewish rabbi. When Erik was four, the family left Hungary for the United States, escaping the antisemitism of the old country, and settled first in Wisconsin and later in New York. They were poor — desperately so. As a boy, Erik sold newspapers and shined shoes to help feed his brothers and sisters, and he never forgot the feeling of having nothing.

At sixteen he read the autobiography of a famous French magician, Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin, and something caught fire in him. He took the name "Houdini" in tribute, paired it with "Harry," and set out to make magic his life. The early years were brutal: cheap circuses, dime museums, half-empty halls. For a long time the audiences were thin and the money was thinner.

Then he found the trick that would change everything. Ordinary card tricks bored the crowds, but the moment Houdini began escaping from handcuffs — challenging local police to lock him up and failing every time to keep him locked — people leaned forward in their seats. He had stumbled onto something powerful: real danger, real chains, and a man who always, somehow, got free.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were the circumstances of Houdini's childhood?

2. Where did the name "Houdini" come from, and what kind of act finally made audiences pay attention?

1. He was born Erik Weisz in Budapest to a poor Jewish family who emigrated to the United States; as a boy he did odd jobs to help support his family.

2. From the French magician Robert-Houdin, whose autobiography inspired him; escaping from handcuffs and police locks was what won the crowds.

Discuss:
  • Houdini reinvented himself completely — new name, new act, new identity. What makes some people able to leave their old self behind so totally?

Part 2 — The man who could not be held

By the early 1900s, "Harry Handcuff Houdini" was touring Europe, and the act had grown into a worldwide sensation. He walked into police stations and dared the officers to chain him; they bolted on their heaviest shackles, and minutes later he stepped out free. In London in 1904, a newspaper had special handcuffs made that were supposed to be impossible to open. Houdini struggled with them on stage for over an hour while thousands watched in agony — and then, at last, held the open cuffs above his head as the hall erupted.

He understood, better than any performer of his age, that the secret ingredient was not the trick but the fear. So he made the stunts ever more deadly. In the Milk Can escape, he was locked inside a giant can filled to the brim with water; the lid was padlocked shut, a curtain was drawn, and the audience counted the seconds, certain he was about to drown. Even more famous was the Chinese Water Torture Cell: hung upside down by the ankles, he was submerged in a glass tank of water and locked in, in full view of the crowd, with only a curtain between life and a watery death.

He was, above all, a master showman. He flew an aeroplane — becoming the first person to make a powered flight in Australia. He hung from cranes high above city streets, wrapped in a straitjacket, and wriggled free as traffic stopped below. Every poster, every interview, every escape was built to make ordinary people believe that this small, fierce man was simply unkillable.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened with the special handcuffs in London in 1904?

2. Describe one of Houdini's water escapes and why it terrified audiences.

1. A newspaper had "impossible" handcuffs made; Houdini struggled for over an hour on stage before finally freeing himself, to a roaring crowd.

2. Either the Milk Can (locked in a water-filled can) or the Chinese Water Torture Cell (submerged upside down in a locked glass tank); the crowd believed they were watching him risk drowning.

Discuss:
  • Houdini said the real act was the fear, not the trick. Why do audiences pay to watch someone they believe might actually die?

Part 3 — The skeptic, and the escape he couldn't make

In his later years, Houdini took on a new mission. After the First World War, grieving families flocked to "mediums" who claimed to contact the dead — for a fee. Houdini, who knew every trick of deception from the inside, was furious that frauds were preying on the bereaved. He began attending séances in disguise, sometimes with a reporter or a police officer beside him, and then standing up to expose the hidden wires, the secret compartments, the sleight of hand. He joined a magazine committee that offered a cash prize to any medium who could prove genuine powers under proper conditions. No one ever claimed it. His most famous target was a celebrated Boston medium known as "Margery," whom he publicly debunked.

Then, in October 1926, came the strange chain of events that no one could have scripted. In Montreal, a student visiting his dressing room asked whether Houdini could really take a punch to the stomach without harm. Before the performer was ready, the young man struck him several hard blows. Houdini was already unwell, and the pain grew worse, but — true to character — he refused to stop performing. He travelled on to Detroit and went on stage with a high fever.

What he did not know was that his appendix had burst, flooding his body with infection. On 31 October 1926 — Halloween — Harry Houdini died of peritonitis, aged fifty-two. The man who had escaped every chain, can, cell and torture device ever built for him could not escape this. Nearly a century later, he remains the most famous magician who ever lived, the immigrant boy who turned the fear of death into the greatest show on Earth.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Houdini's mission in his later years, and why did it matter so much to him?

2. What was the chain of events that led to his death, and what was the eventual cause?

1. Exposing fraudulent mediums who claimed to contact the dead; he hated that they were exploiting grieving families, and he knew the tricks from the inside.

2. A student punched him in the stomach before he was ready; already ill, he kept performing. His appendix had ruptured, and he died of peritonitis on Halloween 1926.

Discuss:
  • There is a dark irony in how Houdini died. Why do you think his death became almost as famous as his life?

Grammar — be about to / be on the verge of / be to

Houdini's whole act lived in one held breath: the second before something happens. English has three neat structures for that knife-edge moment — and each one points at a slightly different kind of "almost." Get them right and you can build suspense in a single sentence.

The three structures, side by side:
• be about to + base verb — something is going to happen very soon, in the next moment: "The crowd believed he was about to drown." "He is about to step into the tank."
• be on the verge / on the point of + -ing (or noun) — extremely close to happening, right at the edge: "Underwater and out of air, he was on the verge of blacking out." "She was on the point of giving up."
• be to + base verb — a planned, arranged or official future; often a formal instruction or scheduled event: "He was to perform the most dangerous escape of his career." "The prisoners are to be released at dawn."
Feel the difference:
• about to = it's happening any second now (imminent).
• on the verge / point of = even closer, often with strong emotion or strain — teetering on the edge.
• be to = it was planned / fixed / ordered — not about how soon, but about arrangement. Common in news, rules and formal narrative: "The escape is to take place at midnight."
