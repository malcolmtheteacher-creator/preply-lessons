Why We Ignore Warnings

The fascinating psychology of human risk

B1-B2 | 45-50 minutes

The Mystery of Ignored Warnings

A storm is coming. Experts say: "Stay home. It's dangerous." But thousands of people go outside anyway. Why? Are they stupid? Or is something more interesting happening in the human brain?

90% of young people who see a "trigger warning" choose to look at the content anyway Flinders University, 2025
70% of people say they need to SEE danger before they believe it AccuWeather Research
3x more likely to follow a warning if you know someone personally affected Columbia University

The Science Behind Our Choices

Useful vocabulary: "warning fatigue" = when we see too many warnings, we stop paying attention

Dr. Laura Myers

Psychologist, Center for Advanced Public Safety, Alabama

"People don't want to change their plans unless they are almost certain the danger will affect them personally. After many warnings that don't produce any impact for their specific area, people become desensitised. They think: 'The last five warnings were nothing. Why would this one be different?'"

Gerald Wilde

Risk Psychologist, Author of "Target Risk"

"Everyone has their own level of 'acceptable risk'. If people believe the real danger is lower than the warning suggests, they will ignore it. And if they have seen others ignore the warning without consequences, they will probably do the same. A warning that is not perceived as needed will not be heeded - even when it is needed."

Dr. Victoria Bridgland

Flinders University, Australia

"We discovered something surprising: warnings often create a 'forbidden fruit' effect. When something is labelled as dangerous or off-limits, it becomes more tempting. The warning creates curiosity. People think: 'What am I missing? I need to see for myself.'"

The Big Psychological Ideas

Optimism Bias "Bad things happen to other people, not to me."
Psychological Reactance "Don't tell me what to do! Now I want to do the opposite."
Warning Fatigue "I've heard this warning 100 times. It's never real."

Think about yourself: Have you ever ignored a warning? Why did you ignore it? Which of these psychological patterns might explain your behaviour?

Language Focus A

Click each expression to explore. These are practical phrases you can use in many situations!

to turn a blind eye (to something)

+
MEANING

To deliberately ignore something, especially something you know is wrong or dangerous. You choose not to see it.

FORM

Subject + turn a blind eye + to + noun/gerund

Common patterns: "turn a blind eye to the problem / the risks / what's happening"

REGISTER

Neutral - works in both casual and professional contexts. Very common idiom.

"Many people turn a blind eye to health warnings on cigarette packets."

= They see the warnings but choose to ignore them

"The government can't turn a blind eye to climate change any longer."
NOW YOU TRY

What is something that society often turns a blind eye to? Why do you think people choose to ignore it?

to take something for granted

+
MEANING

To assume something will always be there or always be true, without appreciating it or thinking about it. Often, we only realise its value when we lose it.

FORM

Subject + take + something/someone + for granted

Can be used with things, people, or situations: "take our safety for granted", "take her for granted"

REGISTER

Universal - very common in everyday English. Essential vocabulary!

"We take clean water for granted until there's a shortage."

= We don't appreciate how important it is until it's gone

"People often take their health for granted when they're young."
NOW YOU TRY

What is something you used to take for granted but now appreciate more? What changed?

it's only a matter of time before...

+
MEANING

Something is certain to happen eventually. We don't know exactly when, but it will definitely happen.

FORM

It's only a matter of time + before + subject + verb

Also: "It was only a matter of time before..." (past)

REGISTER

Neutral to slightly formal. Often used to express inevitability, sometimes with a sense of warning.

"If you keep driving so fast, it's only a matter of time before you have an accident."

= An accident will definitely happen eventually

"With sea levels rising, it's only a matter of time before some coastal cities face serious flooding."
NOW YOU TRY

Complete this sentence with your own prediction: "With the way technology is changing, it's only a matter of time before..."

Language Focus B

Two more useful expressions for talking about risk, learning, and caution.

to learn (something) the hard way

+
MEANING

To learn through painful experience or mistakes, rather than from advice or warnings. You didn't listen, and now you understand why you should have.

FORM

Subject + learn/learned + (lesson/that...) + the hard way

"Learn the hard way" or "learn it the hard way" or "learn that... the hard way"

REGISTER

Informal to neutral. Very common in conversation. Good for storytelling!

"I learned the hard way that you should always back up your computer files."

= I lost important files, and that taught me the lesson

"She learned the hard way that not all financial advice is good advice."
NOW YOU TRY

Tell your teacher about something you learned the hard way. What happened? What did you learn?

to err on the side of caution

+
MEANING

To be extra careful; to choose the safer option when you're not sure. If you must make a mistake, make the mistake of being too careful rather than not careful enough.

FORM

Subject + err on the side of caution

Note: "err" is pronounced like "air" - it means "to make an error"

REGISTER

Slightly formal. Excellent for professional contexts, advice-giving, and decision-making discussions.

"When there's a storm warning, I always err on the side of caution and stay home."

= I prefer to be too careful rather than take risks

"If you're not sure whether the food is still fresh, err on the side of caution and throw it away."
NOW YOU TRY

In what situations do you err on the side of caution? Are there situations where you prefer to take risks?

Key Ideas from Today

Optimism Bias: We naturally believe bad things are more likely to happen to others than to ourselves.

Warning Fatigue: The more warnings we see, the less attention we pay - especially if previous warnings weren't relevant to us.

The Personal Connection: We're 3x more likely to follow a warning if we know someone who was personally affected.
