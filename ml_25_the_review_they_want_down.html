The Review They Want Down

Two stars, honestly earned. It's half eight in the evening, your phone's ringing, and it's him.

The Scene 🎬

Three weeks ago the pipe under your kitchen sink started leaking. Gavin came out the same day, which you were grateful for. He had a look, said it needed a part he didn't carry, charged you ninety pounds for the callout, and said he'd be back Thursday. He wasn't back Thursday. He wasn't back the Thursday after that either. You rang him twice; it went to voicemail twice. You lived with a washing-up bowl under the sink for a fortnight, and eventually you sat down and wrote what happened. Two stars. No insults, no drama — just the fortnight, the bucket, and the two unanswered calls.

He did come back in the end, and he did fix it properly, and he was perfectly pleasant about it. The review had already gone up by then. You didn't take it down, because it was still true.

And now it's half eight on a Tuesday evening, you've got the telly on, and your phone is going. It's Gavin. He is not ringing about a pipe.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Have you ever left a bad review? What made you finally do it — and did you word it more gently than you actually felt?
  2. When one small business gets a bad review, a real person reads it that night. Does knowing that change what you'd write, or should it not?

Who's who

You
The One Who Wrote It ⭐⭐

Not out for revenge and not enjoying this. You wrote something true, you'd write it again, and you'd quite like to finish your evening. Fair, though — genuinely fair.

The other voice
Gavin, the Plumber 🔧

One man, one van, and a listings page that is more or less his entire livelihood. Upset, a bit heated, and not completely wrong about how hard his job is.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how the refusal is done — how it stays quiet instead of getting loud, how it concedes the fair points without moving an inch on the unfair request, and how it offers Gavin something real that isn't deletion.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Gavin gives a reason why the two phone calls went unanswered. What was his reason, and what did the other voice do with it?

2. One thing gets offered and one thing gets refused. What exactly is the trade, and what does it not include?

3. Right at the end, Gavin is told about the reply box. Why does that land better than anything else in the call?

1. He's one man in a van with nobody sitting there to answer the phone. It gets accepted straight away — "No. I know" — and so does the wider point that he isn't a bad plumber. Nothing is argued with that doesn't need arguing with, which is why the facts that are held onto sound solid rather than stubborn.
2. Refund the ninety-pound callout and a line gets added saying he came back and sorted it. The review itself stays where it is. So the trade improves the review without removing it, which means it's an offer rather than a surrender.
3. Because it's the first thing in the whole call that Gavin can actually do. Up to then he's been asking for the one thing he won't get; the reply box hands him a way to put his own side in public, so he goes from powerless to having a next step.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for holding a position while somebody upset leans on you. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. The whole thing turns on volume. He will go up; you don't. Say every one of these a little slower and a little quieter than feels natural, with no edge on it at all, and they land as calm rather than cold. Say the same words fast, or loud, or with a laugh in them, and you've had an argument instead of a conversation — and once it's an argument, the person who is right stops mattering.

📞 Taking the call

Phrase 1

You've picked up, and it's him. You're not hostile, you're just genuinely caught on the back foot. Which opener buys you a second without starting a fight?

A. "Who is this, sorry?"
B. "I wasn't expecting a call about it, I'll be honest."
C. "How did you even get my number?"
B — "I wasn't expecting a call about it, I'll be honest."
C accuses him of something before he's said a word, and A pretends you don't know why he's ringing when you obviously do. B just says the true thing — you're surprised — and "I'll be honest" tacked on the end signals that you're going to be straight with him for the rest of the call, which is worth setting up early.
Phrase 2

He's told you why he's upset. You want to say two things at once: this isn't up for negotiation, and you're not refusing to discuss it. Put the pieces in order:

but I'm happyI stand byto talk about it.what I put,
"I stand by what I put, but I'm happy to talk about it."
To "stand by" something you wrote or said means you still believe it and you're not withdrawing it. What makes the phrase work is the "but" — most people offer one half or the other, either digging in and refusing to engage, or engaging so warmly that the other person assumes they're winning. Saying both at once tells him the outcome is settled while the conversation isn't.

🧾 Holding the facts

Phrase 3

He says the review isn't fair. It is fair — you were careful when you wrote it. Which reply holds that ground?

A. "It's more or less true, most of it."
B. "Maybe I was a bit harsh, I don't know."
C. "Everything in it happened. I've not exaggerated any of it."
C — "Everything in it happened. I've not exaggerated any of it."
A and B both hedge, and hedging invites him to start negotiating over which bits were unfair. C doesn't defend the review or argue about it — it simply reports that the facts are the facts, which is a much smaller claim and therefore a much harder one to attack.
Phrase 4

He's implying you went straight online without warning him. You didn't. Complete the chunk with the ONE missing word that puts the stress on the trying: "I ___ try ringing you twice before I wrote anything."

"I did try ringing you twice before I wrote anything."
You'd normally just say "I tried ringing you twice" — dropping "did" in front of the plain verb adds emphasis and answers an accusation nobody has quite made out loud. It's the spoken equivalent of underlining. Note the order too: you rang first, you wrote second, and saying it in that order shows the review was the last step rather than the first.

🚫 The refusal itself

Phrase 5

Here it is — the moment. He's asked you to remove it. Which one actually closes the door?

A. "I don't really want to take it down."
B. "I'm not going to take it down, I'm afraid."
C. "I'll have a think about taking it down."
B — "I'm not going to take it down, I'm afraid."
To "take down" something online is to remove it. A and C both leave a gap he will spend the next ten minutes pushing on, because "don't really want to" can be talked round and "I'll have a think" is a yes waiting to happen. B is a flat decision, and "I'm afraid" is the small polite word we put on bad news to show we're not enjoying delivering it — it softens the sound without softening the answer.
Phrase 6

You'd like to be reasonable, but reasonable can't mean deletion. Offer him a genuine trade — and shut the other door in the same breath. Put the pieces in order:

I'm not going toIf you refund the calloutdelete it, though.I'll add that you sorted it —
"If you refund the callout I'll add that you sorted it — I'm not going to delete it, though."
A "callout" is the fee a tradesman charges just for coming out to look, and if he "sorted it" he put it right in the end. The move here is to give and refuse in one sentence, because an offer made on its own gets heard as the start of a negotiation. The "though" on the end quietly marks the limit: this far, no further.

🌙 Holding your own line

Phrase 7

It's late, this is your evening, and he rang your personal number. Say so — without apologising and without snapping. Put the pieces in order:

at this time of night,you didn't ring meactually.I'd rather
"I'd rather you didn't ring me at this time of night, actually."
"I'd rather you didn't" is how we ask someone to stop doing something without ordering them to — it states a preference and leaves them the dignity of choosing. The "actually" on the end is doing real work: it marks this as something you've only just decided to mention, which keeps it a passing remark rather than a complaint.
Phrase 8

He's decided you're the sort of person who enjoys destroying small businesses. You're not, and you can prove it. Complete the chunk with the ONE missing word — a negative verb in the past, and nothing after it: "I've left plenty of good ones. This one just ___."

"I've left plenty of good ones. This one just wasn't."
"Ones" here stands in for reviews, and the sentence deliberately stops after "wasn't" rather than finishing with "a good one" — leaving the obvious word out makes it sound understated instead of laboured. The point underneath is that you're not a hostile reviewer, you're an ordinary one who had one bad experience, and that's a fact about your history, not an opinion he can argue with.

📩 Ending it

Phrase 9

He says he's got no way of defending himself. He has — every listings page lets the business answer a review underneath it, in public, where everyone reading the review will see it. In your own words, tell him that. What would you say?

e.g. "You're welcome to reply to it publicly — that's what the reply box is for."
"You're welcome to" isn't permission exactly; it's a way of saying the door is open and you won't mind. The reason this changes the temperature of the call is that it hands him something to do. He rang wanting the one thing you won't give him, and he can put the phone down with something he can act on tonight, on his own, without you.
Phrase 10

Nothing more is going to be achieved and you both know it. Which ending closes the call without slamming it?

A. "Right, well. Bye then."
B. "I'm hanging up now."
C. "I think we'll have to leave it there. Goodnight."
C — "I think we'll have to leave it there. Goodnight."
To "leave it there" means to stop a conversation at the point it's reached, without pretending it's resolved. B is a threat and A is a sulk, whereas C uses "we" and "have to", so ending the call sounds like something the situation requires rather than something you're doing to him. "Goodnight" then makes it final, politely.

🔑 Make them yours

Not backing down is a skill you'll use far away from review pages — with a landlord, a boss, a relative who's decided you're being difficult. Pin three of these to your own life so they're there when you need them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I stand by what I put, but I'm happy to talk about it." — something you've said or written that somebody wanted you to withdraw. What was it?

"I'm not going to take it down, I'm afraid." — a request you should have refused flatly, but softened until it turned into a maybe. What did you say instead?

"You're welcome to reply to it publicly — that's what the reply box is for." — a disagreement where you could hand the other person something to do instead of just a no.

The Twist

😬 He rings back at nine

No script this time. He's not shouting now — he's flat, and it's worse. He'll refund the callout, he says, and about that fortnight: his mum went into hospital the Thursday he was meant to come back, and he didn't want to say that on the phone to a stranger. A woman rang him this morning about a bathroom, read your review while she was talking to him, and went elsewhere. He's not asking you to lie. He's asking you to hide it for a month, just a month, while he gets back on his feet, and he'll refund you double. Start the timer. Every single thing he's said might be true, and all your clean arguments were built for the angry version of him, not this one. Decide — and say it to him. One rule: you don't get to say "let me think about it and ring you back."

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I gave him every point that was true, straight away, without a fight.
  • I refused flatly — he can't have gone away thinking I might still remove it.
  • I stated the facts instead of defending myself or apologising for them.
  • I offered him something real, and made the limit of it clear in the same breath.
  • My voice stayed lower than his the whole way through, right to "goodnight".

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Reviews were sold to us as information — a way for strangers to be honest with each other about who's any good. Mostly they are that. But an honest sentence about a bad fortnight now sits on a public page for years, next to a man's phone number, doing work you never signed up for.

So the awkward truth is that both people in this call are right about something. He's right that it's out of proportion. You're right that it happened, and that a review you'd delete under pressure was never worth reading in the first place.

Which leaves you where most honest disagreements leave you: not winning, exactly. Just still standing where you were, having said so quietly, and having let the other person keep something.

Two bigger questions

  1. A review is one person's fortnight, permanently attached to another person's living. Is that a fair exchange? Would you feel differently if the two stars were yours to carry?
  2. If a review can be taken down whenever the subject rings up and pushes hard enough, what are the remaining reviews actually worth? And who ends up with the good ratings — the good businesses, or the persistent ones?
