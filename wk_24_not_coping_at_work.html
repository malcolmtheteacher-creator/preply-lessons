Not Coping at Work

You've been fine for months. You've said "fine" so often it comes out on its own now. Today you've got ten minutes with your manager, and you're going to say the other thing instead.

The Scene 🎬

It's a Tuesday, about half three, and you're in the small meeting room with the broken blind, because you asked for five minutes and it was the only space free. Your manager is on the other side of the table with his laptop still open. You have rehearsed this on the train roughly nine times, and every version sounded better on the train than it is going to sound now.

Here's what's actually hard about it, and it isn't the vocabulary. You have spent a year building a reputation as the person who absorbs things — who takes the extra account, who answers at eleven at night, who never makes a fuss — and now you have to put a crack in that reputation on purpose, in front of the man who decides what happens to you next. Say it too gently and he'll hear "a bit tired, she'll be fine by Friday". Say it too dramatically and he'll hear "she's leaving". Neither of those gets you the thing you came for.

So the job today is narrow, and quite difficult: be plain about how bad it is, be specific about what would help, and make it impossible to mishear.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When someone at work asks how you are, how often is your answer true? What would have to be different — about the person, or the room, or the day — before you'd answer honestly?
  2. Think of a time you kept going long after you should have said something. What stopped you: the language, the manager, or the version of yourself you'd have had to give up?

Who's who

You
The One Who Always Copes 💼

Good at the job, and better still at looking fine. You've been running on very little since about April. You've booked the five minutes — now you have to use them, in English, without softening it into nothing.

The other voice
Neil Harker 🗂️

Your manager. Decent enough — not a bully, not a monster — but in three years he has never once asked how you are. He isn't ready for this, and he won't handle it smoothly. That doesn't mean it's going badly.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's awkward in places. It's meant to be; that's roughly what this sounds like when it's going well.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Neil's first instinct is to guess at a cause and name it. What does he guess — and how does the speaker refuse the guess without arguing with him?

2. Neil says something that sounds like a small criticism of the speaker but is really an admission about himself. What is it, and what has he just admitted?

3. The speaker asks for two specific things and rules two things out. What are the four?

1. He guesses the Pearson account, because one difficult account is a problem he already knows how to solve. The speaker doesn't tell him he's wrong; they just make it bigger — it isn't one thing, it's all of it — which takes the easy fix away without turning it into a disagreement.
2. "You always seem fine." He offers it as the reason he never noticed, and half-apologises for it while he's saying it, but what he is admitting is that he has read the surface of someone for three years and never once checked underneath.
3. Asked for: someone else fronting Pearson until the new year, and two weeks off. Ruled out: being excused from the work altogether, and resigning. The speaker also takes the deadline away — no answer is needed that day — which makes it easier for Neil to say yes later.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the hardest five minutes of the working year. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. The temptation here is to protect everyone in the room by making the words smaller — a laugh at the end of the sentence, a "sort of", a "but it's fine, honestly". Resist that, because every softener you add is one more reason for your manager to file this under nothing to do. Flat and quiet is the right delivery: ordinary volume, ordinary voice, no drama, and then a pause where you let the sentence sit there rather than rushing in to rescue him from it. The silence after you speak is doing half the work.

🚪 Opening it

Phrase 1

You've got five minutes, and you need a first line that tells him this is serious without announcing a crisis. Put it in order:

about a month ago.I shouldThere's somethinghave said
"There's something I should have said about a month ago."
It does two jobs in nine words: it warns him that a real thing is coming, so he stops half-reading his screen, and it quietly admits you left it too long, which makes everything after it sound honest rather than sudden. Putting a length of time in matters more than it looks, because it stops him hearing all this as one bad day.
Phrase 2

Now the sentence itself. Both of these are true, and both are polite. Which one is harder for your manager to file away and forget?

A. "Things have been quite full on lately, so I might need to look at my workload at some point."
B. "I'm not coping, and I'd rather say it now than fall over in front of a client."
B — "I'm not coping, and I'd rather say it now than fall over in front of a client."
A is made entirely of soft edges — "quite", "might", "at some point" — so there's nothing in it he can act on, and he'll have honestly forgotten it by Friday. B uses the plain word, coping, and then gives him a reason to care in his own currency: to fall over in front of someone means to break down or fail in public, and a client watching that happen is his problem as well as yours. You're not threatening him with it, you're showing him what doing nothing costs.

🩺 Saying how bad it is

Phrase 3

He's trying to pin it on one account, because one account is something he can fix. You need to widen it without picking a fight. Put it in order:

and I've runIt's all of it,It's not one thing.out of road.
"It's not one thing. It's all of it, and I've run out of road."
The picture inside run out of road is a driver who can only keep going while there's still road in front of them — you haven't crashed, but there's nothing ahead, so something has to happen now. It's useful precisely because it blames nobody; it describes the position you're in rather than accusing the man you're talking to, which is what lets him carry on listening.
Phrase 4

He knows your work has been going out on time, so "I'm exhausted" will simply bounce off him. Which line explains how both things can be true at once?

A. "I'm absolutely shattered all the time, you've no idea."
B. "I've been getting by, but I'm getting by on fumes."
C. "Honestly, I'm barely doing my job at the moment."
B — "I've been getting by, but I'm getting by on fumes."
To get by is to manage, but only just, and fumes are the last of the vapour in an empty petrol tank — so the phrase agrees with him that the work has gone out, while telling him what it has been costing to get it out. A only reports a feeling, which he can't check and may not believe. C is a sentence you'd spend the next six months undoing, and it isn't even true.
Phrase 5

One concrete fact will land harder than any adjective, and you want to hand it over without making a speech of it. Put it in order:

if I'm honestsleeping properly,with you.I've stopped
"I've stopped sleeping properly, if I'm honest with you."
Sleep is the fact that changes the temperature of the room, because it isn't an opinion about your workload — it's your health, and he can't argue with it. Hanging if I'm honest with you on the end rather than the front is what keeps it ordinary: said first it sounds like a warning, said last it sounds like something that slipped out, which is roughly how you want it to sound.

🎯 Asking for the change

Phrase 6

He's braced for you to ask to be excused from the hard work while everyone else carries it. Take that fear off the table — say what you're not asking for, then what you are. Complete the chunk: "I'm not asking to be ___ ___. I'm asking for it to change."

"I'm not asking to be let off. I'm asking for it to change."
To be let off something means to be excused from having to do it, and that is exactly what he's quietly afraid you want, so naming the fear and refusing it is the quickest way past it. What's left standing once you've refused it is the real request, and he now has to hear that as a working problem rather than a favour.
Phrase 7

"I need a break" gets you sympathy and nothing else. You want a number he can picture in a diary. Complete the chunk: "Two weeks would ___ ___ enormous difference."

"Two weeks would make an enormous difference."
In English you make a difference — you never do one — and enormous carries more weight here than "big" without tipping over into drama. Most of the work is done by the number, though, because two weeks is something he can agree to on Thursday, whereas "a break" is something he can agree with forever and never arrange.
Phrase 8

He's cornered: he can't fix this in the eight minutes he's got left, and a cornered manager says no. Which line gives him room without giving up your request?

A. "So what are you going to do about it, then?"
B. "Look, forget it, it's fine, we'll sort it out another time."
C. "I don't need an answer today."
C — "I don't need an answer today."
It's generous and it's tactical at the same time, because you've taken away the pressure to decide on the spot — which is the pressure that produces a defensive no — while leaving the request completely intact. Notice it says today, not "whenever": the thing is still coming back. B withdraws the whole conversation and wastes the hardest five minutes of your year, and A picks a fight with the one person whose help you need.

🧷 Making sure it isn't misheard

Phrase 9

He's gone quiet, and behind the quiet he's wondering whether you're about to leave — which is a conversation you didn't come here to have. Which line kills that thought outright?

A. "I'm not sure how much longer I can carry on like this, to be honest."
B. "This isn't me handing my notice in."
C. "Anyway. Don't worry about it, it's not a big deal."
B — "This isn't me handing my notice in."
To hand your notice in is the ordinary British way of saying you formally tell your employer you're leaving, and saying the words out loud is the only way to stop him privately deciding that's where all this is heading. A is the sentence he will hear as a resignation whether you meant it that way or not, and C throws away everything you've just said.
Phrase 10

It's over, and it went better than the version on the train. He was clumsy, but he listened, and you'd like to mark that — without gushing, and without apologising for the last five minutes. Say your closing line out loud, in your own words, before you reveal.

"Thanks for not making that harder than it was."
It thanks him for something real and rather small — he didn't argue, he didn't panic, he didn't tell you everyone's busy — instead of thanking him for a decision he hasn't made yet. And it says, without a drop of self-pity, that the thing was hard, which is a fact he now has on record and may remember the next time he wonders how you are.

The Twist

🎯 He says yes to the wrong half

Back in your own seat, one more round. Neil comes to find you on Thursday, rather pleased with himself: "Right — sorted. Take the two weeks, go, take them now. Ruth's fine with it." Then, after a beat: "Pearson stays with you, though. Nobody else knows that account and I'd be handing them a mess. But you'll come back to it fresh, won't you — two weeks, you'll feel completely different." He's being kind. He thinks he's fixed it. He has given you the small thing and kept the big one, and if you say thank you now, this conversation is finished for another year and you'll be back in this room in March. Start the timer and answer him live: take what's worth taking, say plainly what a fortnight won't do, and ask again for the thing you actually came for — to the same man, who is now expecting to be thanked. One rule: you may not use the word "sorry".

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you actually said out loud. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I used a plain word for it instead of a soft one, and I didn't put a joke on the end to make it easier for him.
  • I gave one concrete fact he couldn't argue with.
  • My ask had a number in it — something he could put in a diary.
  • I said out loud what I was not asking for, so it couldn't be misheard.
  • When he offered me the easy version, I didn't take it just to end the conversation.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one that will go wrong in the real room. Run the twist again and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

The strange part of a conversation like this is how much of the difficulty is made of reputation. You built the version of yourself who copes, deliberately, over years, and it worked — and then it quietly became the thing standing between you and the help.

Neil isn't a villain. He's a man who read the surface of somebody for three years and believed it, which is what most people do, because the surface is right there and the rest of it isn't.

So it falls to you to say it, which is unfair, and also true. The words don't have to be good. They only have to be plain enough that he can't hear something easier instead.

Two bigger questions

  1. In your workplace, and in your country, what actually happens to someone who says out loud that they're struggling? Are they looked after, quietly written off, or a bit of both — and how much of what you'd be risking is real, as opposed to imagined?
  2. Whose job is it to notice? Neil never asked in three years — but the speaker spent three years making sure there was nothing to notice. If you had to divide the responsibility between the two of them, where would you draw the line, and would you draw it in the same place if the manager were you?
