The Man Who Decided to Believe

Cambridge, Massachusetts, 1870: a brilliant young doctor could not get out of bed — until he made one decision he could not prove.

Today: you'll follow a psychologist who asked whether we may believe something without proof — and learn to add emphasis with inversion: never had he… · only when… did he… · not until… did he…
Before we start. Talk these through out loud — there are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • Can a belief be useful even when nobody can prove it? Where do you see that in everyday life?
  • What is the difference between having a good reason to believe something and wanting to believe it?

The vocabulary of this story

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. For almost three years he could hardly work. He had back pain, panic attacks and terrible low moods — a complete breakdown of his health and spirits.
a breakdown (n) — a period when you are so ill or stressed that you cannot cope with normal life. A nervous breakdown; to have a breakdown. Also used for machines and talks: a breakdown in negotiations.
2. Most of his friends were religious. He was a sceptic: whenever someone told him something, he asked, "Where is the proof?"
a sceptic (n) — a person who doubts things until they have been proved. Adjective: sceptical; noun: scepticism. US spelling: skeptic.
3. Putting all your money on one throw of the dice is a wager. A philosopher once said that believing in God is a wager worth making.
a wager (n) — a bet; something you risk on an uncertain result. Also a verb: to wager. "Pascal's Wager" is a famous argument about belief.
4. He lay awake at night in despair, unable to see any reason to go on.
despair (n) — the complete loss of hope. In despair; to despair of something. The opposite is hope.

B · Which word means…?

Three words: evidence · free will · resolve. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which phrase means to decide firmly to do something and to stick to it?
to resolve (v) — he resolved to change his habits. Noun: resolve = determination: her resolve never weakened. Not the same as to solve.
2. Which word means facts or signs that show something is true?
evidence (n, uncountable) — strong / weak / insufficient evidence; evidence for / against a claim. Never "an evidence" or "evidences". One piece: a piece of evidence.
3. Which phrase means the power to make your own choices, not controlled by fate or by forces outside you?
free will (n) — to act of your own free will; the problem of free will. The opposite idea is determinism.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. He called such a choice momentous: it could not be repeated and it would shape everything afterwards. Momentous means…
  (a) very quick   (b) rather boring   (c) very important, with serious effects
(c) — momentous (adj) — a momentous decision / occasion / day. Don't confuse with momentary (lasting a moment).
2. After a deep conversion, a person's whole way of seeing life can change. A conversion is…
  (a) a deep change of belief or way of life   (b) a trip to another country   (c) a long argument
(a) — conversion (n) / to convert (v). Often religious, but also used for any big change: a conversion to vegetarianism. Also a loft conversion — a building changed into something else.
3. He said we should judge a belief by its fruits — by what it produces in a person's life. Here, fruits means…
  (a) the costs   (b) the results and effects   (c) the foods
(b) — the fruits of something = the good results of effort: the fruits of her research / hard work. Usually plural and a little formal; it comes from a very old image of a tree and its fruit.

D · Word building & collocation

1. Build the nouns: to believe → a ___ · to prove → ___ (the thing that proves) · to decide → a ___.
a belief · proof · a decision. Watch the changes: believe → belief (v→f), prove → proof (v→f), decide → decision (-de → -sion).
2. One verb completes all three: to ___ a risk · to ___ a leap of faith · to ___ a chance.
to take — take a risk, take a chance, take a leap of faith. Not "do a risk" (a common error). Compare: make a decision, place a wager.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then a question to discuss.

Part 1 — The young man who could not move

William James was born in New York in 1842 into one of the most remarkable families in America. His brother Henry became a famous novelist. William was brilliant, curious and restless. He tried painting, then chemistry, then medicine, and in 1869 he finished his medical degree at Harvard.

Then he stopped. For almost three years he lived in his parents' house in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and he could do very little. His back hurt, his eyes hurt, his stomach hurt. He had panic attacks and long periods of despair. This was not simply bad luck. He had absorbed the science of his day, which said that the universe works like a giant machine: every event has a cause, and so every thought and every choice is just the result of earlier causes. If that was true, he asked himself, what was the point of trying to do anything?

Years later he described a night of terror in one of his books, as if it had happened to a stranger. He had seen the true meaning of his own helplessness, and it frightened him more than any illness.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to James after he finished his medical degree?

2. What idea from science made him so hopeless?

1. For almost three years he was ill and unable to work — back, eye and stomach pain, panic attacks and despair — and he stayed at his parents' house.

2. The idea that the universe is a machine in which every thought and choice is caused by earlier events, so that free will is an illusion and nothing he did mattered.

Discuss:
  • A belief about the world — not an event — was making him ill. Can what we believe about life affect our health? What examples do you know?

Part 2 — One sentence in a notebook

On 30 April 1870, James finished reading a book by the French philosopher Charles Renouvier. Renouvier defined free will in a simple way: it is the ability to keep a thought in your mind because you choose to, when you could have chosen other thoughts. James wrote in his notebook that he could see no reason why this should be an illusion. Then he made a decision.

"My first act of free will," he wrote, "shall be to believe in free will." He could not prove it. Nobody could. But he decided to act as if it were true, and to see what happened.

The recovery was slow and uneven, but it was real. He began to work on his habits, to take small actions on purpose and to notice that he could choose where to put his attention. Within two years he had a job teaching at Harvard. In 1875 he set up one of the first psychology laboratories in the United States, and in 1890 he published The Principles of Psychology, a book that shaped the subject for decades. Never had a single sentence in a diary done so much work.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Renouvier's definition of free will say, and what did James decide?

2. What happened to James afterwards?

1. Free will is keeping a thought because you choose to, when you could have other thoughts. James could find no reason to call it an illusion, so he decided to believe in it — and to act as if it were true.

2. Slowly he recovered, began to work with his habits and attention, got a teaching job at Harvard, set up one of America's first psychology laboratories (1875) and published The Principles of Psychology (1890).

Discuss:
  • He chose a belief he could not prove, and it changed his life. Was that a rational decision? What would a sceptic say?

Part 3 — The ship that sank, and the leap that didn't

In 1877 an English mathematician, W. K. Clifford, wrote a famous essay called "The Ethics of Belief". He told the story of a shipowner who knows his old ship is probably not safe. The owner pushes his doubts away, convinces himself that it will be fine, and the ship sails with a crowd of families on board. It sinks in the middle of the ocean. Clifford's point was hard to answer: the owner was guilty because he had not looked at the evidence. "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone," Clifford wrote, "to believe anything upon insufficient evidence."

James spent years thinking about this. In 1896 he gave a lecture, "The Will to Believe", in reply. Clifford was right, he said, for most questions — wherever the evidence is available, we must follow it. But some questions are different. He called a choice genuine when it is live (you could really believe either side), forced (you cannot avoid choosing, because not choosing is also a choice) and momentous (the choice is important and will not come again). When a question like that cannot be settled by evidence, he argued, it is not irrational to let our hopes and values decide.

He gave an example. A mountain climber reaches a gap he can only cross with one great jump. If he believes he can do it, he will probably succeed. If he doubts, he will hesitate and fall. Sometimes, James said, faith in a fact helps to create the fact. Later, in 1901 and 1902, he gave a series of lectures in Edinburgh on religious experience, treating it as a psychologist treats any human experience: carefully, without sneering, and by its fruits. He died in 1910. The argument between him and Clifford has never been settled.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Clifford's story about, and what was his rule?

2. When did James say it is acceptable to believe without proof?

1. A shipowner who ignored his doubts about an unsafe ship, and the ship sank with all on board. Rule: it is wrong, always and for everyone, to believe on insufficient evidence.

2. Only when the choice is genuine — live, forced and momentous — and the evidence cannot settle it. Where evidence is available we must follow it.

Discuss:
  • Clifford worried about the shipowner. James worried about the climber. Which danger is worse: believing too easily, or demanding too much proof?

Grammar — inversion for emphasis

When you start a sentence with a negative or restrictive adverbial, English reverses the word order: the auxiliary verb comes before the subject. It sounds serious, dramatic and a little formal — perfect for turning points.

The pattern: negative/limiting phrase + auxiliary (did / had / has / would / can) + subject + main verb.
• Never / Rarely / Seldom + auxiliary + subject: "Never had she seen such a view." · "Rarely do scientists admit they are wrong."
• Only after / Only when / Only then + auxiliary + subject: "Only after the exam did he relax."
• Not until + clause + auxiliary + subject: "Not until the lights went out did anyone notice the smell."
• Little + auxiliary + subject: "Little did they know how long the night would be."
• Not only… but also: "Not only did she win, she broke the record."
Three things to watch:
• If there is no auxiliary, add do / does / did: "Rarely did he complain" (not "Rarely complained he").
• After the auxiliary, the main verb goes back to its base form or participle: did he know (not "did he knew").
• In "only when / not until", the inversion is in the main clause, after the time phrase, not in the clause that starts with when/until.

1. Rewrite it

Rewrite each sentence starting with the words given. Say it, then reveal a model.

1. He had never felt so hopeless. → Never…
Never had he felt so hopeless.
2. He started to recover only when he chose to believe. → Only when…
Only when he chose to believe did he start to recover. The inversion is after the "only when" clause.
3. Clifford rarely accepted any belief without proof. → Rarely…
Rarely did Clifford accept any belief without proof. No auxiliary in the original, so add did; "accepted" goes back to "accept".
4. James did not understand the importance of the decision until years later. → Not until…
Not until years later did James understand the importance of the decision.

2. Choose the auxiliary

Complete each gap with the correct auxiliary verb.

1. Little he know that a few lines in his notebook would become famous.

2. Only after many months of effort he begin to feel better.

3. Never before the idea of free will seemed so urgent to him.

1. did   2. did   3. had

In 1 and 2 the verbs that follow are base forms (know, begin) — so the auxiliary must be "did". In 3 the verb is a participle (seemed) and the time is earlier than the story — so "had".

3. Error hunt

Each sentence has one mistake in the word order or the verb form. Find it, then reveal the correction.

1. "Never he had doubted so much."
Never had he doubted so much. The auxiliary goes before the subject.
2. "Only when he chose to believe did he began to recover."
…did he begin to recover. After "did", the main verb is a base form.
3. "Not only James changed his own life, but he also changed psychology."
Not only did James change his own life, but he also changed psychology. "Not only" starting a sentence needs inversion.

Two sides

A note on the record: the dates and quotations follow the standard accounts of James's notebook and lectures. This is a debate about how we should hold beliefs — not about whether any particular belief is true.

The argument in brief

Clifford's best argument. Say it in your own words, then reveal.
Beliefs are not private. They guide actions, and actions affect other people — the shipowner's wish to believe cost lives. So we have a duty to match our confidence to our evidence, every time. A habit of believing without proof makes everyone easier to deceive.
James's best argument. Say it in your own words, then reveal.
Some questions cannot wait for proof, and not choosing is still a choice. Refusing to believe is itself a bet — on the other side. Where the evidence cannot decide, and the result matters, it is human, and rational, to let our deepest values help to decide.

Is it a "genuine option"?

James's three tests: live (you could really believe either side), forced (you cannot avoid choosing) and momentous (it matters, and it will not come again). Decide for each situation: genuine or not? Say it, then reveal.

1. Whether to accept a job offer in another country, when the offer ends tomorrow and nobody can tell you how it will turn out.
Genuine. Live (both choices are real), forced (no answer is also an answer — the offer disappears) and momentous (it changes your life). The evidence cannot settle it in advance.
2. Whether a new diet is exactly right for everyone, when you could simply wait and read more studies.
Not genuine. It is not forced: you can wait. And more evidence is on its way, so Clifford's rule applies — follow the evidence.
3. Whether to trust a stranger who says the bridge ahead is safe, when you could easily walk around it.
Not genuine. There is a cheap, safe alternative, so it is not forced; and evidence is easy to get. This is the shipowner's mistake in miniature: believing when you could check.
4. Whether to trust that a friendship can survive a hard conversation, when you can only find out by having it.
Genuine. It is James's climber again: the trust you bring may help create the outcome you are trying to predict. No evidence can be collected without taking the risk.
The limits. James's critics point out that "I need to believe it" can excuse almost anything. His reply is built into the three tests: the argument works only for genuine options where evidence is unavailable. For everything else, he agreed with Clifford.
Talk it through:
  • Who is more dangerous: the person who believes too easily, or the person who never commits to anything without proof?
  • Is "I choose to believe it" a reason, or only a feeling? Does it matter?
  • Judge a belief by its fruits: is that a fair test? What could go wrong with it?
  • Can you choose a belief, or does a belief simply happen to you? How would James and Clifford each answer?
