The Stranger on the Train

We were told not to talk to strangers. This is the true story of the scientists who tested that rule on a Chicago train — and of what our acquaintances quietly do for our careers.

Today: you'll read how scientists tested a rule most of us learned as children — don't talk to strangers — and what they found. You'll also practise asking good questions, directly and politely: Where are you going? · Do you mind if I ask what you do?

Part 1 — The train into Chicago

Every weekday morning, trains carry thousands of commuters from the suburbs into the centre of Chicago. Most passengers do the same thing: they sit alone, look at their phones or out of the window, and say nothing. Two psychologists at the University of Chicago, Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder, wanted to know why. Was silence really what people wanted — or were they making a mistake?

At a station called Homewood, near the start of the line, research assistants stopped 118 commuters before they got on the train. Each person was given an envelope with a short survey and a $5 gift card, and one of three instructions. The first group was told: "Please have a conversation with a new person on the train today. Try to make a connection. Find out something interesting about him or her and tell them something about you." The second group was told to keep to themselves and enjoy their solitude. The third group was told to do what they normally did.

At the end of the journey, everyone filled in the survey. The people who had talked to a stranger reported the most positive commute of all three groups. And they did not say it was any less productive.

Then the researchers asked a different group of commuters to imagine the same three instructions. They predicted exactly the opposite: that sitting alone would be more pleasant than talking to a stranger. Another group guessed that fewer than half of other passengers would want to talk. In fact, in the experiment, every commuter in the talking group who returned the survey had managed to have a conversation — except a few who said nobody sat next to them. Not one person reported being rebuffed. The researchers saw the same pattern when they repeated the study on Chicago buses.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were the three instructions that the commuters received?

2. What did people predict — and what really happened?

1. Talk to a new person and try to make a connection; keep to yourself and enjoy the solitude; do what you normally do.

2. They predicted that solitude would be best and that people wouldn't want to talk. In reality, the talkers had the most positive commute, and nobody reported being rebuffed.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think people expect conversations with strangers to go badly? Where does that fear come from?

Part 2 — They liked you more than you think

Even after a good conversation, many of us go home and replay it. Did I talk too much? Was that joke stupid? She probably thought I was boring. In 2018, four psychologists — Erica Boothby, Gus Cooney, Gillian Sandstrom and Margaret Clark — decided to measure this feeling.

In the first study, they put two strangers together in a laboratory and asked them to talk for about five minutes. Afterwards, each person answered two kinds of question: How much did you like your partner? and How much do you think your partner liked you? Again and again, people underestimated how much the other person liked them and enjoyed their company. The researchers called this the liking gap.

They found the same gap in other places. First-year university students who were getting to know the people they lived with showed it — and in their case it lasted for months. Adults at public workshops in the UK called "How to Talk to Strangers" showed it too. Longer conversations, from a couple of minutes to three quarters of an hour, didn't make it go away.

Why does it happen? During a conversation, we are busy watching ourselves and criticising our own performance. Our partner, meanwhile, is busy doing exactly the same. So each of us notices our own awkward moments — and misses the other person's signals that they are enjoying it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What two questions did people answer after the five-minute conversation?

2. What is "the liking gap", and why does it happen?

1. How much they liked their partner, and how much they thought their partner liked them.

2. People underestimate how much the other person liked them. It happens because we are busy criticising our own performance and miss the other person's positive signals.

Discuss:
  • Think of a meeting, interview or first date that you replayed in your head afterwards. Do you think the liking gap was at work?

Part 3 — The strength of weak ties

Decades earlier, a young American sociologist, Mark Granovetter, had interviewed professional, technical and managerial workers in a suburb of Boston who had recently changed jobs. Many of them had found their new job through a personal contact. Granovetter asked a simple question: how often did you see that person at the time?

The answers surprised him. Only about one in six (16.7%) said they saw the contact often. More than half (55.6%) said occasionally, and more than a quarter (27.8%) said rarely — once a year or less. People often corrected him when he asked whether a friend had told them about the job: "Not a friend, an acquaintance." In 1973 he published his famous paper, The Strength of Weak Ties. His explanation: your close friends know the same people and hear the same news as you. Acquaintances move in different circles, so they bring you information you would never hear otherwise.

For almost fifty years, this was a famous idea that was hard to test properly. Then, in 2022, researchers from LinkedIn, MIT, Stanford and Harvard published a study in the journal Science. Between 2015 and 2019, LinkedIn had tested different versions of its "People You May Know" feature on more than 20 million users. Some people were shown more suggestions of distant contacts, others more suggestions of close ones. Over five years, these users made about two billion new connections and took 600,000 new jobs.

The results supported Granovetter — with a twist. Weaker ties helped people change jobs more than strong ties, but only up to a point. The most useful contacts were not complete strangers but moderately weak ties: people who shared some contacts with you, but not many. The researchers also found that weak ties helped most in more digital industries, while in less digital industries, strong ties were more useful.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Granovetter discover about the contacts who helped people find jobs?

2. What did the LinkedIn study add to his idea?

1. Most of them were people the job changers saw only occasionally or rarely — acquaintances, not close friends.

2. Weak ties did help more, but only up to a point: moderately weak ties were the most useful. And the effect depended on the industry.

Discuss:
  • Think about your own career. Who helped you most at important moments — close friends or acquaintances?

Grammar — asking questions: direct and indirect

Every conversation with a stranger starts with a question. Good questions need the right word order — and sometimes a softer, more polite form.

Direct questions
• (Question word) + auxiliary + subject + verb
• "Where do you work?" · "How long have you been commuting?" · "Did you enjoy the talk?"
• Subject questions have no auxiliary: "Who told you about the job?" (Who = the subject) — compare "Who did you tell?" (Who = the object)
• Follow-up questions keep a conversation going: "Really? What was that like?" · "How did you get into that?"
Indirect questions — softer and more polite
• Start with a phrase: Can you tell me… · Do you know… · I was wondering… · Do you mind me asking…
• Then use normal statement word order — no do / does / did:
  Where does she work? → "Do you know where she works?"
  Is this seat free? → "Could you tell me if / whether this seat is free?" (yes/no questions need if or whether)

Don't mix it up: ✗ Can you tell me where is the station? ✓ Can you tell me where the station is?

1. Put the words in the right order

Write each question. Then say it aloud.

1. you / do / how often / the train / take ? →

2. you / tell / could / me / the meeting / where / is ? →

3. job / your / who / you / about / told / current ? →

4. I / was / if / you / wondering / free / were / later →

1. How often do you take the train?   2. Could you tell me where the meeting is?   3. Who told you about your current job?   4. I was wondering if you were free later.
2 and 4 are indirect: statement order after the opening phrase. 3 is a subject question: no did.

2. Which question is correct? Choose a, b or c

1. (a) Do you know what time does the train leave?   (b) Do you know what time the train leaves?   (c) Do you know what time leaves the train?
(b) — indirect question: no does, statement order.
2. (a) Who did call you this morning?   (b) Who called you this morning?   (c) Who you called this morning?
(b) — Who is the subject, so there is no auxiliary.
3. (a) Can I ask where you are from?   (b) Can I ask where are you from?   (c) Can I ask where you from are?
(a) — after Can I ask, use statement order: you are.
4. (a) Could you tell me this seat is free?   (b) Could you tell me is this seat free?   (c) Could you tell me whether this seat is free?
(c) — yes/no questions need if or whether.

Going deeper

The studies in this story are real, and the numbers come from the researchers who ran them. Put together, they suggest that we are often wrong about other people — in a way that costs us.

Three big ideas from the story.

1. Our predictions about strangers are too negative. The Chicago commuters expected rejection and got none. They expected a worse journey and got a better one. Because most of us never test the prediction, we never find out that it was wrong — and the silence continues. The researchers also pointed out a limit: a train conversation is not a friendship, and they measured how people felt after one journey, not over years.

2. We judge ourselves more harshly than others judge us. The liking gap means that after a meeting, an interview or a first conversation, the other person probably enjoyed it more than you think. That matters at work: people who believe they made a bad impression are less likely to follow up, ask for help or start a collaboration.

3. Distant people bring new information. Close friends give support; acquaintances bring news from other worlds. Granovetter's idea, tested again with LinkedIn data fifty years later, suggests that a career is helped not only by the people you know best, but by the many people you know a little — though not by complete strangers, and not equally in every industry.
Talk it through:
  • Many parents teach children "Don't talk to strangers". Is that still good advice for children? When should it change?
  • Is "networking" just a business word for talking to strangers? Why do so many people hate it?
  • LinkedIn could change people's careers by changing the suggestions it showed them. How do you feel about a company running experiments like that on its users?
  • Does working from home reduce our weak ties? What could companies do about it?
