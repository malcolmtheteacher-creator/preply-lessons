Pitching Ideas Internally

C1 Level · Influence & Innovation

The Graveyard of Internal Ideas

Fact: Most good ideas in organizations never get traction. They're mentioned in a hallway, discussed briefly in a meeting, and disappear. Not because the ideas are bad — because they weren't pitched well. Pitching internal ideas is a skill most people never learn.

Why Ideas Get Killed

❌ No clear problem statement: "It would be cool if..." is not a pitch. No one understands why this matters.

❌ No concrete evidence: "I think customers would want this" is not data. Show evidence or lose credibility.

❌ No business case: "It's innovative" doesn't answer "What does it do for us?" Cost, benefit, timeline — these matter.

❌ Wrong audience or timing: Pitching a risky idea when the company is in cost-cutting mode, or pitching to someone with no power to act.

❌ You don't have a sponsor: Good ideas need someone senior to champion them. Without a sponsor, even great ideas die.

❌ Unclear ask: You spend time explaining the idea but never clearly state what you want someone to do about it.

The Innovation Bottleneck

Most organizations say they want innovation. But innovation is risky — it might fail. So when you pitch something new, you're asking someone to stick their neck out. You're also asking them to take resources away from something safe they're currently doing. That's a hard sell.

This is why the best internal pitches aren't "Here's a wild new idea." They're "Here's a small risk we can take to test if this opportunity is real."

Reflect: What's an idea you've had that never went anywhere? Why do you think it died? Was it the idea, or the way it was pitched?

Vocabulary: Pitch & Innovation Language

ROI
Return on Investment — the profit or benefit gained from an investment relative to its cost
Value proposition
The specific benefit or advantage offered by a product, service, or idea
Scalable
Able to grow or expand efficiently without losing effectiveness
Pilot
A small-scale test of an idea or product before full rollout
Proof of concept
Demonstrating that an idea is feasible through evidence or a small test
Iterate
To repeat a process with changes; to improve something through cycles of feedback and refinement
Champion
A person who advocates for and supports a project or idea strongly
Sponsor
A senior person with authority who funds and supports a project or initiative

The One-Page Pitch Framework

A structured way to pitch ideas internally that actually gets traction:

1. Problem (2 sentences max)

State the specific problem you're solving. Be concrete: "Customers abandon checkout after 3 minutes if they can't add a promo code. We're losing 15% of revenue because of this."

2. Solution (2-3 sentences)

Describe your solution simply. "Add promo code field to cart screen" is not a solution. "Redesign checkout flow to show recommended promotions upfront, increasing perceived value and reducing abandonment" is a solution.

3. Evidence (data or precedent)

Why should they believe this will work? Market research? Customer feedback? Competitor examples? "3 of our top 5 customers asked for this" is evidence. "I think customers would want this" is not.

4. Ask (be specific)

What exactly do you want? Budget approval? Headcount? Time? "We'd like to run a 2-week pilot with 10% of traffic to validate the approach" is clear. "I'd like some resources to explore this" is vague.

5. Impact (if approved)

What happens if they say yes? "If we implement this, we expect to reduce checkout abandonment by 8%, which is $2M revenue upside annually." Don't overstate — be conservative. Exceeding expectations builds credibility.

Pitching to Different People

CFO/Finance: Lead with ROI. "Cost $100k, expected benefit $500k annually." They think in financial terms.

CEO/Product: Lead with market impact. "This opens a new customer segment worth $10M" or "Competitors are already doing this."

Your peer: Lead with how this helps them. "This would unblock the Q3 roadmap." Or "This would reduce your support burden."

Your boss: Lead with how this helps them look good. "This directly addresses the CEO's priority around retention."

Your Task: Build and Present a 3-Minute Pitch

10:00

The Challenge

Pick an improvement idea for your company (real or imagined). It could be: a process improvement, a new product feature, a cost savings initiative, a team or structural change, or a market opportunity.

Then prepare a 3-minute pitch using the framework above.

Pitch Preparation Checklist

Model Pitch Example

Problem: "Our support team spends 40% of their time answering repetitive questions about billing. These are questions our customers can answer themselves in documentation. The cost to support is about $500k annually."

Solution: "Create a self-service billing FAQ with embedded video tutorials. Train support to redirect common questions there. Track reduction in support tickets."

Evidence: "We surveyed 50 customers last month — 70% said they'd prefer to self-serve for billing questions. Stripe did something similar and reduced support volume by 30%."

Ask: "I'm requesting 80 hours of engineering time (2 weeks) to build this. No budget required — we have the tools and expertise internally."

Impact: "If we reduce support volume by even 20%, we save $100k annually in labor. The payback is immediate."

Sponsor: "I'd like to run this by [VP of Support] — I think this aligns with their cost reduction goals."

What makes this good: Specific problem with cost attached. Clear solution. Evidence from customers and competitors. No budget needed (lower barrier). Immediate ROI. Identified sponsor. All in under 2 minutes.

Finding Sponsors and Timing Your Pitch

The Sponsor Paradox

Good ideas need sponsors. But you can't get a sponsor without a good pitch. So: start by pitching to peers and mentors who can give you feedback. Refine your pitch. Then pitch to a potential sponsor who has both authority and motivation.

Timing Your Pitch

Bad timing: "We should innovate on customer experience" during a hiring freeze or cost-cutting initiative.

Good timing: Link your pitch to current priorities. "This directly supports the CEO's focus on retention" or "This solves the problem we identified in the last board meeting."

Best timing: When the decision-maker has just publicly committed to something your idea supports.

Handling Rejection

Not every pitch gets approved. That's fine. Good ideas often need multiple iterations. If your pitch is rejected:

  • Ask why (genuine curiosity, not defensiveness)
  • Understand the real objection — it's often not what they say first
  • Come back in 3-6 months with more evidence, better timing, or a smaller ask
  • Don't give up — most big innovations face initial rejection

The Pilot Advantage

Instead of pitching "Let's do this," pitch "Let's test this in a limited way." Pilots are lower risk. The decision-maker can say yes more easily. And if the pilot works, you have proof for the bigger rollout.

Reflection questions:

  • What's an idea you believe in but haven't pitched yet? What's holding you back?
  • Who is a potential sponsor for your idea — someone senior who would benefit if it worked?
  • How could you test your idea on a small scale first?
