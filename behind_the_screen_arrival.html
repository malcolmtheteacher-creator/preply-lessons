BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Arrival

Twelve alien ships appear, and a linguist starts to live her own future as though she's remembering it. Learning a new language really can change how you think — the film just takes that idea much further.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Arrival — a linguist who learns an alien language and begins to experience her own future as memory — discover how much of the science behind it is real, and master the tense English uses to tell a foreseen future as though it were already the past: would, plus was going to and was to.
Before we begin — talk it through: If you could know your whole future today — every joy and every loss — would you want to? Do you think the language you speak changes the way you think, even a little? And how do you imagine we'd first try to talk to an alien species?

The words for today's story

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

6. profound is closest in meaning to: (a) trivial   (b) extremely deep and significant   (c) quick and easy

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. A strong chess player can often foresee her opponent's next three moves before he even touches a piece.
To foresee something is: (a) to prevent it happening; (b) to know or realise it will happen before it does; (c) to forget about it completely.

D. Infer from context — two bigger ideas

a threshold (n) — literally, a doorway; more broadly, the point at which one stage ends and a new one begins.
10. Scientists still debate exactly which animals are sentient — capable of feeling, perceiving and experiencing the world around them, rather than simply reacting like a machine.

The film, in three parts

Denis Villeneuve's Arrival (2016), based on Ted Chiang's novella Story of Your Life. Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — Twelve ships, one language

Twelve enormous alien ships — smooth, dark, shell-shaped — appear without warning, hovering silently above scattered sites around the world. Governments panic; nobody knows why the visitors have come or what they want. The US Army recruits Dr Louise Banks, a linguist who has worked with them before, to try to open communication, pairing her with physicist Ian Donnelly.

Inside the ship, behind a transparent barrier, Louise and Ian meet two enormous creatures they nickname "Abbott" and "Costello" — heptapods, with seven limbs radiating from a bulbous body. The heptapods don't speak the way humans do; instead, they spray circular, ink-like symbols into the air, a written language entirely separate from their spoken sounds. Painstakingly, session by session, Louise begins to learn to read and write it.

As she absorbs more of the language, Louise starts having vivid, disorienting flashes — of a little girl she doesn't recognise, sick, laughing, growing up. She assumes they are memories. She is wrong.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is recruited to communicate with the aliens, and how do the heptapods "speak"?

2. What strange experience does Louise begin to have as she learns the language?

1. Linguist Louise Banks, working with physicist Ian Donnelly; the heptapods communicate through circular written symbols rather than spoken words.

2. She starts having vivid flashes of a young girl's life, which she assumes are old memories.

Discuss:
  • Before any word has been exchanged, twelve nations react to the ships with fear rather than curiosity. Is that a realistic first reaction to the unknown, or a failure of imagination?

Part 2 — The world edges toward war

As different countries translate the heptapods' message independently, a single ambiguous phrase is rendered by China as "offer weapon" and by other nations as something closer to "use tool." Trust between governments collapses; China and Russia sever their data feeds to the rest of the world, and General Shang orders his forces to prepare a strike against the ship on Chinese soil. A group of jumpy American soldiers, convinced the aliens are a threat, plants a bomb inside the ship itself.

Louise's flashes of the girl grow longer and more emotionally overwhelming, bleeding into her waking hours until they threaten to derail the mission entirely. She still cannot explain them. Meanwhile, the clock is running out: without agreement between nations on what the heptapods actually want, humanity is sliding, almost by accident, toward a war nobody has chosen.

Under enormous pressure, and now able to read the heptapods' circular script fluently, Louise confronts "Costello" directly and demands a clearer answer: why are they here, and why now?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does the ambiguous translation of the aliens' message push the world toward war?

2. What is happening to Louise as the crisis builds?

1. A key phrase is translated as "offer weapon" by China rather than something closer to "use tool," so nations stop cooperating and prepare for military action.

2. Her flashes of the unknown girl are intensifying and intruding into her waking life, though she still doesn't understand what they mean.

Discuss:
  • A single mistranslated phrase nearly starts a war between nations. How much of real international conflict do you think comes down to simple misunderstanding rather than genuine disagreement?

Part 3 — A future remembered

The heptapods finally explain themselves: they have come to give humanity their language as a gift, because in three thousand years' time, they will need humanity's help in return. Learning to think in their non-linear script, Louise realises, has begun to unlock a non-linear way of experiencing time itself — she isn't remembering the past in her flashes; she is living moments of her own future as though they were memory.

With war just hours away, Louise uses a piece of future knowledge — a private phone number, and a dying woman's last words that General Shang will one day tell her himself — to call the general directly and repeat his own wife's words back to him. Moved and shaken, Shang stands his forces down, and the other nations follow, averting the war.

Only then does Louise fully grasp what she has learned to see: she will marry Ian, have a daughter named Hannah, and Hannah will die young of an illness with no cure. She also understands that Ian, once he discovers Louise always knew this would happen, will eventually leave her over it. Knowing everything that is coming — every joy and every loss — Louise chooses to live it all anyway.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the heptapods really come to Earth, and how does Louise prevent the war?

2. What does Louise finally understand about her own future?

1. They came to give humanity their language, because they will need humanity's help in three thousand years; Louise uses knowledge from her own future to call General Shang and quote his late wife's words, persuading him to stand down.

2. She will marry Ian and have a daughter, Hannah, who will die young; Ian will eventually leave once he learns Louise always knew. She chooses to live it anyway.

Discuss:
  • Louise chooses a future she knows will bring her devastating grief, because it also brings her joy she wouldn't otherwise have. Would you make the same choice?

Grammar — future-in-the-past (would)

No film needs this structure more than Arrival. English lets you stand at a point in the past and describe what was, at that time, still in the future — telling a foreseen future as though it were already a memory. That is exactly how Louise experiences her own life once she learns the heptapods' language.

The core structure:
• would + base verb — the "future in the past": something that, from an earlier point in time, was still going to happen. "Louise didn't know it yet, but this project would change the rest of her life." "She would have a daughter. She would call her Hannah. She would lose her."
• was/were going to + base verb — a plan or intention that existed at that past point (sometimes later interrupted or changed): "The generals were going to launch a strike within days."
• was/were to + base verb — a more formal, almost fated version, often used for official arrangements or for events that, looking back, feel inevitable: "Within a year, this quiet linguist was to become the woman who prevented a war."
Feel the difference:
• would = the natural, personal way of narrating a foreseen future as memory — exactly Louise's experience of time.
• was going to = a plan or intention already in motion at that past moment.
• was to = a more formal or documentary tone, often implying destiny or an official arrangement.

The Real Story — does language really shape thought?

Arrival is built on a real, century-old idea in linguistics — pushed, by the end of the film, a long way past what any real linguist would claim.

🔤 The idea has a name — and a messier history than the film suggests

The theory is usually called the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, or linguistic relativity: the idea that the language you speak shapes, to some degree, how you think and perceive the world. It's named after the American linguist Edward Sapir and his student Benjamin Lee Whorf — but, tellingly, neither man ever actually published a joint "hypothesis" together. The label was applied by later scholars, gathering their separate writings under one name only after Sapir's death in 1939.

Two versions — one abandoned, one still standing

There's a strong version: that language doesn't just influence thought but entirely determines and limits it — that you literally cannot think a thought your language has no words for. This version, often called linguistic determinism, is largely rejected by modern linguists. Multilingual speakers can hold and translate ideas across languages that supposedly shouldn't be able to contain them, which is hard to square with a strict, deterministic reading.

Then there's a weak version: that language influences habitual thought — the routine, everyday ways people notice, categorise and remember the world — in real but modest and measurable ways, without ever controlling what a person is fundamentally capable of thinking. This weaker claim is the one most linguists today take seriously, and it has genuine experimental support behind it.

Where the real evidence actually is

In studies of colour terms, speakers of languages with fewer basic colour categories (such as Berinmo, spoken in Papua New Guinea) have shown measurably different speed and accuracy at distinguishing certain shades compared with English speakers, whose language slices the colour spectrum differently. In studies of time, the psychologist Lera Boroditsky found that Mandarin speakers, who often describe time using vertical terms, tend to reason about time vertically more readily than English speakers, who typically use horizontal, left-to-right terms. And in languages with grammatical gender — where nouns are masculine or feminine — speakers have been shown to describe the same object with subtly different adjectives depending on its grammatical gender in their language.

None of that lets anyone glimpse the future. Arrival takes this real, if modest and still debated, idea and pushes it to a fictional extreme: that mastering a truly alien, non-linear language could let a person perceive time non-linearly too. Ted Chiang, whose 1998 novella Story of Your Life the film adapts, wrote it to explore what it would mean to live a whole life knowing everything that was coming — a thought experiment, not a scientific claim. Linguists who reviewed the film generally praised its unusually careful depiction of how field linguistics actually works, while noting plainly that its central premise is a leap of imagination well beyond the real hypothesis.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, did you already believe your own language shapes how you think? Has anything here changed your view?
  • The strong version of the idea is rejected, but the weak version has real evidence. Why do you think the strong, more dramatic version is the one most people have heard of?
  • Does knowing the science is only modestly real make the film's central idea more interesting, or does it deflate the magic?
