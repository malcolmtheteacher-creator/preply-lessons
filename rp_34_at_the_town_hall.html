At the Town Hall

A new town, a legal deadline, and a building full of numbered rooms that all close at different times. Somewhere in here is a stamp with your name on it. Between you and it: one form, one queue, and one document you don't have.

The Scene 🎬

Nobody's first month in a new country is complete without one morning like this.

You moved here three weeks ago, and the rule says new residents must register at the town hall within thirty days. Today is day twenty-two. You've brought your passport, your rental contract, two printed photos and a folder of hope.

Behind the desk is Mrs Adeyemi, who has worked in this building for nineteen years and knows things no website says: which rooms actually handle what, which of the listed documents are really required, and the difference between a problem and a Tuesday. Officials like her run on a simple economy — clear questions in, clear answers out. Vague hoping gets you nothing; blaming gets you less.

The skill of this morning isn't paperwork. It's extraction: getting the process, the requirements and the fallback rules out of the one person who knows them — and writing them down, because the second visit is where unwritten answers go to die.

The Conversation 💬

Ground floor, desk 3, a ticket that took forty minutes to be called.

Mrs AdeyemiNumber eighty-four?
YouThat's me. Good morning. I was told I need to register as a new resident — is this the right desk?
Mrs AdeyemiRegistration, yes — you're in the right place, which puts you ahead of half of this queue. New to the town?
YouThree weeks. And this is all quite new to me — what happens first?
Mrs AdeyemiFirst I check your documents, then you fill in the residence form, then I enter you in the system and you get your certificate. Ten minutes, if the folder's in order.
YouLet's find out. Which documents do you actually need from me?
Mrs AdeyemiPassport. Rental contract. And the landlord confirmation — the signed one-page form saying you genuinely live there.
YouAh. I've got everything except the landlord confirmation — is that a problem today?
Mrs AdeyemiHm. It's the one I'm not allowed to wave through. The contract shows you rented it; the confirmation shows you actually moved in. Different things, in the eyes of this building.
YouI see. Could I bring that one in later, or do I have to start again from the ticket machine?
Mrs AdeyemiYou're in luck — I can open your file today, mark it incomplete, and you bring the confirmation within two weeks. No new ticket: incomplete files come straight to this desk.
YouThat's a relief. Just so I've understood: you open the file now, I get the landlord's signature, then come straight back to you — within two weeks?
Mrs AdeyemiExactly so. Blank confirmation forms are in Room 11, first floor — take two, landlords spoil them. And your certificate needs the fee paid at the cash office, Room 6, before I can print it.
YouThat's three rooms and a fortnight — could you jot that down for me? Names and room numbers, so I don't invent my own version on the stairs.
Mrs Adeyemi*writing* Room 11, forms. Room 6, fee — eighteen euros, card is fine. Then desk 3. There.
YouPerfect. And who should I ask for when I come back?
Mrs AdeyemiAdeyemi. Mornings, not Wednesdays. If it's a Wednesday you'll get Mr Brandt, and you'll want your folder in alphabetical order for Mr Brandt.
YouNoted, and slightly feared. Last thing — is there anything else that'll trip me up next time? Anything people usually get wrong?
Mrs AdeyemiTwo things. The landlord must sign in ink — we reject photos of signatures. And check your name matches your passport exactly on every form. Accents included. That one costs people a third visit.
YouInk signature, exact name, accents included. You've made this much less painful than I expected — thank you.
Mrs AdeyemiBring me a complete folder and I'll make it painless. Next — eighty-five?

The Echo 🕰️

In the middle of Beijing stands a palace of nine thousand rooms where, for five centuries, the world's most elaborate bureaucracy decided who could enter, stand where, wear what, and say which words to whom. They called it the Forbidden City, and the name was administration, not poetry: gate after gate, permit after permit, each courtyard forbidden to all but the correctly stamped.

An empire of over a million officials ran on its paperwork — examinations to enter, ranks and robes and seals, forms in triplicate five hundred years before the photocopier. It worked, mostly, because the system outlived every individual in it. And it baffled visitors then for the same reason town halls baffle new arrivals now: the building knows its rules, and doesn't explain them at the gate.

Every town hall is a very small Forbidden City. The difference is that you're allowed to ask — and one desk clerk who's worked the gates for nineteen years will tell you more than the architecture ever will.
