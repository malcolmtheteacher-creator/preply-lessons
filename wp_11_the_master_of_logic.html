The Master of Logic

In which Plato’s greatest student walks out of the Academy, looks at the world rather than away from it, and invents the science of correct thinking

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet Aristotle of Stagira (384–322 BC), the student who dared to contradict his teacher, the tutor to a teenage warrior-king, and the inventor of formal logic. In a single move, he brings philosophy down to earth — asking not “what lies beyond the physical world?” but “how does this world actually work?” You will learn the syllogism, trace the origins of science itself, and begin to sense the price of turning away from Plato’s sky.

A Question to Hold

Plato looked away from the world. He said the real truth lay in the realm of Forms, beyond the senses. His student watched this for twenty years at the Academy.

Then Aristotle looked back.

He said: the real world — the world you can see and touch and observe — is where the answers are. Not in some realm of perfect, eternal Forms, but here, in particular things, in the way they change, in their purposes, in their causes.

This was not rejection of Plato. It was a turn. And that turn — from the eternal and universal toward the physical and particular — is what allows science to be born. Before you can do science, you have to believe that nature, as it actually appears to us, is worth understanding.

II. The Story — From Stagira to the Lyceum

Aristotle’s father was a physician. Even his childhood was different from Plato’s. Where Plato came from an aristocratic Athenian family steeped in politics, Aristotle came from the north, from Stagira in Macedonia, the son of a man who studied bodies and their ailments. This detail matters. Aristotle inherited a different kind of curiosity: not about abstract justice or beauty, but about how things work.

The Student (367–347 BC)

At seventeen, Aristotle came to Athens and joined Plato’s Academy. For twenty years, he remained. Plato valued him so highly that he called him the mind of the Academy. But something was growing in Aristotle as he listened to Plato discourse on the Forms: a doubt.

Plato is a friend, but truth is a greater friend. — Aristotle (traditional saying; source unknown)

When Plato died in 347 BC, Aristotle did not take over the Academy. Instead, he left Athens. The Athenians, still bitter about the city’s defeat by Sparta, would not have welcomed a Macedonian. Aristotle traveled through Asia Minor, studying, teaching, observing. He married the niece of Hermias, a local ruler. In 343 BC, at the age of forty-one, he received a letter from Philip of Macedon.

The Tutor (343–335 BC)

Philip wanted a tutor for his son. The boy was thirteen. His name was Alexander. Aristotle agreed, and for the next eight years, he served as teacher to the man who would soon try to conquer the world. What did Aristotle teach him? We don’t know for certain. But we know this: Alexander carried a copy of Homer’s Iliad with him everywhere, revised by Aristotle’s own hand. The scholar and the warrior were not so far apart.

A strange intimacy: Imagine sitting across from the teenage boy who will become Alexander the Great, teaching him rhetoric, ethics, natural philosophy. Imagine believing you were preparing him to be wise. Then watch him conquer Persia, Egypt, and India. In a sense, Aristotle had helped create the very force that would spread Greek culture across the known world. But did Alexander remain the student Aristotle taught, or did he become something Aristotle never intended? This question haunts all teaching.

When Alexander became king in 336 BC, he did not forget his teacher. He gave Aristotle money, land, and students. And in 335 BC, back in Athens now that Alexander held the city under his control, Aristotle founded his own school: the Lyceum.

The Founder (335–323 BC)

The Lyceum was different from the Academy. Plato’s school had been a place of pure inquiry, of dialogue, of the ascent toward the Forms. Aristotle’s school was a research institute. He and his students collected specimens of plants and animals. They studied the constitutions of Greek city-states. They catalogued knowledge. They observed the world and tried to sort it, to find principles, to build systems.

Aristotle himself was a man of walking and talking. He would teach while strolling through the gardens — so much so that his followers were called the Peripatetics, the walkers. He looked at nature as he walked. He paid attention. He asked: what is this thing? How does it work? What is it for?

The Organon: The Science of Correct Thinking

Of all Aristotle’s inventions, the most important may be his discovery of logic. Not the feeling of rightness that comes from intuition, not the poetic vision that comes from inspiration, but the science of correct reasoning. Aristotle showed that reasoning follows laws, just as nature does. And if you can master those laws, you can solve problems that the unaided mind cannot solve.

The syllogism is the simplest example:

All men are mortal. Socrates is a man. Therefore, Socrates is mortal.

What’s remarkable is not the conclusion (we could guess that). It’s the form. Aristotle showed that this form of reasoning is always true, no matter what you fill in for “all men” and “Socrates.” Reasoning has a structure. And if you understand the structure, you understand how to tell true arguments from false ones.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Categories

Before you can reason about something, you have to be able to describe it. And before you can describe it, you need to know what categories exist. Aristotle proposed ten:

1. Substance — what something is (a man, a horse, a stone)

2. Quantity — how much (three meters, five pounds)

3. Quality — what it’s like (white, hot, bitter)

4. Relation — how it compares (larger than, similar to, different from)

5. Place — where it is (in Athens, in the corner)

6. Time — when it exists (yesterday, at noon, for three hours)

7. Position — how it’s arranged (standing, lying down, facing north)

8. State/Condition — its condition (shod, armed, being-healed)

9. Action — what it does (cutting, burning, heating)

10. Affection — what is done to it (being cut, being heated, being wounded)

These ten categories are not merely clever. They are a map of what exists. Take any object — this book, your hand, the sun — and you can describe it completely using these ten categories. This is Aristotle’s gift to the West: a way of organizing knowledge itself.

The Death

When Alexander died in 323 BC, the political order changed. The Athenians, eager to be rid of Macedonian rule, turned on those connected to it. Aristotle, who had been Alexander’s teacher, was no longer safe. He left Athens, saying (according to the story) that he would not let Athens sin twice against philosophy — a reference to the execution of Socrates, whom Aristotle had never met but whom Plato had loved.

He died a year later, in 322 BC, on the island of Euboea. He was sixty-two years old. He had been a student for twenty years, a tutor for eight, a founder and director for thirteen. In those thirteen years, he had not merely written; he had invented disciplines. Logic. Metaphysics. Biology. Political science. Ethics. Aesthetics. He had looked at the world and said: it is knowable. And that belief changed everything.

III. The Ideas — What Aristotle Left Unresolved

Aristotle was an empiricist. He believed that knowledge comes from the senses, from observation, from looking carefully at how the world actually behaves. This is one of the most powerful ideas in the history of philosophy. It is also incomplete. Let’s trace the three ideas that define his thought — and the gaps they leave behind.

Idea 1: Empiricism

Knowledge begins with sense experience.

Where Plato said the senses deceive us and we must turn away from the physical world toward eternal Forms, Aristotle said: no. The senses are your primary contact with reality. Yes, they can be mistaken. Yes, they show us change and multiplicity. But that is not a reason to dismiss them. That is where you must begin.

This idea gave birth to what would eventually become science. Before you can discover the laws of nature, you have to believe that nature, as it appears, is real and worth investigating.

Idea 2: Formal Logic

Reasoning follows laws that can be studied in themselves.

The syllogism, the categories, the laws of contradiction — these show that reasoning is not merely an art or a talent. It is a science. You can go wrong in reasoning, and there are objective reasons why you go wrong. You can go right, and there are objective reasons why you go right.

This was revolutionary. It meant that philosophy itself could be systematic. You could not merely have intuitions or visions. You had to follow the rules of correct reasoning. And if you did, you could reach truth.

Idea 3: Teleology

Everything that exists has a purpose.

For Aristotle, the fundamental question about any object is not “what is it made of?” but “what is it for?” A knife is for cutting. An eye is for seeing. A human being is for a particular kind of flourishing. This purpose is not imposed from outside; it is built into the nature of the thing itself.

This explains change and motion in a way the Pre-Socratics could not. Things change because they are moving toward their purpose, their end state (telos). An acorn becomes an oak tree. A child becomes an adult. Things are not static; they are in motion toward their natural completion.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Gap: The Unmoved Mover

But here is what Aristotle could not solve. If everything that is in motion must be moved by something else, then either:

Think about it: There is an infinite regress of causes, or there is a first cause that moves all things without itself being moved. Aristotle chose the second. He called it the Unmoved Mover. But here is the problem…

The Unmoved Mover does not love us. It does not know us. It does not even know that we exist. It is pure thought thinking itself, eternally absorbed in its own perfection. And yet it is the cause of all motion, all purpose, all meaning in the cosmos. Everything moves because it is attracted to the perfection of this Unmoved Mover — like a beloved attracts the lover. But the beloved does not have to love back. The beloved does not even have to notice.

This is Aristotle’s deepest unresolved tension: a universe that is purposeful but whose ultimate principle is utterly indifferent. A cosmos that aims at perfection, but whose final cause cares nothing for any of us.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

The Turn

Aristotle disagreed with his teacher Plato on something fundamental: where does truth live? In the realm of Forms, or in the physical world? What does it take to stand against your teacher?

The Tutor and the Conqueror

Aristotle taught Alexander. Did his teaching make Alexander wise? Or did Alexander become something Aristotle never intended? What is a teacher responsible for?

The Syllogism as Power

Aristotle invented formal logic. Is logic a tool for finding truth, or is it a tool for winning arguments? Can it be both? When is logic enough?

Purpose Without Love

Aristotle said everything has a purpose (telos). But the highest cause — the Unmoved Mover — does not care about any of that purpose. Does a purposeful universe require a caring God?

Observation and Wonder

Aristotle looked at the world carefully. He observed. He collected specimens. Does this kind of close observation bring you closer to wonder, or does it destroy wonder?

Categories

Aristotle’s ten categories claim to describe all possible ways of existing. Do they? Can you think of something that doesn’t fit into his system?

A Pattern Emerges

Notice something: Aristotle brings philosophy down to earth. He insists on observation, on the physical world, on logic as a tool we can master. This is powerful. But it leaves a gap. The Unmoved Mover does not love us. The categories describe the world as it is, but tell us nothing about how to be. The next generation of philosophers will try to fill this gap. Some with reason, some with virtue, some with something that reason alone cannot reach.
