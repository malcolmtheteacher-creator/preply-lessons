Is Luck Fair?

If success depends on talent, timing and family as well as effort, who really deserves what they have?

← All the Big Questions

Today: you'll explain how luck complicates ideas of deserving and fairness, and you'll master the inverted structures that make an important point sound forceful.
Warm up — have a go, and don't worry about mistakes. Say whatever comes to mind.

  • If two people work equally hard and one earns far more, is that fair?
  • Does it change your view of a successful person if you learn how much help they had?

The ideas, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The lottery of birth

In 1971 the American philosopher John Rawls published A Theory of Justice, which began with an experiment. Imagine you must choose the rules of a society without knowing what position you would hold in it: rich or poor, strong or weak, clever or not, of any gender or background. Rawls called this the veil of ignorance.

Behind the veil, he argued, you would choose rules that protect the worst-off, because you might be one of them. Rawls's point was that most of what we have comes from the natural lottery: our talents, our families and the time and place of our birth. None of these is earned.

The facts support the idea that circumstances matter a great deal. Economists who study global inequality have estimated that where a person is born explains a very large share of what they will earn in life. Even before effort begins, the starting lines are far apart.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the veil of ignorance?

2. What did Rawls mean by the natural lottery?

1. An imagined situation in which you choose society's rules without knowing your own position in it.

2. That our talents, families and place and time of birth come to us by chance and are not earned.

Discuss:
  • Behind the veil of ignorance, what rule would you insist on, and why?

Part 2 — Moral luck

The philosophers Bernard Williams and Thomas Nagel went further, in essays from the 1970s, and asked whether even our moral standing depends on luck. Take two drivers who are equally careless. Both glance at a phone while driving. One of them is unlucky: a child runs into the road, and the child is killed. The other gets home safely. Almost everyone judges the first driver far more harshly.

Yet the two did exactly the same thing. The only difference is something neither of them controlled. Philosophers call this moral luck. It challenges a principle that goes back to Kant: that we can be fairly judged only for what is under our control.

If that principle were applied strictly, hardly anyone could be praised or blamed, because almost everything we do depends on temperament, upbringing and circumstance. But if it is rejected, we seem to accept that fairness is not the only thing that counts. Nagel himself thought that the problem had no neat solution.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Describe the two careless drivers.

2. What principle does moral luck challenge?

1. Both glance at a phone; a child runs out in front of one and is killed, while the other arrives safely, and yet the first is judged much more harshly.

2. That people can be fairly judged only for what they control.

Discuss:
  • Should the law punish the unlucky driver more than the lucky one? What does your answer imply?

Part 3 — The tyranny of merit

The word meritocracy was invented as a warning. In 1958 the British sociologist Michael Young published a satire, The Rise of the Meritocracy, imagining a society that rewards cleverness and effort so completely that the winners become arrogant and the losers lose all dignity.

In 2020 the American philosopher Michael Sandel made a similar argument in The Tyranny of Merit. If we believe that success is entirely deserved, he suggested, then the successful lose humility and the unsuccessful are told that they deserve their failure. A little recognition of luck might make us kinder and more willing to share.

There are strong replies. People do work, sometimes at great cost, and a society where effort counted for nothing would have little reason to try. Perhaps the right view is the one in between: effort matters, luck matters more than we admit, and the honest response to both is gratitude rather than pride.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the word meritocracy invented?

2. What does Sandel say is the danger of believing that success is fully earned?

1. As a warning, in a satire by Michael Young about a society whose winners become arrogant and whose losers lose their dignity.

2. That winners lose humility and losers are told they deserve their failure.

Discuss:
  • Is it possible to believe both that effort matters and that luck matters more than we admit? What would that change in practice?

Grammar — inversion for emphasis

For a serious point, a speaker can change the usual word order. Putting a negative or limiting expression first, and then inverting the subject and the auxiliary verb, makes a sentence sound formal and forceful.

Three structures:
• Negative adverbial + auxiliary + subject + verb — "Never have I seen such a gap." "Rarely do winners think about the starting line." "Not only did she work hard, but she was also lucky." "Little did he realise how lucky he was."
• No sooner / Hardly + past perfect — "No sooner had she paid the fee than the grant was withdrawn." "Hardly had he arrived when the doors closed."
• Had + subject + past participle (conditional) — "Had it not been for the scholarship, he would never have studied." "Had she known, she would have applied."
Feel the difference:
• The inversion follows the question order: auxiliary, then subject.
• If there is no auxiliary, do / does / did is added: "Not only did she win…"
• Had it not been for… replaces If it had not been for… and belongs mostly to formal or written English.

Deserved or lucky?

Five people who have succeeded or suffered. Say what is earned and what is luck, and which kind of luck it is, then reveal.

1. An athlete born with a rare physical gift trains every day for twenty years.
Natural lottery. The talent was luck, but the training was effort. Rawls would say that even the ability to make an effort may be partly luck.
2. A founder's idea meets a market a month after launch, just as a law changes in its favour.
Timing luck. Nothing about the idea changed, but the circumstances did. This is the luck we find hardest to see in success stories.
3. Two equally careless drivers; only one hits a child.
Moral luck. The outcome was luck, and it changes the way everyone judges them, which is the puzzle Nagel and Williams described.
4. A student from a family of teachers does well in exams.
Circumstantial luck. Books, quiet, expectations and help at home are advantages the student did not earn, though she may still work hard.
5. A person wins a large prize in a lottery.
Pure luck. They are entitled to the prize under the rules, but no one thinks they deserve it. The case separates being entitled from deserving.

A thought experiment

Imagine this:
  • You are behind the veil of ignorance and must choose between two societies: one where the top earns one hundred times the bottom, and one where it is five times. You do not know where you will be. Which do you choose, and what does your answer assume?
  • What would change about the way you talk about your own successes if you accepted that a large part of them was luck?
