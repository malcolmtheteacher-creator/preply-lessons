Can You Trust Your Memory?

People watch a car accident. One word in a question changes what they remember.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The film

In 1974, a scientist called Elizabeth Loftus did an experiment in the USA. Students watched short films of car accidents. After each film, the scientists asked questions.

One question was: 'How fast were the cars going?' But there was a trick. For some students, the question used the word 'hit'. For other students, the question used the word 'smashed'. Everything else was the same.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the students watch?

2. What was the trick?

1. Short films of car accidents.

2. The question used a different word: 'hit' or 'smashed'.

Discuss:
  • Would one word change your memory? What do you think?

Part 2 — The result

The students who heard 'smashed' said the cars were going faster. They said about 41 miles an hour. The students who heard 'hit' said about 34 miles an hour. It was the same film!

One week later, the students came back. The scientists asked, 'Did you see any broken glass?' There was no broken glass in the film. But about one third of the 'smashed' students said yes. Only about one in seven of the 'hit' students said yes.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What speed did the 'smashed' group say?

2. What did the students say about broken glass a week later?

1. About 41 miles an hour; the 'hit' group said about 34.

2. The 'smashed' group often said they saw broken glass, but there was none.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think the word 'smashed' changed the memory?

Part 3 — Why it matters

Memory is not like a video. When we remember something, we build the memory again, and new information can change it. This can happen with words, with photos, or with other people's stories.

This is very important in a police station or in a court. A witness can be very sure about a memory, and still be wrong. Good police officers ask questions carefully, without leading words.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Is memory like a video?

2. Why is this important in court?

1. No. We build the memory again each time.

2. A witness can feel sure and still be wrong.

Discuss:
  • Do you trust your memory? Are some memories more reliable than others?

Grammar — past simple (regular and irregular)

Use the past simple to talk about finished actions in the past.

Regular verbs: add -ed — watch → watched, ask → asked, remember → remembered.
Irregular verbs: change the word — see → saw, go → went, say → said, forget → forgot, do → did.
Questions and negatives: use did / didn't + the base verb.
• Did you see any glass? — No, I didn't see any glass.
• Not: Did you saw. After did, the verb stays in the base form.

What do you remember?

Five situations. Say whether the memory could be wrong, and why. Then reveal an idea.

1. You remember a childhood holiday very clearly, but you have only seen the photos. Is your memory real?
Idea. It may be partly a memory of the photos. Photos can change what we remember.
2. Two friends remember the same party in different ways. Who is right?
Idea. Maybe both are partly right. Memory is not exact.
3. A witness says, 'I am 100% sure.' Is she right?
Idea. Being sure does not mean being right. Sure and correct are different.
4. A teacher asks, 'Did you see the thief's red hat?' (there was no hat). What may happen?
Idea. Some students may remember a red hat later. The question gives them new information.
5. How can you remember things better?
Idea. Write it down soon. Say it aloud. Sleep well.
