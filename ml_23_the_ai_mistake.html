The AI Mistake

There's a number on page four that doesn't exist anywhere. It's ten past three, the report goes to the client at four, and the person who wrote it is sitting two desks away with her coat still on.

The Scene 🎬

You weren't even supposed to be reading it. Beth's client report landed in your inbox this morning marked "FYI, no need to look" — and you looked, because it's a quiet afternoon and page four was about your bit of the business. Retention, up forty-one per cent. Lovely number. Genuinely impressive number.

So impressive that you went to find where it came from, because you'd rather like to use it yourself. And it isn't in the data pack. It isn't in the survey. It isn't in the quarterly figures or the deck or anywhere else you can think of to look, and you've now looked twice. As far as you can tell, the report contains a fact that nobody has ever measured — Beth drafted it with an AI, the AI needed a number there, and it produced one.

It goes to the client at four. Beth is not your junior, not your report, not your problem. You have no authority here whatsoever — you can't tell her to change anything, you can't overrule her, and if you get the tone even slightly wrong this becomes a thing about you, standing over someone's desk at ten past three, being clever about her work. You've got about forty minutes and one shot at how you open your mouth.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Have you ever spotted a mistake in something a colleague, a friend or a family member had already finished and was proud of? Did you say anything — and what decided it?
  2. When someone points out an error in your work, what makes the difference between it feeling helpful and it feeling like an attack? Is it what they say, or how, or where they're standing when they say it?

Who's who

You
The One Who Noticed 🔍

Not her boss, not her enemy, and not enjoying this. You want the number out of the report and Beth's afternoon left intact, and you'd quite like to be back at your desk in ten minutes with nobody having raised their voice.

The other voice
Beth 📄

Drafted the report with an AI, checked what she thought was worth checking, and has already had it signed off. She's tired, she's proud of it, and it goes out at four. Her first instinct will be that you're wrong, and her second will be that she's finished.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Beth is quite sure the figure came from the data pack, and says so twice. Rather than arguing with her, what does her colleague keep doing instead?

2. There's one moment where Beth stops arguing and starts panicking. What two things does her colleague say straight away to bring the temperature back down?

3. Why does the colleague bring up their own mistake — and why the detail about a court case rather than just "I've done it too"?

1. They agree with her first — "that's what I thought too" — and then repeat the plain fact that it isn't there, without ever telling her she's wrong. Beth is allowed to keep believing something reasonable, which means she can change her mind without having lost an argument.
2. They say they aren't telling anyone, and they name the only thing they actually care about — the client seeing it. Naming the small worry out loud is what shrinks the big one, because Beth has just imagined every worse version at once.
3. Because the alternative to "we've both done this" is "you're careless", and Beth has already got there on her own. The court case matters because a bigger, sillier, more specific mistake proves the sympathy is real, whereas a vague "it happens to everyone" would sound like something you say to be kind.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for telling someone their work is wrong when you've no authority, no time, and every reason to want them still speaking to you afterwards. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Everything here depends on volume and pace. This is a conversation held quietly, at half speed, in a voice that says none of this is very interesting — because the moment you sound urgent, or pleased with yourself, the other person stops hearing a colleague and starts hearing an accusation. Flat, low, unhurried, slightly bored. The content is dramatic enough on its own; your voice shouldn't help.

🚪 Getting in the door

Phrase 1

You need her attention for a few minutes and you need her not to panic before you've said anything. Which opener buys you the time without announcing a disaster?

A. "There's a serious problem with your report."
B. "Have you got two minutes before this goes out?"
C. "Are you busy? It's about page four."
B — "Have you got two minutes before this goes out?"
A tells her the verdict before she's even turned round, so she'll defend the report rather than look at it. C sounds ominous without saying anything, which is the worst of both. B is small and practical: it asks for a specific, tiny amount of time, and "before this goes out" quietly explains why it can't wait until tomorrow, without a word about anything being wrong.
Phrase 2

Now the first real question. You already know the answer, but you're asking as though you're the one missing something — which lets her look, rather than defend. Complete the chunk: "Where's this figure ___ ___, do you know?"

"Where's this figure come from, do you know?"
Note that it's a question about the number, not about her, and "do you know?" on the end makes it genuinely open — as though she might have an answer that solves everything. In spoken English this "where's it come from" is far more natural than "where did it come from", and the tag at the end is what stops it sounding like the start of an interrogation.

🔍 Saying what's wrong without saying she's wrong

Phrase 3

She's told you where she thinks it came from. You now have to say that it isn't there — as a report on your own searching rather than a verdict on her work. Put the pieces in order:

and I can'tanywhere.I've had a lookfind it
"I've had a look and I can't find it anywhere."
The whole sentence is about you — what you did, what you couldn't manage — which leaves room for her to be right and you to be blind. "I've had a look" also sounds like five minutes rather than an investigation, so it doesn't announce that you've been building a case against her all afternoon.
Phrase 4

The hard bit. You have to name what you think happened — that the machine produced a fact from nothing. Which version lets her accept it?

A. "That figure's fake."
B. "You've made this up."
C. "I think it might have made it up, to be honest."
C — "I think it might have made it up, to be honest."
B puts her at the centre of it, which is both unfair and unanswerable; A is a flat verdict she has to either accept or fight. C does three things at once — "I think" and "might" leave her room to prove you wrong, "it" puts the blame on the software rather than on her, and "to be honest" signals that you know this is an awkward thing to be saying and you're saying it anyway.
Phrase 5

She doesn't believe software would simply invent a fact. Explain the behaviour plainly, as a known quirk rather than as her personal failure. Put the pieces in order:

rather than sayingit fills the gap inIt does that, apparently —it doesn't know.
"It does that, apparently — it fills the gap in rather than saying it doesn't know."
"Apparently" is doing quiet work here: it means you heard this somewhere rather than worked it out, so you're not the expert correcting her. To "fill in a gap" is to put something into an empty space — and the point of the phrase is that the software would rather put anything there than leave it empty, which is exactly why it's dangerous. Explaining the tool takes the conversation off her and onto a thing you're both dealing with.

🛟 Making it safe

Phrase 6

She's gone from arguing to panicking — the sign-off, the deadline, her name on it. Which reply actually calms her down?

A. "Don't worry about it, these things happen."
B. "I'm not going to say anything to anyone. I just don't want it going to the client."
C. "Well, someone's going to have to tell Dan."
B — "I'm not going to say anything to anyone. I just don't want it going to the client."
A is warm but empty, and the thing she's actually frightened of — that you'll tell people — goes unanswered, so she keeps being frightened of it. C confirms her worst version. B answers the fear directly and then shrinks the whole problem down to one small, fixable thing, which is the only sentence that lets her breathe out.
Phrase 7

Time to fix it rather than discuss it. Which line moves her, without ordering her — remember you've no authority to make her change a word?

A. "You need to take that number out."
B. "Can we pull the number out and just say what we actually know?"
C. "I'd probably lose the number, if I were you."
B — "Can we pull the number out and just say what we actually know?"
A is an instruction you're not entitled to give; C is advice from the side of the room, which leaves her doing the work alone. "Can we" makes it a job the two of you are doing together, and "just say what we actually know" hands her the replacement in the same breath as the problem, so she isn't left staring at a hole on page four.

🤝 Leaving her with her dignity

Phrase 8

She's gone quiet and embarrassed. You want her to know you're not enjoying this and you're not above it. In your own words: tell her about a time an AI put something false into your work, and make clear how you feel about her now. Say it out loud before you reveal.

e.g. "I've done exactly the same thing, so I'm not standing here judging you — mine had a court case in it that never happened."
The useful chunk is "I'm not standing here judging you", where "standing here" quietly admits the awkward thing about the situation, which is that you are literally standing at her desk. And notice it comes with proof: a mistake of your own that's bigger and more embarrassing than hers. Saying "don't be embarrassed" tells her what to feel; a worse story of your own lets her stop feeling it.
Phrase 9

If the AI invented one number, the rest of the report needs checking too — but she's forty minutes from the deadline and can't do that alone. Offer, without taking over. Complete the chunk: "Do you want me to go through the ___ ___ ___ with you?"

"Do you want me to go through the rest of it with you?"
"Do you want me to" leaves the decision hers, which matters when you've just told her that her work is wrong — the last thing she needs is you deciding what happens next as well. To "go through" something is to check it line by line, and "with you" is the difference between helping her and taking it off her.
Phrase 10

Last thing you say. She needs to know this is over and it stayed in this corner of the office. Which line closes it?

A. "Nobody else needs to know — it hasn't gone anywhere yet."
B. "Your secret's safe with me."
C. "Let's just hope nobody spots it."
A — "Nobody else needs to know — it hasn't gone anywhere yet."
B makes it a secret, and a secret is a thing she now owes you; C leaves her frightened for a fortnight. A is better because of the second half: "it hasn't gone anywhere yet" is a fact, not a promise, and it's the fact that makes the whole afternoon survivable — nothing left the building, so nothing needs confessing.

🔑 Make them yours

These phrases are not really about software. They're about the moment you can see something someone else can't, and you have no right to say so, and you have to say so anyway. Pin three of them to your own life so you'll have them when it happens. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"Where's this figure come from, do you know?" — think of something you'd need to question at work or at home. What's the innocent question that opens it?

"I think it might have made it up, to be honest." — where could hedging like this save you? Something you're fairly sure about but can't prove.

"I've done exactly the same thing, so I'm not standing here judging you." — a real mistake of your own you could offer someone. What is it?

The Twist

😬 It wasn't just this one

You've done it. The number's out, the sentence reads better, Beth's shoulders have come down and you've promised her nobody else needs to know. Then, halfway through page six, she says it — quite lightly, not really thinking: "This is the fourth one I've done like this. The other three went out last month." Start the timer. Your neat little fix is gone: three reports are already sitting with clients, you've just given your word that this stays between you, and you have no authority over any of it. Keep the voice you've spent all lesson building — quiet, unhurried, on her side — and work out with her what happens now. One rule: you don't get to solve this by going over her head, and she doesn't get to leave it alone. Whatever you two agree in the next four minutes, she has to be able to walk into Dan's office and say it herself.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I asked about the number before I said anything about the number being wrong.
  • I hedged — I left her room to prove me wrong, and I put it on the software, not on her.
  • When she panicked, I said out loud that I wasn't telling anyone.
  • I made the fix something we were doing together, not something I was instructing her to do.
  • My voice stayed low and unhurried the whole way through — nobody two desks away knew what we were talking about.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Work has always contained wrong numbers. What's changed is where they come from: a mistake used to be a moment of tiredness you could point to — the wrong column, the copied cell, half past four on a Friday. Now the wrong number arrives fluent, confident and well-formatted, in a sentence better written than the ones around it.

Which puts a strange new job on the rest of us. Somebody in every office is going to have to be the person who says "where's this come from" out loud, quietly, to someone who didn't ask — and that person will usually have no authority, no time, and every incentive to leave it alone and let it go to the client.

The tools change every few months. The skill doesn't. It's still the oldest one there is: how to tell somebody something they don't want to hear, in a way that leaves them able to hear it.

Two bigger questions

  1. Whose mistake was the forty-one per cent — Beth's, for not checking, or the software's, for inventing it? Does it even matter, and who does your workplace decide is responsible when a tool gets something wrong?
  2. Beth's colleague promised that nobody else needed to know. Was that promise theirs to make? Where's the line between protecting a colleague and covering something up — and would your answer change if the report had already gone to the client?
