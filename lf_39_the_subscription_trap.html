The Subscription Trap

Fourteen pounds a month for a gym you last visited in February. The website has no cancel button, the chat bot has no cancel option, and the phone line has Dale — who is very nice, and whose entire job is that you don't hang up having cancelled.

The Scene 🎬

You have been meaning to cancel this since about March.

Fourteen pounds a month, going out on the eighth, for a subscription you no longer use. It took ninety seconds to sign up, on a phone, half-watching something else. Cancelling has now taken three separate attempts: a website with no cancel button, a "manage membership" page that loops back to the homepage, and a chat bot that offered to help you with everything except the one thing.

So now you're on the phone, and you've been put through to Dale in retentions. Dale is friendly, quick, and good at his job — and his job is measured on how many people ring up to cancel and don't. He will be pleasant to you for as long as it takes.

None of this is Dale's fault. The maze was designed by people you will never speak to, and he's the human being standing at the end of it. The skill is getting through him without becoming unpleasant to him.

  1. Are you paying for anything right now that you don't use? Why haven't you cancelled it?
  2. Companies make joining easy and leaving hard. Is that just business, or is it something that should be illegal?
You Third attempt at cancelling. Not angry, but completely decided — and aware that being decided is the only thing that will end this call.
Dale A retentions agent. Genuinely pleasant, quite funny, and paid according to how many people he stops from leaving. He has four offers to make and he will make all of them.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for leaving something that has been designed to keep you — politely, quickly, and without having an argument with the person who answers. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Cheerful and immovable is the target. Not cold — cold makes it a battle and battles take longer. Think of the tone you'd use to decline a second slice of cake at someone's house: warm, final, faintly amused. Every "no" should sound identical to the last one; sameness is what tells him the script isn't working.

🚪 Opening the door and standing in it

Phrase 1

The first sentence decides how long this call takes. Say what you want and pre-empt the entire retentions script in one go. Put the pieces in order:

and I'm not lookingI'd like to cancel, please —to be talked out of it.
"I'd like to cancel, please — and I'm not looking to be talked out of it."
The second half is unusual and it works. To talk someone out of something is to persuade them not to do it — naming the manoeuvre before it starts makes it much harder to perform, because he now knows you can see it. It also sets the tone as decided rather than uncertain, and retentions agents are trained to find uncertainty. There isn't any here.
Phrase 2

Explain why you're on the phone at all — the website sent you round in circles. Complete the missing word:

"I've tried doing it online and it just sends me round in ___."

"I've tried doing it online and it just sends me round in circles."
To go round in circles is to keep returning to where you started without progress — a page that links back to the page you came from. Saying it out loud matters for two reasons: it explains why you've taken up a phone line, and it quietly tells him you've already spent time on this and won't be sent back to the website, which is one of the standard moves.
Phrase 3

He's about to start the offers. Give him permission and remove the point of them at the same time — which keeps him friendly. Which version does that?

A. "Please don't offer me anything, I really haven't got time for this."
B. "I know you have to offer me things, so go on — but the answer's going to be no."
C. "Go on then, what have you got? You never know."
B — "I know you have to offer me things, so go on — but the answer's going to be no."
A blames him for a script he didn't write, and he'll get formal with you, which is slower. C is fatal — "you never know" is exactly the opening he's listening for, and he'll now work through all four offers properly. B lets him do his job (he genuinely has to make them, and may be recorded), while pre-announcing the outcome. It's respectful of him and completely unhelpful to the script.

🧱 Refusing without arguing

Phrase 4

Take away his entire toolkit in one sentence. Almost every offer he has is a discount — so tell him money isn't the problem. Complete the missing word:

"It's not the price, honestly. I'm just not ___ it."

"It's not the price, honestly. I'm just not using it."
Strategically the most important line in the call. A retentions script is built almost entirely out of price: discounts, free months, cheaper tiers. If the reason you're leaving isn't money, none of it applies, and saying so early saves you several minutes. It's also true, which makes it easy to repeat calmly when he tries again.
Phrase 5

He offers three months free. Don't argue about whether it's generous — point out what it does and doesn't change. Put the pieces in order:

I'd still be cancellingThree months freein three months.doesn't really help —
"Three months free doesn't really help — I'd still be cancelling in three months."
Notice you're not disputing the value of the offer at all; you're pointing out that it delays the conversation rather than ending it. That's unanswerable, because it's true, and it also tells him something useful: this isn't a negotiation he can win by improving the number. "Doesn't really help" is gentle — you're declining the offer, not criticising it.
Phrase 6

Third offer. You've been polite twice. Now move it along — firmly, but with no edge. Complete the missing words:

"Can we just ___ ___ ___ cancelling it?"

"Can we just get on with cancelling it?"
To get on with something is to proceed with it without further delay. The word doing the work is "we" — it makes the cancellation a shared task you're both now doing, rather than a request he can keep deferring. It's the polite equivalent of putting your hand on the door handle, and most agents will move at this point.

📅 Pinning down the details

Phrase 7

Cancelling isn't the same as stopping paying. Find out exactly what you still owe — and ask it in a way that gets a straight answer. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "What's the actual notice period — am I paying for another month?"
Two questions, and the second one is the real one. "Notice period" gets you a policy; "am I paying for another month" gets you a fact about your own bank account. The word actual is quietly useful — it signals that you want the real answer rather than the marketing one, and it's remarkable how often that alone changes the reply.
Phrase 8

He's given you a date. Say it back to him as a checkable statement, so any misunderstanding surfaces now rather than on your next bank statement. Complete the missing words:

"So ___ ___ ___ in thinking nothing comes out after the fourteenth?"

"So am I right in thinking nothing comes out after the fourteenth?"
"Am I right in thinking…" is one of the most useful checking phrases in English — it invites correction without accusing anyone of being unclear, so nobody has to lose face if the answer is no. Say the consequence in your own words, not theirs ("nothing comes out"), because repeating their phrasing can hide a misunderstanding inside familiar words.

🧾 Getting proof

Phrase 9

A phone call leaves no trace. Ask for the two things that make it real — and give a reason so it doesn't sound like distrust. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Could you send me confirmation by email? I'd rather have it in writing. And can I take a reference number for this, just in case?"
The email proves the cancellation happened; the reference number is the code that lets any future agent find this exact call and see what was promised. Ask for both — they do different jobs. "Just in case" is the softener that keeps it from sounding like you expect to be cheated, even though the reason you're asking is that you slightly do.

🙂 Leaving him alone

Phrase 10

Close by separating the person from the system he works inside. Which ending does that?

A. "Honestly, the way you people make this so difficult is a disgrace."
B. "Thanks Dale — I know that's your job, the offers. You were alright about it."
C. "Right. Well. Finally."
B — "Thanks Dale — I know that's your job, the offers. You were alright about it."
A takes a system's decisions out on a person who has no power over them, and he hears it forty times a day. C is small and sour, and you've already got what you wanted. B costs you four seconds and names the true thing: the maze isn't his, the offers are compulsory, and he was decent inside a job that doesn't make decency easy. Firm with the system, kind to the human — that's the whole lesson.

🔑 Make them yours

These work on any cancellation, resignation or unsubscribing where the other side would rather you didn't — gyms, insurers, broadband, a club, a commitment you've outgrown. Pin three of them to your own life. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I'm not looking to be talked out of it." — what are you paying for, or committed to, that you'd like to end?

"It's not the price, honestly. I'm just not using it." — what's your real reason, said in one plain sentence?

"So am I right in thinking…?" — what would you need to check before you trusted it was done?

⚡ The twist

The eighth comes and fourteen pounds goes out, as agreed. So does another fourteen on the eighth of the following month. You ring back, quote RTN 447192 — and the agent tells you that reference is for a "retention conversation", not a cancellation, and that there's no cancellation on the account at all.

You did everything right and you're still being charged. The email never arrived either. What do you say — and what do you ask for this time that you didn't ask for last time?

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you state what you want and pre-empt the counter-offer in a single sentence?
  • Can you take money off the table so the discounts stop applying?
  • Can you decline an offer without arguing about whether it's a good one?
  • Can you say the same "no" four times without your voice hardening?
  • Can you leave a phone call with written proof and a number?

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Signing up took ninety seconds on a phone. Leaving took three attempts, eleven minutes of hold music and a conversation with a trained professional. That asymmetry isn't an accident or a technical failing — it's a design, and it's paid for by everyone who gives up somewhere in the middle.

What the design really runs on is politeness. Most people can't say the same "no" four times to someone friendly, so they take the three months free, and the three months become a year.

And the person you have to say it to is never the person who built the maze. Dale gets the irritation that belongs several floors above him — which is worth remembering, because being firm and being unkind are not the same thing, and only one of them is necessary.
