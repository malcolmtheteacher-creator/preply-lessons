The Art of Film Criticism

B2 Level · Entertainment & Culture

Your Film Taste

Film is a powerful medium. It tells stories, provokes emotion, challenges perspectives, and creates shared cultural moments. But what makes a film "great"? Is it technical mastery? Emotional impact? Commercial success? Artistic vision? The answer is: it's complicated.

Defining Quality

Different viewers value different things. A film critic might adore an experimental art-house film that most audiences find boring. A blockbuster might be financially successful but critically dismissed. A film might be technically flawed yet emotionally profound. A perfectly executed film might be forgettable. Quality in cinema isn't absolute—it's multifaceted.

Share: What's your favourite film? Why do you love it? What makes it great in your view?

Judging Film Quality

Consider these dimensions when evaluating a film:

  • Storytelling: Is the narrative compelling, coherent, original?
  • Character development: Do characters feel real and evolve meaningfully?
  • Performance: Do actors inhabit their roles authentically?
  • Cinematography: Is the visual language intentional and beautiful?
  • Direction: Does the director have a clear vision? Is it executed skilfully?
  • Editing: Does pacing serve the story? Are cuts deliberate?
  • Sound design & music: Does audio enhance mood and meaning?
  • Themes: Does the film explore ideas with depth and nuance?
  • Originality: Does it bring something fresh or does it rely on clichés?
  • Emotional resonance: Does it move you? Does it stay with you?

Reflection

Which of these dimensions matter most to you? Do you prioritise technical skill or emotional impact? Visual beauty or narrative substance?

Cultural and Historical Context

Understanding a film's context enriches appreciation. A film might be groundbreaking for its time yet feel dated now. Cultural references matter. Production constraints shape choices. A director's biography influences their work. An independent film with a $1 million budget should be evaluated differently than a $200 million blockbuster.

Consider: How does knowing a film's context change how you judge it? Should technical limitations of early cinema affect your evaluation?

Cinema Language

Vocabulary for discussing film and cinema:

Cinematography The art of visual storytelling through camera work, lighting, composition, and colour
Plot Twist An unexpected change in narrative direction that surprises and reframes the story
Character Development The process by which a character's personality, beliefs, or motivations evolve throughout the story
Soundtrack The music and sound design composed for a film to enhance emotional and narrative impact
Box Office The revenue generated by a film through ticket sales; a measure of commercial success
Genre-defining A film that establishes conventions or standards that other films in that genre follow
Underrated A film deserving of praise and recognition but not receiving the acclaim it merits
Blockbuster A high-budget film with wide release, significant marketing, and broad commercial appeal

Phrases for Sophisticated Film Opinions

  • "The cinematography is breathtaking" — visual execution is visually stunning
  • "The pacing is compelling" — the story moves at an engaging speed
  • "The narrative is nuanced" — the story has complexity and subtlety
  • "The performances are convincing" — actors feel authentic in their roles
  • "The film subverts expectations" — it challenges what we anticipate
  • "It's a masterclass in..." — an excellent example of a technique
  • "The film feels dated" — it no longer feels contemporary or relevant
  • "The director's vision is evident" — you can see the filmmaker's artistic intent
  • "It's ultimately hollow" — it looks good but lacks substance
  • "This film resonates deeply" — it connects emotionally and meaningfully

Film Criticism Framework

Example analysis:

"This film excels in its cinematography—each frame is composed with deliberation, using colour symbolically to represent the protagonist's emotional journey. However, the narrative feels conventional, and character development is shallow. The performances are compelling, particularly the lead actor's subtle portrayal of inner conflict. Visually, it's a masterclass in composition; narratively, it's derivative. It's ultimately a beautiful film that would have been stronger with a more original script."

The Review

Contrasting Reviews: Same Film, Different Perspectives

Here are two contrasting reviews of a fictional film called "The Forgotten City":

Review 1: The Enthusiast's Perspective

"The Forgotten City is a visually stunning achievement that rewards patient viewers. Director Maya Patel's meticulous cinematography transforms mundane urban landscapes into landscapes of melancholy beauty. The colour grading—cool blues and desaturated yellows—creates an atmosphere of alienation and disconnection that perfectly mirrors the protagonist's internal state. The pacing is deliberately slow, allowing scenes to breathe and meaning to accumulate. The lead performance is understated yet devastating, with Sarah Chen conveying emotional turmoil through glances and silences rather than theatrical expression. The film doesn't spell out its themes; it trusts the audience to engage intellectually. This is cinema at its most intentional and artful. A stunning, challenging, magnificent work."

Review 2: The Sceptic's Perspective

"The Forgotten City mistakes ponderous pacing for depth and visual affectation for substance. The 165-minute runtime is indulgent—the film lacks sufficient narrative or character progression to justify its length. Director Patel substitutes action with lingering shots of empty streets, expecting us to find profundity in emptiness. The protagonist's motivations remain opaque, making it difficult to empathise rather than engage with the character. The soundtrack is aggressively minimal, which creates boredom rather than contemplative space. Critics may praise its artistic restraint, but restraint without purpose is merely uninvolving. The film had potential—the visual ideas are interesting—but it fails to develop them meaningfully. Ambitious, yes; successful, no."

Comprehension Questions

Answer: Both reviewers agree that the cinematography and visual direction are intentional and distinctive. The enthusiast praises it; the sceptic acknowledges it's "interesting" but questions its purpose.

Answer: The enthusiast sees slow pacing as allowing meaning to accumulate and scenes to breathe. The sceptic views it as indulgent and ponderous, creating boredom rather than depth.

Answer: Both are subjective, but the enthusiast uses more emotionally positive language ("devastating," "magnificent"). The sceptic employs more evaluative, critical language ("uninvolving," "ponderous").

Task: Write Your Own Mini Review

Select a film you've watched recently. Write a review addressing:

  • What's the film about? (brief summary)
  • What works well? (identify specific elements)
  • What doesn't work? (be specific about weaknesses)
  • Who would enjoy this film?
  • Your overall assessment (with nuance, not just "good" or "bad")

Use sophisticated language. Avoid simple judgments like "entertaining" or "boring." Reference specific scenes or technical elements. Aim for 150-200 words. You'll have 5 minutes to write and be prepared to present your review.

Discussion

Cinema vs. Streaming

Film culture has shifted. Theatrical releases compete with streaming options. Many viewers prefer watching at home—it's convenient, affordable, and accessible. Yet cinemas offer communal experience, immersive scale, and freedom from household distractions. Some argue streaming is more democratic; others claim it diminishes the art form. Directors like Denis Villeneuve passionately defend cinema as an intended medium.

Your perspective: Do you prefer cinema or streaming? How does the medium affect your film experience? Should films be experienced in cinemas, or is the story what matters?

Remakes and Sequels

Hollywood increasingly relies on franchises and remakes rather than original stories. This reflects profit incentives—familiar properties are safer investments. Yet originality suffers. Some remakes are excellent and honour the original whilst bringing fresh perspectives (think "Get Out" relative to classic horror). Others feel cynical, capitalising on nostalgia without artistic purpose. The tension is between commercial viability and artistic integrity.

Reflection

Are remakes inherently problematic, or can they be artistically valid? When is a remake justified? How should we distinguish artistic reinvention from cynical cash-grabbing?

Do Film Critics Matter?

Professional film critics wield influence, shaping discourse and sometimes box office performance. Yet criticism is subjective. A film hated by critics might be beloved by audiences and vice versa. Social media has democratised criticism—everyone's opinion is visible. This can be liberating (you don't need expert permission to enjoy what you like) or overwhelming (information overload). The role of critics has evolved, but they still serve valuable functions: helping audiences navigate options, providing informed analysis, fostering appreciation for cinema as art.

Consider: Do you read film reviews before watching? Do critics' opinions influence you? Should they?

Film as Cultural Mirror

Films reflect and shape culture. They reveal what societies value, fear, and desire. They can challenge prejudices or reinforce stereotypes. They're records of their era, capturing historical moments and cultural attitudes. Watching films from different decades and countries provides insight into how people think. This is why studying cinema—not just consuming it—is culturally valuable.

Reflection: What films have changed how you think? What messages do contemporary films send about your society?

Developing Your Critical Eye

Becoming a sophisticated film viewer takes practice. Watch widely—different genres, different eras, different countries. Read analyses alongside watching. Notice technical details. Sit with confusing or challenging films rather than dismissing them. Develop your own critical voice rather than relying on reviews. Learn to articulate not just what you feel but why you feel it. This transforms viewing from passive consumption to active engagement.

Final Thoughts

Film criticism, at its best, deepens our experience and understanding. It's not about arriving at objective truth but rather about articulating perspective thoughtfully, supporting judgments with evidence, and remaining open to challenge and growth. Your film taste will evolve. What you love now might seem dated or shallow later, or it might deepen in meaning. That's the beautiful complexity of engaging with art.

To close: What film has stayed with you most powerfully? Why? What does that reveal about what matters to you?
