Whose Psychology Is It?

If most of what we know about the human mind comes from a narrow slice of humanity, what do we actually know?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The paper

In 2010, the anthropologist Joseph Henrich and the psychologists Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan published a paper in Behavioral and Brain Sciences that has since become one of the most cited critiques of the discipline. They examined the samples used in studies published in leading psychology journals over a five-year period and found that about 96% of the participants came from Western industrialised countries, which hold roughly 12% of the world's population. Around 68% came from the United States alone, and about two-thirds of the American samples were undergraduates, most of them in psychology courses.

On this basis, they calculated that a randomly selected American undergraduate was thousands of times more likely to be a participant in a study than a randomly selected person from outside the West. They called the typical sample WEIRD: Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich and Democratic. The point was not only that these people were few, but that they might be unusual.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the authors find about the samples?

2. Why did they call them WEIRD?

1. About 96% of participants came from Western industrialised countries, with 12% of the world's population; 68% were American, mostly students.

2. Because participants were Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich and Democratic, and might be unrepresentative of humanity.

Discuss:
  • Why is a convenient sample so attractive, and what does it cost?

Part 2 — Outliers on the map

The authors then reviewed evidence from cross-cultural studies suggesting that WEIRD populations are, on several measures, at an extreme. In fairness games in which participants split money, people in small-scale societies behaved quite differently from American students, and the patterns of generosity were related to how far a society depended on trade. In studies of the Müller-Lyer visual illusion, the strength of the effect differed markedly between populations, with some groups hardly affected at all. Differences were also found in reasoning styles, in the sense of self and in moral judgement.

The conclusion was cautious but significant. Some aspects of cognition, such as basic perception or memory, may well be shared. But others, notably those related to the self, morality and social relationships, appear to vary more than was assumed, and it is hard to know which are which without testing across populations.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the cross-cultural studies suggest?

2. What did the authors conclude?

1. That WEIRD populations are at an extreme on several measures, such as fairness choices, illusion perception and reasoning style.

2. That some aspects of cognition may be shared, but others, especially the self, morality and social relationships, vary more than assumed.

Discuss:
  • Which areas of psychology are most at risk from a WEIRD bias? Which are least?

Part 3 — The consequences, and the critics

The effects are not only academic. Diagnostic tools, screening tests and treatments are often developed in one setting and exported. A symptom checklist may measure something different when translated, and a therapy that depends on direct self-disclosure may fit one cultural setting better than another. This has prompted reforms such as the Cultural Formulation Interview in the American diagnostic manual, which asks clients how they and their community understand their problem.

The critique has also been questioned. Some argue that 'WEIRD' is too crude: there is great variety within Western societies and the effects of other factors, such as education and urban living, are easily confused with culture. Others note that many findings do replicate across countries. A more constructive reading is that researchers should state clearly whom their results are about, test generalisations rather than assume them, and collaborate with colleagues across regions. Large international projects are now doing just that.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What practical consequences does the WEIRD problem have?

2. What have critics said?

1. Tools and treatments developed in one setting may not work or may mean something different elsewhere.

2. That the label is too crude, since there is great variety within Western societies and many findings do replicate.

Discuss:
  • What would you want to know about a measure before using it with a client from a different culture?

Grammar — comparative structures

Describing differences, proportions and trends accurately is central to research writing. English offers a range of comparative structures beyond more than and as … as.

Strengthening and weakening comparatives:
• far / much / considerably / significantly more … than: "WEIRD samples are far more common than others."
• slightly / marginally / a little less … than
• nowhere near as … as / not nearly as … as: "Other populations are nowhere near as well studied as Western ones."
• roughly / almost / nearly twice as … as, three times as many … as
Parallel comparatives:
• the + comparative … the + comparative: "The more varied the sample, the more reliable the conclusion."
• increasingly / ever more for a trend: "Samples are increasingly diverse."
• as … as ever, no more … than: "The method is no more valid in one place than in another."

How far does it travel?

Five findings. Say how far you would expect each to generalise and what you would want to test. Then reveal.

1. A visual illusion that differs in strength across societies.
Learned. Perception is shaped, in part, by the environment people grow up in; this shows that even 'basic' processes can vary.
2. A task in which participants split money, with results from American students.
Not universal. Studies across small-scale societies produced very different splits, which relate to how people cooperate in daily life.
3. A model of how short-term memory works.
Probably more general. But details such as strategies, and the effect of schooling, may vary; test before assuming.
4. A questionnaire about feeling good about yourself, used worldwide.
Risky. The concept of the 'self' and how people talk about it varies; the same score may mean different things.
5. A therapy manual developed in one country and exported.
Needs adapting. Examples, language, family roles and expectations all need checking; evidence from the new setting is needed.
