Middle East and North Africa: Stereotype vs Reality

8 lessons on the cultures of the Middle East and North Africa — each one opens with a stereotype and finds out what is really true.

Deserts, camels, oil, bargaining, Ramadan: the Middle East and North Africa attract some of the strongest stereotypes in the world, and a region of about twenty countries is often described as a single place. Start with the Background lesson, then work through the topics. Each lesson asks three questions — what does the stereotype say, what is the reality, and why does the idea exist? — and finishes with practical tips for working, travelling and making friends across cultures.

📖 Background1

Read this first: who lives in the Middle East and North Africa, and how the many cultures connect.

BackgroundThe Middle East and North Africa: One Region, Many Worlds
Background
The region includes Arabs, Persians, Turks, Kurds, Amazigh (Berber) people and many others. Most are Muslim, but Christians, Jews, Druze and other communities have lived there for centuries, and most people live in cities, not deserts.
The stereotype: “The Middle East and North Africa is one place where everyone is the same: Arab, Muslim and living in the desert.”

🧭 Stereotype vs Reality7

Culture, cultural differences and the stories we tell about each other.

1“Everyone Speaks Arabic” — Or Do They?
Languages & communication
Arabic has a shared written form and many spoken varieties that can differ a lot from country to country. Millions of people speak other languages entirely.
The stereotype: “Everyone in the Middle East and North Africa speaks Arabic, and it is one language.”
Second Conditional — If I Spoke …, I Would …Open →
2Camels or Skyscrapers? Desert and City
Land & cities
Deserts cover much of the land, but most people live in cities and along rivers and coasts. Nomadic life still exists, but it is now a small minority.
The stereotype: “Everyone in the Middle East and North Africa lives in the desert and rides a camel.”
Contrast — Although, Whereas, Despite, HoweverOpen →
3Guests, Tea and Dates: What Hospitality Really Means
Hospitality & customs
Hospitality is an important value, rooted in desert life, but it follows customs of its own. Respect for these customs matters more than good intentions.
The stereotype: “People in the Middle East and North Africa are always friendly and generous to every foreigner, so you can do anything.”
Habits and Customs — Used To, Would, Be Used ToOpen →
4Women in the Middle East and North Africa: Beyond One Picture
Women & society
Women's lives vary greatly by country, class, city and family. There are real legal and economic limits in some places and real progress in others, and women are speaking for themselves.
The stereotype: “Women in the Middle East and North Africa all have the same life: covered, silent and without rights.”
Hedging and Generalising — Tend To, Generally, Some, ManyOpen →
5More Than Fasting: What Ramadan Is Really Like
Faith & community
Ramadan is a month of fasting from dawn to sunset, but also of shared meals, charity, prayer and celebration. People work, shop and socialise, just on a different rhythm.
The stereotype: “Ramadan is just a month of hunger and misery when nobody can work or smile.”
Time Clauses — Before, After, Until, As Soon As, WhileOpen →
6Haggling in the Souk: A Friendly Art, Not a Trap
Markets & bargaining
Bargaining is normal in traditional markets (souks, bazaars), but not in supermarkets, malls, restaurants or pharmacies. In a market, it is a social exchange with its own manners.
The stereotype: “Everybody in the Middle East and North Africa haggles over everything, and tourists always get cheated.”
First Conditional and Polite Negotiating LanguageOpen →
7Beyond Oil: A Region That Is Changing
Economy & change
Only some countries have large oil and gas reserves. Incomes range from among the highest in the world to among the lowest, and many countries now invest in solar power, tourism, technology and education.
The stereotype: “The whole region is rich from oil, and everyone is an oil sheikh.”
