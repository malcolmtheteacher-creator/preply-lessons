A White Heron

Sarah Orne Jewett's story of a child, a rare bird, and the price of an answer.

Need an easier version? Read this story at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll examine a decision made in silence by a nine-year-old — and you'll master the conditionals that let you argue about what did not happen: had she spoken, the bird would have been shot.
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • Is there information you would refuse to sell at any price? What makes it different in kind from everything else?
  • Why is it harder to refuse someone charming than someone unpleasant — and is that a weakness or a decent instinct?
  • People say they love wild places, and then want to own a piece of them. Is affection the same as protection?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. He had glimpsed the bird once, months earlier, and had not found it since; every search of the marsh ended the same way. The creature was maddeningly elusive.
elusive (adj) — difficult to find, catch or pin down. Used of animals, memories, meanings and success. Verb: to elude.
2. He walked the woods each day with the gun under his arm, alert for one bird and one bird only. The heron was his quarry.
quarry (n) — the animal or person being hunted. (A different word with the same spelling means a place where stone is cut.)
3. Her hands were torn by the bark, and where she gripped the branches they came away sticky and dark with sap.
sap (n) — the sticky liquid inside a tree. (As a verb, unrelated: to sap someone's strength = to drain it.)

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: scruples · covet · vantage point. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means to want something intensely, especially something belonging to someone else?
to covet (v) — a strong, slightly moralised word for wanting. Adjective: coveted — "the most coveted prize in the sport".
2. Which phrase means a position from which you can see a great deal?
a vantage point (n) — a place giving a wide view; figuratively, a perspective: "from the vantage point of thirty years later".
3. Which word means moral hesitations that stop you doing something profitable?
scruples (n, usually plural) — "He had no scruples about it." Adjective: unscrupulous (willing to do anything); scrupulous also means extremely careful.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. She was torn between the man's approval and the bird's safety, and could not have both. Torn means…
  (a) exhausted   (b) pulled painfully between two things you want   (c) physically injured
(b) — torn (adj) = divided between two desires or duties. "I'm torn between the two offers."
2. To reveal the nest would have been to betray the bird. To betray is to…
  (a) forget about someone   (b) break faith with someone who trusted you   (c) argue with someone publicly
(b) — to betray (v) = to break trust; also to reveal something unintentionally ("her voice betrayed her nerves"). Noun: betrayal.

D · Word building & collocation

1. You know resolve as a verb (to resolve a problem). As a noun it means something else — complete: "She climbed with a fierce ___ that surprised her." What does it mean, and what adjective describes a person full of it?
resolve (n) — firm determination. Adjective: resolute. Collocations: to strengthen / weaken someone's resolve.
2. You know hospitable. Give the noun, and complete the fixed phrase for the unwritten duty to feed and shelter a traveller: the laws of ___ .
hospitality (n) — the laws of hospitality. Note the pressure this puts on Sylvia: he is a guest in her grandmother's house, which makes refusing him harder.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The guest with the gun

A year earlier Sylvia had been a pale child in a crowded manufacturing town, in a street with nothing green in it. Her grandmother had brought her out to the farm, and everyone agreed the loneliness would be hard on a girl of nine. It was not. She had learned the woods as other children learn a house; the slow walk home behind the cow was the best hour of her day, and she felt, for the first time in her life, entirely at ease.

One evening, later than usual, a whistle cut through the trees — not a bird's whistle, which she would have recognised, but a boy's, sharp and determined. A young man stepped out of the shadows with a gun on his shoulder and a game bag at his side, apologised for startling her, and asked whether she thought her people might put him up for the night, since he had lost the road. Sylvia found she could not produce a single word, and walked the rest of the way home in silence while he talked cheerfully beside her.

Her grandmother was hospitable in the old way — supper, a bed, and the family history offered before it was asked for. The visitor explained his business. He collected birds: he had been at it since boyhood, and had hundreds at home, preserved and labelled and kept in glass cases. Sylvia listened, unable to reconcile the two halves of what she heard, because he spoke about the birds with genuine tenderness and he shot them. Then he came to the point. Some miles back, near the marsh, he had seen a white heron — a bird so elusive he had never taken a specimen. He would give ten dollars, he said, to anybody who could show him where it nested.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How has the farm changed Sylvia, and how was that received by the adults?

2. What is the visitor's occupation, and what contradiction does Sylvia notice in him?

1. She moved from a crowded town to an isolated farm. Everyone predicted loneliness; in fact she has come alive there and knows the woods intimately.

2. He is an ornithologist and collector. He speaks about birds with real affection and kills them for his collection — a contradiction Sylvia cannot resolve.

Discuss:
  • Jewett makes the collector likeable, courteous and interesting rather than villainous. What does that choice do to the moral problem?

Part 2 — Ten dollars, and the pine

Ten dollars. Sylvia turned the sum over all the following day as she followed him through the woods, listening while he named birds she had known by sight all year and gave her his pocket knife as a gift. She had never held ten dollars. Neither, so far as she knew, had her grandmother. In a household where every purchase was weighed twice, the money was not an abstraction; it was a roof repaired, a winter made easier.

And she liked him — that was the part she could not put down. She watched him move through the trees and thought him the most admirable person she had ever encountered, and still could not understand the gun. All day she said nothing about the heron, though she thought about nothing else.

There was one place from which the whole country could be seen: an enormous pine, the last of the old growth, standing above the second-growth woods on the ridge. Sylvia had looked up at it many times and imagined that anyone who reached the top would see the world entire, and perhaps the sea. That night she lay awake, and in the grey hour before dawn she let herself out of the house and went to it. If she could climb it, she would see where the heron rose at first light — and then the knowledge would be hers to give or to keep.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is ten dollars not an abstraction in this household?

2. What does Sylvia decide to do, and what does she expect to gain from it?

1. Because the farm is poor and every purchase is counted; the sum represents real relief — neither she nor her grandmother has ever had it.

2. To climb the great pine before dawn and watch where the heron rises, so that she will know the location of the nest and can then choose whether to tell.

Discuss:
  • She climbs in order to know, not in order to tell. Why does the story insist on that distinction?

Part 3 — The vantage point, and the silence

The climb was harder than she had allowed for. She went up a neighbouring oak first, crossed to the pine's lowest limb, and after that there was nothing but the trunk and branch after branch above her. The bark tore her hands; sap made her fingers stick; twice she stopped with the dark world beneath her and went on. The old tree seemed to hold itself steady for her — as though, after a long life, it had developed an interest in what this small determined creature wanted.

At the top she came out into the first light. The sun rose out of the sea, which she had never seen, and lay before her as a shining plain with two white sails on it. The woods rolled away beneath her; hawks flew past below her feet; the world was immeasurably larger than anything a crowded street had prepared her for. And then, from a dead hemlock branch below, the white heron rose like a feather thrown upward, floated past her level, and settled on a bough with its mate close by, calling and preening while the light came on. She knew the nest now, exactly. She kept still until the birds had gone about their business, and then began the long descent.

Her grandmother scolded her for vanishing; the young man woke and saw her face, and understood at once that she had something to tell. He asked her kindly. He mentioned the money again. Her grandmother told her sharply not to be foolish and to speak up. And Sylvia stood in the kitchen with the sound of the pine still in her ears and the sea still in her eyes, and said nothing whatever. She could not make the words come. He left later that morning, disappointed, and the woods went quiet again. She had lost a friend and ten dollars, and for the rest of her life she never told a soul what she had seen from the top of the tree.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Sylvia see from the top of the pine, in order?

2. What happens in the kitchen, and what does her silence cost her?

1. The sunrise, the sea for the first time in her life with two sails on it, hawks flying below her — and then the heron rising from a dead branch and returning to its nest with its mate.

2. Both adults press her to speak and she cannot. He leaves disappointed. She loses his friendship and the ten dollars, and keeps the secret permanently.

Discuss:
  • Jewett gives her no speech, no refusal, no explanation — only an inability to speak. What is being claimed about how such decisions are actually made?

Grammar — third, mixed & inverted conditionals

Everything interesting about this story happened in a silence. To argue about it you need the forms English keeps for the road not taken — and the inversion that lets you drop if altogether.

Third conditional — an imagined past, with imagined past consequences:
If + past perfect, would have + past participle.
• "If she had spoken, he would have found the nest."
• "If they hadn't taken him in, none of it would have happened."
Swap would for might / could to soften: "He might have left empty-handed anyway."
Mixed conditionals — the two halves live in different times. Both are extremely common in real argument:
• Past condition → present result: "If she had taken the money, she would be a different person today."
• Present condition → past result: "If she were the kind of child who cared about money, she would have told him at once."

Inversion — drop if, move the auxiliary to the front. More formal, and more elegant:
• "Had she spoken, the heron would have been shot." (= if she had spoken)
• "Were she older, she might have argued with him." (= if she were older)
• "Had it not been for the pine, she would never have known."
Note: no contractions in the inverted form — ✗ Hadn't she spoken. Use Had she not spoken…

1. Third conditional

Put the verbs in brackets into the correct forms.

1. If the young man (not / lose) his way, he never (find) the farm at all.

2. If Sylvia (speak), her grandmother (spend) the ten dollars on the winter.

3. She (not / see) the nest if she (not / climb) the pine before dawn.

1. hadn't lost / would have found (note "never would have found" is also natural)

2. had spoken / would have spent

3. wouldn't have seen / hadn't climbed

2. Mixed conditionals

Complete each sentence so that the two halves sit in different times.

1. She kept the secret, so she is not ten dollars richer. → If she the secret, she ten dollars richer today.

2. She loves the woods, so she said nothing that morning. → If she the woods, she him immediately.

3. He shot birds for a living, so he is remembered as the villain of the story. → If he birds, he as the villain at all.

1. hadn't kept / would be (past condition → present result)

2. didn't love / would have told (present condition → past result)

3. hadn't shot / wouldn't be remembered

3. Argue it with inversion — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap, using the inverted form where the sentence begins with Had or Were. Read the paragraph aloud when you finish.

(the young man / not / arrive) that evening, Sylvia would have gone on exactly as before. (she / speak) in the kitchen, the heron (end) in a glass case in a distant city. (it / not / be) for the great pine, she would never have known where the nest was. And (she / be) a few years older, she might well (take) the money without a second thought.

Had the young man not arrived · Had she spoken · would have ended · Had it not been for · were she · have taken

Notice the negative placement in the inverted form: Had he not arrived, never "Hadn't he arrived".

Hold on to these forms — the Speak tab is an argument about something that did not happen, which is exactly what they exist for.

Going deeper

Jewett published this in 1886, writing from coastal Maine at the height of a fashion that was emptying American woods: birds were shot in enormous numbers for private collections and for the millinery trade, which used whole wings — and sometimes whole birds — on women's hats. Her contemporary readers would have recognised the young man not as a monster but as a respectable enthusiast, the kind of guest a farmhouse would be glad to feed.

The craft. Count the pressures Jewett assembles on one side of the scale: real poverty, a real sum of money, an adult asking politely, the laws of hospitality, and a child's first experience of being found interesting by someone she admires. On the other side there is a bird, which can ask for nothing. The story's structural masterstroke is the climb, because it changes the scale of everything: from the top of the pine the sea appears, hawks pass below her, and ten dollars becomes a small thing seen from a great height. Then Jewett withholds the speech we expect. Sylvia does not refuse; she is unable to speak, which is a different and far more honest account of how such choices happen at nine years old. And the ending declines to reward her — no thanks, no friend, no money, and a narrator who asks, almost accusingly, whether the birds were better friends to her than the man might have been. The story ends on a question about its own heroine.

Talk it through:
  • The narrator's closing doubt — were the birds worth what she gave up? — undercuts the moral. Why include it?
  • Consider the climb as an argument rather than an adventure. What does the change of altitude do to the value of ten dollars?
  • Sylvia never articulates a principle. Can a decision be moral if the person making it cannot explain it?
  • The collector loves what he destroys. Where does that pattern appear in our own relationship with wild places?

Task B · Did she choose well? (4 minutes)

Build an argument. One case: a poor household needed that money, a decent man was refused an answer for no stated reason, and a nine-year-old made an enormous decision on an impulse she could not explain. The other: some things are not for sale, and she understood that before she had the words for it. Take a side, give three reasons, answer the strongest objection, and say what you would have done in that kitchen.

Then argue the opposite for one minute, using at least one inverted conditional.

Task C · Value, secrecy and wild places (4 minutes)

Take the story's problem outward.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Describe the highest or wildest place you have stood in, and what the view changed about your sense of scale.
  • Should the location of rare species be published or protected? Argue it properly, with counterexamples.
  • Is there a price at which almost anyone would talk? Where is yours, and what does that tell you?
  • Conservation usually costs somebody money. Who should bear it, and why?
  • Describe a place near you that has changed in your lifetime. Use conditionals to describe what would have preserved it.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The story ends by asking whether the birds were worth the loss. Answer the narrator directly.
  2. The collector loves birds and kills them; Sylvia loves them and hides them. Which of them would call themselves a naturalist, and does the label settle anything?
  3. Is her silence a moral act, or an inability to disappoint someone she admired? Does the difference matter to the heron?
