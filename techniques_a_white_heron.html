SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B1/B2 · 50 min

A White Heron

Sarah Orne Jewett's story of a girl, a rare bird, and ten dollars.

Today: you'll tell the story of a girl who is offered more money than she has ever seen for one piece of information — and you'll master the two conditionals you need every time you weigh up a choice: if I tell him… / if I told him…
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • Is there any secret you would refuse to sell, whatever the price? What makes it different from the others?
  • Somebody friendly and impressive asks you for something. Is it harder to say no to them than to someone you dislike? Why?
  • Do people protect things they love, or things they own? Are those the same?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four short activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. The bird stood completely still at the edge of the water on long thin legs, waiting, and then its neck shot forward. It was a heron.
a heron (n) — a tall wading bird with long legs and a long neck, which stands in shallow water hunting fish.
2. Beyond the last field the ground turned soft and wet, full of reeds and standing water, and the cows never went there. It was all marsh.
marsh (n) — low, wet ground; a swamp. Adjective: marshy.
3. She had lived in a noisy manufacturing town, in a crowded street, with no field to walk in. Here on the farm she was, for the first time, at ease.
at ease (phrase) — relaxed and comfortable, with nothing to worry about. Opposite: ill at ease.

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: specimen · reward · trunk. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means a single example of a plant or animal, collected for study?
a specimen (n) — "He had hundreds of specimens in glass cases at home."
2. Which word means money or a prize offered to someone who helps or finds something?
a reward (n/v) — "He offered a reward of ten dollars." · "She was rewarded for her honesty."
3. Which word means the thick main stem of a tree, from the ground to the branches?
a trunk (n) — "She put her arms around the rough trunk and began to climb."

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Her grandmother was hospitable: the young man was given supper, a bed and everything the house could offer. Hospitable means…
  (a) working in a hospital   (b) generous and welcoming to guests   (c) suspicious of strangers
(b) — hospitable (adj) = welcoming and generous to visitors. Noun: hospitality.
2. She was torn: she wanted to please him, and she wanted to protect the bird, and she could not do both. Torn means…
  (a) exhausted   (b) unable to choose between two things you want   (c) physically hurt
(b) — torn (adj) = pulled in two directions at once. "I'm torn between the two courses."

D · Word building & word partners

1. You know to betray. Make the noun ("an act of ___") and complete the pattern: to betray a friend, to betray a ___, to betray someone's ___.
betrayal (n) — to betray a secret, to betray someone's trust. The word is much stronger than "tell": it means breaking faith with someone.
2. One verb fits all three: to ___ a secret · to ___ the truth · to ___ where something is hidden. It is a more formal word than "tell".
to reveal — to make known something that was hidden. Noun: a revelation. Useful opposite: to keep something to yourself.

Now use them — out loud

Talk it through before we read:
  • Using specimen, reward, torn and reveal, predict: what choice do you think this girl is going to face?
  • Which of today's words belong to the world of nature, and which to the world of money? Sort them out loud — and say which side reward is really on.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The stranger with a gun

Sylvia had lived on her grandmother's farm for a year, and she was a different child from the one who had arrived. She had come from a crowded manufacturing town where there was nowhere to walk and nothing green, and everyone had said the quiet countryside would be lonely for her. It was not. She knew the woods now, and the birds knew her, and the slow business of bringing the cow home each evening was the best hour of her day.

One evening, later than usual, a whistle cut through the trees — not a bird's whistle but a boy's, sharp and determined. A young man came out of the shadows with a gun over his shoulder and a bag of collected birds at his side. He was friendly and cheerful; he asked whether he might have a night's lodging, since he had lost his way. Sylvia could not answer a word. She walked home beside him in silence while he talked.

Her grandmother was hospitable, and gave him supper, a bed, and the whole story of the farm. The young man explained that he collected birds — that he had been hunting them since he was a boy, and had hundreds of them at home, preserved and labelled. Sylvia listened, and could not make the two halves fit together: he spoke about the birds with real love, and he shot them. Then he told them what he was looking for. Some miles back, near the marsh, he had seen a white heron, a bird so rare that he had never taken one. He would give ten dollars, he said, to anybody who could show him where it nested.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How has Sylvia's life changed since she came to the farm?

2. What does the young man want, and what does he offer?

1. She moved from a crowded town to her grandmother's farm. Everyone expected her to be lonely, but she has come alive in the woods and knows them intimately.

2. He is a bird collector looking for a rare white heron he glimpsed near the marsh. He offers ten dollars to anyone who can show him its nest.

Discuss:
  • He genuinely loves birds, and he kills them for his collection. Is that a contradiction, or do people do this all the time?

Part 2 — Ten dollars, and the great pine

Ten dollars. Sylvia thought about it all the next day, while she followed the young man through the woods and he told her the names of birds and gave her his pocket knife as a present. She had never had ten dollars. Her grandmother had never had ten dollars at one time. There were so many things that money would buy for a house where every small thing had to be counted.

And she liked him. That was the difficult part. She watched him move through the trees and thought he was the most wonderful person she had ever met, and she could not understand why he carried a gun. She said nothing all day about the heron, though she thought about nothing else.

There was one place in the woods where you could see everything: an enormous old pine, the last of its kind, standing high above the other trees on a ridge. Sylvia had looked up at it many times and imagined that a person at the top could see the whole world, and perhaps the sea. That night she lay awake, and in the grey light before dawn she slipped out of the house and went to the pine. If she could climb it, she would see where the heron rose from at sunrise — and then she would know.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does the offer of ten dollars matter so much to Sylvia?

2. What is her plan, and when does she carry it out?

1. Because the household is very poor — neither she nor her grandmother has ever had that much money at once, and it would buy a great many needed things.

2. To climb the great pine before dawn, so that from the top she can watch where the white heron rises at sunrise and find its nest.

Discuss:
  • She likes him, she needs the money, and she loves the birds. Which of those three pressures do you think is strongest at this point in the story?

Part 3 — What she saw, and what she said

The climb was harder than she had imagined. She went up a neighbouring oak first, then reached across to the pine's lowest branch, and after that there was only the enormous trunk and the branches going up and up. The bark scratched her; her hands grew sticky with sap; twice she stopped, with the whole dark world underneath her, and went on anyway. The tree seemed to hold her, as though it were interested in what this small determined creature wanted.

At the top she came out into the first light. The sun rose out of the sea, which she had never seen before — a great shining plain of it, with white sails moving. Below her the woods rolled away, hawks flew past at her own level, and the whole world was bigger and stranger than anyone in a crowded street could imagine. Then, from a dead hemlock branch below, a white heron rose like a feather blown upwards, floated past her, and settled on a branch with its mate close by, and called and preened as the light came. Sylvia knew exactly where the nest was. She stayed very still until the birds had gone, and then began the long climb down.

Her grandmother scolded her for disappearing. The young man woke, and saw her face, and knew at once that she had something to tell him. He asked her kindly. He reminded her about the money. Her grandmother told her not to be silly and to speak up. And Sylvia stood there with the pine still in her ears and the sea still in her eyes — and said nothing at all. She could not make the words come. He went away later that morning, disappointed, and the woods were quiet again. She had lost a friend and ten dollars, and she never told anyone what she had seen from the top of the tree.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Sylvia see from the top of the pine?

2. What happens when she is asked about the heron — and what does it cost her?

1. The sunrise, the sea for the first time in her life, hawks flying at her own height — and the white heron rising from a dead branch below her and returning to its nest.

2. She stays silent. She cannot speak. He leaves disappointed, and she loses both his friendship and the ten dollars — and keeps the secret for the rest of her life.

Discuss:
  • She does not argue, refuse or explain — she simply cannot speak. Why does the story give her silence instead of a speech?

Grammar — first & second conditional

Sylvia spends a whole day inside two sentences: if I tell him, I get the money — and if I told him, what would happen to the bird? The difference between those two forms is the difference between a real plan and an imagined one.

First conditional — a real possibility, now or in the future:
If + present simple, will + infinitive.
• "If it rains tomorrow, we'll cancel the match."
• "If you don't leave now, you'll miss the bus."
Never put will after if: ✗ if it will rain. You can swap will for might / may / could when you are less certain, or an imperative: "If you see him, tell him to call me."
Second conditional — imagined, unlikely or untrue:
If + past simple, would + infinitive.
• "If I lived by the sea, I'd swim every morning." (I don't live by the sea.)
• "If they offered me the job, I'd take it." (They probably won't.)
With be, careful English uses were for all persons: "If I were you, I'd say nothing." — the standard way to give advice.

The test: Is this really on the table? → first conditional. Am I just imagining it? → second.

1. First or second?

Put the verbs in brackets into the correct forms.

1. If Sylvia (tell) him where the nest is, he (give) her ten dollars. (a real offer, on the table)

2. If she (be) an ordinary child, the choice (not / be) difficult at all. (imagined — she isn't)

3. If you (climb) that tree at dawn, you (see) the sea. (a real possibility for anyone standing there)

1. tells / will give   2. were / wouldn't be   3. climb / will see

Notice sentence 2: "were", not "was" — the imagined form.

2. Change the meaning

Rewrite the idea in the other conditional. Write only the missing words.

1. Real: If he stays another day, he'll find the nest himself. → Imagined: If he another day, he the nest himself.

2. Imagined: If I were Sylvia, I'd keep quiet. → Advice to a friend, real: If you in that situation, quiet. (use an imperative)

3. Imagined: If the bird weren't so rare, nobody would pay for it. → Real: If the bird so rare, nobody for it.

1. stayed / would find   2. are / keep   3. isn't / will pay

Same idea, different distance from reality. That distance is the whole point of the two forms.

3. Argue both sides — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the correct form, then read the whole paragraph aloud.

If Sylvia (speak), her grandmother (be) able to buy things the farm badly needs. If the money (not / matter) so much, the choice would be easy — but it does matter. On the other hand, if she (show) him the nest, the heron (end) in a glass case, and the marsh (be) emptier for ever. If I (be) her, I think I (do) exactly what she did.

spoke · would be · didn't matter · showed · would end · would be · were · would do

The whole paragraph is imagined, so it runs on the second conditional — and finishes with the advice form "if I were her".

Hold on to both forms — in the Speak tab you'll use the first for real plans and the second to argue about what Sylvia should have done.

Going deeper

Jewett published this in 1886 in Maine, in a decade when rare birds were being shot in enormous numbers for private collections and for the feathers on fashionable hats. Readers at the time would have recognised the young man immediately: he was not a villain but a familiar and respectable figure — a scientist, an enthusiast, a man with a hobby.

The heart of it. The story is built so that everything pushes Sylvia one way. The money is real and badly needed. The man is kind, interesting, and the first person to treat her as worth talking to. She is nine years old and he is an adult asking politely. And against all of that there is only a bird, which cannot ask her for anything. Notice that the climb changes the scale of the story: from the top of the pine she sees the sea, the hawks below her, the whole world going on without reference to ten dollars. She comes down knowing something that cannot be said in a farmhouse kitchen — which is exactly why the story gives her silence instead of a speech. She does not win. She loses the money and the friend, and the narrator does not pretend otherwise. What she keeps is the thing she saw.

Talk it through:
  • Why does Jewett make the young man likeable rather than cruel? How would the story change if he were unpleasant?
  • Sylvia never explains her decision — not to him, not to her grandmother, not to us. What is gained by leaving it unspoken?
  • The story ends with a real loss: no money, no friend, no thanks. Is it still a happy ending?
  • Is this a story about nature, about growing up, or about the first time someone chooses their own values over what everyone around them wants?

Task A · Tell the whole story (4 minutes)

Tell A White Heron in your own words. Move through the arc: the girl who came from the town → the whistle in the woods → the visitor and his collection → the ten dollars → the day she says nothing → the great pine before dawn → the sea, the hawks, the heron → the kitchen, the question, the silence.

Use at least two first conditionals and two second conditionals, and at least five key words (heron, marsh, at ease, specimen, reward, trunk, hospitable, torn, betray, reveal).

Task B · Should she have told him? (4 minutes)

Take a side and argue it properly. The household is poor and the money is real; the bird is rare and cannot speak for itself. Build your case with three reasons, answer the strongest objection to your view, and finish with what you would have said standing in that kitchen.

Then argue the opposite side for one minute. Use if I were her… at least once on each side.

Task C · Choices, money and wild places (4 minutes)

Talk about what things are worth, and about places that matter to you.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Describe the highest place you have ever climbed to, and what you could see from it.
  • If you were given a large sum of money on condition that you gave up something you love, what would the condition have to be for you to refuse?
  • A wild place near where you live — describe it, and say what would happen if it disappeared.
  • Should animals be protected by law even when protecting them costs people money? Argue it with conditionals.
  • A secret you were asked to keep — how did you decide whether to keep it? (Keep the details as general as you like.)
  • If you could see one thing from the top of a tall tree anywhere in the world, where would the tree be?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Sylvia gains nothing anyone can measure. What exactly does she come down from the tree with?
  2. The young man loves birds and shoots them; Sylvia loves them and protects them. Which of them would call themselves a nature lover — and who is right?
  3. She is nine. Does the story treat her decision as a child's decision, or as the first adult one of her life?
