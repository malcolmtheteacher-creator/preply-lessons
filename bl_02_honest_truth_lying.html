The Honest Truth About Lying

Expressing degrees of certainty and making fine distinctions

💬 Certainty, Nuance & Boundaries

The Warm-Up

Let's start with a game.

Tell me 3 things about yourself — 2 that are completely true, and 1 that's a complete lie.

I'll try to guess which one is the lie. Then we'll talk about what gave it away — or what almost fooled me.

Great. Now here's something that might surprise you:

The average person lies 1-2 times per day. And most of those lies aren't selfish or malicious. In one study, 61% of the lies people told were "pro-social" — designed to protect someone's feelings, avoid conflict, or show social tact. Your brain lies to be kind.

So what makes a lie different from the truth? And how do we talk about the messy middle — where something isn't quite a lie, but isn't quite honest either?

Today we're exploring why humans lie, and how to express degrees of certainty and make fine distinctions. You'll learn four phrases that let you describe those blurry boundaries with precision.

❌ Vague

"People lie a lot. Most lies are okay because they help people. There's not really a difference between a white lie and a bad lie."

Too general — no nuance

✓ Precise & sophisticated

"It's not so much that lying is always wrong — it's more that we judge lies by their intent. There's a fine line between protecting someone's feelings and manipulating them. That depends entirely on how you define honesty in relationships."

Makes distinctions, acknowledges complexity, questions definitions

Making Distinctions & Challenging Certainty

These four phrases help you express nuance, make fine distinctions, and question how we define things. Click each one to explore it:

"It's not so much that... it's more that..." Click to expand
MEANING

Reject a simple view, then offer a more nuanced one. You're not saying the first thing is completely wrong — just incomplete or misleading.

FORM

It's not so much that [simple view]. It's more that [the real / more complex truth].

PRONUNCIATION

Stress: "It's NOT so much that... It's MORE that..." Pause before "more" to build the contrast.

Example in context

"It's not so much that lying is always wrong. It's more that we evaluate lies based on intent and context. A lie told to spare someone's feelings is fundamentally different from one told to gain advantage."

Now you try:

Use this phrase to reject an oversimplification — about honesty, relationships, work, or anything else. Start with a simple view, then show the real complexity.

"That depends entirely on how you define..." Click to expand
MEANING

Challenge the question itself. Instead of answering yes/no, point out that the answer depends on what the words actually mean. You're questioning the framework.

FORM

That depends entirely on how you define [the key word/concept]. If [definition A], then... If [definition B], then...

PRONUNCIATION
/dɪˈfaɪn/ — stress on the second syllable. "That depends ENtirely on how you DEfine..." Sounds thoughtful and deliberate.

Example in context

"Is that a lie? That depends entirely on how you define honesty. If honesty means saying everything you think, then yes. If it means not actively deceiving someone, then no."

Now you try:

Take a yes/no question and show that the answer depends on definitions. Practice with questions about ethics, relationships, work, or social behaviour.

"There's a fine line between... and..." Click to expand
MEANING

Identify a boundary that's easy to cross. The two things look different, but they're dangerously close. You're warning about a threshold.

FORM

There's a fine line between [one thing] and [another]. One is [X], the other is [Y], but the difference is subtle / easily crossed / hard to spot.

PRONUNCIATION

"There's a FINE line between..." Stress "fine" to emphasise how thin the boundary is. Often followed by a pause before explaining what separates them.

Example in context

"There's a fine line between protecting someone's feelings and manipulating them. Both involve withholding truth, but one comes from care and the other from control. The intention matters."

Now you try:

Identify two similar things that are actually quite different — about honesty, friendship, leadership, or any situation where boundaries matter.

"To what extent is it true that...?" Click to expand
MEANING

Question how universally or completely true something is. It's not "is this true?" but "how true?" — allowing for degrees, exceptions, or partial truth.

FORM

To what extent is it true that [statement]? This assumes the statement has some truth, but questions how much / how widely / in what situations.

PRONUNCIATION

"To what exTENT is it TRUE that..." Rising intonation throughout, with stress on "extent" and "true". Sounds analytical and measured.

Example in context

"To what extent is it true that people always prefer honesty? Some do — but others prefer comfort, tact, or protection from difficult truths. The answer depends on context, culture, and individual values."

Now you try:

Take a common belief or statement and question how universally true it is. Look for exceptions, contexts, or degrees of truth.

The Evidence

Read each finding. Then use the target phrase to discuss what it means — don't just summarise, explore the implications and complexity.

📊 DePaulo's Lie Study (1996): The Prevalence & Nature of Lying

Barbara DePaulo tracked how often people lie. The result: 1-2 times per day on average. But here's the surprising part — 61% of those lies were "pro-social": designed to protect someone's feelings, avoid social awkwardness, or be kind. Only 4% of lies were told purely for personal gain. Most lies aren't selfish. They're social.

Use "It's not so much that... it's more that..." — How would you correct the common assumption about lying?

🧠 Children & Cognitive Development (Talwar & Lee, 2008)

Children who learn to lie early (around age 2-3) actually score higher on tests of cognitive control, theory of mind, and IQ — as long as the lies are told for strategic reasons, not impulse. The ability to lie requires complex thinking: understanding what someone else believes, planning deception, monitoring your own behaviour. Lying and intelligence are linked.

Use "That depends entirely on how you define..." — Is learning to lie a sign of development or of moral decline? What does it depend on?

⚖️ Intent vs. Content: How We Judge Lies (Serota et al., 2010)

Research on how people evaluate lies reveals a surprising pattern: we judge lies based primarily on intent, not on the size of the lie. A small lie told for selfish gain is seen as worse than a large lie told to protect someone's feelings. We also distinguish between omission (leaving out true information) and commission (actively stating something false). A white lie and a vicious lie use similar techniques, but our moral judgment depends entirely on why.

Use "There's a fine line between... and..." — What boundaries matter in lying? What separates acceptable deception from unacceptable deception?

📋 Polygraph Tests & Hiring (National Academy of Sciences, 2003)

Lie detection tests (polygraphs) measure physiological responses — sweating, heart rate, breathing — but they're only 65-70% accurate. Yet many organisations still use them in hiring and security screening. This creates a paradox: we know the test isn't reliable, yet we treat it as authoritative. We've created a system that we don't actually trust, but we trust it anyway.

Use "To what extent is it true that...?" — Is the polygraph a valid measure of dishonesty? To what extent can any test actually detect lying?

Social Media & Authenticity

Your friend posts a holiday photo with the caption "Living my best life." You know they're going through a terrible time. Is the post a lie?

Try: "It's not so much that the caption is false... it's more that..." / "That depends entirely on how you define honesty on social media..."

Workplace Tact

Your colleague asks "Does this presentation look good?" It doesn't. You want to be honest but also kind. What do you say — and is there a "right" answer?

Try: "There's a fine line between constructive criticism and unnecessary cruelty..." / "That depends entirely on how you define your role as a colleague..."

Everyday Politeness

"How was your day?" "Fine." Is this a lie? Does it matter? What's the difference between this and a deliberate deception?

Try: "It's not so much that 'fine' is dishonest... it's more that it's a social convention..." / "To what extent is it true that politeness requires complete honesty?"

Technology & Truth

AI can now generate realistic fake images, videos, and voices. Has lying become fundamentally different in the digital age? Can you even trust what you see?

Try: "It's not so much that lying is new... it's more that the technology has changed..." / "To what extent can we trust digital media when...?"

Cultural Values & Truth

In some cultures, direct honesty is valued above all. In others, protecting face (saving someone from embarrassment) matters more. Who decides what counts as a lie?

Try: "That depends entirely on how you define honesty in different cultural contexts..." / "There's a fine line between respecting cultural values and enabling harmful deception..."

Use "There's a fine line between... and..." in a new sentence — about anything.

No single correct answer — the point is that you can produce the phrase. E.g. "There's a fine line between confidence and arrogance. One is self-assured; the other dismisses others' views."

Use "It's not so much that... it's more that..." in a new sentence.

E.g. "It's not so much that money doesn't buy happiness — it's more that money reduces unhappiness, which is different from creating joy."

Use "That depends entirely on how you define..." to challenge a yes/no question.

E.g. "Does artificial intelligence threaten jobs? That depends entirely on how you define threat. If job loss, then yes. If job transformation, then perhaps. If new job creation elsewhere, then maybe not."
