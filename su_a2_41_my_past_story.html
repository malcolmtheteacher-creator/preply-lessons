Thinking About Money

Money is part of everyone's life. We earn it, spend it, save it, and sometimes lose it! But money stories are often more interesting than we think. They can teach us about priorities, values, and who we are.

In this lesson, you'll tell a personal story about money — something that happened in your past that involved cash, decisions, or interesting moments.

Think About These Questions:

  • How much money do you usually spend each day?
  • Is having lots of money important to you?
  • "Money can't buy happiness." Do you agree or disagree? Why?
  • "The best things in life are free." Do you agree or disagree? Why?

Money Vocabulary

Click each word or phrase to see the meaning.

win money
Receive money as a prize (in a competition, lottery, or game). Example: "I won money in a poker game."
lose money
No longer have money, usually because you spent it badly or lost it. Example: "I lost money on a bad investment."
earn money
Get money from working. Example: "I earn money by teaching English online."
spend money
Use money to buy things. Example: "I spent money on a new laptop."
save money
Keep money for the future instead of spending it now. Example: "I'm saving money for a holiday."
borrow money
Take money from someone — you must return it later. Example: "I borrowed money from my brother to buy a phone."
lend money
Give money to someone — they must return it later. Example: "My friend lent money to me, and I paid her back."
waste money
Spend money on things you don't need. Example: "I wasted money on clothes I never wear."
invest money
Put money into something to make more money in the future. Example: "My parents invested money in a business."
owe money
Need to pay money back to someone. Example: "I owe money to the bank for my car."

Have You Ever...?

Grammar Point: Present Perfect

We use "Have you ever + past participle?" to ask about life experiences. This helps you introduce your story!

Structure: Have + you + ever + past participle?

Example: "Have you ever won money in a competition?" — This is a great way to start telling your story!

Word Linking for Natural Speech

What is Word Linking?

When we speak naturally, words link together. The end of one word connects smoothly to the beginning of the next. This makes your speech sound more native and fluent. Listen carefully to how native speakers connect their words!

Linking Pattern: Consonant + Vowel

When a word ends with a consonant sound and the next word starts with a vowel sound, they link together smoothly.

"I won ↘a prize"

The /n/ sound from "won" links to the /ə/ sound of "a"

"spent ↘a lot"

The /t/ sound from "spent" links to the /ə/ sound of "a"

"I've ↘ever"

The /v/ sound from "I've" links to the /ə/ sound of "ever"

"saved ↘up"

The /d/ sound from "saved" links to the /ʌ/ sound of "up"

Practice Sentences

Read each sentence slowly first, then speed up. Notice how the words flow together:

1. "I won a lot of money in a competition."
2. "Have you ever lost a wallet?"
3. "She spent a fortune on a new car."
4. "I've never earned that much money."

Try This Technique:

2. Read it again at normal speed — let the words connect naturally.
3. Read it at a faster speed — the linking becomes automatic.
4. Record yourself and compare with a native speaker!

Plan Your Story

Think of a time when something interesting happened with money. Maybe you won something, lost something, made a big purchase, or had a funny experience. Use these prompts to organize your thoughts before you tell your story.

Useful Phrases to Start Your Story:

  • "One time I..." — simple and direct
  • "A few years ago..." — shows time passed
  • "I remember when..." — makes it personal
  • "The funny thing was..." — adds interest
  • "Have you ever had a problem with money? Well, let me tell you about the time when..." — great introduction
