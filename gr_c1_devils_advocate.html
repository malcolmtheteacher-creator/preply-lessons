Devil's Advocate

A speaking game for nuanced thinking

Which was harder?

Most people find it harder to criticise what they love. We defend the things we care about. That's human. But today, we're going to practice the opposite: defending positions we don't actually believe in.

Today's goal: Learn to think like a lawyer. Argue both sides of any issue. Discover what you're missing when you only see one perspective.

5 Concession Moves

When you argue the opposite position, these are the language patterns that sound intelligent and balanced. They show you can think in nuance.

Move 1: Acknowledge & Pivot

Start by admitting the other side has a point. Then show what's overlooked.

"While it's true that social media connects people across borders, what's often overlooked is how it fragments our attention and reduces deep conversation."

You're not dismissing their argument. You're adding to it. That's nuance.

Move 2: Steel Man the Opposite

Present the strongest version of the position you disagree with. This shows respect and intelligence.

"The strongest argument against my view would be that money directly removes stress, and less stress does improve wellbeing. That's undeniably true."

You're not attacking a weak version of their argument. You're addressing their best case. That's intellectual honesty.

Move 3: The Spectrum

Show that the issue isn't black and white. There's a spectrum, a gradient, complexity.

"It's not simply that honesty is always better or kindness is always better. There's a spectrum. Sometimes brutal honesty damages relationships. Sometimes protecting someone's feelings through a small lie is kinder."

You're moving beyond "yes or no" thinking. Welcome to adult conversation.

Move 4: Conditional Concession

Accept one premise, then logically follow it to a bigger conclusion.

"If we accept that money genuinely buys happiness up to a certain point, then we also have to accept that money buys happiness unevenly—it matters much more to someone struggling than to someone wealthy."

You're building logical chains. One point leads to another. That's sophisticated thinking.

Move 5: The Synthesis

Bring both sides together. Show where the truth actually lies—in the middle, or in a different dimension entirely.

"The reality is probably somewhere between 'money buys happiness' and 'money doesn't matter.' Money buys security, which allows happiness. But beyond security, more money doesn't reliably make you happier. The relationship is real but not simple."

You're not just splitting the difference. You're showing how both positions are partially true, and where they intersect.

Round 1: Money Buys Happiness

~6 minutes total

1State Your Real Opinion

What do YOU actually think? Do you believe money buys happiness? Say your real opinion out loud. You have 60 seconds. Be honest.

60

2Argue the Opposite

Now you must argue the OPPOSITE of what you just said. If you said "yes, money buys happiness," argue that it doesn't. If you said "no," argue that it does.

You have 2 minutes. Find genuinely strong arguments. Don't be sarcastic or weak. Use the concession moves if you can. Make it believable.

120

3Synthesise Both Sides

Now bring it together. After arguing both sides, what's the truth? You have 90 seconds. Show how both perspectives are partly right.

90

Reflection

Did arguing the opposite change your original opinion? Even slightly? What surprised you about the other position?

Round 2: Social Media Does More Harm Than Good

~6 minutes total

1State Your Real Opinion

What do YOU actually think about social media? Does it do more harm or more good? Say your honest opinion. You have 60 seconds.

60

2Argue the Opposite

Now argue that social media does the opposite of what you said. If you said it's harmful, argue it's beneficial. If you said it's beneficial, argue it's harmful.

You have 2 minutes. Find real, strong arguments. Think about communities, activism, business, connection, mental health — find the legitimate points on the other side.

120

3Synthesise Both Sides

Bring it together. What's the nuanced truth? You have 90 seconds. Show how both positions contain real insight.

90

Reflection

Which concession moves did you use? Did any feel more natural than others? Which one was hardest to use while staying convincing?

Round 3: It's Better to Be Honest Than Kind

~8 minutes total (includes rapid fire)

1State Your Real Opinion

Is it better to be honest or kind? What's your actual position? You have 60 seconds. Think about real situations.

60

2Argue the Opposite

Now argue the opposite. If you said honesty is better, defend kindness. If you said kindness is better, defend honesty.

You have 2 minutes. This is personal — use real examples if you can. Make it compelling.

120

3Synthesise Both Sides

What's the real answer? Show how honesty and kindness can coexist, or where they conflict. You have 90 seconds.

90
