How the photo task works — and how it is lost

Section 3 shows an aviation photograph and six statements. Exactly two are correct. The four wrong ones are engineered to be nearly right — and each one dies on a single precise word. This is not a vocabulary test or a knowledge test. It is a discipline test: claim only what the picture actually shows.

The four ways a wrong option is built

The trickExampleThe killing word
Wrong scope“Both engines are on fire” — when one is.both, all, every, only
Wrong part or side“The main gear has collapsed” — when it's the nose gear; “the right engine” when it's the left.the component, the side
Invented drama“The aircraft is engulfed in flames” — when nothing burns at all.anything you cannot actually see
Wrong state or time“The aircraft is being towed” — when a tug is merely parked nearby; “passengers are evacuating” when the slides hang empty.is being / has been / are …-ing
The golden rule: read each statement as a lawyer, not a storyteller. The story “an emergency landing happened here” may be obviously true — but the statement “the rescue team is evacuating the passengers” is only true if you can point at the rescuers and the passengers and the evacuating, in the pixels.
Warm up — the pointing test (60 seconds)

Look around the room you are in and make six statements about it — four true, two false-but-nearly-true (wrong side, wrong number, wrong state). Say them aloud. Building the traps yourself is the fastest way to see them coming.

The language of seeing — states, parts and honest hedges

Photo statements are written in a compact professional dialect: the parts of aircraft and airfield, the state verbs that describe damage and action, and the hedges — seems, appears — that separate what you see from what you guess.

The state verbs that carry the task

WordMeansIn a statement
intactundamaged, whole“The fuselage appears intact.”
deployedput into position for use“An emergency slide has been deployed.”
deflatedwithout air“The nose tyre seems deflated.”
engulfedcompletely covered (by fire, smoke, water)“The engine is engulfed in flames.”
attendingpresent and dealing with it“Fire appliances are attending.”
overrungone off the end of the runway“The aircraft has overrun into the grass.”
skidded offslid sideways off the surface“The vehicle has skidded off the pavement.”
scrapingdragging along the surface“The tail is scraping the runway.”
vacatedleft, now empty“The runway has been vacated.”
Seems and appears — the professional's hedge: a photo shows surfaces, not certainties. “The tyres seem deflated” and “the wing appears undamaged” claim exactly what a photo can prove — no more. In the exam, options with seems/appears are often correct precisely because they are honestly sized; in your own describing, the hedge makes you sound like an investigator, not a guesser.
1. A photo shows an aircraft stopped on grass beyond the runway end, dust still in the air, gear standing, no vehicles yet. Which statement is safe?
2. Which pair could BOTH be true of one photo — without contradiction?
Speak — states from your own airfield (90 seconds)

Use six of the state words to describe things you have actually seen at work — a deployed slide during a drill, a deflated tug tyre, a vacated stand at night. Real memories glue vocabulary better than any list.

Scene drills — choose exactly two

Two scenes, six statements each, exactly two correct. Click your two, then check. After each drill, the reveal explains which single word killed each wrong option — read those even when you scored two out of two.

Scene 1 — winter morning

Choose the two sentences that best describe the scene, then check.
Why the wrong four die: (a) the aircraft is parked, nothing about it moves — taking off is invented action; (c) only ONE runway is in the picture — both is wrong scope; (e) an engine is visible under the wing — removed contradicts the pixels; (f) no stairs and no passengers appear — you cannot choose what is not there. The true pair describes two different visible actions: the de-icing spray and the plough.

Scene 2 — the stand inspection

Choose the two sentences that best describe the scene, then check.
Why the wrong four die: (b) the figures stand on the ground beside the engine — on the wing is the wrong place; (c) no fuel truck or hose is connected — refuelling is invented action; (e) the right engine is clearly there — a lighter colour is not an absence; (f) one small vehicle is not crowded — wrong scale. The true pair: the people you can point at, and the cones you can point at.

Describe it properly — from choosing to speaking

The multiple choice is only half the skill. The other half — and the half that feeds every speaking mark — is describing a scene yourself, in professional order: the big picture, the aircraft, the people and vehicles, the state of the surfaces — then what you expect next.
The describing frame:
1. “This picture shows…” — one sentence for the whole scene.
2. “The aircraft itself…” — its position and visible state, with seems/appears for judgements.
3. “Around it…” — the people, vehicles, and what each is doing.
4. “What I'd expect next is…” — one professional prediction. This last step is what separates describing from listing.
1. Describe Scene 1 with the frame (2 minutes)

The winter scene: whole picture, the aircraft, the two vehicles and their jobs, the state of the surfaces — then your prediction for the next half hour of operations.
