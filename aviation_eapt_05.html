The non-routine question — how bad is it, really?

In the non-routine recordings something goes wrong: a stuck door, a sick passenger, a suspicious situation, a runway suddenly unusable. The first question is always some form of “What do you understand?” — and the three options usually describe the same event at three different strengths. Your job is to hear exactly how bad the speakers said it was: no worse, no better.

The three-strength pattern

A typical option set looks like this:

1) The aircraft must land immediately.   2) The aircraft needs to land as soon as possible.   3) The aircraft can continue its flight.

All three are about landing. The recording decides which strength is true — and the deciding evidence is usually one phrase: “negative, request normal return” kills option 1; “we prefer to come back” kills option 3. Listen for the sentence that measures the problem.
Beware the drama reflex. When you hear “engine”, “police” or “medical”, the hand wants the most dramatic option. But non-routine is not the same as emergency — most of these recordings are professionals calmly downgrading a scare: “nothing unusual so far”, “negative emergency”, “a slight problem”. The correct option is usually the measured one.
Warm up — three strengths, out loud (60 seconds)

Take this fact: “a passenger feels unwell.” Say it at three strengths: as a routine note, as an urgent request, and as a full emergency — changing only the verbs and one or two words each time. Feeling the scale from the speaking side sharpens the listening side.

Little words, big meaning — where the answer actually lives

The comprehension answer almost never hangs on a technical term. It hangs on a small grammatical word that sets the strength, the time, or the condition. These are the seven that decide most questions.
The wordWhat it decidesHear the difference
may / mightPossibility, not fact“We may be unable to push back” — nothing has failed yet.
must / can'tCertainty, both directions“They must have fixed it” = the evidence says yes. “They can't have” = the evidence says no.
unlessThe escape condition“We hold unless cleared” = we hold, except if clearance comes.
about toImmediacy“About to vacate” = seconds away — but not yet done.
no longer / stillWhether a state has ended“The runway is still occupied” vs “no longer occupied” — opposite answers, one syllable apart.
negativeCancels what follows“Negative emergency” = there is NO emergency — the scary word right after it is the bait.
not more than / fewer thanThe exact boundary“Not more than five” includes five; “fewer than five” excludes it.
1. A pilot reports: “The vibrations have ceased, but we'd prefer to return and have the engine looked at.” What is true?
2. A controller says: “Runway 02 is unavailable as per the notice — they resealed the surface today.” The strongest option is…
Speak the scale (90 seconds)

Improvise one situation — say, smoke seen near a stand — and report it three ways: with may, with must, and with negative + reassurance. Hearing your own voice move the strength dial is the drill.

Words in context — the second question of every audio

Every non-routine audio ends with “What does [word] mean in this context?” — 15 seconds, three near-synonyms. The method: put the candidate word back into the sentence and ask what job it does there. Six rounds, on the clock.

Dialogue drills — non-routine, full format

Two radio conversations where something goes wrong. For each one, press Listen and don't look at the words. Then answer the two questions — press Go to start each 15-second clock. You can listen one more time. When you've answered, open the script to check.

Drill 1 — the passenger who shouldn't be there

P: Ground, Kestrel 7 7 0, we are at the holding point but we need to return to the stand — the cabin crew have found a passenger whose ticket is for a different destination.
GND: Kestrel 7 7 0, roger, is this a security matter or an airline matter?
P: An airline matter only, no security concern — he simply boarded the wrong flight. We need to disembark him and locate his baggage, Kestrel 7 7 0.
GND: Kestrel 7 7 0, understood. Your stand has been taken; taxi to stand 4 2 instead, and be advised the baggage search may take some time.
P: Stand 4 2, copied — we are already running late, please ask the handlers to expedite, Kestrel 7 7 0.
A. 00:15 — What do you understand?
B. 00:15 — What does locate mean in this context?

Drill 2 — the light that may be lying

P: Approach, Vanera 2 1 5, we have an unsafe gear indication — the nose gear shows unlocked. It may be a faulty sensor, but we'd like a low pass so the tower can inspect visually.
APP: Vanera 2 1 5, approved, make a low pass runway 24, the tower will observe. Are you declaring an emergency at this time?
P: Negative for now — if the gear is confirmed unsafe after the pass, we will declare then, Vanera 2 1 5.
APP: Vanera 2 1 5, roger. After the pass, climb to 3,000 feet and expect holding while we coordinate. Fire services will be brought to standby as a precaution.
A. 00:15 — What do you understand?
B. 00:15 — What does on standby mean in this context?

Brief a colleague — turn listening into speaking

The strongest comprehension check there is: retell it. If you can brief the situation accurately in your own words — with the strengths intact — you understood it. If your retelling makes it more dramatic or less conditional than the original, that is precisely the listening error to fix.
1. Brief Drill 2 (90 seconds)

A colleague walks in mid-situation. Brief them on Vanera 215: what the indication showed, why it may be false, what happens next, and under what condition it becomes an emergency. Keep every may, if and for now where the pilot put them.
