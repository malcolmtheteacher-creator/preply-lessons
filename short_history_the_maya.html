The Maya

The civilisation that read the stars, wrote in stone — and never truly disappeared.

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The scribe pressed a sharpened tool into the wet clay, cutting a small, dense glyph — part picture, part sign — that stood for a whole word no letter could spell.
a glyph (n) — a carved or written symbol, especially one used in an ancient picture-and-sound writing system.
2. Night after night the old man climbed the tower with his charts, studying astronomy until he could say, weeks ahead, exactly where the planet Venus would rise.
astronomy (n) — the scientific study of the stars, planets and the movements of the sky.
3. The library held a single surviving codex: a long strip of bark-paper, folded like a screen, its pages covered in painted symbols and bright numbers.
a codex (n) — an ancient hand-made book, especially one written on folded paper or bark; plural codices.
4. The market town did not fall to an enemy. Trade dried up, the wells ran low, families drifted away, and within two generations the whole place had simply begun to collapse.
to collapse (v) — to fall apart and fail completely, often after a long, gradual decline.
5. By marking each sunrise on a great stone wheel, the priests kept a chronology so exact that festivals fell on the right day, year after year, for centuries.
a chronology (n) — an ordered system for fixing the dates of events in time; a record of events in the order they occurred.
6. There was never one king over all of them. Each city-state ruled itself — its own lord, its own army, its own quarrels with the town across the valley.
a city-state (n) — an independent city that governs itself and the land immediately around it, like a tiny country.
7. For two hundred years the marks on the wall were just beautiful shapes; then one scholar worked out the sound behind each one and finally began to decipher them.
to decipher (v) — to work out the meaning of something written in code or in an unknown system.
8. The rains failed one year, then the next, and the next. With no river to fall back on, the fields cracked, the harvest died, and the drought tightened its grip.
a drought (n) — a long period with little or no rain, when water and crops run short.
9. At dawn the priests gathered at the altar to perform the ritual — the same words, the same offerings, the same careful steps their ancestors had followed.
a ritual (n) — a set of actions performed in a fixed order, usually for religious or ceremonial reasons.
10. Above the rooftops rose a steep stone edifice, step upon step climbing to a small temple at the very top, where only the priests were allowed to stand.
an edifice (n) — a large, imposing building, especially one of stone; the Maya crowned theirs with a temple (a building for worship).

The story, in four parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Cities in the forest

Spread across the rainforests of what is now southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize and Honduras, the Maya built one of the great civilisations of the ancient world. Their golden age, which scholars call the Classic period, ran from roughly 250 to 900 AD — overlapping with the height of Rome and lasting long after it fell. At its peak the Maya world may have held many millions of people, far more than the same land supports today.

There was never a single Maya empire. Instead there were dozens of independent city-states, some of which — Tikal, Calakmul, Palenque, Copán — grew into glittering capitals of tens of thousands. Each had its own ruling dynasty, its own gods, and its own long list of rivalries. The great powers, of which Tikal and Calakmul were the largest, fought and schemed against one another for generations, a contest historians have compared to the rival cities of ancient Greece.

Above the forest canopy rose their cities: broad plazas, ball courts, palaces, and steep stone pyramids crowned with temples, the tallest of which climbed more than sixty metres into the sky. To build all this without metal tools, wheels or pack animals was an extraordinary feat — and they did it in one of the harshest landscapes on Earth.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is it wrong to speak of "the Maya empire"?

2. What makes their building achievements especially remarkable?

1. There was no single empire — the Maya were many independent, often rival city-states, each with its own ruler and gods.

2. They raised huge cities and pyramids in dense rainforest without metal tools, the wheel, or pack animals.

Discuss:
  • Many city-states, no central ruler, constant rivalry — yet a shared culture. What might be the strengths, and the dangers, of organising a civilisation that way?

Part 2 — Reading the sky, writing in stone

The Maya, whose calendar was astonishingly precise, tracked the stars for centuries. From flat-topped towers their astronomers followed the sun, the moon and especially Venus, whose movements they could predict years in advance. They calculated the length of the solar year to within minutes of the modern figure, and they understood the idea of zero as a number — something much of the world had not yet grasped.

Time mattered deeply to them. They ran several calendars at once: a 365-day count tied to the seasons, a 260-day sacred round used for rituals, and a vast "Long Count" that numbered the days from a fixed point thousands of years in the past. Almost every important act — a coronation, a war, a temple's dedication — was fixed to an exact date and carved where all could see it.

For they were also writers, the only people in the ancient Americas to develop a full writing system. Their script, of which several hundred signs survive, mixed picture-symbols for whole words with signs for syllables — a true language frozen in stone. They wrote on monuments, on pottery, and in folding bark-paper books, the codices, of which thousands were once made. Tragically, when Spanish priests arrived in the 1500s they burned almost all of them as the work of the devil; only four survive today.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name two things Maya astronomers and mathematicians achieved.

2. What were the codices, and why do so few survive?

1. Any two: they predicted the movements of Venus, calculated the solar year to within minutes, understood zero, and ran several precise calendars at once.

2. Folding bark-paper books of glyphs; Spanish priests burned almost all of them in the 1500s, so only four are left.

Discuss:
  • Only four of thousands of codices survived the burning. What is lost when a writing system, and the books in it, are deliberately destroyed?

Part 3 — The cities fall silent

Then, over roughly a hundred years, something went badly wrong. Across the southern lowlands, one great city after another stopped building, stopped carving dates, and was slowly abandoned. By around 900 AD the proud capitals of the Classic period — Tikal, Copán, Palenque, many of which now lie buried beneath jungle — stood largely empty, their plazas reclaimed by the forest.

For a long time this "collapse" was one of archaeology's great puzzles, a fall about which little could be said with confidence. Today the leading explanation points to climate. Sediment from ancient lakes records a series of severe droughts striking the region in the 8th and 9th centuries — among the worst in thousands of years. In a land of thin soils and no great rivers, where farming depended entirely on the rains, a drought of that length was catastrophic.

But drought alone is probably too simple. Centuries of clearing forest for farmland had worn out the soil; populations had grown vast; and the endless warfare between city-states left rulers ill-placed to cope with crisis. The likeliest answer is a chain reaction, in which failing harvests, overcrowding and political breakdown fed one another until the whole system gave way.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the "Classic Maya collapse" actually describe?

2. Why do most scholars now reject "one single cause" for the collapse?

1. Over about a century (ending around 900 AD), the great southern-lowland cities stopped building, stopped carving dates, and were gradually abandoned.

2. Drought was likely the trigger, but worn-out soil, overpopulation and constant warfare all combined — a chain reaction rather than one cause.

Discuss:
  • A long drought, exhausted land, too many people, leaders distracted by war. How much of that pattern feels relevant to the world right now?

Part 4 — A people who never left

Here the story turns. The word "collapse" is misleading, because the Maya did not vanish. The cities of the southern jungle emptied, but in the north, cities such as Chichén Itzá rose and flourished for centuries more. And when Spanish ships appeared on the coast in the 1500s, millions of Maya were still living on their ancestral land. They were conquered, but never erased.

Today more than seven million Maya people live across Mexico and Central America, speaking some thirty Maya languages descended from the tongue once carved on those stones. They farm the same valleys, keep festivals that echo the old calendar, and increasingly reclaim a heritage long pushed to the margins by the establishment.

Their ancestors' writing, meanwhile, has been brought back to life. For most of the modern era the glyphs were thought unreadable. Then, in the 1950s, a Russian scholar named Yuri Knorozov, working far away in Moscow, argued that the signs recorded the sounds of the Maya language. He was ridiculed at first, but he was right. Scholars have since deciphered the great majority of the script — and the silent stones have started, at last, to speak: not of myths, but of real kings, real wars, and real dates, in the words of the people who carved them.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Give two reasons why "the Maya disappeared" is false.

2. What was Yuri Knorozov's key insight, and what did it make possible?

1. Any two: northern cities like Chichén Itzá thrived after the southern collapse; millions of Maya met the Spanish in the 1500s; over seven million Maya, speaking Maya languages, live in the region today.

2. He argued the glyphs recorded the sounds of the Maya language; this let scholars decipher most of the script and read real Maya history.

Discuss:
  • We talk easily about civilisations that "died." How does it change the story to remember that, here, the people are very much alive?

Advanced relative clauses: whose / many of which

The Maya story is packed with nouns that deserve a second clause of detail — a calendar that was precise, cities that now lie buried, a people about whom little was known. Advanced relative clauses let you fold that extra information smoothly into one sentence, the way a good storyteller does, instead of stopping and starting again.

Non-defining clauses — extra information, set off by commas:
A non-defining relative clause adds detail you could remove without breaking the sentence. It sits between commas (or after one).
• The Maya, whose calendar was astonishingly precise, tracked the stars for centuries.
• They built great cities, many of which now lie buried beneath jungle.
Four patterns to master:
• whose = "of whom / of which" for possession (people and things): astronomers, whose towers still stand…
• quantifier + of which / of whom — many of which, some of whom, several of which, the largest of which, all of whom: dozens of city-states, some of which grew huge. (Note: of which, never of that.)
• preposition + whom / which in a formal register: a fall about which little could be said; a people about whom little was known.
• the extent to which — a fixed formal phrase for "how far / how much": scholars still debate the extent to which drought caused the collapse.

1. Add the right relative word

Complete each non-defining clause with whose, of which or of whom.

1. The Maya, writing was the only full script in the ancient Americas, carved dates into stone.

2. They produced thousands of codices, only four survive today.

3. The Spanish priests, many saw the books as the work of the devil, burned them.

4. There were dozens of city-states, the largest were Tikal and Calakmul.

1. whose (possession: the writing of the Maya)

2. of which (four out of the things — codices)

3. of whom (many out of the people — priests)

4. of which (the largest out of the things — city-states)

2. Join the two sentences into one

Combine each pair into a single sentence with a non-defining relative clause. There's more than one good answer — read yours aloud.

1. The astronomers tracked Venus. Their predictions were years ahead of the event.

2. The cities were abandoned around 900 AD. Most of them now lie under jungle.

3. Knorozov made one bold claim. Scholars long doubted it.

1. The astronomers, whose predictions were years ahead of the event, tracked Venus.

2. The cities, most of which now lie under jungle, were abandoned around 900 AD.

3. Knorozov made a bold claim, which scholars long doubted. (More formal: …a bold claim, the truth of which scholars long doubted.)

3. The formal touch — preposition + which/whom, and "the extent to which"

Complete each sentence with the more formal pattern in brackets.

1. Before the 1950s, the Maya were a people little was known. (preposition + relative pronoun)

2. The collapse was a disaster almost nothing could be said with certainty. (preposition + relative pronoun)

3. Historians still argue about the to drought caused the fall. (a fixed phrase meaning ‘how far / how much’)

1. about whom (a people about whom little was known)

2. about which (a disaster about which almost nothing could be said)

3. extent … which (the extent to which drought caused the fall)

Carry these into the Speak tab: when you describe a place, a person or a people, try adding a comma and a "whose…", "many of which…" or "about whom…" clause to layer in detail.
