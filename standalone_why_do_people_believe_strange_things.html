🧠 Why Do People Believe Strange Things?

The Psychology of Conspiracy Theories

Standalone Conversation Lesson | C1-C2

The Strange Case of Paloma

What would make a Cambridge graduate with an 80% chance of survival refuse the treatment that could save her life?

Paloma's Story

In late 2023, Paloma Shemirani – a 23-year-old Cambridge graduate with her whole life ahead of her – received devastating news. Doctors found a mass in her lung: non-Hodgkin's lymphoma.

But there was hope. Her oncologists told her she had an 80% chance of recovery with chemotherapy. For most people, this would be a clear choice.

Paloma refused the treatment. Seven months later, she was dead.

80% Paloma's survival rate if she had accepted chemotherapy

Why Did She Refuse?

Paloma's mother, Kate Shemirani, was a former NHS nurse who had become one of the UK's most prominent conspiracy theorists. During the COVID-19 pandemic, Kate told her followers that vaccines were designed to "change people's DNA" and that chemotherapy was "pumping mustard gas into your veins."

She was struck off the nursing register in 2021 for spreading medical misinformation that "put the public at significant risk of harm."

When Paloma was diagnosed, her mother allegedly spent two days at the hospital and texted Paloma's boyfriend: "TELL PALOMA NOT TO SIGN OR VERBALLY CONSENT TO CHEMO OR ANY TREATMENT."

A Childhood Filled with Conspiracy Theories

Paloma's brothers, Gabriel (her twin) and Sebastian, describe growing up with conspiracy theories as the "soundtrack" to their childhood:

  • The Royal Family were "shape-shifting lizards"
  • 9/11 was an "inside job"
  • The Sandy Hook school shooting was staged
  • COVID-19 was a "plandemic"

After their sister's death, the brothers went public. Gabriel told BBC Panorama: "I wasn't able to stop my sister from dying. But it would mean the world to me if I could make it that she wasn't just another in a long line of people that die in this way."

37% of Americans believe the FDA is hiding natural cures for cancer because of pressure from drug companies (Oliver & Wood research)

The Bigger Picture

Why Do People Believe?

Research shows that conspiracy thinking is driven by three core needs:

  1. Epistemic – The need to understand why things happen
  2. Existential – The need to feel safe and in control
  3. Social – The need to feel special or belong to a group

Interestingly, research shows that the best predictor of believing one conspiracy theory is believing another – even if they contradict each other. Some people simultaneously believed Princess Diana was murdered AND that she faked her own death!

50%+ of Americans believe at least one conspiracy theory (University of Chicago research)

Key Expressions (Part 1)

👆 Click each card to reveal meaning, examples, and practice prompts

1. "To fall down the rabbit hole"
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: To become deeply absorbed in a topic or interest, especially one that leads you to increasingly extreme or unusual beliefs. Often suggests going deeper and deeper into something without realising how far you've gone.
Example: "Paloma's brothers said their mother fell down the rabbit hole of conspiracy theories during their childhood – starting with 9/11 theories and eventually believing COVID was a 'plandemic.'"
NOW YOU TRY: Tell your partner about a time you or someone you know "fell down the rabbit hole" of something – it doesn't have to be conspiracy theories! (YouTube recommendations? A new hobby? True crime podcasts?)
2. "To buy into something"
Formality: Neutral/Informal
Meaning: To believe and accept something completely, especially an idea, claim, or story. Often implies believing something that may not be entirely true or reliable.
Example: "Research shows that people who buy into one conspiracy theory are likely to buy into others – even contradictory ones. It's less about the specific theory and more about a general mindset."
NOW YOU TRY: What's something many people "buy into" that you think is questionable? (A health trend? A business philosophy? A social media myth?)
💡 Speaking Tip: Both expressions suggest critical thinking about beliefs – but without being aggressive. They're useful when you want to discuss beliefs respectfully but maintain some skepticism.

Key Expressions (Part 2)

👆 Click each card to reveal meaning, examples, and practice prompts

3. "To be taken in by something/someone"
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: To be deceived or fooled by something or someone. Suggests being convinced by something that turned out to be false or misleading.
Example: "Paloma was taken in by her mother's alternative health advice. Hospital staff documented concerns about 'parental influence' – they could see what was happening but couldn't stop it."
NOW YOU TRY: Have you ever been taken in by something that turned out to be false? (A scam? Fake news? Someone's convincing story?)
4. "To go against the grain"
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: To do or believe something different from what most people do or believe; to resist the normal or expected way of doing things.
Example: "Kate Shemirani built her online following by positioning herself as someone brave enough to go against the grain of mainstream medicine. Her followers see her as a truth-teller fighting the establishment."
NOW YOU TRY: In what ways do you "go against the grain" in your life? (Career choices? Lifestyle? Opinions?) Is going against the grain always good?
5. "To have second thoughts"
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: To begin to doubt a decision you've made or start to change your mind about something. To reconsider.
Example: "According to her boyfriend, Paloma seemed to have second thoughts about refusing treatment at times, but by then she was isolated from friends and family who might have helped her reconsider."
NOW YOU TRY: Describe a time you had second thoughts about an important decision. Did you change your mind, or did you stick with your original choice?
💡 Quick Review – All 5 Expressions:
  1. Fall down the rabbit hole – Get deeply absorbed (often too deep)
  2. Buy into something – Believe/accept something (possibly questionable)
  3. Be taken in by – Be deceived or fooled
  4. Go against the grain – Do/believe differently from most people
  5. Have second thoughts – Start to doubt or reconsider
