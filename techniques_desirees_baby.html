SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B2/C1 · 50 min

Désirée's Baby

Kate Chopin (1893) — a proud man judges his wife by the colour of their child's skin, and the judgement turns on him

Read in three parts

Kate Chopin wrote this story in 1893, but set it in the Louisiana of the 1850s — a slave-holding society in which a person's ancestry decided whether they were counted as free or as property. That brutal system is the ground the story stands on, and Chopin wrote to expose its cruelty, not to endorse it. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The last lines change the meaning of everything before them; do not skip ahead.

A note before you read. This story turns on the racism of its time, including the enslavement of Black people and the idea that ancestry could make a person "less" human. Those ideas are shown so that Chopin — and we — can judge them. The word "quadroon" appears in the text: in the 1850s it labelled a person of partly African descent. It is not a word we use today; it is kept here only because it is Chopin's own, and because the story's whole purpose is to turn that labelling against the man who trusts it.

Part 1 — Désirée and the marriage

Madame Valmondé had found her, a toddling little girl, asleep in the shadow of the big stone pillar at the gateway of the Valmondé estate. She grew to be beautiful and gentle, affectionate and sincere — the idol of Valmondé. It was no wonder, when she stood one day against the stone pillar in whose shadow she had lain eighteen years before, that Armand Aubigny riding by and seeing her there, had fallen in love with her.

That was the way all the Aubignys fell in love, as if struck by a pistol shot. The wonder was that he had not loved her before; for he had known her since his father brought him home from Paris, a boy of eight. The passion that awoke in him that day swept along like an avalanche, or like a prairie fire, or like anything that drives headlong over all obstacles.

Monsieur Valmondé grew practical and wanted things well considered: that is, the girl's obscure origin. Désirée was nameless. What did it matter about a name when he could give her one of the oldest and proudest in Louisiana? Armand ordered the corbeille from Paris, and contained himself with what patience he could until it arrived; then they were married.

Madame Valmondé had not seen Désirée and the baby for four weeks. When she reached L'Abri she shuddered at the first sight of it, as she always did. It was a sad-looking place. Big, solemn oaks grew close to it, and their thick-leaved, far-reaching branches shadowed it like a pall. Young Aubigny's rule was a strict one, too, and under it his negroes had forgotten how to be gay, as they had been during the old master's easy-going and indulgent lifetime.

Pause and discuss:
  • Désirée is a foundling — no one knows where she came from. In this society, why is her "obscure origin" a danger waiting to happen, even though everyone loves her?
  • Armand falls in love "as if struck by a pistol shot," and his passion is compared to an avalanche and a prairie fire. What do those images warn us about how he loves — and how he might turn?
  • The narrator slips in that under Armand the enslaved people "had forgotten how to be gay." What has Chopin quietly told us about the man before the crisis even begins?

Part 2 — The baby, and Armand's rejection

The little cochon de lait! Look at his legs, mamma, and his hands and fingernails — real fingernails. Zandrine had to cut them this morning. Désirée had laughed with delight; but as the weeks passed, a strange, an awful change came into the house. First it was a disquieting suggestion; an air of mystery among the blacks; unexpected visits from far-off neighbours who could hardly account for their coming. Then a strange, an awful change in Armand's manner, which she dared not ask him to explain.

When the baby was about three months old, Désirée awoke one day to the conviction that there was something in the air menacing her peace. She sat in her room, one hot afternoon, and looked at the child, then at the boy who fanned him — a little quadroon boy. She looked from one to the other, over and over. She grew pale and could not move.

She sent for Armand. When he came, she did not lift her eyes to look at him.

"Armand," she called to him, in a voice which must have stabbed him, "look at our child. What does it mean? Tell me."

He coldly but gently loosened her fingers from about his arm and pushed the hand away from him.

"Tell me what it means!" she cried despairingly.

"It means," he answered lightly, "that the child is not white; it means that you are not white."

A quick conception of all this injustice made her more courageous. She stretched her hand toward the letters she had begun to write.

"It is a lie; it is not true, I am white! Look at my hair, it is brown; and my eyes are gray, Armand, you know they are gray. And my skin is fair," seizing his wrist. "Look at my hand; whiter than yours, Armand," she laughed hysterically.

"As white as La Blanche's," he returned cruelly; and went away, leaving her alone with their child.

Pause and discuss:
  • Armand's love vanishes the instant he suspects the child is not white. What does this show about what he actually loved — his wife, or the "proud old name" she carried for him?
  • Désirée defends herself by pointing to her fair skin and grey eyes. Why is it tragic that she is forced to argue on the racists' own terms — as if the accusation itself were reasonable?
  • Armand answers "It means that you are not white" as if that settles everything. Where does his certainty come from, and what has he not thought to question?

Part 3 — Her disappearance, and the letter

She wrote to her mother. "My mother, they tell me I am not white. Armand has told me I am not white. For God's sake tell them it is not true. You must know it is not true. I shall die. I must die. I cannot be so unhappy, and live."

The answer that came was as brief: "My own Désirée: Come home to Valmondé; back to your mother who loves you. Come with your child."

When the letter reached her she went with it to Armand's study, and laid it open upon the desk before which he sat. She was like a stone image: silent, white, motionless after she placed it there.

In silence he ran his cold eyes over the written words. He said nothing. "Shall I go, Armand?" she asked in tones sharp with agonized suspense.

"Yes, go."

"Do you want me to go?"

"Yes, I want you to go."

She turned away like one stunned by a blow, and walked slowly towards the door, hoping he would call her back. He said nothing. She did not take the broad, beaten road which led to the far-off plantation of Valmondé. She walked across a deserted field, where the stubble bruised her tender feet, so delicately shod, and tore her thin gown to shreds. She disappeared among the reeds and willows that grew thick along the banks of the deep, sluggish bayou; and she did not come back again.

Some weeks later there was a curious scene enacted at L'Abri. In the centre of the smoothly swept back yard was a great bonfire. Armand Aubigny sat in the wide hallway and it was he who dealt out to a half dozen negroes the material which kept this fire ablaze. A graceful cradle of willow, with all its dainty furbishings, was laid upon the pyre. And then there was the corbeille, and finally the letters — little scribblings that Désirée had sent him during the days of their espousal. But among these last was a scrap; the remnant of an old letter left in a drawer. It was part of a letter from his mother to his father. Armand read it. She was thanking God for the blessing of her husband's love:

"But above all," she wrote, "night and day, I thank the good God for having so arranged our lives that our dear Armand will never know that his mother, who adores him, belongs to the race that is cursed with the brand of slavery."

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • Read the mother's letter again. Armand condemned his wife and sent his own child away over the very same ancestry he himself carries. What has Chopin done to his certainty in a single paragraph?
  • The letter uses the phrase "the race that is cursed with the brand of slavery." The curse is not in anyone's blood — it is in the society that brands people. How does the story make that point without ever saying it outright?
  • Désirée is gone into the bayou with the baby before the truth surfaces. Why does Chopin refuse to let Armand's discovery undo the harm — and what is she saying about prejudice by doing so?
  • Your turn: Armand ruins the people he loves by trusting an assumption he never examined. When have you seen someone act with total certainty on a belief that later proved false — and what did that certainty cost?

Grammar — Mixed Conditionals

This story is built on a chain of "if onlys." If Armand had not been so proud, Désirée would still be alive. If he were a gentler man, he would not have sent her away. Notice that each of those sentences crosses two different times — a past cause with a present result, or a present truth with a past result. That crossing is exactly what a mixed conditional does.

1. Past condition → present result

Use this when something in the past still shapes the present. The pattern is If + past perfect (had + past participle), then would + base verb:

"If Armand had known the truth, he would not be so proud now."
"If Désirée had never been a foundling, no one would doubt her today."

What Chopin does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are the reason a story published in 1893 still lands like a slap today — and why it stands so firmly against the prejudice it depicts.

1. The twist — the letter that recasts everything

For most of the story we watch Armand judge Désirée and read her as the one with the hidden "flaw." Then a single scrap of an old letter — his mother's — reveals that the African ancestry he condemned is his own. The discovery reframes every earlier line: his coldness, his certainty, his "proud old name." Chopin withholds the truth until the last paragraph so that the reversal, when it comes, forces us to re-read the whole story with new eyes.

2. Irony — the accuser is the accused

The engine of the story is irony. Armand builds his whole cruelty on the belief that Black ancestry makes a person lesser — and he is the one who carries it. His sentence "It means that you are not white" indicts him more than anyone. Chopin turns the prejudice back on the person who wields it, showing that the system he trusts cannot even reliably identify its own victims. The irony is not there for a clever shock; it exposes the whole idea of "race" as a fiction that harms indiscriminately.

3. Foreshadowing and symbolism — light, dark, and the fire

The ending is prepared from the first page. Chopin foreshadows it through Désirée's "obscure origin" and Armand's long-absent, Paris-dwelling father. She weaves a pattern of light and dark — skin argued over as "whiter," the house "shadowed like a pall," Désirée standing "white, motionless" — so that colour is never neutral. And the final bonfire symbolises Armand's attempt to burn away his past; instead the flames deliver the one letter that damns him. The fire meant to erase the truth is what surfaces it.

4. Social commentary — the story judges the prejudice

Chopin does not merely describe a slave society; she critiques it. By making the proud slaveholder the secret bearer of the ancestry he despises, she dismantles the logic of racial hierarchy from the inside. The mother's letter names slavery as a "brand" — a mark imposed by society, not a fact of blood — and so the story implicates the whole system rather than any bloodline. Written by a white Southern woman in 1893, it quietly condemns the world she was born into.

Task B · Prejudice and the assumptions we don't examine (4 minutes)

Armand acts with total certainty on an assumption he never once questions — and it destroys the people he loves. Talk about a time you saw someone (or a group, or an institution) treat an assumption as unquestionable fact. Take it in three steps:

  1. what the assumption was, and why it went unexamined;
  2. who was harmed by it, and how;
  3. what it would have taken for someone to stop and question it.

Use at least one mixed conditional ("If they had questioned it, things would be different now") and two effect verbs from the Technique tab, such as exposes or implicates.

Task C · If things had been different (3 minutes)

Task D · How an ending can reframe a whole story (4 minutes)

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. Chopin makes the racist the secret victim of his own prejudice. Is that an effective way to argue against racism, or does it risk letting the reader off too easily? Why?
  2. Désirée is forced to defend herself on the racists' own terms — "look how fair my skin is." Why is even her defence a kind of trap, and how does the story see through it?
  3. The mother's letter calls slavery a "brand" — a mark imposed by society, not a fact of blood. How does that single word carry the story's whole argument?
  4. The truth surfaces too late to save anyone. Is Chopin saying prejudice can never be undone, only exposed — and does that make the ending more powerful, or more bleak?
