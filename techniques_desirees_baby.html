1. The convent kept a book of every foundling left at its door — children who arrived with no name, no history, and no one to claim them.
a foundling (n) — a young child abandoned by unknown parents and discovered and raised by others.
2. He came from an obscure family; nobody in the parish could say where his people were from or who they had been.
obscure (adj) — not clearly known or understood; hidden, uncertain in origin.
3. Grey moss hung from the branches and lay over the whole plantation like a pall, as if the house itself were in mourning.
a pall (n) — a dark cloth spread over a coffin; figuratively, a gloom or heaviness that hangs over a place.
4. Their grandfather was endlessly indulgent — he forgave every fault, laughed at every prank, and refused the children nothing.
indulgent (adj) — lenient; readily allowing, forgiving, or giving in to others.
5. There was something disquieting in the neighbours' smiles; nothing was said, and yet she felt watched wherever she went.
disquieting (adj) — causing unease or worry; subtly disturbing.
6. The letter contained no open threat, yet every polite line of it felt menacing, like a hand closing slowly around her wrist.
menacing (adj) — threatening; suggesting that harm or danger is approaching.
7. "Nobody will believe me," she said despairingly, letting the papers slip from her hands to the floor.
despairingly (adv) — in a way that shows the complete loss of hope.
8. He waited for the verdict in agonized silence, twisting his hat in his fingers until the brim tore.

sluggish (adj) — moving slowly, heavily, or with little energy.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss. The last lines change the meaning of everything before them — don't skip ahead.

Before you read. Kate Chopin wrote this story in 1893, but set it in the Louisiana of the 1850s — a slave-holding society in which a person's ancestry decided whether they were counted as free or as property. That brutal system is the ground the story stands on, and Chopin wrote to expose its cruelty, not to endorse it. The text keeps some of the language of its time — the word "quadroon", for example, labelled a person of partly African descent. These are not words we use today; they are kept here only because they are Chopin's own, and because the story's whole purpose is to turn that labelling against the man who trusts it.

Part 1 — Désirée and the marriage

Madame Valmondé had found her, a toddling little girl, asleep in the shadow of the big stone pillar at the gateway of the Valmondé estate. She grew to be beautiful and gentle, affectionate and sincere — the idol of Valmondé. It was no wonder, when she stood one day against the stone pillar in whose shadow she had lain eighteen years before, that Armand Aubigny riding by and seeing her there, had fallen in love with her.

That was the way all the Aubignys fell in love, as if struck by a pistol shot. The wonder was that he had not loved her before; for he had known her since his father brought him home from Paris, a boy of eight. The passion that awoke in him that day swept along like an avalanche, or like a prairie fire, or like anything that drives headlong over all obstacles.

Monsieur Valmondé grew practical and wanted things well considered: that is, the girl's obscure origin. Désirée was nameless. What did it matter about a name when he could give her one of the oldest and proudest in Louisiana? Armand ordered the corbeille from Paris, and contained himself with what patience he could until it arrived; then they were married.

Madame Valmondé had not seen Désirée and the baby for four weeks. When she reached L'Abri she shuddered at the first sight of it, as she always did. It was a sad-looking place. Big, solemn oaks grew close to it, and their thick-leaved, far-reaching branches shadowed it like a pall. Young Aubigny's rule was a strict one, too, and under it his negroes had forgotten how to be gay, as they had been during the old master's easy-going and indulgent lifetime.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is Désirée, and why does Monsieur Valmondé worry about the marriage?

2. What kind of place is L'Abri under Armand's rule?

1. A foundling — found asleep at the stone pillar of the Valmondé gateway as a toddler. Her origin is obscure and she has no name of her own, a dangerous thing in a society obsessed with ancestry. Armand doesn't care: he will give her his own proud name.

2. A sad-looking place, shadowed by solemn oaks "like a pall". Armand's rule is strict; the enslaved people there "had forgotten how to be gay", as they had been under his easy-going, indulgent father.

Discuss:
  • Armand falls in love "as if struck by a pistol shot", and his passion is compared to an avalanche and a prairie fire. What do those images warn us about how this man loves — and what he might do when love turns?

Part 2 — The baby, and Armand's verdict

The little cochon de lait! Look at his legs, mamma, and his hands and fingernails — real fingernails. Zandrine had to cut them this morning. Désirée had laughed with delight; but as the weeks passed, a strange, an awful change came into the house. First it was a disquieting suggestion; an air of mystery among the blacks; unexpected visits from far-off neighbours who could hardly account for their coming. Then a strange, an awful change in Armand's manner, which she dared not ask him to explain.

When the baby was about three months old, Désirée awoke one day to the conviction that there was something in the air menacing her peace. She sat in her room, one hot afternoon, and looked at the child, then at the boy who fanned him — a little quadroon boy. She looked from one to the other, over and over. She grew pale and could not move.

She sent for Armand. When he came, she did not lift her eyes to look at him. "Armand," she called to him, in a voice which must have stabbed him, "look at our child. What does it mean? Tell me."

He coldly but gently loosened her fingers from about his arm and pushed the hand away from him. "Tell me what it means!" she cried despairingly.

"It means," he answered lightly, "that the child is not white; it means that you are not white."

A quick conception of all this injustice made her more courageous. "It is a lie; it is not true, I am white! Look at my hair, it is brown; and my eyes are gray, Armand, you know they are gray. And my skin is fair," seizing his wrist. "Look at my hand; whiter than yours, Armand," she laughed hysterically.

"As white as La Blanche's," he returned cruelly; and went away, leaving her alone with their child.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What signs tell Désirée something is wrong before anyone says a word?

2. What does Armand declare the baby's appearance to mean, and how does he treat Désirée?

1. A disquieting suggestion in the air: an air of mystery in the house, unexplained visits from far-off neighbours, and an awful change in Armand's manner that she dares not question.

2. He declares, "lightly", that the child is not white — and therefore that Désirée is not white. He coldly pushes her hand away, answers her cruelly, and leaves her alone with their child.

Discuss:
  • Désirée defends herself with the racists' own measuring stick — grey eyes, fair skin, a hand "whiter than yours". Why is it tragic that this is the only defence her world leaves her?

Part 3 — The bayou, and the letter

She wrote to her mother. "My mother, they tell me I am not white. For God's sake tell them it is not true. I shall die. I cannot be so unhappy, and live." The answer that came was brief: "My own Désirée: Come home to your mother who loves you. Come with your child."

She went with the letter to Armand's study and laid it open on the desk before him. She was like a stone image: silent, white, motionless. In silence he ran his cold eyes over the written words. "Shall I go, Armand?" she asked in tones sharp with agonized suspense.

"Yes, go." "Do you want me to go?" "Yes, I want you to go."

She turned away like one stunned by a blow, and walked slowly towards the door, hoping he would call her back. He said nothing. She did not take the broad, beaten road to Valmondé. She walked across a deserted field, where the stubble bruised her tender feet and tore her thin gown to shreds; and she disappeared among the reeds and willows along the banks of the deep, sluggish bayou, and did not come back again.

Some weeks later a curious scene was enacted at L'Abri. In the centre of the back yard burned a great bonfire, and it was Armand who fed it. The willow cradle was laid upon the pyre; then the corbeille; then the letters Désirée had sent him in the days of their espousal. Among them was the remnant of an old letter, left in a drawer — from his mother to his father. Armand read it. She was thanking God for the blessing of her husband's love:

"But above all," she wrote, "night and day, I thank the good God for having so arranged our lives that our dear Armand will never know that his mother, who adores him, belongs to the race that is cursed with the brand of slavery."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Madame Valmondé's answer offer, and what does Désirée actually do?

2. What does Armand find among the things he is burning, and what does it reveal?

1. Her mother offers her a home: "Come home… Come with your child." But Désirée does not take the road to Valmondé. She walks across the deserted field with the baby, disappears into the reeds along the deep, sluggish bayou, and does not come back.

2. The remnant of a letter from his own mother to his father — revealing that Armand's mother "belongs to the race that is cursed with the brand of slavery". The ancestry he condemned in his wife and child is his own.

Discuss:
  • Chopin ends with the letter in Armand's hands — not with Désirée, and not with what Armand does next. What does the story gain, and what does it deliberately refuse us, by stopping exactly there?

Grammar — Mixed Conditionals

This story is built on a chain of "if onlys" — and every one of them crosses a border between two times. If Armand had read the letter earlier (past), Désirée might still be alive (present). If he were a gentler man (present, permanent), he would not have sent her away (past). That crossing is exactly what a mixed conditional does.

Mix 1 — past cause → present result: If + past perfect, … would/might/could + base verb.
"If Armand had read his mother's letter earlier, Désirée might still be alive."
"If Désirée had never been left at the gate, no one would doubt her now."
Use it when something that happened (or didn't happen) in the past still shapes the present.
Mix 2 — present cause → past result: If + past simple (often were), … would have + past participle.
"If Armand were a gentler man, he would not have sent her away."
"If pride did not rule that house, one letter would never have destroyed it."
Use it when a permanent truth about a person or a world explains something that already happened. The test is always: which half is past, and which half is still true now?

1. Join each pair into ONE mixed conditional — past cause, present result

1. Armand did not question his own certainty. He is alone at L'Abri now. →

2. Désirée's parents left her at the gate. Her origin is a mystery to everyone. →

3. His mother hid the truth from him. He does not know who he really is. →

1. If Armand had questioned his own certainty, he would not be alone at L'Abri now.

2. If Désirée's parents had not left her at the gate, her origin would not be a mystery (to everyone).

3. If his mother had not hidden the truth from him, he would know who he really is.

2. Join each pair into ONE mixed conditional — present cause, past result

1. Armand is a proud man. He sent his wife and child away. →

2. Désirée is gentle and trusting. She accepted his verdict almost without a fight. →

3. That society judges people by ancestry. One baby's face destroyed a family. →

1. If Armand were not so proud, he would not have sent his wife and child away.

2. If Désirée were less gentle and trusting, she would not have accepted his verdict almost without a fight.

3. If that society did not judge people by ancestry, one baby's face would never have destroyed a family.

3. Complete the retelling

Fill the gaps so each sentence becomes the mixed conditional the meaning requires. Use these verbs, each in the form the time-mix demands: read · be · ask · surface. Then read the finished paragraph aloud.

If Armand his mother's letter earlier, Désirée might still alive today (past cause → present result). If he a humbler man, he would questions instead of passing sentence (present nature → past act). And if the truth before the wedding, there would no one at L'Abri to mourn now (past cause → present result).

If Armand had read… might still be alive · If he were… would have asked… · if the truth had surfaced… there would be no one…

Every "if" in this story crosses a time border — and that crossing is the tragedy itself: the past can never be revised, only revealed.

Going deeper — take the idea further

You have the story and the words. Now push the ideas outward — argue, disagree, change your mind. Do all of this out loud. There are no right answers here, only good reasons.

Useful language for building an argument: "What's striking is…", "You could argue that…", "It's not X that matters, it's Y…", "Seen from 1893… but seen from today…", "That may be true, but…".
1 · An identity assigned by others

Désirée never chooses who she is: the Valmondés give a nameless foundling a name, and years later Armand takes it away with a single sentence. To what extent is identity something other people hand to us — names, nationality, class, reputation? Who holds that power in a society, and can a person ever fully take it back? Draw on characters from history, books or film.

2 · The proudest name in Louisiana

Armand protects his family name at the cost of the family itself. Why do families, institutions and even nations defend reputation so fiercely — and at what point does pride stop being dignity and become cruelty? Think of an example from history or fiction where a "good name" was valued above the people who carried it.

3 · Reading it in 1893 — and reading it now

Chopin published this story thirty years after slavery was abolished, for readers who had grown up inside the world it condemns. What might her first readers have felt that we can't? What do we see that they perhaps couldn't? And once you know how it ends — does the story lose its power on a second reading, or gain a different one?

4 · The endings Chopin refused to write

She could have ended with Désirée safe at Valmondé, or with Armand imploring forgiveness, or with the truth arriving in time. Instead: a letter, a fire, and silence. Imagine each alternative ending out loud — what would each one say the story is "about"? Why is the ending she chose the harshest, and perhaps the most honest, of them all?

Task C · Madame Valmondé looks back (4 minutes)

Retell the story through the eyes of Madame Valmondé, years later, explaining it to a stranger. She saw L'Abri and shuddered; she saw the baby at four weeks and looked at him for a long time. What did she notice, and when? Why did she write only "Come home… Come with your child" — and no more?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The story's cruellest line is only six words: "Yes, I want you to go." Why does Chopin give the catastrophe such quiet, ordinary language — and why does it hurt more than a speech would?
  2. Chopin lets the truth surface only when it can no longer save anyone. What does that timing itself say about how prejudice works?
  3. The mother's letter calls slavery a "brand" — a mark imposed by a society, not a fact of anyone's blood. How does that one word carry the story's whole argument?
