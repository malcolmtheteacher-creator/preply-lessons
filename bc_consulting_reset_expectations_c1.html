Resetting Expectations When a Project Slips

C1 Level · Consulting · Client Communication

Consulting · Dewey 658.4

First Thoughts

You are four weeks into a twelve-week engagement. The analysis has surfaced more complexity than the proposal anticipated, and you now know that the original end-date is no longer realistic.

The client sponsor is already nervous. Your instinct is to promise you will absorb the slippage. Your better judgement says: reset the expectation now, with evidence, before it reaches the steering committee.

Take a moment with each question below. Share as much detail as you like — specific examples, what you would actually say, what tripped you up last time.

1. When was the last time a project you led slipped? How did you first find out, and how quickly did you tell the client?

2. What is the real cost of waiting to tell a client about a delay — not the schedule cost, but the trust cost?

3. Some consultants believe the client always knows before they are told. Do you agree? What signals do clients pick up on?

4. What is the difference in how you say it when the slippage is your fault versus the client's fault?

Quick opener — pick whichever you prefer:

  • Imagine you have ten minutes before a call with a client to tell them the project will be six weeks late. What is the very first sentence you will say?
  • A partner once told you: “The client will forgive a delay but never forgive the surprise.” How far do you agree with that?

Key Language

Useful chunks of language for this conversation. Read each phrase aloud and think about when you would actually reach for it.

Opening the conversation (leading with the fact)

  • “Thanks for making time. I wanted to bring you up to speed on something before it reaches the steering committee.”
  • “I want to flag something early so we have options, rather than be reacting later.”
  • “I am going to be upfront with you — we are tracking behind the original plan and I want to walk you through why.”

Explaining without making excuses

  • “When we scoped this, we were working from three assumptions. Two of those have turned out to be different in reality.”
  • “The data landscape is more fragmented than any of us anticipated, and that has added genuine work.”
  • “I am not going to pretend this is entirely outside our control — we should have tested that assumption harder upfront.”

Proposing the reset

  • “What I would like to propose is that we extend the close-out by four weeks and re-baseline the deliverables.”
  • “There are essentially three paths open to us, and I would like your view on which one fits best.”
  • “I am not asking for more budget today. I am asking for realistic runway.”

Rebuilding confidence

  • “To make sure we are not here again, I am going to send you a weekly flash update every Friday.”
  • “I will re-baseline the plan with you this week and send the change-request formally on Monday.”
  • “You have my commitment that any further variance will be flagged within 48 hours, not at the next milestone.”

The Scenario

The situation: Twelve-week strategy engagement for a mid-sized manufacturing client. You are at week four. The data-room is worse than expected; two workstreams that were supposed to run in parallel now have to run sequentially. You estimate the end-date slips by four weeks. Your sponsor is the COO — supportive but under pressure from her CEO, who personally signed off the scope.

Your task: Lead a fifteen-minute call with the COO. By the end of the call she needs to understand what has slipped, why, and what you propose — and she needs to feel that her choice to hire you was still the right call.

Step 2 · Deliver

When you are ready, role-play the conversation out loud. Be ready to handle the follow-up questions below.

Follow-up questions to handle:

  • The CEO is going to ask me if we should be pulling you off and bringing another firm in. What do I tell him?
  • What is your confidence that the new date will actually hold?
