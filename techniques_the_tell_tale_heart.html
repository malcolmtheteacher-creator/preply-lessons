SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B2/C1 · 50 min

The Tell-Tale Heart

Edgar Allan Poe (1843) — a man insists, again and again, that he is perfectly sane while he tells you how he killed an old man over one pale eye

Read in three parts

Poe hands us a narrator who will not stop telling us how calm and rational he is. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The trick of the story is in the voice: the harder he protests his sanity, the less we believe him. Watch the rhythm — the dashes, the short bursts, the repetitions. Do not skip the ending; the last lines are the whole point.

Part 1 — The eye

True! — nervous — very, very dreadfully nervous I had been and am; but why will you say that I am mad? The disease had sharpened my senses — not destroyed — not dulled them. Above all was the sense of hearing acute. I heard all things in the heaven and in the earth. I heard many things in hell. How, then, am I mad? Hearken! and observe how healthily — how calmly I can tell you the whole story.

It is impossible to say how first the idea entered my brain; but once conceived, it haunted me day and night. Object there was none. Passion there was none. I loved the old man. He had never wronged me. He had never given me insult. For his gold I had no desire. I think it was his eye! yes, it was this! He had the eye of a vulture — a pale blue eye, with a film over it. Whenever it fell upon me, my blood ran cold; and so by degrees — very gradually — I made up my mind to take the life of the old man, and thus rid myself of the eye for ever.

Now this is the point. You fancy me mad. Madmen know nothing. But you should have seen me. You should have seen how wisely I proceeded — with what caution — with what foresight — with what dissimulation I went to work! And every night, about midnight, I turned the latch of his door and opened it — oh, so gently! And then, when I had made an opening sufficient for my head, I put in a dark lantern, all closed, closed, so that no light shone out, and then I thrust in my head.

I was never kinder to the old man than during the whole week before I killed him. And every morning, when the day broke, I went boldly into the chamber, and spoke courageously to him, calling him by name in a hearty tone, and inquiring how he had passed the night. So you see he would have been a very profound old man, indeed, to suspect that every night, just at twelve, I looked in upon him while he slept.

Pause and discuss:
  • The narrator's very first word is "True!" and he immediately demands "why will you say that I am mad?" Who is he arguing with — and what does it tell us that he is arguing at all?
  • He insists he had no motive: no greed, no hatred, no injury. He killed for an eye. Does having no rational motive make him more frightening, or less?
  • He offers his own caution and cunning as proof of his sanity. Why does this "proof" work against him?

Part 2 — The killing

Upon the eighth night I was more than usually cautious in opening the door. A watch's minute hand moves more quickly than did mine. Never before that night had I felt the extent of my own powers — of my sagacity. I could scarcely contain my feelings of triumph. To think that there I was, opening the door, little by little, and he not even to dream of my secret deeds or thoughts.

The old man sprang up in bed, crying out — "Who's there?" I kept still and said nothing. For a whole hour I did not move a muscle, and in the meantime I did not hear him lie down. He was still sitting up in the bed listening; — just as I have done, night after night, hearkening to the death watches in the wall.

Presently I heard a slight groan, and I knew it was the groan of mortal terror. I knew the sound well. Many a night, just at midnight, when all the world slept, it has welled up from my own bosom, deepening, with its dreadful echo, the terrors that distracted me. The old man's hour had come!

When I had waited a long time, very patiently, I resolved to open a little — a very, very little crevice in the lantern. So I opened it — you cannot imagine how stealthily, stealthily — until at length a single dim ray, like the thread of the spider, shot from out the crevice and fell full upon the vulture eye. It was open — wide, wide open — and I grew furious as I gazed upon it.

And now have I not told you that what you mistake for madness is but over-acuteness of the senses? — now, I say, there came to my ears a low, dull, quick sound, such as a watch makes when enveloped in cotton. I knew that sound well too. It was the beating of the old man's heart. It increased my fury, as the beating of a drum stimulates the soldier into courage.

But even yet I refrained and kept still. I scarcely breathed. Yet the beating grew louder, louder! I thought the heart must burst. And now a new anxiety seized me — the sound would be heard by a neighbour! The old man's hour had come! With a loud yell, I threw open the lantern and leaped into the room. He shrieked once — once only. In an instant I dragged him to the floor, and pulled the heavy bed over him. I then smiled gaily, to find the deed so far done. But, for many minutes, the heart beat on with a muffled sound. This, however, did not vex me; it would not be heard through the wall. At length it ceased. The old man was dead.

Pause and discuss:
  • Before the murder he hears the old man's heart "such as a watch makes when enveloped in cotton." Whose heartbeat do you think he is actually hearing — and how do we know that question matters?
  • "The old man's hour had come!" — he says it twice. What does this repetition do to the rhythm of the scene, and to him?
  • He smiles "gaily" the instant the deed is done. What is missing from this reaction — and why is its absence so chilling?

Part 3 — The heart that would not stop

If still you think me mad, you will think so no longer when I describe the wise precautions I took for the concealment of the body. I worked hastily, but in silence. First of all I dismembered the corpse. I cut off the head and the arms and the legs. I then took up three planks from the flooring of the chamber, and deposited all between the scantlings. I then replaced the boards so cleverly, so cunningly, that no human eye — not even his — could have detected anything wrong. There was nothing to wash out — no stain of any kind — no blood-spot whatever. I had been too wary for that.

When I had made an end of these labours, it was four o'clock — still dark as midnight. As the bell sounded the hour, there came a knocking at the street door. I went down to open it with a light heart, — for what had I now to fear? There entered three men, who introduced themselves, with perfect suavity, as officers of the police. A shriek had been heard by a neighbour during the night; suspicion of foul play had been aroused; information had been lodged at the police office, and they had been deputed to search the premises.

I smiled, — for what had I to fear? I bade the gentlemen welcome. The shriek, I said, was my own in a dream. The old man, I mentioned, was absent in the country. I took my visitors all over the house. I bade them search — search well. I led them, at length, to his chamber. I brought chairs into the room, and desired them here to rest from their fatigues, while I myself, in the wild audacity of my triumph, placed my own seat upon the very spot beneath which reposed the corpse of the victim.

The officers were satisfied. My manner had convinced them. I was singularly at ease. They sat, and while I answered cheerily, they chatted of familiar things. But, ere long, I felt myself getting pale and wished them gone. My head ached, and I fancied a ringing in my ears: but still they sat and still chatted. The ringing became more distinct: — it continued and became more distinct: I talked more freely to get rid of the feeling: but it continued and gained definiteness — until, at length, I found that the noise was not within my ears.

No doubt I now grew very pale; — but I talked more fluently, and with a heightened voice. Yet the sound increased — and what could I do? It was a low, dull, quick sound — much such a sound as a watch makes when enveloped in cotton. I gasped for breath — and yet the officers heard it not. I talked more quickly — more vehemently; but the noise steadily increased. Why would they not be gone? I paced the floor to and fro with heavy strides, as if excited to fury by the observations of the men — but the noise steadily increased. Oh God! what could I do? I foamed — I raved — I swore!

But the noise steadily increased. It grew louder — louder — louder! And still the men chatted pleasantly, and smiled. Was it possible they heard not? Almighty God! — no, no! They heard! — they suspected! — they knew! — they were making a mockery of my horror! — this I thought, and this I think. But anything was better than this agony! Anything was more tolerable than this derision! I could bear those hypocritical smiles no longer! I felt that I must scream or die! and now — again! — hark! louder! louder! louder! louder!

"Villains!" I shrieked, "dissemble no more! I admit the deed! — tear up the planks! — here, here! — it is the beating of his hideous heart!"

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • The narrator was winning. The police were satisfied; the body was undetectable. Nothing forced his confession except a sound only he could hear. So what actually defeats him?
  • Is the heartbeat real, imagined, or his own pulse pounding in his ears? Poe never tells us. Why is leaving it unanswered more powerful than answering it?
  • He insists the officers must have heard the heart and were "making a mockery" of him. What does that accusation reveal about the state of his mind?
  • Your turn: The narrator could not live with a sound that no one else could hear. Have you ever felt that a secret, or a guilt, was somehow audible — as if everyone could tell? What does that feeling do to a person over time?

Grammar — Emphasis: do / so / such / too / enough

This narrator is desperate to make us feel things — his sanity, his calm, his terror. So the whole story is built on emphasis: structures that turn the volume up on a verb, an adjective or a result. Poe leans on three of them again and again, and you can borrow all three.

1. Emphatic do / does / did — stressing the verb

In a normal positive sentence we do not use do: "I heard it." To insist on it — to contradict a doubt — we add stressed do / does / did before the bare verb: "I did hear it." Poe even pushes it into a question for force: "But why will you say that I am mad?" Use it when someone doubts you, or to push back against an unspoken "no."

1. "You think I felt nothing — but I love the old man," he insists. (emphatic auxiliary, present)

2. "You suppose I heard no heartbeat. I hear it — clearly," he says of that night. (emphatic auxiliary, past)

3. The disease sharpen his senses, he claims — it did not dull them. (emphatic auxiliary, past, one verb)

1. do (do + bare verb "love"; contradicts the doubt)

2. did (did + bare verb "hear"; past emphasis)

3. did (did + bare verb "sharpen")

2. so + adjective/adverb, such (a) + noun — stressing a quality (often + that)

so + adjective/adverb (+ that): "He opened the door so gently that nobody woke." such (a/an) + (adjective +) noun (+ that): "It was such a dim ray that it barely lit the eye." / "He took such precautions that no eye could detect the body." Remember: so goes with an adjective or adverb alone; such wraps around a noun.

1. He proceeded calmly the old man never suspected him. (adverb structure + result)

2. He replaced the boards cunningly no human eye could detect them. (adverb structure + result)

3. It was a faint sound at first only he could hear it. (noun structure + result)

4. He took wise precautions that there was no blood-spot to wash out. (noun structure, plural noun)

1. so … that (so + adverb "calmly" + that)

2. so … that (so + adverb "cunningly" + that)

3. such … that (such + a + adjective + noun "sound" + that)

4. such (such + plural noun "precautions"; no "a" before a plural)

3. too + adjective + to, adjective + enough + to — the limit and the threshold

too + adjective + to = more than enough, with a negative result: "He was too wary to leave a single blood-spot." (so he left none). Adjective + enough + to = sufficiently, with a positive result: "The old man was not cautious enough to suspect him." Note the word order: too goes before the adjective; enough goes after it.

2. He believed he was clever deceive the police. (sufficiency + positive result)

3. In the end the silence was unbearable endure, and he confessed. (excess + negative result)

1. too … to (too + adjective "thin" + to + bare verb "wake")

2. enough to (adjective "clever" + enough + to + "deceive")

3. too … to (too + adjective "unbearable" + to + "endure")

What Poe does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are why a story from 1843 still gets inside a reader's head today.

1. The unreliable narrator — the more he insists, the madder he sounds

The narrator spends the whole story trying to prove one thing: that he is sane. But Poe makes every proof backfire. Listing your own cunning, your caution, your foresight is not what a calm man does — it is what a man betrays himself doing. The story undermines its own speaker: each "see how rational I am" makes us trust him less. Poe destabilises the ground under the voice, so that we read past what the narrator says to what he accidentally reveals.

2. Dramatic irony — he thinks he is winning; we hear him unravelling

By the police visit, the narrator is certain he has triumphed: the body is hidden, the officers are charmed, he even sits on the floorboards above the corpse. He believes he is in control. We, however, can hear the panic rising in his sentences before he names it. This gap — his confidence against our dread — is dramatic irony. Poe juxtaposes what the narrator claims with how he sounds, and the distance between the two ironises his every boast.

3. Repetition and rhythm — the dashes that race the pulse

Read the last page aloud and you cannot stay calm. Poe accelerates the prose with dashes and short clauses — "I foamed — I raved — I swore!" — and stacks repetitions that climb: "louder — louder — louder!" The syntax itself speeds up and breaks down, so the form enacts the breakdown it describes. The rhythm mirrors the racing heart, pulling the reader's own breathing into the narrator's collapse.

4. The symbolic heartbeat — guilt made audible

The beating heart is never explained. It may be the dead man's, the narrator's own pulse, or pure hallucination. That ambiguity is the power: Poe symbolises the narrator's guilt as a sound he cannot silence, a conscience that grows louder the harder he denies it. The heartbeat externalises what should be hidden, so that the very crime he concealed so perfectly is the thing that betrays him — not through evidence, but through the noise inside his own head.

Speaking tasks

Task A · Retell the story in your own words (3 minutes)

Before we discuss it, tell the story back to me as if I had never read it — in your own words, not line by line. Aim for about eight to ten sentences.

As you go, use at least five words from the Vocabulary tab and reach for the emphatic structures from the Grammar tab — at least one stressed do/does/did ("he did hear it") and one so … that or such … that. These beats will help you: a nervous man insists he is sane · an old man's pale, film-covered eye haunts him until he resolves to kill him · for eight nights he watches, stealthily, while the man sleeps · on the eighth night he hears the heart, grows furious, and smothers him · he dismembers the body and hides it under the floorboards · the police arrive, he is calm — until a heartbeat only he can hear drives him to confess.

Task B · Guilt you cannot silence (4 minutes)

The narrator's downfall is not evidence — it is a sound inside his own head that will not stop. Talk to me about guilt that refuses to go quiet. Take it in three steps:

  1. a time you, or someone you know, kept a secret or carried a guilt;
  2. how it felt to act normally on the surface while it churned underneath;
  3. whether confessing or telling the truth actually brought relief — or made it worse.

Use at least one emphatic do/did ("I did feel it") and one too … to or enough … to ("it was too heavy to carry alone"), plus two effect verbs from the Technique tab.

Task C · An obsession that takes over (3 minutes)

The whole crime grows from one fixation: a single eye. Tell me about an obsession — a fear, a habit, a detail, a person — that took up far more space in someone's mind than it deserved. It can be yours, or one you have watched in others.

Walk me through how a small thing grew until it crowded out everything else. Was it irrational, or did it have its own strange logic? Build the account around so … that and such … that ("it became such a fixation that…", "it grew so loud in his head that…").

Task D · Convince me you're calm when you're not (4 minutes)

The narrator sits chatting pleasantly with the police while panic rises inside him. Pick a real situation where you had to appear calm while you were anything but — an interview, a difficult conversation, a moment of bad news, a performance.

Tell it to me the way he tells his story: insist, with emphatic do/did, that you were perfectly in control ("I did stay calm", "I was fine"). Then let the cracks show — a detail that does not fit, a protest a little too strong — so that I start to doubt you. At the end, ask me whether you sounded as calm as you claim.

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The narrator gives no real motive — he kills over an eye. Is a crime with no rational reason more disturbing than one we can understand? Why?
  2. We spend the whole story locked inside one mind, with no other point of view. What has Poe made us share, simply by giving us no escape from the voice?
  3. The heartbeat may be guilt, conscience, or madness — Poe never says. Do you think a guilty conscience really works like this, or is that a comforting story we tell about wrongdoing?
  4. If the old man could speak one sentence after the confession, what do you imagine he would say — and would it change how you judge the narrator?
