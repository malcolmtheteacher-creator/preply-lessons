Explaining a Late Delivery

B2 Level · Logistics · Customer Communication

Logistics · Dewey 388

First Thoughts

Your driver is stuck at a Channel port. The paperwork has an issue. A delivery your customer is expecting tomorrow morning will now arrive on Friday at the earliest. The customer has a production line that depends on it.

You have two choices: wait until the customer calls you furious, or call them now with what you know, what you don’t know, and what you are doing about it. One of these builds trust. One destroys it.

Take a moment with each question below. Share as much detail as you like — specific examples, what you would actually say, what tripped you up last time.

1. When something goes wrong in a delivery, what is the real question the customer needs answered first?

2. Is it better to call before you have all the answers, or wait until you can give them a complete picture? Why?

3. How much detail about the problem helps the customer, and when does it stop helping?

4. What is the cost of finding out about a delay from the customer rather than telling them yourself?

Quick opener — pick whichever you prefer:

  • Give me the first sentence of the call — before any apology, explanation, or excuse.
  • A dispatcher once said: “The phone call you don’t want to make is the one that saves the account.” What did they mean?

a delivery / a consignmentThe shipment being moved. ‘Consignment’ is slightly more formal.
ETA / ETDEstimated Time of Arrival / Departure. Standard logistics shorthand.
a delayA slip from the planned timing. Can be small (hours) or significant (days).
customs clearanceThe process of getting goods officially through a border. A common source of delay.
a re-routeA change to the planned path of a delivery — usually to get around a problem.
a contingencyA backup plan for when something goes wrong. Customers want to hear you have one.
to expediteTo speed something up — usually by paying extra or calling in a favour.
a next steps updateA short, clear message saying what happens next and when you’ll update again.

Key Language

Useful chunks of language for this conversation. Read each phrase aloud and think about when you would actually reach for it.

Opening the call — the right way

  • “I wanted to call you as soon as we knew — I’d rather you heard this from me than from a tracking system.”
  • “I have some news on your consignment for tomorrow morning, and I want to walk you through it now.”
  • “Before I explain, I want to say: I know what this delivery means for your line, and I’m treating it that way.”

Explaining clearly, without hiding

  • “The truck is currently held at the port — there’s a paperwork issue at customs clearance.”
  • “Realistically, your delivery will not arrive tomorrow morning as planned. Our current best case is Friday.”
  • “I want to be honest: I don’t have a firm ETA yet, but I expect to have one within the next two hours.”

Showing what you’re doing

  • “My team is on the phone to our clearance agent right now — they are the ones who can move this fastest.”
  • “I’ve already got a second driver on standby in Dover so that as soon as the goods clear, we’re moving.”
  • “I’m looking at whether we can split the load and expedite the urgent items ahead of the rest — I’ll confirm within the hour.”

Closing — commitments, not comfort

  • “You will hear from me again by 11:00 today, whether I have new information or not.”
  • “If your line needs to make a decision in the meantime, please call me directly — not the general line.”
  • “I’m sorry for the disruption. You’ll get my full attention until this consignment is with you.”

The Scenario

The situation: You work in logistics for a third-party carrier. A full-load delivery for a key manufacturing customer — originally due at their site tomorrow at 07:00 — is held at Dover customs because of a paperwork error on the export documents. Best case: cleared this afternoon, delivered Friday morning. Worst case: not until Monday. The customer has a production line starting tomorrow at 08:00.

Your task: Call the customer logistics manager now. Deliver the news, explain what you know, what you don’t know, and what you are actively doing. Leave them with three clear commitments and a named next contact time.

Step 2 · Deliver

When you are ready, role-play the conversation out loud. Be ready to handle the follow-up questions below.

Follow-up questions to handle:

  • Why didn’t you tell me yesterday? You must have known.
  • What does my production line do tomorrow morning? Do you have a plan for me?
  • Can you guarantee Friday? Because I need to tell my director.
  • Who is paying for the overtime we’ll need on Saturday because of you?
