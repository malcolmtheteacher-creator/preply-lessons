The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A plan to cross a continent

In 1914, Sir Ernest Shackleton set out on what he called the Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition. The goal was breathtaking in its ambition: to make the first-ever crossing of Antarctica on foot, from one side of the frozen continent to the other. His ship was named, with what would turn out to be terrible irony, the Endurance. The crew numbered twenty-eight men.

The Endurance left in August 1914, just as the First World War was breaking out in Europe. Shackleton offered his ship and crew to the war effort, but he was told to sail on. So they did — south, towards the bottom of the world, carrying with them sledges, dogs and supplies for a journey across the ice that no one had ever attempted.

But the expedition went wrong before it had truly begun. The men never even reached the continent they had come to cross. In early 1915, while still at sea, the Endurance became trapped in the pack ice of the Weddell Sea. Frozen solid into the floes, the ship could go neither forward nor back. And so, helplessly, it began to drift — locked in the ice, carried wherever the ice chose to go, for month after month.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What exactly was Shackleton going to do, and what was his ship called?

2. What had gone wrong by early 1915 — before the real expedition had even started?

1. He was going to make the first-ever crossing of Antarctica on foot, with a crew of 28. His ship was the Endurance.

2. Before they could land, the ship became trapped in the pack ice of the Weddell Sea and drifted, frozen in, for months — so the crossing never began.

Discuss:
  • Shackleton named his ship the Endurance after his family motto: "By endurance we conquer." Knowing how the story starts, what do you make of that name now?

Part 2 — The ship is lost, the men are not

For months the ice held the Endurance in its grip, and slowly that grip tightened. In October 1915 the pressure of the pack ice finally became too much: the floes squeezed the wooden hull until it began to splinter and break. Shackleton gave the order no captain wants to give. He told his men to abandon ship. In November 1915 the Endurance was crushed, and sank beneath the ice. The plan to cross Antarctica was over before it had begun.

Now twenty-eight men were stranded on a drifting sheet of ice, in one of the most hostile places on Earth, with no ship, no radio that could reach the world, and no hope of rescue. For months they survived in makeshift camps on the ice itself, eating seal and penguin, waiting for the floes to carry them somewhere — anywhere — they might escape. When the ice finally broke up beneath them, they launched the three small lifeboats they had saved from the ship.

In April 1916, after a perilous voyage through freezing, broken seas, all twenty-eight men reached land: Elephant Island, a bleak, uninhabited rock far from any shipping route. They were, at last, standing on solid ground — but it was a place no rescuer would ever think to look, and no one in the world knew they were alive.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to the Endurance, and when?

2. How did the men get off the ice, and where did they end up?

1. In October 1915 the ice crushed the ship; Shackleton ordered it abandoned, and it sank in November 1915.

2. After months camped on the drifting ice, they escaped in three small lifeboats and reached Elephant Island — a bleak, uninhabited rock — in April 1916.

Discuss:
  • They had reached solid land at last — yet they were arguably in more danger than before. Why might safety and rescue be two completely different things?

Part 3 — The impossible voyage

Shackleton knew a hard truth: no one was coming to Elephant Island, because no one knew it held survivors. If the men were to live, someone would have to go and fetch help. So he made one of the boldest decisions in the history of exploration. He took five men and the largest of the lifeboats — the James Caird, an open wooden boat barely twenty-two feet long — and set out to sail some 800 miles across the most violent stretch of ocean on the planet to the island of South Georgia, where there was a whaling station, and people.

For more than two weeks the six men endured an ordeal almost beyond belief: mountainous waves, freezing spray, the constant threat of capsizing, navigating by a few brief glimpses of the sun. Against all odds, they reached South Georgia. But they had landed on the wrong, uninhabited side of the island. So Shackleton and two of the men did something no one had ever done: they crossed South Georgia's icy mountains on foot, without proper equipment, and walked into the whaling station to raise the alarm.

Even then, the rescue was not over. The sea ice kept driving Shackleton's relief ships back, and several attempts failed. But he refused to give up on the men he had left behind. Finally, in August 1916, he broke through and rescued every single one of them from Elephant Island. After nearly two years, an expedition that had failed at its every stated goal could claim the most astonishing success of all: not one member of the crew had died. It is remembered today as one of history's greatest feats of survival and leadership.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Shackleton make the voyage in the James Caird, and what did he have to do after reaching South Georgia?

2. How did the whole story finally end — and why is it remembered as it is?

1. Because no one knew the men on Elephant Island were alive, he sailed a tiny open boat ~800 miles to South Georgia to raise the alarm — then crossed the island's icy mountains on foot to reach the whaling station.

2. After several failed attempts, he returned in August 1916 and rescued every man. After nearly two years, not a single member of the expedition died — remembered as one of history's greatest feats of survival and leadership.

Discuss:
  • Every original aim of the expedition failed — yet it is told as a triumph. What does that tell us about how we decide whether something "succeeded"?

The grammar this story runs on: the future seen from the past

This is a story full of plans and predictions that never came true. To tell it, you need a special set of forms — the ways English talks about the future as it looked from a point in the past. They are perfect for plans that went wrong.

The tools:
• was / were going to — an intention or plan made earlier: "they were going to cross Antarctica…" (and usually: …but they never did).
• would — the future seen from a past viewpoint, especially in reported speech and predictions: "Shackleton was sure they would survive."
• was / were to — a formal, almost fated way to mark a plan or something destined to happen: "the Endurance was to carry them to Antarctica."
And a quick nod to the forms the men themselves would have used at the time — will and going to — for the predictions they made in the moment: "We will reach South Georgia."

1. was / were going to — the plan that didn't happen

Use was / were going to + verb for an intention formed in the past — very often one that life then overturned.

1. They (cross) Antarctica on foot, but the ice trapped the ship first.

2. The crew (land) on the continent, yet they never even reached it.

3. Shackleton (lead) the men across the ice, but instead he had to lead them home alive.

1. were going to cross

2. were going to land

3. was going to lead

Notice how each plan is followed by "but" — the form sets up an expectation the story then breaks.

2. would and was/were to — predictions and plans, seen from then

Use would for a future imagined from a past moment (often in reported thought or speech), and was / were to for a formal, fated-sounding plan.

1. Shackleton promised that he (return) for the men on Elephant Island.

2. As the ship sailed south, no one imagined that it (never / reach) land.

3. The little James Caird (carry) six men across 800 miles of ocean — a journey few thought survivable.

4. The men were certain their captain (come) back, and they were right.

1. would return

2. would never reach

3. was to carry (the fated/destined sense; "would carry" is also natural)

4. would come

3. Put them together — and the men's own predictions

Fill each gap with the form the meaning needs. The first gaps look at the plan from now; the last lines quote what the men might have said at the time, using will / going to. Read it aloud once finished.

The expedition (make) history; instead, it became a fight to survive. Shackleton knew the men (die) if he waited, so he set out for South Georgia, telling them: "I (come) back for you — we (not / leave) anyone behind."

was going to make (the plan, now broken) · would die (future seen from his past viewpoint) · will come / am going to come (his own promise, in the moment) · will not leave / are not going to leave

See the shift: from outside the story we use "was going to / would"; inside it, the men speak with "will / going to".
