The Emperor's New Clothes

Hans Christian Andersen's tale of two swindlers, a vain ruler, and the one voice honest enough to break the spell.

Today: you'll retell one of the sharpest tales ever written about pride and pretending — and you'll master reported speech, the machinery English uses to tell us what everyone said, what no one dared to admit, and what one small voice finally cried out.
Warm up — talk before you read. Answer these out loud, in full sentences.
  • Have you ever agreed with a group even though, privately, you thought they were wrong? What made you go along with it?
  • Why is it sometimes easier to stay silent than to say what you really see?
  • Who, in your experience, is most likely to blurt out an awkward truth — and why?

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The two swindlers took the shopkeeper's money for goods they never intended to deliver, then vanished before anyone noticed.
a swindler (n) — a person who cheats others out of money by deception; a con artist.
2. The prince was so vain that he kept three mirrors in every room and changed his outfit six times before lunch.
vain (adj) — excessively proud of one's appearance, abilities or importance.
3. The tailors set up their loom and pretended to pass shuttles back and forth, though not a single thread ran between the wooden frames.
a loom (n) — a machine or frame for weaving thread into cloth.
4. The gown was of the finest fabric — a silk so light it seemed to float when she turned.
fabric (n) — cloth; woven or knitted material used to make clothes.
5. Rather than confess his confusion, the minister decided to pretend he understood every word of the report.
to pretend (v) — to behave as if something is true when you know it is not.
6. No one wished to appear incompetent in front of the boss, so they all nodded as if the plan made perfect sense.
incompetent (adj) — lacking the skill or ability to do a job properly.
7. The crowd flattered the singer endlessly, praising a performance that everyone privately knew had been dreadful.
to flatter (v) — to praise someone too much or insincerely, often to please them or gain something.
8. Once one person laughed, the rest of the room could not conform to the polite silence any longer, and everyone joined in.
to conform (v) — to behave in the way that most people do, or that is expected, rather than acting differently.
9. The whole banquet turned out to be a hoax — the "royal guest" was an actor, and the invitations were fake.
a hoax (n) — a trick or false story made to deceive people, often for amusement or gain.
10. When the child spoke the plain truth, the guilty officials shrank back in shame, unable to meet anyone's eyes.
to shrink back (v) — to move or lean away, especially out of fear, shame or embarrassment.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The emperor who loved only clothes

Many years ago there lived an emperor who cared for nothing in the world so much as fine new clothes. He spent all his money on them. He took no interest in his soldiers, no interest in the theatre, no interest in driving through his forests — unless it was to show off a new outfit. He had a different coat for every hour of the day, and where other rulers might be found in council, he was almost always in his dressing room.

One day two swindlers arrived in his city. They let it be known that they were weavers, and that they could weave the most magnificent fabric imaginable. Not only were the colours and patterns unusually beautiful, they said, but the cloth had a wonderful power: it was invisible to anyone who was hopelessly stupid, or unfit for the post he held. Such clothes, the emperor thought, would be splendid indeed. If he wore them, he would be able to tell the clever men in his kingdom from the fools — and he would know at once which of his officials were unfit for their posts.

So he paid the two men a large sum of money and told them to begin at once. They set up two looms and pretended to work, though the frames stood entirely empty. They demanded the finest silk and the purest gold thread, which they packed away in their own bags, and then they worked far into the night at looms with nothing on them at all.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the emperor care about more than anything else?

2. What special power did the swindlers claim their cloth had, and why did this idea appeal to the emperor?

1. Fine new clothes — he spent all his money on them and neglected everything else, including governing.

2. They claimed the cloth was invisible to anyone who was stupid or unfit for their post; the emperor liked the idea because it would let him identify the fools and the unfit officials around him.

Discuss:
  • The swindlers didn't just promise beautiful cloth — they attached a test to it. Why is a trick far more powerful when it makes people afraid of what admitting the truth would say about them?

Part 2 — Nobody dares to admit the truth

After a while the emperor grew curious about how the weaving was coming along, but he felt a little uneasy when he remembered that a stupid man, or one unfit for his post, would not be able to see the cloth. He decided to send his honest old minister first. The minister went into the room where the two swindlers sat working at their empty looms. "Heaven help me," he thought, opening his eyes wide, "I cannot see anything at all." But he did not say so. Both swindlers begged him to admire the exquisite pattern and the glorious colours, pointing to the bare loom, and the poor old minister stared and stared. He could see nothing, because there was nothing there. Yet he dared not admit that he saw nothing, for that would mean he was either a fool or unfit for his office. So he said that the cloth was charming, and reported to the emperor that it was truly magnificent.

The emperor sent other trusted officials, and each returned with the same story. Every one of them looked and looked, saw the empty looms, and told the emperor that the fabric was the most beautiful he could imagine. Soon the whole city was talking of the wonderful cloth. At last the emperor went to see it for himself, surrounded by his finest courtiers. The rascals were weaving away at looms without a thread. "Is it not magnificent?" said the officials who had gone before. And the emperor, seeing nothing whatsoever, was horrified. "Am I a fool? Am I unfit to be emperor?" But aloud he declared that the cloth had his highest approval, and everyone around him agreed, though not one of them could see any more than he could.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the honest old minister actually see, and what did he tell the emperor instead?

2. When the emperor himself came to look, what did he think privately, and what did he say out loud?

1. He saw nothing at all, because the looms were empty. But he dared not admit it, so he told the emperor the cloth was magnificent.

2. Privately he feared he might be a fool or unfit to rule; out loud he declared the cloth had his highest approval — and everyone agreed.

Discuss:
  • Each person believed they were the only one who couldn't see the cloth. How does that private fear turn a whole crowd of individuals into a silent, agreeing mass?

Part 3 — "But he has nothing on!"

The swindlers stayed up all night before the great procession, pretending to take the cloth from the looms, cutting the air with huge scissors, and sewing with needles that had no thread. At last they announced that the clothes were ready. The emperor came with his noblest courtiers, and the two rogues lifted their arms as if holding something up. "Here are the trousers. Here is the coat. Here is the cloak," they said. "The whole suit is as light as a spider's web." The emperor took off all his clothes, and the swindlers pretended to dress him, garment by garment, while he turned this way and that in front of the mirror.

"How well the new clothes suit Your Majesty!" everyone cried. "What a splendid fit!" The chamberlains who were to carry the train stooped and pretended to lift it from the floor, for they dared not let it be seen that they held nothing at all. And so the emperor walked in procession beneath a fine canopy, and all the people in the streets said that his new clothes were beyond compare. No one would let it appear that he could see nothing, for that would prove him unfit for his post or a fool. Never had the emperor's clothes been such a success.

"But he has nothing on!" a little child cried out at last. "Listen to the voice of innocence," said the father, and one person whispered to another what the child had said. "He has nothing on — a child says he has nothing on!" "But he has nothing on!" the whole crowd was shouting at last. The emperor shivered, for he suspected they were right. But he thought to himself, "I must go through with it now, procession and all." So he held himself stiffer than ever, and the chamberlains walked on behind, carrying the train that was not there at all.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the swindlers "dress" the emperor before the procession?

2. Who finally spoke the truth, and how did the crowd react once the words were out?

1. They pretended to take the cloth from the looms and to dress him garment by garment, though he was in fact wearing nothing.

2. A little child cried out that he had nothing on; the whisper spread until the whole crowd was shouting it — yet the emperor, suspecting they were right, proudly walked on to the end of the procession.

Discuss:
  • Even after the truth is out, the emperor keeps walking. Why do you think Andersen ends the story this way, rather than with the emperor stopping in shame?

Grammar — reported speech

This whole story runs on what people said versus what they secretly thought. The minister said the cloth was beautiful; the courtiers reported that it was magnificent; no one dared to admit that they saw nothing — until a child cried out that the emperor had no clothes. That is reported speech: telling us what was said without quoting it word for word.

The basic move — "backshift": when we report what someone said in the past, the tense usually shifts one step back.
• present → past: "It is beautiful" → He said (that) it was beautiful.
• past → past perfect: "I saw nothing" → He admitted (that) he had seen nothing.
• will → would: "I will wear them" → He said he would wear them.
• can → could, must → had to: "I can see the cloth" → She claimed she could see the cloth.
Say vs tell, and reporting verbs:
• say (that) — no person needed: He said that the cloth was fine.
• tell + person (that) — always names who is told: He told the emperor that the cloth was fine.
• Richer verbs carry meaning: admit that, claim that, report that, declare that, whisper that, cry (out) that, insist that. Choose the verb that shows how it was said.
• Some verbs take a different pattern: dare to admit, refuse to say, agree to wear (verb + to + base form).

Going deeper

Andersen published this tale in 1837, and almost two hundred years later "the emperor's new clothes" has become an everyday phrase for a shared pretence that everyone can see through but no one will name. The story's real subject is not clothing at all — it is the strange power that groups have over what individuals are willing to say out loud.

Why it endures: the trick works only because each person is frightened of what the truth would reveal about them. To see nothing is to be a fool. So a whole city agrees to a lie, each one certain that everyone else can see what they cannot. It takes a child — too young to fear looking foolish — to say the obvious. The tale is about vanity, yes, but even more about conformity, social pressure, and the rare courage it takes to tell the truth.

Talk it through:
  • Why is it specifically a child who speaks the truth? What does the child have that all the adults have lost?
  • The emperor keeps walking even after he hears the crowd. Is he a fool, or is he showing a kind of terrible courage? What is Andersen saying about power that cannot admit a mistake?
  • Where do you see "the emperor's new clothes" in the real world — in fashion, business, politics, or online? Think of a time when "everyone knew" something was false but no one would say so.
  • If you had been in that crowd, honestly — would you have shouted the truth, stayed silent, or convinced yourself that you really could see the cloth? What would it have cost you either way?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The swindlers succeed not because their trick is clever, but because they understand human fear. What does the story suggest about how deception really works?

  3. The emperor keeps walking even after the truth is spoken. In your own life, or in public life, why is it so hard to stop a mistake once everyone is committed to it?
