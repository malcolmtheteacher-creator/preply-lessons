Warm-up

Before you read anything else, talk. Don't worry about getting it perfect.

SPEAK

Think of a time you were kept waiting for ages, or something happened that turned your day upside down — a delayed flight, a flat that fell through, the day you met someone important. Tell the story out loud, right now, however messy it comes out.

Listen in on this conversation

Maya: You look shattered. What happened?

Jae: Don't ask. We'd been sitting on the runway for two hours before they finally let us off the plane.

Maya: Two hours? Why?

Jae: Turns out they'd forgotten to load half the luggage. While we were all just sitting there, staff were running around trying to sort it out.

Maya: Nightmare. Did you miss your connection?

Jae: Of course. I'd been planning this trip for months, and it nearly fell apart in one afternoon.

Look at what Jae does in these four lines. At least four different verb structures are hiding here, all working together to tell one clear story. Can you spot them? Say it before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "We'd been sitting on the runway for two hours" — a stretch of time, before another past moment.
2. "they'd forgotten to load half the luggage" — one completed action, finished even earlier.
3. "staff were running around" — the background scene, happening around everyone.
4. "I'd been planning this trip for months" — another stretch of time, the buildup before it all went wrong.

This is how real stories work: one tense carries the main events, and the others fill in what came before and what was happening alongside.
Today: past simple, past continuous, past perfect simple and past perfect continuous, all layered together — so you can tell a proper story about something that happened to you, with the backstory in exactly the right place.

Setting the Scene

The main events of your story, and everything that was happening around them.

Spot the pattern

  • "I was standing at the gate when they announced the delay."
  • "We were queuing for security when my phone died."
  • "It started raining just as we landed."

Two jobs, working together. What's each form doing? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
was / were + -ing paints the scene — something already in progress. The plain past simple is the single event that cuts through it. Together they tell you what was going on and what actually happened.
was / were + verb-ing  …  when + past simple  (the event that interrupts or arrives)

Quick check

1. You're mid-story: tell me what you were doing right before the gate changed. Use the continuous form of queue:

"I for coffee when the gate change flashed up on the screen."

2. While we were all still queuing at the desk, the woman behind the counter finally ___ our booking. Use the past simple of come across:

"...she finally our booking."

Now say it

SPEAK

Tell me about a moment today or recently when something interrupted what you were doing. Start with "I was ...ing when..."

The Longer Backstory

Sometimes, before your story even starts, something else has already happened.

Spot the pattern

  • "By the time we boarded, they had already cancelled two other flights."
  • "When I got to the flat, the landlord had already rented it to someone else."
  • "She'd never been to that city before that trip."

What job is had + done doing here? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
had + past participle jumps back to an earlier point — something finished before the main story moment. It's the past of the past: if the whole anecdote already happened, had shows what came even earlier inside it.
had (+n't) + past participle  (+ already / never / just / by the time)

Quick check

1. We got to the flat viewing ten minutes late — the agent ___ the keys to another couple already. Use give:

"...the agent the keys to another couple already."

2. By the time the ambulance arrived, the shop ___ already, for the night. Use close:

"...the shop already for the night."

3. Turn these two sentences into one, using the past perfect: "The film started. We got to the cinema."

Now say it

SPEAK

Tell me about a time you arrived somewhere and found out something had already happened without you — good or bad. Use "had already..." at least once.

How Long Had It Been Going?

Not just what happened before — how long it had been happening.

Spot the pattern

  • "I'd been waiting for over an hour when the flight was finally called."
  • "We'd been looking for a flat for weeks before we found the right one."
  • "By the time we met, I'd been living in that city for two years."

What's the job of had been + -ing here? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
had been + -ing measures the stretch of time leading up to that earlier past moment — the past version of "have been doing". Use it when the duration is the point, not just the fact that something happened.
had been + verb-ing  (+ for / since / by the time)
Two traps: state verbs still refuse the -ing form, even here — "I'd known him for years", never "I'd been knowing him". And if a single action is already finished, not a stretch of time, the simple form is enough: "the plane had landed", not "had been landing".

One more shift: how things used to be

  • "When we were kids, we would spend every summer at my grandmother's."
  • "I used to be terrified of flying."
  • "My dad would drive incredibly slowly on motorways."

One of these forms refuses to describe a state. Which, and why? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
would works for repeated actions and habits (would spend, would drive) — but it refuses states. "I would be terrified" isn't something a native speaker would say. States like be, have, like, live (as in where you lived) take used to only.
would + base verb  (repeated actions only)
used to + base verb  (habits AND states)

Quick check

1. By the time she finally called back, you'd already spent two hours doing this. Use the continuous form of wait:

"By the time she finally called back, I for two hours."

2. A childhood state, not a repeated action. Use be:

"As a kid, I absolutely terrified of the dark."

3. A repeated family habit. Use take:

"Every Sunday, my dad us fishing, rain or shine."

Now say it

SPEAK

Tell me something you used to do as a kid that you don't do any more, and something that had already happened in your life by the time you started school. Use both "had been ...ing" and "used to" somewhere in there.
