Five Moves for Difficult Conversations

These aren't scripts. They're frames for thinking. Click each one to see how it works in practice.

1. The SBI Frame: Situation → Behaviour → Impact +

Why it matters: The hardest conversations fall apart because they're personal. "You're unreliable" is about character. "The report was 3 days late" is about one action. This frame lets you be specific, observable, and non-personal.

Situation: "In the project planning phase, we agreed the report would be delivered Tuesday."
Behaviour: "The report arrived Friday morning."
Impact: "This means the team couldn't begin their phase on schedule, which puts us 4 days behind with clients expecting delivery in 3 weeks."

The benefit: You've described a problem without attacking character. The other person can disagree with your characterization of the facts — but they can't deny the facts themselves.

2. The Pause Before the Point +

Why it matters: The second you say the difficult thing, the other person stops listening. Their nervous system activates. You've got maybe 5-10 seconds of actual listening left. Use silence before the difficult thing to make them ready.

"I want to talk about something. [Pause. Let them settle.] This isn't easy to bring up, but it matters. [Another pause.] In the last three projects, the deliverables have missed the deadline each time."

The benefit: The pause signals "something important is coming." It gives their nervous system time to prepare. It also shows you're not attacking — you're being thoughtful.

3. The Distinction: Separate the Person from the Behaviour +

Why it matters: When you confuse the person with their actions, people stop hearing you. They become defensive about who they are. When you separate them, you keep the relationship intact while addressing the real problem.

Broken: "You're someone who doesn't follow through."
Clear: "I've seen some gaps in the follow-through on the last two projects. That's not who I think you are generally — but in these two cases, it's what happened."

The benefit: You're giving them permission to stay in relationship with you while you address the behaviour. You're not calling them a "bad person" — you're saying "this one behaviour is the problem."

4. The Invitation: Create Space After the Difficult Thing +

Why it matters: The hardest conversations aren't monologues. After you deliver the difficult message, the other person's perspective becomes crucial. If you don't invite it, they'll assume you're just judging them.

"I wanted to hear what's actually going on from your side. What do you see differently? What am I missing here?"

The benefit: You've signalled that you're genuinely trying to understand, not just deliver a judgment. This is where the real conversation starts — and where the relationship either repairs or breaks.

5. The Boundary Statement: Clear, Calm, Non-Aggressive +

Why it matters: Some conversations require more than feedback — they require a change in behaviour. Boundary statements are firm but respectful. They say "this is what I need" not "this is what's wrong with you."

"What I need from this conversation is for us to agree on one thing: going forward, deliverables arrive on the agreed date. That's non-negotiable. If something is at risk, you tell me a week early — you don't wait until day-of."

The benefit: You're clear about what needs to change. You're not leaving it ambiguous. And you're using the language of need ("I need") not blame ("You always").

Your Difficult Conversation

Pick one scenario. You have 3 minutes to prepare. Then deliver the full conversation. Record yourself or speak aloud — someone needs to hear it.

A. The Performance Conversation

Context: You manage a team of 5. One team member has consistently missed deadlines over the last 4 weeks. They're missing their deliverables by 2-3 days each time. You haven't addressed it yet. Today you're going to.

Your challenge: Have the conversation. Start with SBI. Create space for their perspective. End with a boundary statement about what you need going forward.

B. Conflict Mediation

Context: Two of your senior colleagues cannot work together. They disagree on approach, they don't trust each other's decisions, and they're starting to pick sides in the team. You manage one of them. The conflict is affecting team dynamics. You need to address it with the person you manage.

Your challenge: Have the conversation. Acknowledge the situation without taking a side. Make it clear this tension is affecting the team and needs to change. Invite their perspective.

C. Breaking Bad News

Context: Your company is restructuring. A project your team member led for 8 months is being cancelled. As a result, there's a possibility their role changes or they're redundant. You need to tell them today. It's not final yet, but it's likely.

Your challenge: Have the conversation. Be honest about what you know and don't know. Show you care about what happens to them. Describe what comes next.

Reflection & Language Bank

What You've Practiced

The structure that works: Pause before the difficult thing. Use SBI (Situation, Behaviour, Impact) to stay factual. Separate the person from their behaviour. Invite their perspective. End with a clear boundary or ask.

What makes it work: It's not the words — it's that you genuinely care about understanding them while holding them accountable. Difficult conversations work when the other person believes you're trying to solve this together, not judge them.

Why people avoid them: Because they're uncomfortable. But they're also the conversations that define you as a leader. The people who can have them with grace — who can be honest while staying in relationship — are the ones people trust and want to work with.

Language Bank: Difficult Conversations

Opening: Signal What's Coming
"I want to talk about something. It's not easy, but it matters."
"I've noticed something I'd like to address with you."
"I'm bringing this up because I care about how you're doing and how the team is doing."
The SBI Frame: State the Facts
"In the [timeframe/project], [specific situation]. [Specific behaviour happened]. This means [impact]."
"The deadline was Tuesday. The work arrived Friday. This means we're now behind schedule."
Distinguish Person from Behaviour
"I'm not saying you're a bad person. What I'm saying is that in this instance, [behaviour]."
"I know you care about this team. And in the last two projects, [specific behaviour]."
Invite Their Perspective
"I wanted to hear from you — what's going on?"
"What's your take on this? What am I missing?"
"Help me understand what happened from your side."
Acknowledge Without Excusing
"I hear that things have been difficult. That makes sense. And what I need is still [specific need]."
"I understand where you're coming from. I also need us to [clear boundary]."
Set a Boundary: What You Need Going Forward
"What I need going forward is..."
"For this to work, I need to see [specific behaviour]."
"The non-negotiable here is [clear standard]. If things are at risk, tell me early."
Close: Affirm the Relationship
"I'm bringing this up because I want this to work — for you and for the team."
"I still believe in your ability to do this. I just need to see it change."
"I want us to move forward from this together."
