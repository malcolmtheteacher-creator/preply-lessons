The Spider's Thread

Akutagawa's story of a lotus pond in Paradise, a robber at the bottom of Hell, and a rescue line as thin as silk.

Today: you'll watch a man climb out of Hell on a single spider's thread — and lose everything with one word: mine. And you'll master the grammar of self and others: himself, by himself, each other — and the verbs that never take a reflexive at all.
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • Can one good act make up for a lifetime of bad ones? Should it?
  • In an emergency — a fire, a sinking ship — do people mostly help each other, or save themselves? What do you think, and why?
  • Have you ever been given a second chance you didn't deserve? What did you do with it?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. The thread was so fine that it shone like a single line of silver light, and it looked as though a breath could break it. It was terribly slender.
slender (adj) — thin and delicate; of people, attractively slim. Figuratively: a slender chance, a slender majority = only just enough.
2. He had killed, set houses on fire and robbed travellers, and in all his life he had done only one kind thing. He was a wicked man.
wicked (adj) — morally very bad, evil: the wicked witch. Informally (BrE slang) it can mean "excellent" — That's wicked! — so check the context.
3. The Buddha looked down on the pond with a compassionate face, for he felt the suffering of every creature in it.
compassionate (adj) — feeling and showing sympathy for others' suffering. Noun: compassion. Collocations: show compassion, compassionate leave (time off work after a death in the family).

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: sinner · dangle · swarm. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means a person who has broken religious or moral laws?
a sinner (n) — from a sin / to sin. Light use: "Chocolate cake for breakfast — I'm a sinner."
2. Which verb means to hang or swing loosely in the air?
to dangle (v) — legs dangling over the edge. Figuratively, to dangle something in front of someone = to offer it as a temptation.
3. Which word means a large, moving crowd of insects — or of people?
a swarm (n), to swarm (v) — a swarm of bees; tourists swarmed into the square; the place was swarming with police.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. He cried out, "This thread is mine! Who gave you permission to climb it?" His words were utterly selfish. Selfish means…
  (a) thinking only of yourself   (b) confident in yourself   (c) honest about yourself
(a) — selfish; opposite selfless or unselfish. Compare self-confident, self-aware, self-centred — English has dozens of self- words.
2. Kandata was utterly exhausted and could climb no further, so he stopped to rest. Exhausted means…
  (a) slightly bored   (b) extremely tired   (c) very frightened
(b) — exhausted = completely tired out. It's an extreme adjective, so absolutely exhausted, not very exhausted. Noun: exhaustion.

D · Word building & collocation

1. Kandata once chose not to kill a spider. He spared its life. Complete: "Please, ___ me the details." · "Can you ___ five minutes?"
spare — three uses: spare someone's life (not kill), spare me the details (don't tell me), spare five minutes (give time you don't strictly need). As an adjective: a spare room, spare time.
2. The Buddha sees what has happened. He is not angry — he is sorrowful. Make the noun, and complete the idiom: "to drown your ___s" (= to drink alcohol to forget your problems).
sorrow (n) → sorrowful (adj, literary) = deeply sad. to drown your sorrows. Formal: "It is with great sorrow that we announce…"

  • Using wicked, slender, swarm and selfish, predict what happens when a thread is let down into Hell.
  • Sort today's words into words of kindness, words of evil, and words that describe movement or appearance.

The story, in three parts

Akutagawa's story, retold here in English and lightly shortened. Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The lotus pond

One day the Buddha was walking by himself along the edge of the lotus pond in Paradise. The lotus flowers in the pond were all as white as pearls, and from their golden centres there rose, without end, a perfume more wonderful than words can describe. It was morning in Paradise.

At last the Buddha stopped at the edge of the pond and, through a gap between the leaves that covered the water, looked down at what lay below. For under the lotus pond of Paradise lies the very bottom of Hell, and through the crystal-clear water the River of Three Ways and the Mountain of Needles could be seen as plainly as if through a glass.

There, at the bottom of Hell, among the other sinners, the Buddha's eye fell on a man called Kandata. He had been a great robber who had murdered, set houses on fire and done every kind of evil. And yet he had done one good deed. Once, walking through a deep forest, he had seen a little spider crawling by the side of the path. He had lifted his foot to crush it, then stopped himself. "No," he had thought, "small as it is, it has a life too. It would be a pity to take it for nothing." And he had let the spider go on its way.

As he looked down into Hell, the Buddha remembered this. If it were possible, he thought, he would like to save the man in return for that single kindness. Just then, on a jade-green lotus leaf beside him, a spider of Paradise was spinning a beautiful silver thread. The Buddha took the thread gently in his hand, and let it down, straight through the gap between the white lotus flowers, far, far down into the depths of Hell.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How can the Buddha see Hell from Paradise?

2. What was Kandata's one good deed, and how does the Buddha decide to reward it?

1. Hell lies directly under the lotus pond, and the water is so clear that he can look down through it.

2. He spared a little spider instead of crushing it. The Buddha lowers a spider's silver thread into Hell to give him a way out.

Discuss:
  • Kandata spared the spider because "it has a life too". Is that a real moral act, or just a passing mood? Does the motive matter?

Part 2 — The thread

At the bottom of Hell, in the Pool of Blood, Kandata was floating up and sinking down with the other sinners. It was pitch dark everywhere, and the only thing that glittered in the darkness was the needles of the terrible mountain. The silence was like the silence of the grave; the only sound was the faint sighing of the sinners, who were too exhausted even to cry. Anyone who has fallen this far has been so worn out by the tortures of Hell that he no longer has the strength to weep. So even Kandata, great robber that he was, could do nothing but choke on the blood and struggle there like a dying frog.

But then, lifting his head by chance, Kandata saw a slender silver thread sliding down towards him out of the far-off sky, shining faintly, as if afraid that someone might see it. He clapped his hands with joy. If he held on to this thread and climbed it, he would surely get out of Hell. Perhaps he could even climb into Paradise itself. Then he would never again be driven up the Mountain of Needles, or pushed down into the Pool of Blood.

He took hold of the thread at once and began to climb with all his strength. As a former robber, he was used to this kind of thing. But the distance between Hell and Paradise is thousands of miles, and at last even Kandata was so tired that he could not climb another inch. He stopped to rest himself and, dangling from the thread, looked down.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Describe Hell as Akutagawa shows it: what can Kandata see and hear?

2. Why does Kandata stop climbing?

1. Total darkness, a pool of blood, the glittering needles of the mountain, and silence broken only by the faint sighs of exhausted sinners.

2. Because the distance is enormous and he is too tired to go further, so he rests and looks down.

Discuss:
  • The thread comes down "as if afraid that someone might see it". Why might Akutagawa give the thread a feeling like this? What is he preparing us for?

Part 3 — "This thread is mine!"

The Pool of Blood was now hidden in the darkness far below. If he kept going, it might be easier than he had feared to get out of Hell. "I've done it! I've done it!" he laughed. But then he saw that, below him on the thread, countless sinners were climbing up after him, like a line of ants, higher and higher, as if they too had forgotten everything else.

Kandata was so frightened and amazed that for a moment he simply hung there with his mouth open. How could this slender thread, which seemed ready to break even under him, hold the weight of so many? If it broke now, even he would fall back into Hell. And still the sinners came swarming up out of the dark in their hundreds and thousands. So Kandata shouted in a great voice: "Hey, you sinners! This thread is mine! Who gave you permission to climb it? Get down! Get down!"

At that very moment the thread, which until then had held perfectly, broke with a snap — exactly at the point where Kandata was hanging. Spinning round and round like a top, he fell headlong, down and down into the darkness.

Standing at the edge of the lotus pond, the Buddha had watched it all. When Kandata had sunk like a stone to the bottom of the Pool of Blood, the Buddha's face became sorrowful, and he walked on again. But the lotus flowers of Paradise were not troubled at all. Their white petals swayed round his feet, and from their golden centres a wonderful perfume rose without end. In Paradise, it was nearly noon.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Kandata see below him, and what does he shout?

2. Where exactly does the thread break, and how does the Buddha react?

1. Thousands of sinners climbing up the thread after him. He shouts that the thread is his and orders them to get down.

2. Exactly at the point where Kandata is hanging — just above his hands. The Buddha looks sorrowful and walks on; the lotus flowers are not troubled at all.

Discuss:
  • The thread held thousands of sinners — until Kandata spoke. What is Akutagawa saying about what really broke it?

Grammar — reflexives, each other & the language of self

The whole story turns on one question: does Kandata think of himself, or of others? English grammar makes the same distinction — and learners often get it subtly wrong.

Three uses of -self:
• Reflexive — the subject and object are the same person: He stopped himself. The sinners couldn't save themselves.
• Emphatic — "that person and nobody else": He might climb into Paradise itself. The Buddha himself let the thread down.
• by + -self = alone / without help: The Buddha was walking by himself. Kandata wanted to escape by himself.
But — themselves vs each other: The sinners pushed each other (A pushed B, B pushed A) ≠ The sinners pushed themselves (each one pushed his own body).
The traps (C1).
• Many verbs are reflexive in other languages but not in English: relax, worry, wake up, get dressed, feel, concentrate, remember, sit down, meet. ✗ I relaxed myself → ✓ I relaxed. ✗ We met ourselves → ✓ We met.
• Fixed expressions: help yourself, enjoy yourself, behave yourself, make yourself at home, find yourself (= discover you are somewhere): Kandata found himself alone on the thread.
• After prepositions of place, use the object pronoun: He looked behind him. He saw sinners below him — not below himself. But: He thought only about himself (not a place).

1. Reflexive or not?

Write a reflexive pronoun or an object pronoun (him, you…) — or write – if no word is needed.

1. After the climb, Kandata couldn't relax : he kept worrying about the thread.

2. He had to pull up, inch by inch.

3. He looked down below and saw thousands of sinners.

1. – (relax is not reflexive in English)   2. himself   3. him (after a preposition of place — not himself)

2. Themselves or each other?

Choose the one that fits the meaning.

1. In Hell, the sinners were too tired even to speak to .

2. Kandata and the spider never saw again.

3. If the sinners had thought less about , the thread might have held.

1. each other   2. each other   3. themselves

3. The Buddha's thoughts — complete the paragraph

Write a reflexive pronoun, each other, an object pronoun (him, them…), or –. Then read it aloud slowly, as the Buddha.

"I gave him a chance , with my own hands. He could have helped the others; instead he looked down at the people below and saw only enemies. If they had helped — one holding the thread steady for the next — they might all have climbed out together. But he thought only of . He wanted Paradise all by . Well, he must blame . And yet I cannot feel happy about it, and I cannot enjoy this morning as I did."

myself (emphatic) · him (place) · each other · himself · himself · himself · – (feel is not reflexive) · myself

The grammar of the story in one paragraph: each other would have saved them; himself destroyed them all.

Going deeper

Ryūnosuke Akutagawa (1892–1927) is often called the father of the modern Japanese short story; Japan's most important literary prize is named after him, and two of his stories became Kurosawa's film Rashomon. "The Spider's Thread" (Kumo no Ito) appeared in 1918 in Akai Tori ("The Red Bird"), a new magazine of literature for children. Akutagawa's source was probably a tale called "The Spider-Web" in Karma (1894), a book of Buddhist-style stories by the American writer Paul Carus. There is a striking parallel in Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov too: a wicked woman is offered an onion to pull her out of the lake of fire, and fails for exactly the same reason.

The craft. The story is built like a sandwich: Paradise at the start, Paradise at the end, and Hell in the middle. The frame is the point. At the beginning it is morning; at the end it is "nearly noon". A whole human drama of hope, effort, panic and destruction has taken place, and Paradise has hardly noticed. The lotus flowers "were not troubled at all". That cool distance is what makes the story so disturbing: it's not a sermon, it's a calm observation. Look also at what breaks the thread. It holds a thousand sinners without trouble, and breaks at the exact instant Kandata says "mine", "exactly at the point where Kandata was hanging". The weight that breaks it is not physical. The story is over in about ten minutes of reading time and a few hours of story time, and it contains a complete theory of selfishness: whoever tries to save himself alone, loses himself.
Talk it through:
  • The story begins and ends with the beautiful, untroubled lotus flowers. Does this make Paradise seem peaceful — or cold and indifferent?
  • Is the Buddha's test fair? He gives a slender thread to a man surrounded by thousands of desperate people. Was Kandata set up to fail?
  • Akutagawa wrote this for a children's magazine. What does he want children to learn? Would you read it to a child?
  • Kandata spared a spider out of a sudden, passing feeling. Is goodness more often a habit or a moment?

Task A · Narrate the whole story (4 minutes)

Tell The Spider's Thread as if to someone who has never read it — slowly and calmly, like Akutagawa. Move through the arc: morning in Paradise, the lotus pond → the view into Hell → Kandata and the spared spider → the silver thread → the climb → the rest and the look down → the swarm of sinners → "This thread is mine!" → the snap → the Buddha walks on; nearly noon.

Use at least three different reflexive or emphatic forms, each other once, and at least five key words (slender, wicked, compassionate, sinner, dangle, swarm, selfish, exhausted, spare, sorrowful).

Task B · Did Kandata deserve to fall? (4 minutes)

Build an argument. One view: Kandata showed his true nature at the decisive moment, and the fall was just. Another: any terrified human being clinging to a thread above Hell would have panicked; the test was cruel, and the Buddha's sorrow is an admission of that. Take a side, give three reasons, deal with the strongest objection, and say what you think Akutagawa believed.

Then argue the opposite for one minute — the harder direction is where the language comes from.

Task C · Me first? (4 minutes)

The story asks whether we save ourselves or each other. Take that into today's world.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Panic buying, queue-jumping, grabbing the last seat: when do ordinary people behave like Kandata?
  • A real disaster where people helped each other — or failed to. What made the difference?
  • Can a society be built on everyone looking after themselves? Argue for or against.
  • "Pulling up the ladder behind you": someone who succeeds and then makes it harder for others. Give an example.
  • A small act of kindness you remember — given or received — that mattered more than it should have.
  • Heaven, hell, karma: how does your culture picture reward and punishment? How does this story compare?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. If Kandata had said nothing, would the thread have held? What makes you think so?
  2. The thread broke "exactly at the point where Kandata was hanging", so every sinner below him fell too. Were they punished for his selfishness — or would each of them, in his place, have shouted the same thing?
  3. The Buddha is "sorrowful" but simply walks on. Is that compassion, or indifference? What would you have wanted him to do?
  4. Before you go — from memory: make three sentences about the story that show the difference between themselves and each other, and explain why we say he relaxed, not he relaxed himself.
