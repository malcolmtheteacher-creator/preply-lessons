Bletchley Park

Ten thousand people in wooden huts who read the enemy's mail, told no one for thirty years, and may have shortened the war by two.

Today: you'll tell the story of the country house where a secret army of codebreakers read Germany's most protected messages — and you'll master relative clauses with quantifiers, the structure English uses to say something about part of a group: ten thousand staff, most of whom were women; millions of messages, none of which was meant for British eyes.

Now — the words for today's story

Today the words come in families. Each item shows two forms of a word and leaves one out. Say the missing form aloud, and say what the family means — then reveal the full family and check both your word and your meaning. Watch the stress: it often moves when the form changes.

1. verb: to decipher · noun: a decipherment · adjective: ________
Something so badly written that nobody can read it is not …?
to decipher (v) · a decipherment (n) · decipherable (adj) — to work out the meaning of something written in code, in a foreign script or in terrible handwriting. Its opposite, indecipherable, is the far commoner adjective.
2. adjective: painstaking · adverb: ________ · related noun: pains (as in to take pains over something)
He copied out every letter … slowly and carefully.
painstaking (adj) · painstakingly (adv) — done with great care and attention to small details, over a long time. It has nothing to do with pain in the sense of hurting: the "pains" here are efforts.
3. verb: to intercept · noun: ________ · adjective: intercepted
The radio operators listened all night; each catch was an …?
to intercept (v) · an interception (n), also an intercept (n, technical) · intercepted (adj) — to stop or catch something on its way from one place to another, especially a message, a signal or an aircraft.
4. adjective: classified · verb: ________ · noun: classification
The files were stamped TOP SECRET. Who decides that? Someone has to … them.
classified (adj) · to classify (v) · a classification (n) — officially secret; kept from the public for reasons of state security. The verb also has the everyday meaning "to sort into groups".
5. noun: an anomaly · adjective: ________ · adverb: anomalously
One message behaved strangely — it was …
an anomaly (n) · anomalous (adj) · anomalously (adv) — something that does not fit the normal pattern; an irregularity. Note the stress: an-OM-aly, an-OM-alous.
6. verb: to eliminate · noun: ________ · adjective: eliminated
You cross off every wrong answer until one remains. That process is called …?
to eliminate (v) · elimination (n) · eliminated (adj) — to remove or rule out completely. A process of elimination means finding the answer by discarding everything it cannot be.
7. noun: ingenuity · adjective: ________ · adverb: ingeniously
What a clever design! That is an … solution.
ingenuity (n) · ingenious (adj) · ingeniously (adv) — cleverness at inventing things or solving hard problems in original ways. Careful: ingenious (clever) is not ingenuous (innocent, naive).
8. noun: discretion · adjective: ________ · adverb: discreetly
She said nothing, asked nothing and looked away. She was very …
discretion (n) · discreet (adj) · discreetly (adv) — the good judgement to keep quiet about sensitive things, and to act carefully so as not to attract attention. (A different word from discrete, meaning separate.)
9. noun: tedium · adjective: ________ · adverb: tediously
Eight hours of the same task, night after night. The work was …
tedium (n) · tedious (adj) · tediously (adv) — the quality of being long, slow and boring, especially through repetition.
10. adjective: posthumous · adverb: ________ · (this family has no everyday noun)
The apology came in 2013. He had died in 1954. It came …
posthumous (adj) · posthumously (adv) — happening or given after a person's death: a posthumous pardon, a posthumous album, a novel published posthumously. The "h" is silent: POST-yum-us.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — An unbreakable machine

Bletchley Park is an unremarkable Victorian mansion in Buckinghamshire, a building whose architecture has been described by unkind visitors as several styles arguing with one another. It was bought in the late 1930s for the Government Code and Cypher School, and part of its appeal was pure geography: it stood beside the railway junction at Bletchley, on the line that joins Oxford and Cambridge, the two universities from which a great many of its future staff would be quietly recruited. When war came in 1939, the codebreakers moved in. Within a few years the lawns had disappeared under wooden huts and brick blocks, and the population had swollen from a few dozen people to something approaching ten thousand.

Their problem had a name: Enigma. It looked like a heavy typewriter in a wooden box, and it was used across the German armed forces to turn plain German into a stream of nonsense letters. Inside sat a set of rotating wheels, or rotors, which shifted position with every single key stroke, so that pressing the same letter twice produced two different letters. In front of them was a plugboard, on which pairs of letters were swapped by cables before and after they passed through the rotors. The wiring, the order of the rotors, their starting positions and the plugboard pairs made up the day's settings — and the settings were changed daily, at midnight.

The result was a number of possible configurations so vast that it is usually written as a power of ten rather than spoken aloud. German cryptographers ran the arithmetic, looked at the answer, and concluded that no enemy could ever work through so many combinations in the few hours before the settings changed again. They were, on the arithmetic alone, correct. What they had not allowed for was that a cipher is not only a machine; it is also the people who operate it. Enigma had one structural weakness — a letter could never be enciphered as itself — and its users had habits. Those two facts, and an extraordinary amount of ingenuity, were the crack in the wall.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was this particular house chosen?

2. What made German cryptographers so confident that Enigma could not be broken?

1. Partly for its position: it sat by the railway junction at Bletchley, on the line between Oxford and Cambridge, the universities from which many staff were recruited. It was also large, private and outside London.

2. The rotors and plugboard produced an astronomically large number of possible settings, and the settings were changed every day — so, on the arithmetic, no enemy could search them all in time.

  • The Germans trusted a calculation and ignored the human beings using the machine. Where else do we see people trusting a system's mathematics and forgetting its users?

Part 2 — The huts

The first breaks into Enigma were not British at all. Through the 1930s a small team of Polish mathematicians — above all Marian Rejewski, with Jerzy Różycki and Henryk Zygalski — attacked the machine with pure mathematics and built devices of their own to hunt for its settings. In July 1939, weeks before the invasion of their country, they handed their work to British and French intelligence at a meeting near Warsaw. Bletchley therefore did not begin from nothing; it began from a Polish head start, a debt that went unacknowledged for decades and is now, rightly, part of every honest telling. On that foundation Alan Turing and Gordon Welchman designed the Bombe: a large electromechanical machine, clattering and hot, which raced through possible settings and did not identify the right one so much as eliminate the impossible ones, leaving a handful of candidates for humans to test.

The Bombe needed a way in, and the way in was German routine. Cryptanalysts called it a crib — a piece of text they could guess was present. Weather stations sent reports at the same hour in the same words; units signed off in the same formula; one station reportedly transmitted "nothing to report" so faithfully that it became a gift. Every anomaly in the traffic, every operator who reused a setting or sent the same message twice on two networks, opened a door. Around this sat an immense, painstaking machine of human labour: signals were intercepted at listening stations along the coast, motorcycled to Buckinghamshire, logged, sorted, and indexed on hand-written cards, so that what one hut deciphered could be checked against everything already known.

Roughly three-quarters of the staff were women, many of them Wrens of the Women's Royal Naval Service, who ran the Bombes through night shifts of noise, oil and tedium, most of whom were never told what the machines were for. Dilly Knox led a section of formidable linguists; Joan Clarke worked on naval Enigma; Mavis Batey broke into the Italian naval traffic, work which fed the British victory off Cape Matapan in 1941. Meanwhile a second and harder target, the Lorenz cipher used for Hitler's highest-level communications, was unpicked by Bill Tutte and John Tiltman — Tutte reconstructing a machine he had never seen. To break it fast enough, Tommy Flowers, a Post Office engineer, built Colossus out of thousands of valves: an electronic machine, programmable by switches and plugs, and now widely regarded as a landmark in the history of computing.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the Polish cryptanalysts contribute, and when?

2. What was a "crib", and why did it matter to the Bombe?

1. Rejewski, Różycki and Zygalski broke Enigma mathematically before the war and shared their methods and machines with British and French intelligence in July 1939, giving Bletchley its starting point.

2. A crib was a piece of plain text the codebreakers could reasonably guess was in a message — routine weather reports, standard sign-offs. It gave the Bombe something to test settings against, so the impossible ones could be eliminated.

Discuss:
  • Bletchley's success rested on machines, on mathematics, and on thousands of people doing repetitive work in the dark. Which of the three deserves the most credit — and why do the machines usually get it?

Part 3 — Thirty years of silence, and what it left behind

Intelligence from the broken traffic was codenamed Ultra, and it was handled with a discretion that bordered on the ruthless. Nothing could be acted upon in a way that might let the Germans deduce that their cipher was broken, since a single change of procedure would have closed the door. Commanders were given the substance but rarely the source; cover stories were invented — a reconnaissance aircraft conveniently sent over a convoy, an imaginary agent — so that any German enquiry would find an innocent explanation. Ultra was classified at a level above top secret, its circulation restricted to a small number of officers, most of whom knew only what their own operations required. In the Battle of the Atlantic, where the reading of naval Enigma helped convoys steer around the U-boat lines, the effect was measured in ships and lives.

Everyone who worked there signed the Official Secrets Act, and for about thirty years the silence held — an astonishing fact when you consider the numbers involved. Churchill is said to have called them the geese that laid the golden eggs and never cackled; the remark is reported rather than documented, but it captures the arrangement exactly. Men and women went home in 1945 to families who assumed they had spent the war doing dull clerical work, and said nothing. Some let their spouses believe it for the rest of their lives. Only in the 1970s, with the first published accounts, did the story begin to come out — by which time many of those who had done the work were dead, and some of the survivors found that no one quite believed them.

The echoes are long. Colossus and the thinking behind it fed directly into the birth of modern computing, though the machines themselves were broken up and the details stayed classified for decades, so that the pioneers went publicly uncredited. The organisation continued as GCHQ, and the questions Bletchley raised — how much of everyone's communication a state may read, and who watches the watchers — have only grown louder. Alan Turing was prosecuted in 1952 for homosexual acts, then a crime in Britain, and died in 1954; he received a posthumous royal pardon in 2013, and a later law extended pardons to thousands of others. The site itself was nearly demolished before being saved as a museum. Historians commonly estimate that the work shortened the war by around two years — an estimate, not a measurement, and one that is argued over, but almost nobody argues it was worth nothing.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why were cover stories invented around Ultra intelligence?

2. Name two long echoes Bletchley Park left behind.

1. So that the Germans would not deduce that Enigma had been broken. If they had suspected, they would have changed their procedures and the source would have closed.

2. Any two of: the birth of modern computing via Colossus and Turing's ideas; the continuation of the work as GCHQ and the debate about state surveillance; Turing's prosecution and posthumous pardon; the site saved as a museum; and the estimate that the war was shortened by around two years.

Discuss:
  • Thousands of people kept this secret for thirty years, and many died without recognition. Was that silence a price worth paying — and could any government ask for it today?

Grammar — relative clauses with quantifiers

This story is full of large groups that need dividing up. Ten thousand staff — but three-quarters were women. Millions of messages — but only some were readable. Dozens of officers received Ultra — but none knew where it came from. English has an elegant way of saying this in one sentence: a quantifier plus of whom or of which, opening a non-defining relative clause.

How it's formed:
noun phrase + comma + quantifier + of whom (people) / of which (things) + the rest of the clause.
• "Nearly ten thousand people worked there, most of whom were women."
• "They built machines, none of which survived the war intact."
• Quantifiers that fit: all, both, most, many, much, some, several, few, half, none, neither, one, two, a number of, the majority of.
• Also with superlatives and numbers: "three Polish mathematicians, the best known of whom was Rejewski".
• With a possessive idea, use whose: "a mansion whose architecture nobody admired".
When we use it — and the trap:
• To comment on part of a group already mentioned, without starting a new sentence. It is non-defining: the comma is obligatory, and the information is extra.
• It belongs to written and careful spoken English — history, reports, presentations. In casual speech we usually split the sentence in two.
• The trap: of them is not a relative pronoun. ✗ "Ten thousand staff, most of them were women." That is two sentences glued together. Either write most of whom were, or drop the verb: "Ten thousand staff, most of them women." Both are correct; they are not interchangeable.
• After a preposition, the same rule: "the Wrens, for many of whom the work was pure tedium".

1. Join them up

2. They built several Colossus machines. Not one survived the war intact. → They built several Colossus machines, survived the war intact.

3. Three Polish mathematicians broke Enigma first. The best known is Marian Rejewski. → Three Polish mathematicians broke Enigma first, is Marian Rejewski.

2. none of which

3. the best known of whom (superlatives work here too: "the best known of whom is Marian Rejewski")
