You win a very large lottery prize. Two years later, how happy will you be compared with now? Choose one.

Money and happiness: true or false?

Psychologists have studied lottery winners and incomes for decades. Here are six claims about that research. Decide which are true.

1. The winners said they enjoyed everyday pleasures more after they won.
False. They reported enjoying everyday pleasures a little less than before.
2. In 1978, researchers interviewed 22 lottery winners and 22 non-winners and found that the winners were not much happier overall.
True. It was a small study, but it became famous.
3. A 2010 study of a large survey found that day-to-day mood stopped improving at an income of around $75,000, while satisfaction with life kept rising.
True. That is why the figure is quoted so often.
4. Every later study confirmed the $75,000 plateau.
False. A later analysis found no clear plateau for most people, and the figure is debated.
5. Large later studies of lottery winners found that winning made no difference to how satisfied people were with life.
False. Larger, more recent studies have found that winning raised life satisfaction for years.
6. Several experiments suggest that spending money on other people can raise happiness more than spending on yourself.
True, although the effects tend to be small.
Talk about it:
  • Why do small, early studies often make the best headlines?
  • Is happiness the same thing as being satisfied with life? What is the difference?

The evidence, in three steps

Before each part, make a prediction out loud. Then read, and check yourself.

Part 1 — Winning the lottery

Before you read: People who win a big prize become used to it. What do you think happens to the things that used to give them pleasure?

In 1978, the psychologists Philip Brickman, Dan Coates and Ronnie Janoff-Bulman interviewed 22 people who had recently won sizeable prizes in the Illinois state lottery, along with 22 people who had not, and 29 people who had been paralysed in accidents. They asked them how happy they were now, how happy they had been before, and how much they expected to enjoy everyday pleasures.

The findings made headlines. The lottery winners did not rate themselves as significantly happier than the others, and they took less pleasure in ordinary events such as chatting with a friend or reading a magazine. Brickman proposed that a big positive event raises the standard against which everyday experiences are judged, an idea later called the hedonic treadmill.

The hedonic treadmill describes our tendency to return to a stable level of happiness despite major changes in our circumstances.
hedonic treadmill (n) — the idea that people quickly adapt to improvements, so they need more to feel the same pleasure.
Adaptation to a new car or salary is quick; within months it becomes the new normal.
adaptation (n) — the process of getting used to a new situation. The verb is adapt.

Part 2 — The plateau and the argument

Before you read: One famous number, $75,000, is often quoted. What could be wrong with using one number for everyone?

A larger piece of research appeared in 2010. Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton analysed hundreds of thousands of responses to a US survey and concluded that day-to-day emotional wellbeing rose with income up to roughly $75,000 a year and then levelled off, whereas overall life evaluation continued to rise. The finding was widely reported as 'money stops buying happiness at $75,000'.

In 2021, Matthew Killingsworth used a different method: he repeatedly asked more than 30,000 workers how they felt in the moment. He found no plateau; wellbeing continued to rise with income. The two researchers then worked together. Their 2023 paper suggested that both might be partly right. For most people, happiness tends to rise with income, but among the least happy fifth of respondents it levelled off at higher incomes, perhaps because money cannot solve other sources of unhappiness.

Which word means "not sure; not clearly proven"? (i_________)
inconclusive (adj) — Example: "The findings are inconclusive."

Part 3 — It is not only how much, but how

Before you read: Besides how much money people have, what else might affect how happy it makes them?

Whatever the answer on income, many researchers argue that how people spend money matters at least as much as how much they have. In a series of experiments, Elizabeth Dunn and colleagues found that people who spent small sums on others, as a gift or a donation, tended to report greater happiness than those who spent the same on themselves. Spending on experiences, such as a trip, often seems to produce more lasting satisfaction than spending on objects, perhaps because we adapt to possessions faster.

Money also appears to buy something less visible: control over time, security and choice. Surveys often find that people who feel they have enough to avoid constant worry are markedly more satisfied than those who do not. That may explain why the debate is not really about whether money matters, but about when, for whom, and in what ways.

Describing data and hedging

When we report research, we describe numbers and trends, and we choose how strongly to claim something. Hedging shows you know the evidence has limits.

Trends: rose, fell, levelled off, peaked, plateaued.
Proportions: around a third, roughly half, a small minority.
Hedges: seems to, appears to, tends to, may, suggests that, is likely to. Compare: Money makes people happy (too strong) and Money appears to make people somewhat happier (careful).

What does the evidence support?

Five claims about money and happiness. Choose I agree, I disagree or It depends. Then defend your choice using a hedge, for example seems to or tends to. Check the evidence last.

1. "Money can't buy happiness."appears to
Too strong. Most evidence suggests income is linked to wellbeing, especially when it removes insecurity. A safer version: 'Beyond a certain point, extra income appears to matter less.'
2. "Lottery winners are no happier than anyone else."tends to
Overstated. The first study was small. Larger, more recent studies of lottery winners have found that winning does tend to raise life satisfaction.
3. "$75,000 is the magic number."may
Doubtful. The figure came from one dataset and a particular measure, and later work found no clear plateau for most people.
4. "Spending on others makes you happier than spending on yourself."suggests that
Mostly supported. Several experiments suggest this, although the effects are often small and may depend on the kind of giving.
5. "If you adapt to wealth, there's no point in earning more."is likely to
A leap. Adaptation is partial and varies by what we gain; security and time may be valued for a long time.
