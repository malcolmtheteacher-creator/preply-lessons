The Vikings

Seafarers from the north who raided, traded, settled — and reached a new world — before vanishing into legend.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of a people who were far more than raiders, and discuss how reputations get distorted over time — using relative clauses and purpose clauses to add precision to your speaking.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The first attack was a raid — a sudden strike from the sea, over almost before the monks understood what was happening, and then gone again.
a raid (n) — a fast, surprise attack to steal or destroy, after which the attackers quickly leave. (verb: to raid)
2. Their long, narrow longships could cross open ocean yet still be rowed up a shallow river, far inland, where no other ship could follow.
a longship (n) — the slim, fast Norse wooden ship, light enough for shallow water yet strong enough for the open sea.
3. A monastery on a quiet island, full of gold and undefended monks, made an almost irresistible target.
a monastery (n) — a community of monks, and the building where they live, pray and store their treasures.
4. To plunder a place is to strip it of everything valuable by force — and the raiders plundered the island and sailed away rich.
to plunder (v) — to steal goods violently, especially during a war or raid. (noun: plunder — the stolen goods)
5. They were not only fighters but skilled navigators, reading the sun, the stars and the flight of birds to find their way across empty water.
a navigator (n) — someone who plans and directs the course of a ship. (verb: to navigate)
6. Many of them came not to fight but to settle — to clear land, build farms and raise families in a country far from home.
to settle (v) — to make a permanent home in a new place. (noun: a settlement, a settler)
7. Their sagas — long stories of heroes and voyages, told for generations and only later written down — preserved their history.
a saga (n) — a long traditional Norse story of heroic deeds and journeys, mixing history and legend.
8. A bold voyage across the North Atlantic took them past Iceland and Greenland, and on towards an unknown shore.
a voyage (n) — a long journey by sea.
9. Like every people, they had their gods, their feasts and their beliefs — Odin, Thor and a whole world of myth that shaped how they saw life and death.
a belief (n) — something accepted as true; here, religious ideas and faith. (verb: to believe)
10. Much of what people "know" about them is a myth — a popular but false picture, like the horned helmets they never actually wore.
a myth (n) — a widely believed story or idea that is not actually true; also, a traditional tale about gods and heroes.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The terror from the sea

On a summer day in 793, ships appeared off the coast of north-east England and made for the little island monastery of Lindisfarne. The men who landed plundered its treasures, killed or carried off the monks, and were gone almost before help could arrive. To the Christians who wrote it down, the attack was unthinkable — a holy place, violated out of nowhere. Most historians take that raid as the moment the Viking Age began.

The raiders were Norse people from Scandinavia — what is now Norway, Sweden and Denmark. The word "Viking" really describes an activity more than a nation: to "go viking" meant to sail off in search of plunder. For the next two and a half centuries, their longships would appear again and again along the coasts and rivers of Europe. These ships were the secret of their success: slim, fast and shallow, light enough to be dragged across land, yet seaworthy enough to cross open ocean. In them, Norse navigators travelled without maps or compass, reading the sun, the stars, the wind and the sea itself.

To their victims they seemed like a sudden punishment from the sky — striking where they were least expected, and vanishing before any army could gather. Monasteries, rich and undefended, were a favourite target. For a while, it must have felt as though no coast in Europe was safe.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened at Lindisfarne in 793, and why does it matter to the story?

2. What made the longships so effective — and what did Norse navigators use instead of maps?

1. Norse raiders attacked and plundered the island monastery of Lindisfarne in 793, killing or carrying off the monks. It is usually taken as the moment the Viking Age began.

2. The longships were slim, fast, shallow and light enough to drag over land, yet able to cross open ocean. Navigators used the sun, stars, wind and sea instead of maps or a compass.

Discuss:
  • The people who wrote the story down were the victims. How might that have shaped the picture of the Vikings that came down to us?

Part 2 — Not just raiders

If raiding were the whole story, the Vikings would be a footnote. In fact, the same people who terrified the monks were also among the great traders, settlers and explorers of their age. Their ships carried furs, walrus ivory, amber and enslaved people along trade routes that ran from the Arctic to the markets of the Middle East. They founded towns: Dublin, in Ireland, began as a Viking trading base. In northern France, a Norse leader was granted land that took its name from the "Northmen" — Normandy.

Many came to stay. Norse families settled across large parts of Britain and Ireland, farming the land and mixing with the people already there, until the line between "Viking" and "local" slowly disappeared. Others pushed out into the empty North Atlantic, settling the Faroe Islands and then Iceland, and founding a tiny, struggling colony on the ice-bound coast of Greenland.

And from Greenland came the most astonishing voyage of all. Around the year 1000, Leif Erikson sailed west and reached the coast of North America — a land he called Vinland, after the wild grapes his crew reportedly found. For a long time this was treated as a mere legend in the sagas, until archaeologists uncovered the remains of a Norse settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, dated to almost exactly that time. The Vikings had reached the Americas around five centuries before Columbus.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Besides raiding, what three other roles did the Vikings play — and can you give an example of each?

2. What did Leif Erikson achieve, and what proved it was not just a story?

1. Traders (routes from the Arctic to the Middle East; Dublin as a trading base), settlers (Britain, Ireland, Iceland, Greenland; Normandy named after the "Northmen"), and explorers (out across the North Atlantic).

2. Around the year 1000 he reached North America ("Vinland") — about 500 years before Columbus. The discovery of a Norse site at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland confirmed the saga.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think the "raider" image stuck so firmly, while the traders, settlers and explorers were largely forgotten?

Part 3 — Myth, belief, and the end of an age

The Vikings had a rich inner world of their own. They worshipped gods such as Odin, the one-eyed god of wisdom and war, and Thor, the thunder-god with his hammer; their beliefs filled a whole universe of giants, monsters and a final battle at the end of the world. Much of this survives only because it was preserved in the sagas — long stories of voyages and feuds, passed down by mouth and written down, mostly in Iceland, after the age itself was over.

One famous image, though, is pure invention: the horned helmet. There is no good evidence that Vikings ever wore horns into battle — they would have been useless and dangerous in a fight. The picture comes mainly from the nineteenth century, from romantic paintings and an opera, long after the real Vikings were gone. It is a perfect example of how a powerful image can drown out the truth.

The Viking Age is usually said to end in 1066. That September, a Norwegian king, Harald Hardrada, invaded England in a last great bid for its throne and was defeated and killed at the Battle of Stamford Bridge. The age of the longship raids was effectively over. Yet the Vikings did not really disappear — they had already settled, married and blended into the peoples of Britain, Ireland, France and beyond. Their blood, their words and their stories were now simply part of Europe.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How do we know so much about Viking gods and stories — and what is wrong with the famous horned helmet?

2. What event is usually taken to mark the end of the Viking Age, and what really became of the Vikings?

1. Much survives through the sagas, written down mostly in Iceland after the age ended. The horned helmet is a myth — there is no real evidence Vikings wore horns; the image comes from the 19th century (romantic art and an opera).

2. The defeat and death of Harald Hardrada at the Battle of Stamford Bridge in 1066. The Vikings didn't vanish — they had already settled and blended into the peoples of Britain, Ireland, France and beyond.

Discuss:
  • The horned helmet was invented centuries later, yet almost everyone "knows" it. What other historical pictures might be myths we've never questioned?

The grammar this story runs on: relative & purpose clauses

To talk about the Vikings well, you need two tools constantly: a way to add information about who or which thing you mean, and a way to say why something was done. Both appear all over the story above.

The two tools:
• Relative clauses — add detail about a noun, joined with who (people), which / that (things), where (places): "the Norse seafarers who raided Lindisfarne," "the ships that could cross open ocean," "Iceland, where the sagas were written down."
• Purpose clauses — say why, with to, in order to / so as to, or so that + clause: "they built longships in order to cross open ocean," "they sailed west so that they could find new land."

1. Relative clauses — choose who / which / that / where

Add the information about the right noun. Use who for people, which or that for things, where for places.

1. Lindisfarne was a monastery stood on a quiet island off the coast.

2. The Norse navigators crossed the Atlantic used the sun and stars, not maps.

3. Iceland, the sagas were finally written down, preserved the old stories.

1. which / that (a thing — the monastery)

2. who (people — the navigators)

3. where (a place — Iceland)

2. Purpose clauses — say why

Complete each gap with to, in order to, or so that + a clause, to express the reason.

1. They designed shallow ships sail far up rivers.

2. Leif Erikson sailed west he could reach the unknown land.

3. The Vikings raided monasteries seize their gold and silver.

1. to / in order to (+ verb: "to sail far up rivers")

2. so that (+ a full clause: "so that he could reach…")

3. to / in order to ("to seize their gold")

3. Put them together — combine the sentences

Join each pair into one sentence using a relative clause and a purpose clause. Then read your version aloud.

1. The Vikings built longships. The longships were light. They wanted to drag them over land. →

2. Dublin was a town. The Vikings founded it. They wanted to trade more easily. →

1. e.g. "The Vikings built light longships that they could drag over land in order to reach inland rivers." (Other versions work, as long as you use a relative clause + a purpose clause.)

2. e.g. "Dublin was a town which the Vikings founded so that they could trade more easily."

These two structures let you pack detail and reason into a single, confident sentence — exactly what you'll need in the Speak tab.

Task B · How reputations get distorted (4 minutes)

The Vikings are remembered above all as violent raiders — and they wear horned helmets they never owned. How do reputations get distorted over time? Build an argument about who gets to write history and why the dramatic version usually wins, using the Vikings and any other examples you know.

Task C · A people behind the cliché (4 minutes)

Choose any group, place or person that has a strong popular image — a country, a city, a historical figure, even a profession — and pull the cliché apart. What is the simple image people carry, and what is the fuller, truer picture behind it?

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Pirates — remembered as bloodthirsty cut-throats with eyepatches and buried treasure, but many were former sailors who fled brutal navy ships to escape conditions that were even worse.
  • The Gladiators of Rome — pictured as doomed slaves fighting to the death, yet some became wealthy celebrities who chose to fight, trained like athletes, and survived for years.
  • Genghis Khan — known as a destroyer who slaughtered millions, but also the ruler who built roads, protected trade across Asia, and granted religious freedom so that merchants could move safely.
  • The Wild West "cowboy" — the lone gunslinger of the films, when the real cowboys were poorly paid ranch workers, many of them Black or Mexican, who spent most of their time herding cattle, not shooting.
  • A country with a fixed image — somewhere outsiders reduce to one stereotype (food, weather, a single event) — and the richer reality that locals, who live there every day, actually know.
  • A profession people misjudge — bankers, lawyers, traffic wardens, influencers — a job that exists to do something useful, but which carries a reputation that's only half the truth.
