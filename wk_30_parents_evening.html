Parents' Evening

Ten minutes per family, two small chairs, and Mrs Novak sitting down opposite you with hope on her face. Lena is lovely, Lena is bright — and Lena has handed in almost nothing since half term. You have both halves of that sentence to say.

The Scene 🎬

Mixed news is the hardest news: too much praise and the problem gets lost, too much problem and the parent stops hearing you.

You teach Year 7 English, and tonight is parents' evening — the conveyor belt of ten-minute conversations that parents remember for years. Lena Novak is twelve: a genuinely gifted writer, kind in a quiet classroom-changing way, and — since half term — a girl who has stopped handing things in and gone silent in discussions she used to lead.

Across the little table is her mother, who has come straight from work and wants, like every parent on the list, to hear that everything is fine. Your job is precision under time pressure: real praise that isn't packaging, one clearly named problem with evidence, no diagnosis you're not qualified to make — and a plan with her name in it as well as yours. Parents can't fix what they haven't clearly heard; children can't be helped by adults who spent the ten minutes protecting each other's feelings.

One more thing, and it governs everything: the parent is not the audience for this news. She's the other half of the team that acts on it.

  2. Why do parents hear criticism of a child as criticism of themselves? Can a sentence be built to prevent that?

The Conversation 💬

7:40pm. The hall hums. Mrs Novak sits down with her coat still on — ready to leave fast if this goes the way she fears.

Mrs NovakLena Novak — I'm her mum. Go on then. Is she behaving?
YouThanks for coming in, Mrs Novak — I've been looking forward to this one. And yes, beautifully. Let me say where we are: there's a lot going well, and one thing I want us to work on together.
Mrs Novak…One thing. Right. Give me the good first, then.
YouI'll start with the good, because it's real — this isn't the polite bit before bad news. Lena is one of the two or three genuine writers I have this year. This is her October essay — read that opening paragraph when you get home. And she's kind in a way that changes a classroom: when Amir joined in September speaking almost no English, it was Lena who sat with him at lunch. Nobody asked her to.
Mrs NovakShe never tells me any of this. She never tells me anything lately. …So what's the one thing?
YouHere it is, plainly. What I'm seeing is homework not coming in — most weeks, not just once — and a girl who's gone quiet in discussions she used to lead. Four of the last six pieces are missing, and that started, quite sharply, after half term.
Mrs NovakFour of six? She tells me it's done. She sits in her room every night with the door shut, and she TELLS me it's done. So is she lying to me now, is that what you're—
YouI don't think "lying" is the most useful word for what's happening — and I want to be clear about something else, because it matters: this isn't about ability. The October essay proves the ability. It's about something getting in the way, and I don't yet know what. Which is really why I'm glad you're here — you know her better than I do. Does any of this ring true at home? The quietness, the door shut?
Mrs Novak…She's been quieter, yes. Since around then. I thought it was just — twelve. The age. Is it not just the age?
YouIt may partly be the age — you'd know, and I only see her four hours a week. What I can tell you is the change was sharp, not gradual, and sharp changes usually have a reason. I'm not going to guess at it tonight, and I don't think we should panic either. It's nothing we can't turn around — she's twelve, the talent is sitting right there, and we've caught it in weeks, not years.
Mrs NovakSo what do we do? Because I've been sitting here tonight feeling like the last person to know my own daughter.
YouYou're not the last to know — you're the first person I've told, and the only one who can do half of this. So here's what I'll do at school: I'll check in with her on Fridays — two minutes, not a spotlight — and homework goes into a shared log you can actually see, so "it's done" becomes something you can both look at together. And here's where you can help at home: not an interrogation — just the log open once a week, and maybe the October essay left on the kitchen table where she can see you've read it.
Mrs NovakThe essay on the table. You think that'll do more than me asking?
YouI think being admired works on Lena — I've watched it work all autumn. Asking makes her defend; being read makes her write. Let's speak again in three weeks and see where we are — I'll ring you, you won't have to chase me. And Mrs Novak, take this bit home more than anything else I've said: she's a good kid having a rough patch. Those are different things, and we treat them differently.
Mrs Novak…A rough patch. Alright. *standing, coat finally off her shoulders* Three weeks. And — thank you for the essay. I'll read it tonight.

  2. Mrs Novak nearly turns the meeting into a fight — over what word? How does the teacher defuse it?
  3. What exactly is the plan? List both halves and the checkpoint.
1. The October essay (physical proof of the writing talent, sent home to be read) and the Amir story (kindness nobody asked for). "Because it's real" pre-empts the parent's fear that praise is just the cushion before the blow — this praise has evidence attached.

2. "Lying" — "so is she lying to me now?" The teacher declines the word ("I don't think 'lying' is the most useful word"), refuses to make it about honesty at all, and immediately reframes: not ability, something in the way — then hands the mother the expert role: "you know her better than I do."

3. School: Friday check-ins (two minutes, no spotlight) and homework in a shared log. Home: the log opened together weekly, the essay left on the kitchen table — admiration, not interrogation. Checkpoint: the teacher rings in three weeks.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for delivering mixed news about someone to someone who loves them — parents' evenings, appraisals, care meetings, references. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The parent across the table is listening for one thing under all your words: is this person for my child or against her? Every phrase here answers "for" — which is precisely what earns you the right to be completely honest about the problem.

🗺️ Framing the meeting

Phrase 1

The job: the map of the whole conversation, given upfront. Complete it:

"There's a lot going well, and one thing I want us to work on ___."

"There's a lot going well, and one thing I want us to work on together."
The honest map, drawn before the journey: proportion ("a lot" versus "one thing") and partnership ("us… together") in a single sentence. Now she knows the shape of the next ten minutes — no ambush coming, no whitewash either. And "work on" beats every synonym for "problem": it arrives already pointed at a solution.

Take it anywhere: "There's a lot going well, and one thing I want us to work on together." Appraisals, project reviews, difficult references — the mixed-news opener.
Phrase 2

The job: making the praise believable — not packaging. Which version?

A. "She's a lovely girl, really lovely, we're all very fond of her. Now—"
B. "I'll start with the good, because it's real."
C. "Before I get to the problems, the positives."
B — "I'll start with the good, because it's real."
Every parent knows the feedback sandwich, and A tastes exactly like its bread — vague fondness, no evidence, clearly the run-up to something. C openly labels the praise as an appetiser. B does something disarming: it names the suspicion ("you think this is the polite bit") and denies it — then must immediately pay for that claim with specifics. The essay. The Amir story. Praise with evidence is the only kind that survives the meeting.

Take it anywhere: "I'll start with the good, because it's real." Then prove it, with named examples, before any "but" arrives.
Phrase 3

The job: naming the problem — as a pattern, with numbers. Complete it:

"What I'm seeing is homework not coming in — most ___, not just ___."

"What I'm seeing is homework not coming in — most weeks, not just once."
Three disciplines in one sentence. "What I'm seeing" claims only your own evidence — observation, not verdict. The pattern-not-incident framing ("most weeks, not just once") is what makes it serious without exaggeration. And the numbers — four of the last six, sharply since half term — do the convincing that adjectives never could. Vague concerns get dismissed; documented patterns get acted on.

Take it anywhere: "What I'm seeing is ___ — most weeks, not just once." Feedback of every kind: describe the pattern, date the change, bring the count.

🛡️ Protecting the child from the problem

Phrase 4

The job: separating what she can't do from what she isn't doing. Complete it:

"This isn't about ___ — it's about something getting in the way."

"This isn't about ability — it's about something getting in the way."
The sentence that decides whether the parent leaves frightened or mobilised. "She can't" is a verdict about the child; "something's in the way" is a puzzle the adults can solve — and the October essay sitting on the table is the proof the distinction is real. Notice also what the teacher refuses to do: name the something. No amateur diagnosis, no guessing at bedrooms. The evidence stops where the classroom door does.

Take it anywhere: "This isn't about ability — it's about ___ getting in the way." Struggling colleagues, failing students, your own bad month.
Phrase 5

The job: handing the parent the expert's chair. Put the pieces in order:

does any of thisYou know herring true at home?better than I do —
"You know her better than I do — does any of this ring true at home?"
The hinge of the meeting. Until this sentence the parent is a defendant hearing charges; after it she's a consultant being briefed — and consultants contribute instead of contesting. Ring true = match what you already know. It's also simply honest: you have four hours a week of the child; she has twelve years. The quietness-since-half-term detail only surfaced because this question invited it.

Take it anywhere: "You know ___ better than I do — does any of this ring true?" Parents, partners of patients, managers of your colleagues — recruit the person with the fuller picture.
Phrase 6

The job: sizing the problem honestly — serious, and solvable. Complete it:

"It's nothing we can't ___ ___."

"It's nothing we can't turn around."
Said after the problem has been named in full — that's what separates it from a platitude. The reasons come attached: she's twelve, the talent is proven, it's been weeks not years. Hope with evidence is fuel; hope without it is anaesthetic. And "we" is still doing quiet work — whoever's turning this around, it's plural.

Take it anywhere: "It's nothing we can't turn around" — but only after the honest accounting, never instead of it.

📋 The plan, and the exit

Phrase 7

The job: the two-sided plan — your half first. Complete it:

"Here's what I'll do at school — and here's where you can ___ at ___."

"Here's what I'll do at school — and here's where you can help at home."
Order is everything: your commitments first (Friday check-ins, the shared log), hers second — and hers framed as "help", not homework-for-parents. Note how concrete both halves are: not "more support" but a named day, a visible log, an essay on a kitchen table. A plan the parent can picture is a plan that survives the drive home.

Take it anywhere: "Here's what I'll do — and here's where you can help." Every joint problem, at work and at home: your half first, their half specific.
Phrase 8

The job: the checkpoint — so tonight isn't a one-off. Complete it:

"Let's speak again in three ___ and see where we ___."

"Let's speak again in three weeks and see where we are."
A plan without a checkpoint is a wish. The date turns tonight's alignment into a project with a review built in — and "I'll ring you, you won't have to chase me" removes the burden from the person already carrying most of it. Three weeks is deliberate: long enough for the plan to bite, short enough that drift can't set in.

Take it anywhere: "Let's speak again in ___ and see where we are." Close every hard conversation with the date of the next one.
Phrase 9

The job: the sentence she should carry home. Complete it:

"She's a good kid having a hard ___ — those are ___ things."

"She's a good kid having a rough patch — those are different things."
A rough patch — a difficult period that passes. This is the meeting's parting gift, and its deepest idea: separating who someone is from what they're going through. A parent who drives home holding "good kid, rough patch" treats the child as someone to help; one holding "she's been lying to me" treats her as someone to confront. Same facts — entirely different Tuesday at that kitchen table. Say it last; last sentences are the ones that get kept.

Take it anywhere: "___ is a good ___ having a rough patch — those are different things." Colleagues, friends, yourself in a bad season.
Phrase 10

The job: the opening that sets the whole temperature. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Thanks for coming in — I've been looking forward to this one."
Seven words against ten years of parents'-evening dread. "Looking forward to this one" only lands because it's checkably true — you were, because of the essay, because of Amir — and it instantly reclassifies the meeting from tribunal to conversation between two people on the same side. The coat came off her shoulders in the last line of the scene; it started coming off here.

Take it anywhere: "Thanks for coming in — I've been looking forward to this one." Any meeting the other person has been dreading more than you have.

⚡ The twist

Round 1 again — and when you ask whether any of this rings true at home, Mrs Novak looks at the table for a long moment and says, very quietly: "Her father moved out in March. She made me promise not to tell the school. She said she didn't want to be 'the divorce kid'. So now you know — and now I've broken a promise to her."

There it is: the something in the way, and a mother holding two kinds of guilt at once. You are her English teacher, not her counsellor — but the next thirty seconds are yours. What do you say about the promise, what do you offer, and where — carefully — does this information go?

The Echo 🕰️

Marie Curie won two Nobel Prizes, but one of her strangest experiments ran at her kitchen table. Unimpressed by Parisian schools, she and a circle of professor friends built a teaching cooperative: each parent-scientist taught the children their own subject — Marie took physics, real physics, with real equipment, for ten-year-olds. She believed children could handle far more truth than schools dared give them.

Her daughter Irène was a quiet, awkward girl who lost her father at eight and grew up in the shadow of the most famous woman alive — a rough patch by any measure. Marie's response was neither pressure nor protection. It was evidence-based belief: she saw the ability, named it, worked alongside it, and waited. Irène grew up to win the Nobel Prize in Chemistry herself, in 1935 — the only mother-daughter pair in the prize's history.

Every parents' evening runs the same quiet experiment: an adult who has seen what a child can do, telling the people who love her — with evidence — that the ability is real and the rough patch is not the whole story. Sometimes the results take twenty years to publish.
