Dealing with Disaster

B2 Upper-Intermediate English Lesson | Crisis Management & Advice

Context: Crisis Situations

In this lesson, we'll explore how to discuss disasters, express different levels of certainty, and give advice in critical situations.

Think About It

What would you do in each of these emergency situations?

  • Forest fires sweep through a nearby area
  • Your car breaks down in the desert
  • The local river floods the surrounding area
  • Temperatures drop to -10°C during a severe cold snap
In each case, you would need to stay calm, contact emergency services, gather essential supplies, follow official warnings, and help others if safe to do so.

Why Does This Matter?

When facing a crisis, you need to:

  • Assess the likelihood and severity of the situation
  • Give and receive clear, practical advice
  • Express what you believe will happen and what people must do

Expressing Certainty & Possibility

When dealing with disasters, you need to express different levels of certainty—from 100% sure to completely unlikely.

High Certainty (Very Likely → Certain)

Absolutely Inevitable:
If there's a total blackout, a rise in crime is absolutely inevitable.
The evacuation will happen—it's absolutely impossible to avoid it.
Very Likely / Quite Likely:
If there's severe flooding, it's very likely that you'll have to evacuate.
If your car breaks down in the desert, it's quite likely that you'll be stranded for a long time.

Medium Certainty (Possible)

Possible / Fairly Possible:
In extreme cold weather, it's possible that there will be power outages.
It's fairly possible that roads will become impassable.

Low Certainty (Unlikely → Impossible)

Quite Unlikely / Highly Unlikely:
If there are forest fires, it's quite unlikely that you'll be able to use all the roads.
Finding a mobile signal in the desert is highly unlikely.
Absolutely Impossible:
During a pandemic lockdown, it's absolutely impossible to go to public places.

Check Your Understanding

Highest to Lowest Certainty: absolutely inevitable → very likely → quite likely → possible → fairly possible → quite unlikely → highly unlikely → absolutely impossible

Phrases for Giving Advice

In a crisis, clear, direct advice is essential. Here are the key phrases from most to least direct:

Strong/Essential Advice

You really must... / You absolutely must...
You really must evacuate before nightfall.
You absolutely must stay hydrated in the desert.
If I were you, I'd...
If I were you, I'd contact the emergency services immediately.
If I were you, I'd gather supplies and head to higher ground.

Firm but Less Absolute Advice

You might want to... / You might consider...
You might want to check the emergency broadcast system regularly.
You might consider staying with friends outside the danger zone.

Gentle Reminders

Don't forget to... / Make sure you...
Don't forget to bring important documents if you evacuate.
Make sure you have a full tank of petrol before the weather gets worse.

Practice Matching

"You really must..." and "You absolutely must..." are the most direct and urgent. "If I were you, I'd..." is slightly softer. "You might want to..." is gentler still. "Don't forget to..." is the least forceful.

Speaking Practice: Disaster Scenarios

Practise combining certainty expressions with advice phrases.

Scenario 1: Blackout in the City

Situation: A total blackout has affected your entire city. Electricity is expected to be down for at least 24 hours.

Complete this advice combining certainty and a phrase:

A rise in crime is _____ (quite likely/absolutely inevitable), so you really must _____ (your advice).

"A rise in crime is quite likely / absolutely inevitable, so you really must stay indoors and lock your doors securely."

Scenario 2: Car Breakdown in the Desert

Situation: Your car has broken down in the middle of the desert. Mobile signal is very weak.

Complete this advice:

Finding help is _____ (highly unlikely/quite unlikely), so if I were you, I'd _____ (your advice).

"Finding help is highly unlikely, so if I were you, I'd stay with your vehicle, ration your water, and try to find some shade."

Scenario 3: Severe Flooding

Situation: The local river has flooded, and authorities have issued evacuation warnings.

Complete this advice:

You having to evacuate is _____ (very likely/quite likely), so don't forget to _____ (your advice).

"You having to evacuate is very likely, so don't forget to take important documents, medications, and irreplaceable items with you."

Scenario 4: Extreme Cold

Situation: Temperatures have dropped to -10°C and are falling further. Schools and many businesses have closed.

Complete this advice:

Power outages are _____ (possible/quite possible), so you might want to _____ (your advice).

"Power outages are possible, so you might want to stock up on food, water, and warm bedding just in case."

Your Turn: Create Your Own Public Announcement

Imagine you are the Minister of Public Safety. Choose one disaster scenario and prepare a short public announcement.

Pick Your Disaster:

Option 1: Forest fires are spreading rapidly—residents must evacuate

Option 2: A pandemic requires lockdown—non-essential businesses must close

Option 3: A hurricane is approaching—coastal areas must prepare

Option 4: An industrial accident is affecting air quality—people should stay indoors

Your Announcement Should Include:

  • What is the situation? (2-3 sentences)
  • What are the possibilities? (Use 2-3 certainty expressions)
  • What should/shouldn't people do? (Use 2-3 advice phrases)

Lesson Wrap-up

You've learned how to handle crisis situations through language. Here's what you now know:

Key Takeaways

✓ Expressing Certainty:

You can now range from "absolutely inevitable" to "absolutely impossible" depending on how confident you are about what will happen.

✓ Giving Effective Advice:

You can choose between urgent ("you really must") and gentler ("you might want to") depending on the situation and your relationship with the listener.

✓ Communicating in Emergencies:

You can now combine these expressions to give clear, practical guidance in crisis situations.

Reflection Questions

1. Which certainty expression surprised you?

Think about which expression you hadn't used before, or which situation made it most useful.

2. When would you use "if I were you" vs. "you really must"?

Use "you really must" for genuinely dangerous situations. Use "if I were you" for suggestions that are still serious but allow more personal choice.

3. What's the most important skill you've practised today?

Consider whether it was expressing uncertainty precisely, giving appropriate advice, or combining both in natural speech.

Next Steps

Continue building these skills by:

  • Watching news reports and noting how journalists express certainty
  • Reading advice columns and identifying the phrases used
  • Practising your public announcement with friends or family
  • Exploring other high-stakes scenarios that require careful language
