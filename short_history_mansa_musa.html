The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The golden empire

In the early fourteenth century, the Mali Empire stretched across a vast band of West Africa — from the Atlantic coast deep inland, taking in much of what we now call Mali, Senegal, Guinea and beyond. Its wealth rested on two things the medieval world craved: gold and salt. The gold fields of the south and the salt mines of the Sahara met in Mali's markets, and whoever controlled that trade controlled one of the richest crossroads on Earth.

Onto this throne, around 1312, came Musa Keita I — Mansa Musa, the title mansa meaning something like "emperor" or "king of kings". Under his rule the empire was enlarged and its trade routes were tightened into a single, well-governed network. Cities such as Timbuktu and Gao were drawn firmly into his realm. Order was kept across thousands of miles of desert and savannah, and the caravans moved in safety.

Just how rich Mansa Musa was is almost impossible to state. Later writers reached for the largest words they had — and modern historians, warning that no true figure can be given, still sometimes call him the wealthiest individual in all of human history. His fortune was not counted; it was simply beyond counting.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What two goods was the wealth of the Mali Empire built on?

2. What does the title "Mansa" mean, and roughly when did Mansa Musa come to the throne?

1. Gold and salt — the trade routes carrying them crossed in Mali's markets.

2. "Mansa" means roughly "emperor" or "king of kings"; he came to the throne around 1312.

Discuss:
  • We measure wealth today in currencies and stock markets. Why might it be genuinely impossible to say who the "richest person in history" really was?

Part 2 — The pilgrimage that shook the world

A devout Muslim, Mansa Musa set out in 1324 on the hajj — the pilgrimage to Mecca that every Muslim who is able should make. But this was no quiet journey of faith. According to the accounts that came down to us, a caravan of tens of thousands set off across the Sahara: soldiers, officials, servants and enslaved people, together with a long train of camels, each said to be loaded with heavy bars of gold, and hundreds more carrying gold dust.

The procession crossed thousands of miles of desert and, in 1324, reached Cairo, then one of the great cities of the Islamic world. There Mansa Musa gave gold away on a scale nobody had witnessed — to officials, to the poor, to anyone he met, spending and donating with breathtaking generosity. And here the story turns strange: so much gold was poured into Cairo's economy that its value collapsed. Prices rose, the gold-based currency was thrown into disorder, and the effects of that inflation were felt in the region for years. A single man's piety and pride had, in passing, damaged the finances of a great city.

The pilgrimage also carried his name into the wider world. Merchants and travellers spread astonished stories of the African emperor and his impossible wealth. Decades later, when a famous European atlas was drawn, Mansa Musa was pictured on it — enthroned in the Sahara, a golden crown on his head and a nugget of gold held out in his hand — the image by which Europe first came to imagine the riches of West Africa.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Mansa Musa travel to Mecca in 1324, and what did his caravan carry?

2. What happened to Cairo's economy because of his visit?

1. He made the hajj, the pilgrimage every able Muslim should make; his enormous caravan carried camels loaded with gold bars and gold dust.

2. He gave and spent so much gold in Cairo that its value collapsed, causing inflation that was felt in the region for years.

Discuss:
  • Mansa Musa's generosity accidentally wrecked a city's economy. Can doing good on a huge scale sometimes cause real harm? Think of a modern example.

Part 3 — The long echoes

Mansa Musa returned home changed by what he had seen, and he turned his gold to a lasting purpose. Architects and scholars were brought back with him, and building began. In Timbuktu and Gao, mosques were raised — among them the great Djinguereber Mosque of Timbuktu, built in mud-brick and still standing today. Around them, Timbuktu grew into one of the most celebrated centres of learning in the medieval world: libraries were filled, scholars were paid, and books, it was said, became worth more than almost anything else one could trade.

For a time, students and teachers were drawn to Timbuktu from across the Muslim world to study law, mathematics, astronomy and the Qur'an. A city that Europe had barely heard of became a byword for scholarship and wealth alike. Under Mansa Musa, Mali reached the height of its power and its prestige.

Empires do not last. In the centuries after his death, Mali declined and was eventually overtaken by rivals, and Timbuktu's golden age faded. But the echoes carried far. Mansa Musa is remembered as one of the wealthiest and most consequential rulers who ever lived, and his story is a standing reminder that in the medieval world, some of the richest, most learned and most powerful courts on Earth were African — a truth that, for centuries, was too easily forgotten.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Mansa Musa build with his gold, and what did Timbuktu become known for?

2. What lasting lesson does his story teach about the medieval world?

1. He built mosques (including the Djinguereber Mosque) and funded libraries and scholars; Timbuktu became a famous centre of learning and scholarship.

2. That some of the richest, most learned and most powerful courts of the medieval world were African — a truth long overlooked.

Discuss:
  • Mansa Musa chose to spend his fortune on mosques, libraries and learning. If you had unlimited wealth, what would you want to be remembered for building?

Grammar — the passive (past)

So much of this story is about things being done to cities and money, often without us caring exactly who did them: mosques were built, gold was given away, a currency was thrown into disorder. That is the natural home of the passive voice — and it is one of the surest signs of a fluent, formal storyteller.

How the past passive is formed:
was / were + past participle (the third form of the verb: built, given, made, brought, filled).
• Active: "Mansa Musa built the mosque." → Passive: "The mosque was built by Mansa Musa."
• Active: "Servants filled the libraries." → Passive: "The libraries were filled."
Why choose the passive?
• When the doer is unknown or unimportant: "Gold was carried across the desert." (by whom? it doesn't matter)
• When you want to foreground the thing done to, not the doer: "Timbuktu was transformed into a centre of learning."
• Add the doer only if it matters, with by: "The empire was enlarged by Mansa Musa."
• It gives writing a formal, historical tone — which is exactly why it fills chronicles and textbooks.

1. Make it passive

Rewrite each active sentence in the past passive. (You usually don't need to name the doer.)

1. Musa's men built many mosques. → Many mosques .

2. The caravan carried the gold across the desert. → The gold across the desert.

3. Scholars filled the libraries with books. → The libraries with books.

1. were built

2. was carried

3. were filled

2. Choose the correct participle

Complete each passive sentence with the past participle of the verb in brackets.

1. Mansa Musa often the richest person in history. (call)

2. So much gold away that prices collapsed. (give)

3. Architects back from Cairo to build the mosques. (bring)

1. is called / was called (is called — for a lasting reputation; was called — historically)

2. was given (give → given)

3. were brought (bring → brought)

3. Tell the story — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the past passive (was/were + past participle) of the verb in brackets. Read it aloud once you're done.

In 1324 a huge caravan (prepare) for the journey to Mecca. In Cairo, so much gold (spend) that the currency (throw) into disorder. Back home, great mosques (raise), and Timbuktu (transform) into a centre of learning.

was prepared · was spent · was thrown · were raised · was transformed

Notice how the passive keeps the focus on the caravan, the gold, the mosques and the city — not on the many people who did the work.
