BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

The Tree of Life

A Texas family grieves the death of a son while wordless images show the birth of the universe itself. The cosmology and biology behind those images are real.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of The Tree of Life — a 1950s Texas family grieving the death of a son, intercut with a wordless sequence showing the birth of the universe — discover the real science behind that sequence, and master nominalisation: turning verbs and adjectives into the abstract noun phrases that give English its most reflective, formal register.
Before we begin — talk it through: Think of a family you know well. Is one parent usually stricter and one gentler, or is that too simple a way to see people? When something painful happens inside a family, do people usually grow closer, or drift apart? And do you ever look up at the night sky and think about how small a single human life is against the age of the universe?

The film, in three parts

Terrence Malick's The Tree of Life (2011). This is not a film with a tight plot — it moves in fragments of memory, held together by images and a handful of whispered voices. Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it. The subject is grief, so we'll handle it with care.)

Part 1 — A telegram, and a family

In 1950s Waco, Texas, the O'Brien family lives an ordinary life shaped by two very different people. Mr. O'Brien, the father, is stern and exacting: a man of frustrated ambitions who believes the world rewards will, discipline and dominance, and raises his three sons — Jack, R.L. and Steve — accordingly. Mrs. O'Brien, the mother, is gentle and luminous: she moves through the same house offering tenderness, forgiveness and acceptance, seemingly untroubled by the harder terms her husband lives by. A whispered voiceover early in the film sets out the contrast plainly: there is the way of nature, and there is the way of grace.

Into this ordinary life a telegram arrives. It tells Mrs. O'Brien that one of her sons — R.L., nineteen years old — has died. The film never explains how. We see only the aftermath: her scream of grief on the lawn, the father's stunned collapse at work, and a series of hushed, half-finished sentences from friends and relatives trying and failing to offer comfort. A whispered prayer rises over the images, addressed to no one in particular and everyone at once: why?

The middle-aged Jack (played as an adult by Sean Penn), now an architect in a modern glass-and-steel city, wakes on the anniversary of his brother's death still carrying the question. His mind drifts back — not in an orderly story, but in the scattered, non-linear way memory actually works — toward his childhood, his parents, and something far larger than either.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What are the "two ways" the voiceover contrasts, and which parent is linked to each?

2. What news arrives by telegram, and what does the film choose not to explain?

1. "The way of nature" (will, discipline, dominance — linked to the stern father) and "the way of grace" (love, tenderness, acceptance — linked to the gentle mother).

2. The telegram tells Mrs. O'Brien that her son R.L. has died at nineteen; the film never explains how he died.

Discuss:
  • Why might a film deliberately choose not to explain how a character died? Does the silence make the grief feel more or less real to you?

Part 2 — The birth of the universe

At the height of Mrs. O'Brien's grief, the film does something almost unheard of: it leaves the family entirely and, for around twenty wordless minutes, shows the birth of the universe itself. Cascading light becomes swirling gas; gas collapses into stars; stars scatter into galaxies. A young, molten Earth cools under volcanic skies. Single cells appear in shallow water. Jellyfish drift through blue light. Millions of years compress into images: the first plants, the first creatures crawling from the sea, and — startlingly — dinosaurs, moving through ferns on a primeval coastline.

In one small, much-discussed moment, a flesh-eating dinosaur finds another dinosaur lying wounded and vulnerable on a riverbank. It presses a clawed foot down on the creature's neck — and then, inexplicably, lifts its foot and walks away, leaving it alive. The scene is quiet and almost throwaway, but it plants something enormous: the suggestion that mercy, or something like it, existed long before any human being did.

The sequence ends as an asteroid strikes the Earth, and the film cuts — without comment — straight back to a maternity ward in the 1950s, where Mrs. O'Brien has just given birth to Jack. The universe's entire history has, in effect, been folded into the space between one family's grief and one family's joy.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the film show for roughly twenty minutes, right after the news of R.L.'s death?

2. Describe the small moment involving two dinosaurs. What does it seem to suggest?

1. A wordless sequence depicting the birth of the universe, the formation of stars and Earth, and the emergence and evolution of life, including dinosaurs.

2. A predator dinosaur holds down a wounded one, then lets it live instead of killing it — suggesting that something like mercy or compassion existed even in the deep prehistoric past.

Discuss:
  • Why place the birth of the universe directly beside one family's private grief? What is the film suggesting about the size of a single human loss against that much time?

Part 3 — Growing up between nature and grace

Back in Waco, young Jack grows up caught between his parents' two ways of living. He idolises his mother's gentleness but also craves his stern father's approval, and finds himself, uncomfortably, becoming a little like the man he resents — impatient, controlling, capable of cruelty towards his younger brothers even as he longs to be loved and to love in return. Childhood here is rendered as a series of vivid, fragmentary sensations rather than a plotted story: sprinklers on a lawn, a father's hands at the piano, a screen door banging, the particular fear and thrill of a father's anger.

As an adult, Jack moves through his sterile, modern workplace still haunted by these fragments and by the unresolved question of his brother's death and his own guilt and resentment towards his father. The film never explains exactly what Jack feels guilty about — only that the feeling persists, decades on.

In the film's final movement, imagery becomes overtly dreamlike: Jack wanders a vast, sunlit beach where every person from his past — his younger and older selves, his brothers, his parents as they were and as they might be reconciled — appears together, embracing, forgiven, at peace. Whether this is heaven, memory, imagination or simply Jack's own hope is left entirely open. The film ends not with an explanation, but with an image of acceptance.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What tension does young Jack grow up inside, according to the film?

2. Describe the film's final image, and what the film leaves deliberately unclear about it.

1. The pull between his mother's way of grace and his father's way of nature — he loves and resents his father, and fears becoming like him.

2. A vast beach where everyone from Jack's life appears together, reconciled and at peace; the film never clarifies whether this is heaven, memory or imagination.

Discuss:
  • The film ends with an image of reconciliation rather than an explanation. Do you find that satisfying, or frustrating? What would you have wanted instead?

Grammar — nominalisation

No film reaches for this more naturally than The Tree of Life, whose whispered voiceover constantly turns raw experience into abstract, contemplative nouns. Nominalisation means turning a verb or adjective into a noun phrase — it is the single biggest step between everyday spoken English and the reflective, formal register of essays, philosophy and voiceover narration.

How it works:
• "She lost her son" (verb) → "the loss of her son" (noun)
• "He grew aware" (verb + adjective) → "his growing awareness" (noun phrase)
• "The universe began" (verb) → "the beginning of the universe" (noun)
Common noun endings to listen for: -tion/-sion (creation, destruction), -ment (bereavement, judgement), -ness (tenderness, awareness), -ing (yearning, suffering), and irregular forms (lose → loss, grieve → grief).
Why writers use it, and how to unpack it: nominalisation turns an event into a "thing" you can discuss, analyse or carry through a sentence — it creates distance and abstraction, which is exactly the tone a reflective voiceover wants. To unpack a nominalisation back into plain speech, ask "who did what, to whom?" and rebuild the clause: "the father's resentment of his son" unpacks to "the father resented his son."

1. Nominalise the idea in brackets

Read the sentence and complete the noun phrase with the correct nominalised form.

1. Stars and galaxies formed out of gas and dust over millions of years. → The of stars and galaxies out of gas and dust took hundreds of millions of years. (form → noun)

2. The mother grieved for her son in near silence. → took over the house, and no one spoke about it directly for days. (grieve → noun)

3. The father was often absent from his sons' small joys, present only for correction. → His from their small joys stayed with Jack long into adulthood. (absent → noun)

1. formation (form → formation)

2. Grief (grieve → grief, an irregular noun form)

3. absence (absent → absence)

2. Unpack the nominalisation

Turn the noun phrase back into a clause with a subject and a verb.

The Real Story — the science, and the questions, behind the images

The twenty wordless minutes at the centre of The Tree of Life are not fantasy. They compress real cosmology and biology, made with an almost defiantly old-fashioned method — and they sit beside one of the oldest questions in philosophy and religion.

🎬 The effects were built from real chemistry, not computers

Terrence Malick brought in Douglas Trumbull — the visual-effects legend behind 2001: A Space Odyssey (1968) and Blade Runner (1982) — to design the creation sequence, working alongside visual-effects supervisor Dan Glass. Rather than build the universe with CGI, which Malick felt looked too synthetic, the team used practical, in-camera methods: chemicals, paint, fluorescent dyes, smoke, liquids, CO₂, flares, spinning dishes, fluid dynamics and high-speed photography, filmed to see which combinations produced convincing cosmic and organic shapes. It was Trumbull's first feature-film effects work in nearly thirty years.

The universe really did begin — and life really did climb this path

The sequence follows the real, currently accepted scientific timeline. The universe began in the Big Bang, roughly 13.8 billion years ago, expanding from an extremely hot, dense state. Gravity gradually pulled matter into the first stars and galaxies; heavier elements formed inside dying stars and were scattered into space, eventually seeding new solar systems — including, about 4.5 billion years ago, our own. The young Earth was indeed a molten, volcanic world before it cooled enough for oceans and, eventually, life.

Life's first steps — single cells in water, then more complex organisms, then animals moving onto land — followed over billions of years of real evolutionary history, culminating (long after jellyfish and early fish) in the age of dinosaurs the film depicts. The famous "mercy" moment — one dinosaur choosing not to kill another it could easily have killed — is not a documented historical event; no fossil records a single animal's choice. It is Malick's own invented image. But it is a deliberate, thoughtful one: real evolutionary biologists do study genuine altruistic and restrained behaviour in animals, and the scene uses that plausibility to ask whether something like compassion could exist before humans, or morality, ever did.

An old question, asked again: why do the good suffer?

The film opens with an epigraph from the biblical Book of Job: "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? … When the morning stars sang together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy?" (Job 38:4, 7). In the Book of Job, a righteous man suffers terrible losses and demands an explanation from God; God's reply, quoted here, does not answer "why" — it instead points to the overwhelming scale and mystery of creation itself. Malick uses the same move: instead of explaining why R.L. died, the film answers with the birth of the universe.

This is theodicy — the ancient philosophical and theological problem of why bad things happen to good people in a world said to be governed by love or justice. The film's own "nature vs. grace" framework offers one way of sitting with that question without resolving it: nature is indifferent, competitive, often cruel, yet grace — love, tenderness, forgiveness — persists inside it anyway, for no guaranteed reward. The Tree of Life won the Palme d'Or, the top prize at the 2011 Cannes Film Festival, and remains one of the most divisive major award-winners of its decade — audiences at its premiere reportedly booed as loudly as others cheered.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, did you know the creation sequence used real chemistry rather than CGI? Does that change how you see it?
  • The cosmology and evolutionary timeline are real; the "mercy" moment between the dinosaurs is invented. Does knowing that change how the scene affects you?
  • The Book of Job never actually answers "why do the good suffer" — it answers with the vastness of creation instead. Do you find that a satisfying kind of answer, or an evasion?
