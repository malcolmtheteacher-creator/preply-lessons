Circles and a Cricket Machine

A Cambridge logician didn't invent overlapping circles — and said so. But he used them so well that his name stuck. He also built a machine that bowled out an Australian star.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — A family of preachers

John Venn was born in Hull, in the north of England, in 1834, into a family of evangelical churchmen. His grandfather, also called John Venn, had been rector of Clapham and a leader of the Clapham Sect, the group that campaigned against slavery. Everyone expected young John to become a priest too.

In 1853 he went to Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, and in 1857 he graduated as sixth Wrangler — sixth among the best mathematics students of his year. Two years later he was ordained as a priest.

But in 1862 he returned to Cambridge to teach moral sciences — subjects that included logic and probability. Teaching changed the direction of his life. He needed ways to make difficult arguments clear to his students.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did his family expect him to become?

2. What does “sixth Wrangler” mean?

3. Why did he return to Cambridge in 1862?

1. A priest.

2. He was ranked sixth among the best mathematics students of his year.

3. To teach moral sciences, including logic and probability.

Discuss:
  • Have you ever followed a path that other people expected, and then changed direction?

Part 2 — Circles that were not new

In July 1880, Venn published a paper with a long title: “On the Diagrammatic and Mechanical Representation of Propositions and Reasonings”. In it, he used overlapping circles to show logical statements. With three circles, every possible combination — in A and B but not C, in all three, in none — has its own space.

He never claimed the idea was his own. Circles had been used for logic before, most famously by the mathematician Leonhard Euler in his Letters to a German Princess of 1768, and Venn called his diagrams “Eulerian circles”. He later wrote: “Of course the device was not new then, but it was so obviously representative of the way in which any one, who approached the subject from the mathematical side, would attempt to visualise propositions, that it was forced upon me almost at once.”

What Venn added was a system: his diagrams show every possible overlap, even the empty ones. The name “Venn diagram” came later; the philosopher Clarence Irving Lewis used it in 1918.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. When did Venn publish his paper?

2. What did Venn call his diagrams?

3. What did Venn add to the older circles?

1. In July 1880.

2. “Eulerian circles”.

3. A system that shows every possible overlap, even the empty ones.

Discuss:
  • Is it fair that the diagram carries Venn's name, not Euler's?

Part 3 — The machine that bowled a star

In 1883 Venn left the clergy, having concluded that his beliefs were incompatible with the Church of England. In the same year he was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society. Later in life he turned to history, and with his son compiled an enormous register of past members of the university.

He also had a rare talent for building machines. One of them bowled cricket balls. When the Australian team visited Cambridge in 1909, the machine reportedly bowled out one of their star batsmen, Victor Trumper. In 2024, the university's engineering department built a new version of it.

Venn died in 1923. Today he is remembered in a stained-glass window at his old college, on a bridge in Hull, and in almost every classroom in the world. Not bad for a man who borrowed his most famous idea — and was honest enough to say so.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Venn leave the clergy?

2. What happened when the Australian team visited Cambridge?

3. Give two ways Venn is remembered today.

1. He concluded that his beliefs were incompatible with the Church of England.

2. His bowling machine reportedly bowled out their star batsman, Victor Trumper.

3. Any two: a stained-glass window at his college, a bridge in Hull, Venn diagrams in classrooms.

Discuss:
  • Why do some people become famous for ideas they didn't invent?

How the tool works

Each circle is a group. Where the circles overlap, things belong to both groups; outside the circles are things that belong to neither. Here, two job offers are compared.

Using it, step by step

  1. Choose what you are comparing — two or three things — and draw one circle for each.
  2. Put features that only one thing has in its own part of its circle.
  3. Put shared features in the overlap. With three circles, check every overlap, including the middle.
  4. Read the picture: is the overlap big or small? Are the differences the ones that matter to you?

Try it yourself

Two friends are choosing a holiday: the mountains or the coast. Where on the diagram does each feature go?

1. Cheap flights from their city.
2. Skiing and hiking trails.
3. A theme park.
4. Fresh seafood restaurants by the harbour.
5. Beautiful views and local festivals.
6. Boat trips and surfing lessons.

1. Both — both places have it — the overlap.

2. Mountains only — only the mountains offer this.

3. Neither — neither place has one — outside both circles.

4. Coast only — only the coast has a harbour.

5. Both — shared by both — the overlap.

6. Coast only — only possible by the sea.

Where the tool breaks

Size isn't shown. A big area on the page might hold two tiny features; a Venn diagram doesn't measure importance.

Too many circles. Beyond three, the diagram quickly becomes hard to read.

Things aren't always in or out. Real categories are fuzzy: is a tomato a fruit or a vegetable?

Talk it through:
  • Draw a Venn diagram of you and someone you know. What surprised you?
  • When is a list of pros and cons better than a Venn diagram?

Grammar — both, either, neither — and so do I

Venn diagrams are about what things share. English has a small family of words for exactly that — and a quick way to say “me too” and “me neither”.

both … and — two things together; both of + us / them / the …
“Both the hotel and the flat have a pool.”  “Both of them speak French.”
either … or — one or the other; neither … nor — not one and not the other.
“We can meet either on Monday or on Friday.”  “Neither the bus nor the train goes there.”
either of / neither of + plural noun — usually with a singular verb in careful English.
“Neither of the plans is perfect.”
so / neither + auxiliary + subject — for agreeing.
“I love jazz.” — “So do I.”  “I can't swim.” — “Neither can I.”  “She went to Rome.” — “So did we.”
Watch out: no double negative: ~~Neither of them didn't come~~ → “Neither of them came.”
