Warm-up

Out loud, before anything else — don't worry about mistakes, just talk.

SPEAK

Think of two flats, jobs, or cafés you've compared recently. Describe them as a pair, out loud — try "It's both X and Y" or "Either X or Y".

Listen in on this conversation

Jess: Have you decided on a flat yet?

Priya: Not really — it's between two. One's both bigger and cheaper, but it's further from the station.

Jess: And the other?

Priya: Smaller, but neither noisy nor far from work. Even though it costs more, I think I'll take it.

Priya links her ideas in three different ways in just four lines. Can you spot all three before you check? Say them out loud first.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "both bigger and cheaper" — both...and, two matching qualities pulling the same way.
2. "neither noisy nor far from work" — neither...nor, two things ruled out together.
3. "Even though it costs more" — a fair exception: the fact doesn't change her decision.

This is why they matter: paired linkers let you compare precisely, and admitting a fair exception lets you sound honest instead of vague.
Today: pairing ideas exactly — both...and, either...or, not only...but also, neither...nor, no sooner...than — and admitting a fair exception with although / even though / even if — so you can compare things and be honest about the downside without stumbling over the grammar.

Two at Once

Pairing two ideas so a listener hears them as one balanced thought — as long as the two halves match.

Spot the pattern

  • "The new café is both cheap and excellent."
  • "You can either walk there or take the bus — it's ten minutes either way."
  • "She not only fixed the printer but also cleaned the whole office."

What has to be true about whatever comes after each half of the pair? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Whatever follows the first half must match, grammatically, whatever follows the second half — two adjectives, two verb phrases, two nouns. That's why "both cheap and excellent" works (adjective + adjective) and "not only fixed... but also cleaned..." works (verb phrase + verb phrase). Break that match and the sentence stops sounding balanced.
both + [X] + and + [Y]
either + [X] + or + [Y]
not only + [X] + but (also) + [Y]  ([X] and [Y] must be the same type of phrase)
Common trap: "I liked the flat both for the price and it's location." — the second half suddenly has its own subject, so it no longer matches. Repeat the preposition instead: both for the price and for the location.

Quick check

1. Two plans for Saturday: a walk in the park, or the cinema. Tell a friend what you could do — use either...or:

"We could ."

2. The new flat is close to the station, and it's cheap — two good things at once. Tell a friend, starting with both:

"It's ."

3. The restaurant on the corner has two problems worth mentioning: it's expensive, and the service is slow. Warn a friend — use not only...but also:

"It's ."

Neither Way

Ruling two things out together, agreeing fast, and telling a story where one thing happens straight after another.

Spot the pattern

  • "Neither the manager nor the assistant was in when I called."
  • "I haven't had lunch yet." — "Neither have I."
  • "No sooner had we sat down than the waiter brought the wrong order."

Look at the word order in the second and third lines — what's different from a normal sentence? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
neither...nor joins two things and rules them both out — the verb agrees with whichever noun is nearest to it ("the assistant was", not "were", because "assistant" is singular).

For short agreeing replies, the verb flips in front of the subject: Neither have I, not "Neither I have".

No sooner had also flips: subject and "had" swap places, and the second half is joined by than (not "when").
neither + [noun/verb] + nor + [noun/verb]  (verb agrees with the nearer noun)
Neither + have/do/can/was... + subject  (short agreeing reply)
No sooner + had + subject + past participle + than + [clause]
Common trap: "neither...or" — neither only ever pairs with nor. "Or" belongs to "either".

Yes, But...

Admitting a downside without changing your decision — a fair exception, not an excuse.

Spot the pattern

  • "Although the queue was long, we still got served in ten minutes."
  • "I like this street, even though it's noisy at night."
  • "Even if it's cold, the market still happens every Saturday."
  • "It's expensive. Worth it, though."

The first two are known facts. The third one isn't decided yet. What's the difference in meaning? Say it, then check.

Check the idea CLICK
although / even though / though = a real, known fact you're admitting ("it IS noisy — and I still like it"). even if = uncertain or hypothetical — the result stays the same either way, whatever happens ("cold or not, the market happens").

though can also sit alone at the very end of a casual sentence, doing the same job as "but" in reverse: "It's expensive. Worth it, though."
although / even though / though + [subject + verb]  (a fact, a real contrast)
even if + [subject + verb]  (uncertain — the result doesn't change either way)
..., + [subject + verb] + , though.  (casual, added at the end)
Common trap: "even though of the cost" — although / even though need a full clause (a subject and a verb), never "of + noun". For a noun, use despite or because of instead.
