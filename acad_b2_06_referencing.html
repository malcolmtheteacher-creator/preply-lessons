What Is Academic Referencing?

Referencing is the practice of acknowledging the sources you used in your work. When you quote, paraphrase, or use someone else's ideas, you must cite where that information came from.

Why Referencing Matters

Integrity: Referencing gives credit to original authors. It's not only polite—it's ethically required.

Credibility: Citing sources shows you've done research and aren't just making claims. It strengthens your arguments.

Accountability: Readers can check your sources and verify claims themselves.

Conversation: Academic writing is a conversation with other researchers. References show where you're building on existing work.

Plagiarism: What It Is and Why It Matters

Plagiarism is using someone else's words or ideas without crediting them. At university, plagiarism carries serious consequences:

  • Failing the assignment
  • Failing the course
  • Expulsion from the university
  • Damage to your academic and professional reputation

Types of Plagiarism

  • Direct plagiarism: Copying text word-for-word without quotation marks
  • Mosaic plagiarism: Paraphrasing slightly but keeping the same structure and logic without citation
  • Accidental plagiarism: Forgetting to quote or cite; using a source so similar to original you "re essentially plagiarizing
  • Self-plagiarism: Submitting the same work for multiple assignments

Important: Even accidental plagiarism is plagiarism. Universities assume you intentionally plagiarized unless you can prove otherwise.

The difference between B2 and university standards: B2 learners often paraphrase without citing, thinking they've "changed" the text enough. At university, if the ideas come from a source, you must cite even if you've paraphrased completely. The ideas belong to the original author, not your wording.

How to Reference: Basic Formats

Different disciplines use different citation styles. Your university will specify which one (usually Harvard, APA, or Chicago). Below are simplified examples.

In-Text Citations

Direct Quote (Harvard):
"Motivation is central to learning" (Smith 2020, p. 45).
Paraphrase (Harvard):
According to Smith (2020), motivation plays a crucial role in the learning process.
Multiple authors:
(Smith et al. 2020) — "et al." means "and others"

Reference List Format (Harvard)

Journal article:
Smith, J., Brown, M. and Jones, L., 2020. "Learning and motivation." Journal of Education, 45(3), pp.123-145.
Book:
Smith, J., 2019. The Art of Academic Writing. London: Academic Press.
Website:
Brown, T., 2021. Understanding plagiarism. [online] Available at: www.example.com [Accessed 15 March 2023].

Quoting vs. Paraphrasing

Original text: "The pandemic fundamentally changed workplace dynamics, shifting millions of workers to remote arrangements indefinitely."
Direct quote: "The pandemic fundamentally changed workplace dynamics, shifting millions of workers to remote arrangements indefinitely" (Jones 2021, p. 8).
Paraphrase (CORRECT): According to Jones (2021), the pandemic resulted in a permanent shift toward remote work for large numbers of employees.
Paraphrase (WRONG — still plagiarism): The pandemic fundamentally changed workplace dynamics, shifting many workers to remote arrangements permanently. (No citation — this is plagiarism even though wording changed!)

When to Cite

Cite whenever you:

  • Use someone's exact words (direct quote)
  • Paraphrase someone's ideas
  • Reference specific data or statistics
  • Describe someone's research or findings
  • Use someone's unique framework or model

You Don't Need to Cite:

  • Common knowledge ("The Earth orbits the sun")
  • Your own original ideas or analysis
  • General information that appears in many sources without a specific origin

Rule of thumb: When in doubt, cite. It's far better to over-cite than under-cite.

Practice: Identifying Plagiarism

Scenario 1: Direct Quote without Citation

Text submitted: "Climate change represents the most pressing environmental challenge of our time, threatening ecosystems and human populations alike."

Original source (Brown 2022): "Climate change represents the most pressing environmental challenge of our time, threatening ecosystems and human populations alike."

Is this plagiarism? YES. Word-for-word copying without quotation marks and citation. This is direct plagiarism, the most serious type.

Scenario 2: Paraphrase Without Citation

Original source (Smith 2020): "Students who engage in active learning strategies, such as self-testing and elaboration, demonstrate superior long-term retention compared to passive reading."

Text submitted: "Active learning approaches, including self-testing and explaining concepts, lead to better long-term memory than simply reading passively."

Is this plagiarism? YES. The wording is changed, but the ideas, structure, and examples are all from the original. Without citation, this is mosaic plagiarism.

Scenario 3: Correct Paraphrase with Citation

Original source (Smith 2020): "Students who engage in active learning strategies demonstrate superior retention."

Text submitted: "Research indicates that students who participate actively in their learning process retain information more effectively (Smith 2020)."

Is this plagiarism? NO. The wording is different, the citation is present, and the student has understood and reformulated the idea.

Scenario 4: Using Your Own Previous Work

Situation: You submit an essay for Course A, then submit the same essay for Course B without telling either professor.

Is this plagiarism? YES. This is self-plagiarism. Both professors expect original work written for their course specifically. Using the same essay twice is academic dishonesty.

Practice: Correct the Plagiarism

Fix this plagiarized passage:

Original source (Johnson 2021): "The correlation between sleep deprivation and academic performance is well-documented, with studies consistently showing that students who sleep less than six hours perform significantly worse on examinations."

Plagiarized text: "Sleep deprivation has a significant correlation with academic performance. Students who sleep less than six hours perform much worse on exams, according to multiple studies."

Corrected version: Research demonstrates a clear relationship between insufficient sleep and lower exam performance, particularly when students sleep fewer than six hours nightly (Johnson 2021).

What changed: Reformulated in own words, added specific citation, kept the author's core finding.

Academic Integrity Beyond Referencing

Other Academic Integrity Issues

  • Collusion: Working with others and submitting the same work as if it's your own individual work
  • Contract cheating: Paying someone to write your work or buying essays online
  • Misrepresentation: Claiming you did research you didn't, or falsifying data
  • Unauthorized assistance: Getting help that goes beyond what's permitted

Your University's Academic Integrity Code

Every university has specific rules about what constitutes plagiarism and cheating. Read your university's academic integrity policy carefully. It will specify:

  • How much collaboration is permitted
  • Whether AI tools (ChatGPT, etc.) are allowed
  • Required citation style
  • Consequences for violations

Practical Tips for Avoiding Plagiarism

  1. Always record your sources. Note titles, authors, publication dates as you research.
  2. Use your own language. When paraphrasing, read the source once, then write without looking at it.
  3. Quote sparingly. Paraphrase most ideas; use direct quotes only for particularly eloquent or precise statements.
  4. Use citation software. Tools like Zotero or Mendeley format references automatically.
  5. Check your work. Before submitting, review every paraphrase and quote to ensure citations are present.
  6. When unsure, ask. Email your professor if you're uncertain whether something needs a citation.

Remember: Academic integrity isn't about rules and punishment. It's about honesty, respect for others' work, and participating ethically in the academic community. Proper referencing demonstrates that you respect intellectual property and contribute honestly to scholarly conversation.
