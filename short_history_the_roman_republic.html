The Roman Republic

For nearly five hundred years, Rome ruled itself without a king — until the men who saved it became the ones who ended it.

Now — the words for today's story

1. After driving out their last king, the people swore they would never again kneel to one ruler; from then on they would be a republic, governed by elected officials answerable to the citizens.
a republic (n) — a state with no king or queen, in which power is held by the people and their elected representatives.
2. Nothing of importance was decided until the matter had been debated in the Senate, where a few hundred of the oldest and wealthiest men argued, voted, and steered the affairs of the state.
the Senate (n) — the council of senior, influential men who advised on and guided the running of the Roman state.
3. Each year the assembly chose two men to serve as consul, sharing the highest office between them so that no single person could ever rule alone.
a consul (n) — one of the two most senior elected leaders, who held office together for a single year.
4. On voting days the men of the city would gather in a great assembly in the open square, raise their hands, and decide together which laws would pass and which leaders would serve.
an assembly (n) — a large gathering of citizens who meet to vote on laws and choose their leaders.
5. Officials who took bribes, sold their votes and handed jobs to their friends spread a quiet corruption through the government, rotting it from the inside while pretending to serve the public.
corruption (n) — dishonest behaviour by people in power, such as taking bribes or abusing their position for private gain.
6. In a true emergency, when the city was in danger, the people would appoint a single dictator for a few months — one man given total command — and then expect him to give the power straight back.
a dictator (n) — a ruler with complete, unchecked power; in Rome, originally a temporary office for a crisis, later a permanent grip on power.
7. Year after year the legions marched further out, defeating neighbour after neighbour and annexing their lands, until a single city had come to rule territory across three continents.
to annex (v) — to take control of another country or territory and make it part of your own, usually by force.
8. The two generals turned their soldiers not against a foreign enemy but against each other, and the streets ran red as countrymen killed countrymen in a bitter civil war.
a civil war (n) — a war fought between groups of people within the same country.
9. He was eaten up by ambition: not content with wealth or a fine house, he wanted to climb higher than any man before him, whatever it cost and whoever stood in his way.
ambition (n) — a strong desire to achieve power, success or fame.
10. As a plebeian, he owned no great estate and carried no ancient family name; men like him had to struggle for generations before they could hold office beside Rome's oldest ruling houses.
a plebeian (n) — an ordinary Roman of the common people, as opposed to the wealthy, aristocratic patrician families.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A city without a king

According to tradition, in 509 BC the Romans drove out their last king, a tyrant remembered as Tarquin the Proud, and swore a furious oath: never again would one man rule them. In place of the king they built a republic — a system in which power was shared, watched and handed back. Instead of a single ruler for life, the citizens elected two leaders called consuls, who held office together for just one year. Each consul could block the other, and at the end of the year both stepped down. The idea was simple and deliberate: split the power, limit the time, and no one man could ever become a king again.

Above the consuls sat the Senate, a council of several hundred experienced men, mostly from the old wealthy families. The Senate did not pass laws on its own, but it controlled money and foreign policy, and its advice was rarely ignored. Below it, assemblies of ordinary citizens met in the open to vote on laws and elect officials. For genuine emergencies there was one more office: in a crisis the Romans could appoint a single dictator with total command — but only for six months, after which he was expected to lay his power down. Every part of the system was designed to check every other part.

It was not equal — at first the rich patrician families held nearly everything, while the common people, the plebeians, had little. However, over two centuries the plebeians fought, bargained and won real rights, including officials of their own to defend them. Meanwhile, the same Romans were busy fighting their neighbours. War followed war, and slowly, almost without noticing, the city began to expand across Italy.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the Romans give themselves two consuls instead of one ruler, each for only a year?

2. What was unusual about the office of dictator in the early Republic?

1. To stop any single man becoming a king: the two consuls could block each other, and their power lasted only one year before they had to step down.

2. It gave one man total power, but only temporarily — for a crisis, and for no more than six months, after which he was expected to give the power back.

Discuss:
  • The Republic was built around a fear of one person holding too much power. Do the countries you know still share that fear today? How do they guard against it?

Part 2 — Conquest and cracks

Over the next few hundred years Rome grew from a single city into the master of the Mediterranean. It crushed its great rival Carthage in a series of long, savage wars, and went on to swallow Greece, Spain and much of the Near East. Treasure, land and hundreds of thousands of slaves poured into Italy. Rome had become enormously prosperous — but the wealth was not shared, and that imbalance would slowly tear the Republic apart.

The richest families bought up vast estates and worked them with slaves, while ordinary citizen-farmers, away for years fighting Rome's wars, came home to find their small farms ruined or sold from under them. A landless, angry crowd swelled in the city. In 133 BC a young official named Tiberius Gracchus tried to give public land back to the poor. The Senate's elite saw a threat to their wealth, and Tiberius was beaten to death in the street by a mob of senators and their supporters. His brother Gaius took up the same cause a decade later; in 121 BC he too was hunted down and died. For the first time, Roman politics had been settled not by votes but by blood.

Once that line had been crossed, it could not be uncrossed. Ambitious generals noticed something dangerous: soldiers were now loyal to the commander who paid and rewarded them, not to the Republic itself. The general Marius rebuilt the army around such men. His rival Sulla then did the unthinkable — in 88 BC he marched his own legions on the city of Rome itself, seized power, and had himself made dictator, no longer for six months but for as long as he pleased. He hunted down his enemies, posting their names on public lists for anyone to kill. The old rules were breaking, one by one. Corruption spread, and meanwhile the gap between rich and poor grew wider still.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Rome's huge conquests end up damaging the Republic at home?

2. What happened to the Gracchus brothers, and why did it matter for the future?

1. The wealth, land and slaves from conquest went to the rich, who bought up huge estates; ordinary farmer-soldiers lost their land and became a poor, angry crowd, deepening the divide between rich and poor.

2. Both Gracchus brothers tried to help the poor and were killed by their opponents (Tiberius in 133 BC, Gaius in 121 BC). It showed that Roman disputes could now be settled by violence rather than votes.

Discuss:
  • When soldiers became loyal to a general rather than to the state, the danger began. Why is it so risky when people's loyalty shifts from a system to a single person?

Part 3 — The last consul, the first emperor

Into this broken world stepped Julius Caesar — brilliant, charming, and consumed by ambition. He won fame and a fortune conquering Gaul (roughly modern France), and behind him stood an army that adored him. The Senate, fearing his power, ordered him to give up his command and return to Rome alone. Instead, in 49 BC, Caesar led his soldiers across a small river called the Rubicon, the border he was forbidden to cross with an army. It was an act of open war against the Republic, and there was no going back.

Caesar won the civil war that followed and had himself made dictator — and then, in 44 BC, dictator for life. To a city that had spent four and a half centuries terrified of kings, this was unbearable. On 15 March 44 BC, a group of senators surrounded Caesar and stabbed him to death in the name of the Republic, certain they were saving it. However, they had killed the man, not the problem. His death did not restore the old system; it simply opened one final round of civil war.

Out of that last bloodletting came Caesar's young heir, Octavian. He defeated his rivals — among them Mark Antony and the Egyptian queen Cleopatra — and stood alone at the top. Octavian was far shrewder than Caesar. He kept the comforting old words — Senate, consul, Republic — and even claimed to have restored them. In reality he held all the power himself. In 27 BC the Senate gave him a new name, Augustus, "the revered one." The Republic was over. Rome now had, in everything but name, a king again — and we call him an emperor. The system built to make sure no one man could rule had ended with exactly that.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did crossing the Rubicon in 49 BC actually mean, and why was it so serious?

2. Did killing Caesar save the Republic? What happened instead?

1. Caesar led his army across the river he was forbidden to cross with troops, defying the Senate. It was an open declaration of war on the Republic, with no way back.

2. No. His assassins killed the man but not the underlying crisis; it triggered another civil war, from which Octavian emerged to take sole power and become Augustus, ending the Republic for good.

Discuss:
  • Octavian kept all the old names — Senate, consul, Republic — while quietly holding the real power himself. Why might a ruler prefer to keep the appearance of the old system rather than openly sweep it away?

Grammar — Linking Ideas: however / meanwhile / moreover

You already join ideas with little words like because and so. This story needs something bigger. To tell it well, you have to set one fact against another, run two events side by side, and pile up your evidence. For that, English reaches for discourse markers — linking words such as however, meanwhile and moreover that connect whole sentences and signal exactly how they relate.

What each one signals:
• however / nevertheless — a contrast; "this is true, but here is the opposite": "The system was clever. However, it could not survive ambitious generals."
• on the other hand / in contrast — two sides set against each other: "The rich grew wealthier. On the other hand, ordinary farmers lost everything."
• meanwhile — at the same time, somewhere else: "In Rome, senators argued. Meanwhile, in the provinces, discontent grew."
• moreover / furthermore — adding a further, stronger point: "Caesar was popular. Moreover, he commanded an army that adored him."
• consequently — a result; "because of that, this happened": "Soldiers were loyal to their general. Consequently, the Republic was in danger."
Position and punctuation — this is where learners slip:
• These markers usually stand at the start of a new sentence, followed by a comma: "The Senate held the real power. However, ambitious generals were rising."
• They are not the same as because / so, which join two halves inside one sentence: "The army was loyal to Caesar, so the Senate was afraid." You would not write "…, however the Senate was afraid" as one sentence — start a fresh one.
• Keep a full stop (or a semicolon) before the marker and a comma after it: "Rome expanded. Meanwhile, discontent grew."
