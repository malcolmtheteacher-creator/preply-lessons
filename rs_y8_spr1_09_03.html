Islamic Birth Rites and Marriage

What You'll Learn

You will explore Islamic birth rites (Adhan, Aqiqah, Bismillah) and the Islamic wedding (Nikah), understanding how these ceremonies welcome a child into the faith and unite a couple in sacred marriage.

Reflection:

Encounter: Islamic Birth Rites

The Adhan (Call to Prayer)

One of the first things done when a Muslim baby is born is to whisper the Adhan (the Islamic call to prayer) in the baby's right ear. This rite is called Tahnik. The Adhan is the proclamation of Islamic faith, and by speaking it to the newborn, the family welcomes the child into the Islamic community and establishes the child's identity as a Muslim.

The Aqiqah Ceremony

Days or weeks after birth, an Aqiqah ceremony is performed. In this rite, an animal (usually a lamb, goat, or cow) is sacrificed, and the meat is shared with family, friends, and the poor. The baby's head may be shaved, and the hair's weight in silver or gold is given to charity. The child is officially named at this ceremony.

Significance: Aqiqah means "to cut" and represents the cutting of the baby's bonds to the mother and the child's entry into the wider community. The sharing of meat distributes blessings and creates social bonds.

The Bismillah Ceremony

When a child begins to eat solid food (around 6 months), a Bismillah ceremony may be performed. The child is given their first taste of solid food while the family recites "Bismillah ar-Rahman ar-Rahim" (In the name of God, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful). This marks the child's participation in the sustenance of the community and their relationship with Allah.

Stages in Islamic Birth Rites

Adhan

Separation: Baby is welcomed into Islam. The Adhan marks the baby's religious identity from birth.

Aqiqah

Incorporation: Baby is officially named and welcomed into the community. Meat is shared, creating bonds with family and the wider community.

Explore: Islamic Marriage (Nikah)

In Islam, marriage (Nikah) is a sacred contract between two people and their families, witnessed by the community and Allah. The marriage ceremony is relatively simple but profoundly meaningful.

The Nikah Contract

The essential element of Islamic marriage is the Ijab (the offer) and Qabul (the acceptance). The groom (or his representative) offers marriage to the bride, and she accepts. This contract is witnessed by the Qadi (Islamic judge) or an Imam and at least two witnesses.

The Mahr

A crucial element of Islamic marriage is the Mahr—a gift given by the groom to the bride. This can be money, jewellery, or property. The Mahr is the bride's exclusive property and demonstrates the groom's commitment and responsibility. It's not a bride price but an honorarium of respect.

The Wedding Celebration

After the formal contract, there's typically a celebration (Walimah) with a feast. The Walimah includes the community—family, friends, neighbours—in witnessing and celebrating the marriage. This public celebration is important; it makes the marriage known to the community and creates witnesses to the union.

Islamic perspective on marriage: Marriage is one half of faith. In Islam, marriage is not just a social contract but a spiritual union between two people and, by extension, two families and communities.

Evaluate: Ritual and Identity

The Power of Islamic Rites

Islamic birth rites and marriage rites don't just mark social transitions; they establish the person's religious identity and their relationship with Allah and the community. The rituals are relatively simple, but their spiritual significance is profound.

Your reflection:

Review: Key Concepts

Adhan and Aqiqah

Birth rites welcoming the baby into Islam. The Adhan whispers the Islamic faith in the baby's ear; the Aqiqah officially names the baby and shares meat with community and the poor.

Bismillah

Ceremony when the child begins eating solid food, marking their participation in the sustenance of the community.

Nikah (Marriage)

An Islamic marriage contract involving Ijab (offer) and Qabul (acceptance), witnessed by the community. The groom gives a Mahr (gift) to the bride. The Walimah (feast) celebrates the marriage publicly.

Central insight: Islamic rites of passage are relatively simple in form but carry profound spiritual meaning. They establish identity, create community bonds, and connect the individual to Allah and Islamic tradition.
