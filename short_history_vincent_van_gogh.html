Vincent van Gogh

A painter the world ignored in life — and could not stop loving the moment he was gone.

Today: you'll tell the story of a life of failure that became one of art's greatest legends, and discuss recognition that arrives too late — using the language of concession and contrast (although, despite, whereas, and yet) to weigh what was against what came after.

Key words — guess first, then check

2. She sat by her sister's hospital bed every single night for a year, never once complaining — the kind of devotion that asks for nothing in return.

4. After months of sleepless nights and impossible pressure, he finally sat down at his desk and could not stop shaking — a complete breakdown, and no work got done for weeks.

an asylum (n) — (historical) an institution for the care of people with mental illness.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The late beginning

Vincent van Gogh was born in 1853 in the south of the Netherlands, the son of a country pastor. For most of his twenties he looked like a man who would never find his place. He tried to make a living as an art dealer and was dismissed; he tried teaching; he tried the church, training as a preacher and working among poor miners in Belgium with such fierce, self-punishing zeal that his superiors decided he was unsuitable. One by one, the doors he pushed against closed. To almost everyone who knew him, he was a misfit — intense, awkward, impossible to settle.

It was only at the age of twenty-seven, after all of this, that he finally decided to become an artist. He had no formal training to speak of and very little money, but he threw himself into drawing and then painting with extraordinary discipline. And from the very start there was one person who believed in him: his younger brother, Theo, an art dealer in Paris. Theo sent money, paints and encouragement, month after month, year after year. The two wrote hundreds of letters, and it is in those letters — frank, searching, often beautiful — that we hear Vincent thinking aloud about art, about loneliness, and about what he was trying to do.

Without Theo's devotion, there would simply be no Van Gogh. He paid for the brushes, the canvases and the rent; he kept his brother alive. Vincent knew it, and the debt weighed on him. He was producing work nobody wanted, on money he had not earned — and he was painfully aware that the person paying for his great experiment was the brother he loved most.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What had Vincent tried — and failed at — before he turned to art, and how old was he when he started?

2. In what ways did Theo support him, and why was that support so important?

1. He tried being an art dealer, a teacher, and a preacher (working among poor miners in Belgium) — failing at each — and only became an artist at twenty-seven, with no real training.

2. Theo, his younger brother, sent money, paints and constant encouragement, and the brothers exchanged hundreds of letters. Without that financial and emotional support, Vincent could not have painted at all.

Discuss:
  • He found his calling only after years of failure, and only because someone else paid for it. Does that change how we should think about "natural talent"?

Part 2 — The yellow house and the breakdown

Once he had found painting, Van Gogh worked at a pace that is hard to imagine. In roughly a decade he made around two thousand works of art — drawings, sketches and some nine hundred paintings — among them the blazing Sunflowers and, later, The Starry Night. Few artists have ever been so prolific, and fewer still have changed so fast, his colour growing bolder and stranger with almost every month.

In 1888 he moved to Arles, in the south of France, drawn by the light and the colour of the landscape. He rented rooms in a small house and painted it yellow — the famous "Yellow House" — and dreamed of turning it into a community where artists could live and work together. He persuaded the painter Paul Gauguin to join him. For a few intense weeks they painted side by side, but the two strong, difficult men clashed constantly, and the dream curdled into tension. In December 1888, after a bitter quarrel, Van Gogh suffered a severe breakdown and, in his distress, cut off part of his own ear. Gauguin left. The community was over before it had truly begun.

What followed was a long struggle with his mental health. The next year he admitted himself to an asylum at Saint-Rémy, where his illness came in waves — periods of darkness broken by periods of astonishing clarity. And in those clear stretches he painted some of his greatest work, including The Starry Night, with an intensity that seemed only to deepen as his life grew harder. Through all of it, almost nobody was buying. He had given the world a flood of masterpieces, and the world had barely noticed.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How much did Van Gogh produce, and what are two of his most famous paintings?

2. What was the "Yellow House," and what happened there at the end of 1888?

1. Around two thousand works in about a decade, including some nine hundred paintings — among them Sunflowers and The Starry Night.

2. It was the house in Arles he hoped to turn into an artists' community with Gauguin. After weeks of clashes and a bitter quarrel, Van Gogh had a severe breakdown and cut off part of his ear; Gauguin left and the dream collapsed.

Discuss:
  • Some of his finest work came during his illness, in the asylum. Is there a real link between suffering and creativity — or is that an idea we find comforting but should question?

Part 3 — Fame that came too late

In 1890 Van Gogh moved to Auvers-sur-Oise, a village near Paris, to be closer to Theo and under the eye of a sympathetic doctor. He was still painting furiously. But in July of that year he received a gunshot wound and died two days later, at the age of thirty-seven. It has traditionally been ruled a suicide, and that remains the most widely accepted account; some historians have since questioned the details, and the exact circumstances are still debated. What is certain is that a brilliant, exhausted man died believing himself a failure, having sold almost nothing in his lifetime.

Theo was devastated, and never recovered. He died only six months later, in early 1891. It seemed, for a moment, as though the whole story might simply vanish — two brothers gone, a stack of unsold canvases, a bundle of letters. But it did not vanish, and the reason was a single, determined woman.

Theo's widow, Jo van Gogh-Bonger, inherited hundreds of Vincent's paintings and the brothers' letters. Almost everyone advised her to let it go. Instead she made it her life's work to champion him. She lent the paintings to exhibition after exhibition, built relationships with dealers and critics, and edited and published the letters so the world could meet the man behind the work. Slowly, then suddenly, recognition came. Within a few decades Van Gogh was one of the most famous and beloved artists who had ever lived — and today his paintings sell for tens of millions. It is a profound irony: the man who could not sell a canvas to feed himself became, after his death, beyond price. The fame he longed for arrived in full — but only once he was no longer there to feel it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Van Gogh die, and what state was he in when he died?

2. Who was largely responsible for his posthumous fame, and how did she achieve it?

1. He died in 1890 at thirty-seven, from a gunshot wound — traditionally ruled a suicide, though the exact circumstances are debated. He died believing himself a failure, having sold almost nothing.

2. Theo's widow, Jo van Gogh-Bonger. After Theo also died, she inherited the paintings and letters and spent her life promoting them — lending works to exhibitions and publishing the letters — which built his lasting fame.

Discuss:
  • His fame depended almost entirely on one person choosing not to give up on his work. How much of what we call "greatness" is really luck and other people's belief?

The grammar this story runs on: concession & contrast

This is a story built on a contradiction. He failed in life, yet triumphed after death; he sold almost nothing, yet produced a flood of masterpieces. To hold two opposing facts together in one sentence — to say "this was true, and despite that, so was its opposite" — English uses the language of concession and contrast.

The key distinction is what follows the linker:
• although / even though / though + a clause (subject + verb): "Although he sold almost nothing, he never stopped painting."
• despite / in spite of + a noun or an -ing form (no clause): "Despite his poverty, he produced over two thousand works." / "In spite of being ignored, he kept going."
• whereas / while set two sides side by side: "Whereas he died unknown, his brother's widow made him world-famous."
• and yet / nevertheless land the contrast as a second sentence: "He was ignored in life; and yet today he is one of the most loved painters of all."

1. although / even though / though + clause

These take a full clause (subject + verb). Complete each with the linker that fits, then read it aloud.

1. he sold almost nothing in his lifetime, he never stopped painting.

2. He kept working with burning intensity his illness came in waves.

3. Theo had no money to spare, he paid for his brother's paints and rent.

1. Although / Even though / Though (+ a full clause: he sold…)

2. although / even though / though

3. Although / Even though / Though

2. despite / in spite of + noun or -ing

These take a noun or an -ing form, never a full clause. Rewrite the idea so it fits.

1. Despite (his poverty and illness), he produced over two thousand works.

2. In spite of (be) ignored by the public, he believed in his work.

3. Despite (sell) almost no paintings, he is now beyond price.

1. his poverty and illness (a noun phrase — no verb)

2. being (in spite of + -ing)

3. selling (despite + -ing)

3. whereas / while · and yet / nevertheless — two-sided contrast

Use whereas / while to balance two facts in one sentence, and and yet / nevertheless to swing a second sentence against the first. Read each one aloud once you've finished.

1. Van Gogh died believing himself a failure, the world now calls him a genius.

2. He could not sell a single canvas to feed himself; his paintings now sell for tens of millions.

1. Whereas / While (two sides balanced in one sentence)

2. and yet / nevertheless (the contrast lands as a second statement)

Notice: although / even though take a clause; despite / in spite of take a noun or -ing; whereas / while weigh two sides; and yet / nevertheless answer back.

Few lives turn on a contrast as sharp as Van Gogh's — failure against legacy — which is exactly why this grammar is worth getting fluent in, ready for the Speak tab.

Task C · A clash of expectation and reality (4 minutes)

Tell the story of any person, work or idea you choose — a writer, a band, an invention, a business — that was overlooked at the time and only valued later. Keep the contrast doing the work: weigh what was expected against what actually happened, using "although…", "despite…", "whereas…" and "and yet…".

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • A relative whose talent — a beautiful singing voice, a gift for storytelling, a knack for fixing anything — was never taken seriously: "although she had a real gift, nobody ever encouraged her."
  • A teacher or mentor whose lessons you dismissed at the time but now repeat to others word for word: "despite ignoring her advice back then, I quote it constantly now."
  • A book, film or album that flopped on release and was rediscovered decades on — "Moby-Dick sold barely a few hundred copies in Melville's lifetime, and yet it is now a classic."
  • An inventor or scientist who died poor while others profited — "Whereas others grew rich on the idea, Tesla (or Rosalind Franklin) got almost no credit."
  • A small business, shop or local craft that closed just before its trade became fashionable: "despite holding on for years, they shut just as the trend arrived."
  • A decision in your own life — a course you dropped, a city you left, a friendship you let fade — that looks different now: "although it felt right at the time, I see it differently today."

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. His paintings now sell for tens of millions, while he could not afford food. Who really benefits from "the price of art" — and does that price have anything to do with the art itself?
  2. Theo, and then Jo, gave their lives to a man and a body of work the world ignored. What does it take to keep believing in something nobody else can see?
  3. We love the idea of the misunderstood genius who is only recognised after death. Is that a true picture of how art works — or a romantic story we tell ourselves?
