Rip Van Winkle

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four short activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. He would fish all day without a single bite and help any neighbour with any job — but the one thing he could not face was profitable labour on his own farm: a kind, hopeless idler.
an idler (n) — a person who avoids work; someone happily lazy. Verb: to idle; adjective: idle.
2. His wife lectured him morning, noon and night about his laziness — the neighbours pitied him as a henpecked husband ruled by her endless nagging.
nagging (n/adj) — continual complaining and criticising, especially at home. Verb: to nag someone.
3. High in the mountains he heard a sound like distant thunder rolling through a ravine — a deep, narrow valley between the cliffs.
a ravine (n) — a deep, narrow valley with steep sides, usually carved by water.

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: keg · slumber · beard. Match each definition to the right word — out loud, then reveal.

1. Which word means a small barrel for drink?
a keg (n) — "The stranger carried a stout keg upon his shoulder and beckoned Rip to help."
2. Which word means a deep sleep (a literary word)?
slumber (n/v) — "He sank into a deep slumber." The most famous slumber in American literature.
3. Which word means the hair that grows on a man's chin and cheeks?
a beard (n) — Rip wakes to find his beard has grown a foot long and turned grey.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. "The strangers were playing at ninepins, and every roll echoed like thunder." Ninepins is…
  (a) a card game   (b) an old form of bowling, with nine wooden pins   (c) a musical instrument
(b) — ninepins is old-fashioned bowling. In the story, the mysterious mountain men bowl in total silence — only the balls thunder.
2. "When he told his story, some villagers winked at each other." To wink is to…
  (a) close one eye briefly as a signal   (b) laugh loudly   (c) shake your head
(a) — to wink = to close one eye quickly as a private signal, here meaning "we don't quite believe him".

D · Word building & collocations

1. You know to recognise. Make the noun ("the village had changed beyond ___") and the negative adjective ("the streets were completely ___able").
recognition — "changed beyond recognition" · unrecognisable. Both describe exactly what Rip finds when he walks back down.
2. You know rust (the red-brown coating on old iron). Make the adjective: "his gun was ___" — and use the same word for a skill you haven't practised: "my French is a bit ___".
rusty — literal for the old firelock, figurative for unpractised skills. Twenty years will make anything rusty.
3. Collocation check: which verbs fit time? "___ asleep" (begin sleeping), "time ___ by" (passes), "___ up with the news" (stay informed)."
fall asleep · time goes by · keep up with the news. Rip falls asleep, twenty years go by, and he has not kept up with anything.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The kindest, laziest man in the village

In a little village at the foot of the Catskill Mountains, in the days when America still belonged to King George the Third, there lived a simple, good-natured fellow named Rip Van Winkle. He was a great favourite among all the good wives of the village, who took his part in all family squabbles; the children shouted with joy whenever he approached — he helped at their sports, made their playthings, taught them to fly kites and shoot marbles, and told them long stories of ghosts, witches, and Indians. Not a dog would bark at him in the whole neighbourhood.

The great error in Rip's character was an insuperable aversion to all kinds of profitable labour. It could not be from lack of perseverance: he would sit on a wet rock all day and fish without a murmur, even when he was not encouraged by a single nibble. He would carry a fowling-piece on his shoulder for hours, trudging through woods and swamps. He would never refuse to assist a neighbour, even in the roughest work; the women of the village employed him to run their errands and do little odd jobs their less obliging husbands would not do for them. In a word, Rip was ready to attend to anybody's business but his own. His own farm was the worst-conditioned in the county; his fences were continually falling to pieces; his cow would either go astray or get among the cabbages.

His wife, Dame Van Winkle, kept a very different opinion of him. Morning, noon, and night, her tongue was incessantly going: everything he said or did was sure to produce a torrent of household eloquence. Rip had but one way of replying — he shrugged his shoulders, shook his head, cast up his eyes, and said nothing. His only true friend was his dog Wolf, who was as henpecked as his master. For a long while, Rip consoled himself by frequenting a kind of club of the sages of the village, which held its sessions on a bench before the small inn, discussing village gossip and drowsy stories about nothing at all. But even from this stronghold poor Rip was at length routed by his wife, who would break in upon the tranquillity of the assemblage and call the members all to naught. Poor Rip was at last reduced almost to despair; and his only alternative, to escape the labour of the farm and the clamour of his wife, was to take gun in hand and stroll away into the woods.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the paradox at the centre of Rip's character?

2. Why does Rip keep escaping to the woods?

1. He is endlessly patient and hard-working for everyone else — fishing all day, helping every neighbour, running errands — but cannot bear a stroke of profitable work on his own farm, which is falling to pieces.

2. To escape the two pressures of his life: the endless labour his farm demands and Dame Van Winkle's ceaseless scolding, which has even driven him from the bench of village sages outside the inn.

Discuss:
  • Everyone loves Rip except his own wife — and she has to run the failing farm. Is the story being fair to Dame Van Winkle? Retell one paragraph from her point of view.

Part 2 — A strange evening in the mountains

One fine autumn day, Rip had scrambled to one of the highest parts of the Catskills, squirrel-shooting. Late in the afternoon he threw himself, panting, on a green knoll, and lay musing as evening gathered — dreading, as usual, the terrors of Dame Van Winkle waiting at home. As he was about to descend, he heard a voice from a distance calling, "Rip Van Winkle! Rip Van Winkle!" He looked round and saw a strange figure slowly toiling up the rocks, bending under a burden: a short, square-built old fellow, with thick bushy hair and a grizzled beard, dressed in the antique Dutch fashion. He carried a stout keg upon his shoulder, and made signs for Rip to approach and assist him. Though rather shy and distrustful, Rip complied with his usual alacrity, and together they clambered up a narrow gully — the bed of a mountain torrent.

As they ascended, Rip every now and then heard long rolling peals, like distant thunder, coming from a deep ravine. Passing through, they came into a hollow, like a small amphitheatre, surrounded by perpendicular precipices. In the centre, on a level spot, a company of odd-looking personages were playing at ninepins. They were dressed in a quaint, outlandish fashion — doublets, high-crowned hats and feathers — and they reminded Rip of the figures in an old Flemish painting. What seemed particularly strange was that, though these folks were evidently amusing themselves, they maintained the gravest faces and the most mysterious silence; nothing interrupted the stillness but the noise of the balls, which, whenever they were rolled, echoed along the mountains like rumbling peals of thunder.

His companion emptied the keg into large flagons and made signs to Rip to wait upon the company. He obeyed with fear and trembling; they quaffed the liquor in profound silence and returned to their game. By degrees Rip's awe subsided. He even ventured, when no eye was fixed upon him, to taste the beverage. One taste provoked another; he repeated his visits to the flagon so often that at length his senses were overpowered, his eyes swam in his head, his head gradually declined — and he fell into a deep sleep.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who does Rip meet on the mountain, and what does the stranger want?

2. What is strange about the company playing ninepins?

1. A short, square-built old man in antique Dutch clothes, carrying a keg up the mountain. He silently signals Rip to help him carry it to a hollow high in the rocks.

2. They play in complete, grave silence — no talk, no laughter — and the only sound is the rolling balls echoing like thunder. Rip serves them drink; then, tasting it himself again and again, he falls into a deep sleep.

Discuss:
  • Irving never explains who the silent bowlers are — village legend later says they are the ghosts of Hendrick Hudson's crew. Does the mystery work better unexplained? Why do you think they never speak?

Part 3 — Twenty years in one night

On waking, he found himself on the green knoll. It was a bright sunny morning. "Surely," thought Rip, "I have not slept here all night!" He looked for his gun, but in place of the clean, well-oiled fowling-piece he found an old firelock lying by him, the barrel encrusted with rust, the stock worm-eaten. Wolf, too, had disappeared. His beard, he discovered with astonishment, had grown a foot long — and grey. As he rose to walk, he found himself stiff in the joints. With some difficulty he got down into the glen; strangely, the gully up which he and his companion had climbed the night before was now a rushing stream, and of the amphitheatre no opening remained. He decided there was nothing for it but to go home — and dread Dame Van Winkle.

As he approached the village, he met a number of people, but none whom he knew, which somewhat surprised him, for he had thought himself acquainted with everyone in the country round. Their dress, too, was of a different fashion. They all stared at him with equal marks of surprise, and whenever they cast their eyes upon him, invariably stroked their chins. The village itself was altered: it was larger and more populous; rows of houses stood where none had been; strange names were over the doors, strange faces at the windows. His own house he found gone to decay — the roof fallen in, the windows shattered, the door off the hinges. He hurried to the old inn — but it too was gone, and in its place stood a rickety building with a tall pole before it, bearing a strange flag of stars and stripes; even the familiar face on the old sign was changed: the ruby face of King George had become a blue coat and a cocked hat, and underneath was painted in large characters: GENERAL WASHINGTON. There was, as usual, a crowd of folk about the door — but a busy, bustling, disputatious crowd, arguing about elections, congress, liberty and other words which were a perfect Babylonish jargon to the bewildered Van Winkle. While he slept, the American Revolution had come and gone.

"Does nobody here know Rip Van Winkle?" he cried at last, in despair. "Oh, Rip Van Winkle!" exclaimed two or three. "Oh, to be sure! That's Rip Van Winkle yonder, leaning against the tree." Rip looked, and beheld a precise counterpart of himself as he went up the mountain: apparently as lazy, and certainly as ragged. It was his own son. And then a fresh, comely woman pressed through the crowd with a child in her arms — his daughter Judith, now grown, who told him gently that her mother too had passed away some years before. Rip's story was soon told, for the whole twenty years had been to him but as one night. In time the villagers accepted it — the oldest inhabitant confirmed the legend of Hendrick Hudson's silent crew, who kept their vigil in the mountains with their game of ninepins every twenty years. Rip went to live with his daughter, took his old place on the bench at the inn door, and was reverenced as one of the patriarchs of the village, a chronicler of the old times "before the war". And to this day, when the summer thunder rolls around the Catskills, the people of the village say that Hendrick Hudson and his crew are at their game of ninepins.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name four changes Rip discovers on waking — in himself and in the village.

2. What great historical event did Rip sleep through, and how is he treated in the end?

1. Any four of: his gun is rusty and worm-eaten; his dog is gone; his beard is a foot long and grey; his joints are stiff; the village is bigger and full of strangers; his house is a ruin; the inn is gone; King George's portrait has become General Washington; his children are grown.

2. The American Revolution — he fell asleep a subject of King George and woke a citizen of the United States. In the end he lives happily with his daughter, takes his old bench at the inn, and becomes the village storyteller, honoured as a chronicler of the old days.

Discuss:
  • Rip slept through a revolution — and honestly doesn't seem to mind. What is Irving gently suggesting about how much (or how little) great political changes touch ordinary daily life?

Grammar — change over time: still, anymore, no longer, yet, already

This is the perfect story for the little words English uses to compare then and now. Rip's world is a before-and-after photograph: some things are still there, some are gone and exist no longer, some have already changed, and some he hasn't discovered yet.

The four tools:
• still — continuing, unchanged: "The mountains still stand where they always stood." (before the main verb, after be)
• not … anymore / no longer — a change; something has stopped: "He doesn't live there anymore." = "He no longer lives there." (anymore at the end of a negative sentence; no longer mid-position, more formal)
• yet — expected but not happened, in negatives and questions: "He hasn't heard the news yet." "Have you eaten yet?"
• already — sooner than expected: "The town has already doubled in size."
Everyday examples:
• "My childhood home is still there, but the cinema isn't there anymore."
• "She no longer works at the bank — she's already started her own company."
• "I haven't visited my old school yet, but I still think about it."

Going deeper

Irving published this story in 1819, in The Sketch Book — and it became one of the first pieces of American fiction the whole world read. He borrowed the sleeper legend from German folklore and replanted it in the Catskills, giving young America an instant piece of mythology, complete with a mock-scholarly frame insisting the tale is "absolute fact".

The heart of it. The story hides a sly political joke: Rip sleeps through the American Revolution and barely notices. The sign over the inn swaps King George's face for General Washington's — but it is the same sign, repainted; the crowd outside argues more loudly, but it is still a crowd outside an inn. Irving lets one man's harmless idleness measure how much of ordinary life survives even the greatest upheavals. And there's a second, gentler joke: sleeping twenty years costs Rip almost nothing — he wakes into old age, the one stage of life where his talents (storytelling, idling, being adored by children) are finally respectable. The tyranny he actually escaped, the story slyly notes, was not King George's.

Talk it through:
  • Rip misses the Revolution and integrates back into life within days. What is the story saying about the difference between history as headlines and history as daily life?
  • Is Rip's ending a reward or a quiet tragedy? He never changes, never grows — he simply becomes old enough for his laziness to look like wisdom. How do you read it?
  • Dame Van Winkle never gets to tell her side. Modern readers often see her differently than 1819 readers did. Make her case in two or three sentences.
  • Every culture has a sleeper legend — Germany, Ireland, Japan, China all have one. Why does this story keep being retold everywhere? What fear — or wish — does it express?
