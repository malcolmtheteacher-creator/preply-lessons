The Industry That Trained Its Replacement

For thirty years, human translators built the world's translation archives. Then the machines read them — and the first industry AI truly came for was the one that taught it to speak.

Today: you'll work through the live case of the language industry — the first profession to meet AI at full force — and you'll master the language of comparison: nowhere near as accurate, twice the speed at a tenth of the cost, the gap has all but closed.

The vocabulary of this case

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. A hospital admits a patient who speaks only Vietnamese; within sixty seconds, a trained voice is on the line, converting every question and answer in real time. That is interpreting — and it is a different profession from translation.
interpreting (n) — converting spoken language live; translation works on written text. An interpreter works in hospitals, courts and emergency calls; a translator works on documents. The distinction decides who survives this story.
2. Everything a translation firm had ever translated — millions of matched sentence pairs, source and target side by side — sat stored in databases called translation memories. Collectively, this is a parallel corpus.
a corpus (n, plural corpora) — a large body of text collected for analysis; parallel = the same content in two languages, aligned sentence by sentence. The industry's proudest asset — and, it turned out, the perfect textbook for a machine.
3. Today most agencies no longer ask translators to translate. The machine produces a draft, and the human corrects it — a task the industry calls post-editing, paid at a fraction of the old rate.
post-editing (n) — formally MTPE, machine-translation post-editing: reviewing and repairing machine output. The word that redefined a profession's job description — and its invoice.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: liability · a freelancer · per-word rate. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means a self-employed professional selling their work job by job?
a freelancer (n) — to freelance, freelance work. The industry's workforce is overwhelmingly freelance — which meant the disruption arrived without a single redundancy announcement.
2. Which term means the standard unit of pricing in translation — payment per unit of text?
the per-word rate — translation's price metre. Watching it move — and for whom it collapsed versus held — is how you read this entire case.
3. Which term means legal responsibility for harm caused by an error?
liability (n) — to accept / carry liability; liable for. The quiet hero of the case: a machine can produce words, but it cannot yet be sued — and where errors cost lives or lawsuits, humans keep the job.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Court interpreting, medical interpreting, emergency-line interpreting held firm while document translation prices fell. Analysts called these segments defensible. This means…

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — The invisible industry

Almost nobody outside it can name a translation company, and almost nothing in global commerce moves without one. The language-services industry — worth on the order of sixty to seventy billion dollars a year by the mid-2020s — is the plumbing of globalisation: every drug label in twenty-four EU languages, every aircraft manual, every subtitle, every patent filed abroad, every contract that crosses a border. Its structure is distinctive: a handful of large firms coordinating work that is actually performed by hundreds of thousands of freelancers, paid by the word, invisible even to the clients whose voices they become.

One corner of the industry is more dramatic than the rest, and worth meeting properly: over-the-phone and video interpreting. Hospitals, courts, police forces and emergency lines in immigrant societies cannot function monolingually — in the United States, access to an interpreter in healthcare is a legal right — so specialised firms keep thousands of interpreters on call around the clock, reachable in seconds, covering two hundred languages and more. An interpreter on such a line might carry a heart-attack call at nine, an asylum interview at ten, and a child-custody hearing before lunch — pure performance under pressure, where a mistranslated word can cost a case, a diagnosis, or a life. Keep this corner of the industry in mind; it behaves differently in every act of the story that follows.

And for decades, the whole trade had one quiet habit that becomes the case's hinge: it kept its work. Every translated sentence was stored beside its original in "translation memories," so that repeated content need never be paid for twice. Public institutions did the same at monumental scale — the European Parliament alone published decades of proceedings, professionally translated, in over twenty languages. By the 2010s, humanity had accumulated the largest parallel corpus of carefully matched sentences ever created: hundreds of millions of examples of exactly how an expert converts one language into another. Nobody thought of it as anything but an archive.

Part 2 — The archive wakes up

Machine translation was, for fifty years, the future that reliably failed to arrive. Rule-based systems produced word salad; the statistical systems of the 2000s — including early Google Translate — produced something better than nothing and worse than embarrassing, good enough for gist and a running joke among professionals. Then, in late 2016, Google switched its translation service to a neural network trained on parallel text, and the quality jump was so abrupt that it made global headlines: overnight, in major language pairs, output went from broken to frequently fluent. The training data that made this possible was, to a very large degree, the accumulated archive — the memories, the public corpora, the decades of human sentences. The industry had spent thirty years, without knowing it, writing its replacement's textbook.

Two ironies followed, each worth savouring. The first: in 2017, Google researchers looking for a better translation architecture published a paper called "Attention Is All You Need," introducing the transformer — the invention that later powered ChatGPT and the entire modern AI wave. The technology that would unsettle every knowledge profession was built, first and specifically, to translate. The second irony was European: that same summer, a small company in Cologne — DeepL, grown out of the translation-search engine Linguee — launched a neural translator that independent evaluations repeatedly ranked above the American giants' in its core languages. One of the finest translation machines on earth came from a mid-sized German firm; the disrupted industry's own continent produced the sharpest disruptor, later valued in the billions.

Inside the profession, the ground shifted within a few years. Agencies re-priced: why pay full rate for translation when the machine's draft was nearly right? The working offer became post-editing — clean up the machine, at half the rate or less — and the per-word price of ordinary commercial translation began a slide it never reversed. When large language models arrived after 2022, the pressure jumped again: now the machine could match tone, rewrite, localise idiom. Industry surveys filled with freelancers reporting collapsing volumes in "easy" segments; veteran translators of manuals and marketing copy watched decades of expertise re-priced as review work. And in 2025 a well-known language-learning company became the public face of the shift when its "AI-first" strategy memo — with contractors replaced where the machine sufficed — leaked into a week-long consumer backlash. The argument had left the trade press and reached the general public.

Part 3 — The fortress of consequences

Yet the industry did not shrink — and that paradox is the case's second half. Total language-services revenue kept growing through the disruption, because demand exploded: at machine prices, the world began translating things that would never have justified human rates — every product page, every support ticket, every video's subtitles in thirty languages. The pie grew while the per-word slice collapsed. The companies that thrived changed what they sold: not words but assurance — workflows in which machines produce volume and humans carry judgement, with the price attached to the risk of the content rather than the length of it. A tourist menu goes through raw; a pacemaker manual, a court bundle, a drug label goes through people, because when those are wrong, someone must be accountable — and a model cannot be sued, sworn in, or struck off.

Which is why the spoken corner of the industry — the interpreting lines — became the fortress. Demand for medical and legal interpreting kept rising through the AI decade: migration did not pause, regulations still required qualified humans, and no hospital's lawyers would put an unaccountable system alone between a doctor and a consent form. The interpreter's product was never really words-per-minute; it was a credentialed human being, present in the moment, responsible for what both sides understood — closer to a nurse or a court officer than to a document. Machines entered as assistants — transcribing, suggesting, covering languages no one could staff — while the accountable human stayed on the line. The pattern generalises, and by the mid-2020s every profession watching AI was studying it: automation flows fastest where errors are cheap and slowest where someone must answer for them.

The case remains live, and honest telling requires the open questions. Model quality keeps rising; the boundary of "errors are cheap" keeps moving; some interpreting itself — routine retail calls, basic support — is already crossing to machines, and the fortress holds only its high ground. Meanwhile the profession's pipeline is quietly cracking: if the entry-level work that once trained juniors is automated, where does the next generation of experts come from — the same succession problem Novo Nordisk faces with molecules, arriving here as people. The question this industry answered first, every other profession now faces in translation — so to speak: when the machine can produce your output, what exactly is it that you sell? The translators who are still standing have an answer, and it fits on an invoice: not the words — the responsibility for them.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Explain the paradox: prices collapsed, yet revenue grew.

2. Why did interpreting hold while document translation re-priced — and what is the generalised rule?

1. Induced demand: at machine prices, vastly more content became worth translating — support pages, subtitles, listings — so total industry revenue grew even as the per-word rate and the human share of each word fell.

2. Because its product is an accountable, credentialed human in a high-stakes live moment — required by regulation, backed by liability — not text volume. The rule: automation moves fastest where errors are cheap, slowest where someone must answer for them.

Grammar — comparison & degree

This whole case is an argument about how much: how much better, how much cheaper, how fast the gap is closing. C1 English measures those things precisely — and the difference between "better" and "marginally better" can be a business model.

Grading the gap:
• Big gap: far / considerably / vastly + comparative — "Raw machine output is vastly cheaper."
• Small gap: slightly / marginally / a touch + comparative — "The new model is marginally more fluent."
• Negative comparison, graded: not quite as … as (close) · not nearly as / nowhere near as … as (far apart) — "In 2010, machine output was nowhere near as good as human work; by 2020 it was not quite as good; today, in some pairs, the difference is hard to find." That sentence is the case in one line.
Multiples, fractions and moving gaps:
• twice / three times as fast as · half the price of · at a tenth of the cost — "Twice the speed at a tenth of the cost."
• The gap verbs: the gap narrowed / widened / closed; machine quality is catching up with / has all but caught up with human work; "all but" = almost entirely.
• The more…, the more…: "The cheaper translation became, the more the world translated."
• Comparative + and + comparative: "The defensible segment grows smaller and smaller."

Precision warning: "better" without a grader is advocacy, not analysis. Every comparative in a briefing should carry its degree — how much better, and at what.

Going deeper

A note on the record: market sizes and rate movements are rounded from industry research, which varies by definition; the freelancer experience is drawn from published surveys and reporting, and it varies enormously by language pair, specialism and country — averages hide both the casualties and the winners.

What the case actually tests. First, the data question nobody priced. The archive that trained the machines was assembled from millions of professionals' work, under contracts written before anyone imagined a corpus could learn. Nothing was stolen in law; everything was transferred in fact — the Blockbuster lesson's shape (nobody decided; the structure decided) applied to intellectual labour. Every profession now feeding its output into workplace systems is signing the same undated agreement, and this industry is what the signature costs.

Second, the anatomy of the fortress. What survived was not the hardest language work but the most consequential — a distinction professions routinely get wrong about themselves. Skill is not the moat; accountability is. The practical audit for any job: list your tasks, and beside each write not "can a machine do this?" but "who answers if this is wrong, and must they be human — by law, by insurance, by the client's fear?" The second column is the career.

Third, the pipeline paradox — this case's export to every other field. The entry-level work now automated was also the training ground; a profession that automates its juniors' work is eating its own succession. The industry's emerging answers — deliberate apprenticeships inside post-editing, credentialing, specialisation earlier — are improvised, and whether they work is genuinely unknown. It is the Novo Nordisk patent-cliff problem with people instead of molecules: today's seniors are running on expertise whose factory has closed, and nobody has yet certified the replacement line.

Talk it through:

  • Should the builders of the parallel corpus — the translators — have any ongoing claim on what learned from it? Design the mechanism, or argue why none is owed.
  • The fortress is stakes plus regulation. Is professional regulation here protecting the public, protecting incomes, or both — and how would you tell the difference from outside?
  • Series thread: Kodak's core rotted invisibly; this industry's re-priced in daylight while its revenue grew. Which is harder to manage through — and which company in this series handled its version best?
