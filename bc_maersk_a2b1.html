Ten Days Without Computers

A shipping company that carried a fifth of the world's containers lost every computer it owned in two hours — and it was not even the target.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then answer the questions before you read the next part.

Part 1 — Two hours in Copenhagen

On the afternoon of 27 June 2017, screens started going black in the head office of Maersk in Copenhagen.

They went black one after another, faster than a person could walk between them. Laptops restarted and showed a message asking for money. Telephones connected to the network died. People ran along the corridors shouting at colleagues to pull the cables out of the wall.

In about two hours, the company lost almost every computer it had: around forty-five thousand laptops and several thousand servers.

Maersk carried about one fifth of all the containers in the world. Now its booking system was dead. At ports from New Jersey to Rotterdam to Mumbai, the gates did not know which lorry could collect which box.

And here is the strangest part of this story. Nobody had attacked Maersk.

The virus, later called NotPetya, had been hidden inside an update to a piece of Ukrainian accounting software. Almost every company working in Ukraine had to use that software by law. From there the virus spread by itself, at machine speed, through company networks.

It looked like a normal attack asking for money. It was not. It destroyed the information permanently, on purpose. Paying was pointless.

Anyone connected to Ukraine — medicine companies, chocolate factories, shipping lines — was simply in the way. Governments later said it was one country attacking another.

The total damage was about ten billion dollars: the most expensive computer attack in history. Maersk's share was around two hundred and fifty million.

But that afternoon, nobody was counting money. Ships full of containers were arriving at ports every few minutes, waiting for instructions.

Part 2 — Paper, WhatsApp and a hard disk from Ghana

What happened in the next ten days is why people still teach this story.

The official systems were gone, so the unofficial company appeared. Staff at the port gates looked at lorries and decided with their own judgement, writing lists by hand. Bookings — normally thousands every hour through the website — were written in personal notebooks and organised through WhatsApp groups and free email accounts made that morning.

Older employees who remembered how the work was done before computers suddenly became the most important people in the building.

But to rebuild the network, the company needed one thing it did not have: a clean copy of its own directory — the system that knows who everybody is. Every copy had been destroyed.

Every copy except one.

In Ghana, a single server had been switched off during the attack, because of a local power cut. One unplanned blackout in West Africa had saved the only surviving copy of the company's identity.

The disk was flown to Britain. In the stories people tell, employees carried it by hand in a relay, like an organ for a transplant.

From that one disk, the network was grown again. A temporary command centre was set up in England. Staff and contractors worked day and night. Four thousand new servers and tens of thousands of machines were reinstalled.

The core was rebuilt in about ten days. Full recovery took months.

Part 3 — Luck is not a plan

Afterwards, the chairman of Maersk did something unusual. He spoke about it in public, honestly.

He said the company's computer security had been "average" — and that average was not good enough for a company the world's trade depends on. He described the ten days, thanked the staff for extraordinary work, and then said the thing most boards only whisper.

The company had been lucky. And luck is not a control.

Inside Maersk, the attack did what ten years of security budget requests had not. Computer risk moved from a slide in the IT department to the top of the board's agenda. The company spent heavily on separating its networks, on being able to recover, and on the boring discipline of knowing what breaks when something else breaks.

Across the whole industry, the attack changed the map. It showed three things. The most dangerous attack may be one aimed at somebody else. A backup is worth nothing if it depends on the thing that just died. And insurance written for wars between armies fits badly onto wars between computers.

But the honest question is the one the chairman raised and nobody has answered.

Maersk recovered because its people improvised brilliantly — and because a power cut in Ghana happened at exactly the right moment. Take away that luck, and ten days might have been ten weeks. Some later analyses suggest ten weeks could have threatened the whole company.

The company proved it was strong. But it had not designed that strength.

So every company that studies this case has to ask itself one question. How much of our recovery plan is really a plan? And how much of it is a Ghana we have not found yet?

Telling the story of an event: was doing and did

When you describe an incident, you need two past tenses working together. One for the background, one for the events.

Past continuous (was / were + -ing) = the longer situation, already happening. → "Staff were working normally."

Past simple = the short action that arrived. → "Then the screens went black."

Put them together with when and while: "Staff were working normally when the screens went black."

The rule that helps: while is usually followed by the long action (while they were working); when is usually followed by the short one (when the virus arrived).

TenseUseExample
was / were + -ingthe background, in progressShips were arriving at the ports.
past simplethe event that happenedThe system crashed.
while + past continuousthe long actionWhile staff were running down the corridor…
when + past simplethe short action…when the screens went black.

Find the mistake

Each sentence has one mistake. Say the correct sentence out loud, then check.

1. While the staff worked, the virus was spreading through the network.

2. The screens were going black at 2pm exactly.

3. When the system was crashing, everybody ran to the corridor.

4. They was working all night to rebuild the servers.

5. The disk arrived in Britain while somebody carried it by hand.

1. While the staff were working, the virus spread through the network. (after while, use the long action)

2. The screens went black at 2pm exactly. (an exact moment = past simple)

3. When the system crashed, everybody ran to the corridor. (after when, the short action)

4. They were working all night to rebuild the servers. (they takes were)

5. The disk arrived in Britain after somebody carried it by hand. (or: while somebody was carrying it)
