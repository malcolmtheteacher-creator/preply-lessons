The Rosetta Stone

A broken stone that helped people read a lost language again.

Today: tell the story of the Rosetta Stone, and talk about who should own old objects. Grammar: the passive (present and past simple).

  7. an object

Meanings

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — A stone in the sand

In 1799, French soldiers were in Egypt. The army of Napoleon was fighting there. The soldiers were building a wall near a town called Rosetta. While they were working, one soldier found a big, dark stone. There was writing on it.

This was not a normal stone. It was very ancient — more than two thousand years old. And it had the same words on it three times, in three different scripts.

At the top were Egyptian hieroglyphs — small pictures. In the middle was another old Egyptian script. At the bottom was ancient Greek. This was very important. People could still read Greek, but nobody could read the hieroglyphs.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who found the stone, and where?

2. What three scripts were on the stone?

1. French soldiers found it in 1799, near a town called Rosetta in Egypt.

2. Egyptian hieroglyphs, another old Egyptian script, and ancient Greek.

Talk about it:
  • The soldiers found the stone by accident. Have you ever found something important by accident?

Part 2 — Reading the secret

The British won the war against the French in 1801. So the stone was given to the British, and it was sent to London. It is still in the British Museum today.

But why was the stone so special? People could read the Greek part. The Greek words were the same as the hieroglyphs. So the Greek was a "key". It could help people read the old pictures at last.

Two clever men tried to do this. One was English: Thomas Young. He found some important clues. The other was French: Jean-François Champollion. In 1822, Champollion understood the secret. The hieroglyphs were not only pictures — some of them were sounds, like letters! After more than a thousand years, people could read ancient Egyptian again.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the stone come to London?

2. Who finally read the hieroglyphs, and when?

1. The British won the war in 1801, so the stone was given to them and sent to London (the British Museum).

2. Champollion, a French man, in 1822. (Thomas Young, an English man, found important clues first.)

Talk about it:
  • Two men wanted to be the first to read the stone. Do you like to compete? Why or why not?

Part 3 — Whose stone is it?

This was a huge moment. Now people could read all the old Egyptian writing — on temples, on walls, and on tombs. A whole ancient world came back to life. The Rosetta Stone was the key to all of it.

Today, the stone is in London. Many people visit it every year. But it is not in Egypt, the country where it was made.

Now there is a big question. Egypt says, "This is our stone. Please give it back." But the British Museum says no. Some people think old objects belong to one country. Other people think they belong to the whole world. What do you think?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was reading the stone so important?

2. What does Egypt want today?

1. Because now people could read all the other old Egyptian writing too. A whole ancient world came back to life.

2. Egypt wants the stone back. But the British Museum says no.

Talk about it:
  • The stone was made in Egypt but it is now in London. Where do you think it should be?

Grammar: the passive (present & past simple)

This story is about a stone. Many things happen to the stone. For this, we use the passive.

Past simple passive = was / were + past participle: "the stone was found," "the words were carved."
Present simple passive = is / are + past participle: "the stone is kept in London," "it is visited by many people."
We use the passive when the thing is more important than the person — or when we do not know who did it.

1. Past simple passive — was / were

1. The stone (find) in 1799.

2. The words (carve) into the stone long ago.

3. The hieroglyphs (decipher) in 1822.

1. was found

2. were carved ("the words" is plural, so we use "were")

3. were deciphered

2. Present simple passive — is / are

1. Today, the stone (keep) in the British Museum.

2. It (visit) by many people every year.

1. is kept

2. is visited

The stone is the important thing, so it stays at the front of the sentence.

3. Tell a short part of the story

Put the verbs in the passive (past or present). Then read it out loud.

The stone (make) more than two thousand years ago. It (lose) for a long time. Then it (find) by soldiers. Now it (show) in a museum in London.

was made · was lost · was found · is shown

Three things happened in the past (was), and one is true now (is).

Task B · Who should own it? (3 minutes)

The stone was made in Egypt, but it is in London. Who should own old objects — the country where they were made, or the museum that has them now? What do you think, and why?

Task C · A famous object (3 minutes)

Tell the story of any famous object you choose — a painting, a building, a crown, a statue. Where was it made? Where is it now? Why is it special?

Not sure what to talk about? Choose one:

  • A secret code or password you made with a friend when you were young. How was it made? Who could read it?

  • An old map or photo that was found in a box and finally explained where your family came from.
  • A song in another language that you did not understand, until the words were translated for you.
