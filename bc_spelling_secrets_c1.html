English: A Language That Can't Spell Itself

English borrows from Latin, French, Greek, Norse, and Germanic languages — and kept the spelling from all of them. The result? A language where though, through, tough, and thought all look similar but sound completely different.

The Three Biggest Spelling Traps

Most English spelling mistakes fall into a few predictable categories. Which of these do you find most challenging?

Double Letters Words with repeated consonants that are easy to miss: accommodation, occurrence, embarrass
Letter Sequences When letters appear in an unexpected order: receive (not recieve), rhythm (no standard vowel!)
Silent Letters Letters you write but never say: the b in subtle, the k in knowledge, the b in debt

The Pattern Behind the Chaos

Double letters in English usually appear for a reason:

1. After a short vowel sound: occur, effect, support

2. When a Latin prefix meets the root: ac- + commodate = accommodate, oc- + cur = occur

3. When adding suffixes: occur + -ence = occurrence (the R doubles), embarrass + -ing = embarrassing

Using the three patterns above, explain why "accommodation" has double C and double M. How does understanding the Latin prefix ad- + com- + modus help?

"I Before E" — The Most Famous Broken Rule

You probably learned: "I before E, except after C."

This works for: receive, ceiling, receipt, deceive, conceive

But it fails for: weird, seize, either, protein, their

A better version: "I before E, except after C — when the sound is /iː/." Most of the exceptions have a different vowel sound.

Test the rule:

Say each word aloud: receive, weird, ceiling, their, conceive, protein. Which ones have the /iː/ sound (like "see")? Does the extended rule work better?

Complete with IE or EI:

1. rec___ve    2. bel___ve    3. ach___ve    4. dec___ve    5. perc___ve    6. conc___ve

Show Answer
1. receive (after C)   2. believe (not after C)   3. achieve (not after C)   4. deceive (after C)   5. perceive (after C)   6. conceive (after C)

Rhythm — The Vowel-Free Mystery

Rhythm is one of the most unusual words in English: it has no standard vowels (A, E, I, O, U). The Y does all the vowel work.

A classic way to remember it: "Rhythm Helps Your Two Hips Move" — each first letter spells R-H-Y-T-H-M.

Can you say the mnemonic aloud and walk through how it maps to the spelling? Then try to think of another sentence where the first letters spell R-H-Y-T-H-M.
