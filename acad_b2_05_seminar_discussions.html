Participating in Seminars

B2 Level · Academic Speaking

5:00

What Is a Seminar?

A seminar is a discussion-based class where students and teachers interact as colleagues exploring ideas together. Unlike lectures where a professor talks and students listen, seminars are collaborative.

Seminar Expectations

  • You've read assigned materials before class
  • You prepare questions or notes about the reading
  • You actively participate in discussion
  • You listen to others and build on their points
  • You disagree respectfully when you have different views
  • The professor guides rather than dominates

Why Seminars Matter

Seminar participation helps you:

  • Develop critical thinking through discussion
  • Hear multiple perspectives on complex topics
  • Practice academic speaking and defending ideas
  • Build confidence in expressing views
  • Often, improves your seminar mark (participation counts)

The Challenge for B2 Learners

Speaking in seminars is harder than you might expect:

  • Pressure to respond quickly—no time to write
  • Multiple people talking; hard to find entry points
  • Need academic register and complex sentences simultaneously
  • Anxiety about making mistakes in front of the group
  • Difficult subject matter at your reading level but challenging for speaking

The good news: Seminars in English-speaking universities know many students are non-native speakers. Professors expect some language mistakes. What they value is thoughtful contribution and respectful engagement.

Reality check: Even native speakers sometimes make mistakes in seminars. Your professor will value the content of your idea, not perfect English. Speaking imperfectly is far better than staying silent.

Language for Seminar Participation

How to Enter the Discussion

To get attention politely:
"Could I add something here?"
"I'd like to contribute to this point..."
"May I offer a different perspective?"
To build on someone's point:
"That's an excellent point, and it raises a question about..."
"Following on from what you said, I think..."
"That connects to the author's claim that..."
To agree:
"I completely concur with that."
"That's a valid point."
"Absolutely, and I would add that..."

How to Disagree Respectfully

Soft disagreement (preserving the relationship):
"I see your point, but I'd interpret the evidence differently..."
"That's interesting, though I wonder if..."
"I appreciate that perspective, yet the text could also be read as..."
Direct disagreement (when needed):
"I disagree on that point because the research shows..."
"Actually, the evidence contradicts that conclusion..."
"I'd challenge that interpretation based on..."

How to Ask for Clarification

To understand another participant:
"Could you elaborate on what you mean by...?"
"I'm not sure I follow your argument. Are you saying...?"
"What's the connection between X and Y that you're making?"
To ensure you"ve understood correctly:
"So your argument is that...?"
"Just to confirm, you're claiming that...?"
"Is your point that...?"

How to Handle Uncertainty

When you're not completely sure:
"I might be misremembering, but didn't the author say...?"
"I think the point is..., though I could be wrong."
"Based on my reading, it seems like..., but I'd need to check."

Key principle: Academic discussion values ideas, not perfection. You can speak hesitantly, use simple structures, and still contribute meaningfully. Focus on your ideas, not your English.

Practice: A Simulated Seminar

Below is a seminar discussion excerpt. Read it and practice responses.

Professor: "We've read the case study on remote work policies. What's your assessment? Is remote work beneficial or problematic?"

Student A: "I think it's beneficial because employees have flexibility and better work-life balance."

Student B: "But doesn't it create isolation? And how do new employees learn company culture?"

How Would You Respond?

To build on Student A"s point
"That's a good point about balance. I'd add that the research shows remote workers often have better productivity because they have fewer office distractions."
To support Student B"s concern
"I agree with that concern. The case study mentions that new hires struggled without face-to-face mentoring. However, I think the solution is hybrid models rather than eliminating remote work entirely."
To introduce a new perspective
"Both points are valid. But I think we"re overlooking the economic impact. Reduced office space cuts company costs significantly, which could be reinvested in training."
To challenge the discussion"s assumptions
"I wonder if we"re assuming everyone values remote work equally. The case study only surveyed tech workers. What about industries where in-person work is essential?"

Preparing for Your Next Seminar

Before Seminar:

  • Read the assigned material thoroughly
  • Write 3-5 questions or key points from your reading
  • Note any arguments you disagree with and why
  • Look up unfamiliar vocabulary before class
  • Practice saying your ideas aloud (reduces anxiety)

During Seminar:

  • Take brief notes on discussion points
  • Aim to contribute at least 2-3 times
  • Don"t wait to be perfect—contribute even if uncertain
  • Listen carefully to others before responding
  • Support your claims with evidence from the reading

After Seminar:

  • Review your notes within 24 hours
  • Consider points you wished you"d made
  • Prepare these for the next discussion
  • Note any vocabulary you struggled with

Overcoming Anxiety

Fear: "I'll make a mistake and embarrass myself."
Reality: Everyone makes mistakes. Native speakers do too. Your professor expects some language errors from non-native speakers and doesn't judge you for them.

Fear: "My idea isn"t good enough."
Reality: Seminars are about exploring ideas, not having the "right" answer. A question or a different perspective is a valid contribution.

Fear: "I can"t think fast enough."
Reality: You don't need to respond instantly. Say: "That's interesting. Let me think about that for a moment..." This gives you time and shows thoughtfulness.

Your goal: Not to be perfect, but to engage. Each seminar you participate in makes the next easier. By mid-semester, seminar discussion will feel natural.
