Employee Engagement & Motivation

C1 Level · People Management

What Drives People?

Walk into any organisation and you'll see the same pattern: some teams are energised and productive; others feel like they're just going through motions. The difference isn't pay or job title. It's engagement.

Reflect:

  • When have you felt truly engaged at work? What made that possible?
  • When have you felt disengaged? What changed?
  • What do you think drives engagement for your team members?
  • How well do you understand what motivates each person individually?

The Motivation Paradox

For decades, organisations assumed money was the primary motivator. Give people bigger salaries and they'll work harder. But research—from Herzberg to Daniel Pink—shows something more complex.

Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory: Pay and benefits are "hygiene factors." Without adequate pay, people are demotivated. But more pay beyond that threshold doesn't drive engagement. Instead, engagement comes from meaning, autonomy, mastery, and recognition.

Pink's autonomy-mastery-purpose model: People are motivated by the freedom to direct their own work (autonomy), the opportunity to get better (mastery), and belief that what they're doing matters (purpose).

The disconnect: Most organisations focus on what's easiest to control and measure (pay, hours, tasks) and neglect what actually drives engagement (meaning, autonomy, growth).

The Cost of Disengagement

Disengaged employees don't just do less. They do the absolute minimum, and they infect others with cynicism. They take longer to do things, make more mistakes, and create friction with colleagues.

When your best people feel disengaged, they leave. You lose institutional knowledge, relationships with key partners, and team morale plummets as survivors wonder if they're next.

The bottom line: engagement isn't a nice-to-have. It's the foundation of productivity, quality, retention, and culture.

Consider: What's one sign that someone on your team is becoming disengaged? How would you respond?

Vocabulary & Key Concepts

Master the language of engagement to understand and foster it in others.

Engagement The emotional commitment and effort people invest in their work; feeling invested and valued.
Retention Keeping talented people on your team; reducing voluntary turnover and building tenure.
Autonomy Freedom and authority to make decisions about how work gets done; discretion in execution.
Purpose Understanding why work matters; connection to a larger mission or impact beyond the paycheck.
Recognition Acknowledgment and celebration of someone's contributions, achievements, and value to the team.
Burnout Physical and emotional exhaustion from chronic work stress; loss of energy, cynicism, and reduced effectiveness.
Psychological Safety Belief that you can take interpersonal risks (speak up, admit mistakes) without fear of embarrassment or punishment.
Discretionary Effort Extra effort people choose to put in beyond what's required; work they do because they care, not because they must.

Building Engagement: A Framework

1. Clarity on Purpose: People need to understand not just what they're doing, but why it matters. How does their work contribute to the team's goals and the organisation's mission? Can they see the impact?

2. Autonomy in Execution: Within clear boundaries, give people freedom to decide how to do their work. People engage when they have agency. Micromanagement erodes engagement faster than almost anything.

3. Investment in Growth: People want to develop and improve. Provide clear paths for learning, challenge them with stretch assignments, and invest in their development. They'll repay it with commitment.

4. Recognition and Feedback: People need to know their contributions are seen and valued. This doesn't always mean money. Specific recognition ("You handled that client conversation brilliantly because...") often means more than a bonus.

5. Psychological Safety: People disengage when they feel they can't be honest. Create a culture where questions, mistakes, and difficult conversations are safe. This requires you to model vulnerability and respond non-defensively when challenged.

6. Values Alignment: People want to work for organisations—and with leaders—whose values align with theirs. If there's a gap between what the organisation claims to value and what it actually does, engagement erodes.

Scenario: The Engagement Crisis

Your team's engagement survey results are poor. Scores are down 25% from last year. Exit interviews reveal people feel undervalued, see limited growth, and don't understand how their work matters. You need to address this honestly with the team—without being defensive or making false promises.

10:00

Your Task: Write the Opening Address

You're calling a team meeting to address the engagement survey results. Write what you'd say in the first 3 minutes. Your goals: acknowledge the problem honestly, take some responsibility, commit to change, and create hope that things will improve.

Effective structure:

1. Lead with honesty (30 seconds): "I've reviewed our engagement survey results, and they're sobering. Satisfaction is down, people don't feel valued, and we're losing good people. I'm not happy about that, and I own part of this."

2. Acknowledge impact (30 seconds): "I understand that when you don't feel valued or see a path forward, work becomes just work. It's hard to care. And I know that affects you personally—your sense of progress, your wellbeing, even your confidence."

3. Avoid defensiveness (30 seconds): "I'm not going to tell you what we got right or make excuses. What I want to know is: what changed? What do you need from me and this team?"

4. Commit to action (30 seconds): "We're going to listen, understand the real issues, and then make changes. I can't promise everything will be perfect, but I can promise transparency and real effort."

Deep Dive Questions

After the opening, you're going to have honest conversation. What three questions would you ask the team to get to the real issues? Write them out—they should be open-ended and invite real answers, not just "yes/no."

Powerful questions:

"When you think about staying on this team versus looking elsewhere, what's the gap? What would need to be true for you to feel excited about your work here?"

"Looking back, when did things start feeling different? What happened?"

"If you were redesigning this team and role from scratch, what would you change about how we work, what we focus on, and how people are developed?"

Why these work: They're open-ended, they assume problems are real (not just perception), and they invite solutions rather than just complaints. They also signal that you're genuinely interested in their experience, not just fixing a survey score.

Beyond Surveys: Real Engagement

Engagement surveys are useful, but they're a lagging indicator. By the time you get the results, disengaged people are already job hunting. The real work is continuous.

Reflection questions:

  • How often do you have real conversations with team members about engagement, meaning, and growth?
  • When someone seems disengaged, what's your first move?
  • How do you celebrate wins and acknowledge contributions?
  • What does autonomy look like in your team? Do people have it?
  • How do you help people see the impact of their work?

Monthly One-on-Ones

The best engagement investment you can make is regular, intentional one-on-ones. Not status updates on projects. Conversations about:

  • How they're doing personally and professionally
  • What energises them vs. what drains them
  • What they want to learn and grow into
  • Whether they feel valued and see a future here
  • What they'd change about work if they could

These conversations, done well, surface issues before they become turnover. They also signal that you care about them as people, not just workers.

The Generational Perspective

Different generations have different engagement drivers. Gen Z and younger millennials prioritise purpose, flexibility, and growth even more than older generations. Remote workers prioritise trust and autonomy. Parents with kids might prioritise flexibility and understanding.

There's no one-size-fits-all engagement approach. The key is knowing your people well enough to understand what matters to each of them.
