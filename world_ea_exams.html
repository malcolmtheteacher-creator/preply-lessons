Exam Hell: Do East Asian Students Only Study?

Huge entrance exams, evening cram schools and “tiger parents”. But school life is more than books — and many people want reform.

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The university entrance system is highly competitive.
competitive (adj) — involving people trying to do better than each other. Example: “The university entrance system is highly competitive.”
2. The national curriculum is heavy on maths and science.
curriculum (n) — the subjects taught in a school or course. Example: “The national curriculum is heavy on maths and science.”
3. Many families spend a large part of their income on tutoring.
tutoring (n) — private teaching, often given outside school. Example: “Many families spend a large part of their income on tutoring.”
4. Students say the pressure to succeed can be exhausting.
pressure (n) — a feeling of worry because other people expect a lot from you. Example: “Students say the pressure to succeed can be exhausting.”
5. Over thirteen million candidates take the national exam in China.
candidate (n) — a person taking an exam. Example: “Over thirteen million candidates take the national exam in China.”
6. Countries are often ranked by their students' test results.
rank (v) — to give or have a position in a list. Example: “Countries are often ranked by their students' test results.”
7. The government introduced a reform to reduce homework.
reform (n) — a change to improve a system. Example: “The government introduced a reform to reduce homework.”
8. Seoul introduced a curfew for private classes at ten o'clock.
curfew (n) — a rule that says people must be home or stop an activity by a certain time. Example: “Seoul introduced a curfew for private classes at ten o'clock.”

The stereotype: “East Asian students do nothing but study — and their parents push them all the time.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The big exam days

China's national university entrance exam, the gaokao, is held over two or more days every June, and more than thirteen million candidates have taken it in recent years. In South Korea the national exam, the suneung, is held on one day in November. Offices and shops open later, police give students lifts to the test centre, and planes are not allowed to take off or land during the English listening test.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. When is the gaokao held?

2. What happens to planes during the Korean English listening test?

1. Every June, over two or more days.

2. They are not allowed to take off or land.

Discuss:
  • What would your country do on a day when millions of people take the same exam?

Part 2 — The pressure

Many students also go to private classes after school: roughly three in four in South Korea, where they are called hagwon, and many in Japan, in schools called juku. In the 1990s Seoul introduced a ten o'clock curfew for these schools. Students say results can decide their university, their job and even their chances of marrying, and the stress is a serious concern. Governments are responding: in 2021, China restricted for-profit tutoring.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. About how many students in South Korea attend private classes?

2. What did China do in 2021?

1. Roughly three in four.

2. It restricted for-profit tutoring.

Discuss:
  • Where is the line between healthy effort and unhealthy pressure?

Part 3 — More than books

School life includes much more than exams. In Japan, students clean their own classrooms, serve lunch to each other and join after-school clubs. Korean and Chinese schools also have sports days and festivals. The term “tiger mother” comes from a 2011 book by Amy Chua about her own Chinese-American family, and many parents in the region describe a mix of high expectations and a lot of love. And countries from the USA to Finland are asking the same question: how much exam pressure is too much?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What do Japanese students do in school besides study?

2. Which country is mentioned as asking the same question about exam pressure?

1. They clean classrooms, serve lunch and join clubs.

2. The USA or Finland (and many others).

Discuss:
  • Which part of school life do you remember best — lessons, or everything else?

Grammar — Comparatives & Superlatives

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
Comparing:
• short adjectives: “Korean students attend more private classes than students in many countries.” / “The exam is harder.”
• superlatives: “the gaokao is one of the largest exams in the world.”
• intensifiers: “far more competitive”, “slightly less stressful”, “not nearly as relaxed as…”
• double comparative: “The more you study, the more tired you feel.”

1. Choose the right form

Complete each sentence about this topic.

1. The gaokao is one of exams in the world. (the largest / larger)

2. you study, the more tired you feel. (The more / More)

3. Japanese school days are relaxed than the stereotype suggests. (more / most)

1. the largest

2. The more

3. more

2. Now try these

1. Private classes in Korea are common than in many other countries. (far more / far most)

2. Is a competitive exam system fair than other systems? (more / most)

3. School is stressful than it was twenty years ago. (not nearly as / not as nearly)

2. more

3. not nearly as

Going deeper

The belief that education is the best way to improve your life has deep roots. In imperial China, exams for government jobs were open to many men from ordinary families for over a thousand years, and that history still shapes ideas about hard work and fairness. Today, exams can look like the fairest way to choose, because they measure the same thing for everyone. But when only a few places are at the top, the competition can feel endless.

Worth noticing: Comparing: -er / more … than, the -est / the most, the more … the more …, far / slightly more … than, not nearly as … as.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Be careful with questions about grades or university. In some families, results are a sensitive subject. Ask about hobbies, favourite subjects or what someone wants to do in the future instead, and you will get a warmer answer.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Is an exam the fairest way to decide who gets a university place?
  • Would you swap your school system for a more competitive one — or a less competitive one?
  • What is more important at school: results or happiness?
