Why This Matters

Academic debate isn't about your actual opinion — it's about understanding all sides of a question deeply. When you can argue both positions persuasively, you:

  • Understand the strongest case against yourself
  • Learn which arguments are actually evidence-based vs emotional
  • Discover where reasonable people genuinely disagree
  • Build flexibility in your own thinking

Debate Moves: Six Core Techniques

Click each to see what it is and how you'll use it in a real debate.

1. The Opening Statement▼

What it is: Your opening statement sets the frame. You state your burden of proof, define key terms, and signpost how you'll argue.

Real example

"This house believes the internet has done more harm than good. I define 'harm' as measurable damage to mental health, social cohesion, and democratic institutions. I'll argue in three parts: first, that internet addiction affects 60 million people globally; second, that social fragmentation has increased polarisation; third, that misinformation spreads faster than truth. The proposition need only show that harms outweigh benefits — and we'll demonstrate precisely that."

Why it works

You've defined the terms (not the opposition), stated your argument structure (so judges know what's coming), and clarified your burden (we need to show X, not Y). You've won the frame before the substantive debate begins.

2. The Proof▼

What it is: Evidence + warrant. You present a fact, then explain why it matters. Claim → evidence → impact.

Real example — weak

"The internet has caused mental health problems." (Where's the evidence? Why does it matter?)

Real example — strong

"The American Psychological Association reports that heavy social media use correlates with 25% higher rates of anxiety and depression in adolescents. This matters because one-third of teenagers now use social media daily, meaning millions are at risk. Mental health degradation in young people affects their educational outcomes, employment prospects, and social development — creating costs society bears for decades."

Why it works

You've given a specific source, concrete numbers, and explained the causal chain (why this fact proves your point). It's not just true — it's devastating.

3. The Point of Information (POI)▼

What it is: A respectful interjection during the opposition's speech. You ask a question or challenge a specific claim, and they must answer briefly.

Real example

Opposition says: "The internet connects billions of people and enables education access worldwide."

You raise your hand and say: "Point of information — when you say 'education access', are you accounting for the fact that 90% of the world's poorest people have no reliable internet? Are you addressing the digital divide that actually increases inequality?"

If they accept:

They must answer your question in 20 seconds. You've either caught them in an unfounded claim or made them defend a weak point.

If they decline:

"I'll come back to that" — still powerful, because you've highlighted a gap in their argument that judges noticed.

4. The Rebuttal▼

What it is: Directly engaging with their actual argument, not a strawman version. You show why their evidence doesn't prove their point or why their logic fails.

Real example — weak

"They said the internet is good, but actually it's bad." (You haven't addressed their specific argument.)

Real example — strong

"The opposition claims that e-commerce has reduced poverty. True, but their evidence conflates correlation with causation. Yes, internet access and poverty reduction happened simultaneously — but 70% of the poorest billion still have no access. Meanwhile, we know from ILO data that automation enabled by the internet has displaced 20 million manufacturing jobs in the Global South. Their benefit cherry-picks winners while ignoring losers."

Why it works

You've taken their specific claim, shown the logical flaw, and provided your own evidence countering it. You're not attacking a strawman — you're dismantling their actual argument.

5. The Weigh▼

What it is: Both sides may have made valid points. The weigh explains why your argument matters more. What's the scale? The scope? The certainty?

Real example

"The opposition is right that the internet enables video calls with distant relatives — that's a genuine benefit. But scale this against the harms we've proven: we're talking about millions suffering mental health crises, widespread democratic erosion, and exploitation of vulnerable populations. The opposition's benefit is individual and emotional; ours are systemic and affect billions. When you weigh a personal convenience against societal collapse, the scale is clear."

Why it works

You've conceded their point (building credibility) but shown that even their best case is outweighed by yours. You're arguing not just that you won points, but that your points matter more.

6. The Summary▼

What it is: Crystallise the debate. What was the clash? Why did you win it? Don't introduce new material — distil what was already said.

Real example

"The opposition's entire case rests on benefits of connectivity. But we've proven that the internet doesn't connect equitably — it amplifies inequality. We've shown harms outweighing benefits: mental health damage, political polarisation, job displacement. The opposition conceded our evidence on mental health but offered only weak rebuttals. On every clash — equity, harm scope, systemic impact — we've won. The motion passes."

Why it works

You're not re-arguing; you're reminding judges what happened and why it matters. This is where the debate crystallises in judges' minds.

Challenges from the Floor

Six questions from different audience perspectives. Each reveals as you progress. 45 seconds to respond to each.

1
(The Expert) Your methodology assumes X. Isn't that assumption questionable given research showing Y?

You've made a causal claim, but the expert is pointing out that your evidence doesn't actually prove causation — correlation isn't enough. Can you defend the logical leap you made?

0:45
2
(The Naive) Could you explain what you mean by that technical term? I'm not sure all of us followed.

You've used jargon that might have lost part of your audience. Can you translate it into plain English without dumbing it down? This tests whether you actually understand your own argument.

0:45
3
(The Hostile) This contradicts the findings of [major scholar/study]. How do you account for that?

Someone in the audience knows more about your topic than you expected. They've caught a contradiction between your argument and established research. How do you respond without panicking?

0:45
4
(The Scoper) Isn't this just a specific case of the much broader problem of Z? What does your argument add?

Someone is questioning whether your narrow argument matters at all — whether you're addressing a symptom rather than the disease. How do you defend your scope and significance?

0:45
5
(The Practical) This is theoretically interesting, but what are the practical implications? Who should act on this?

Your argument is abstract. The questioner wants to know: so what? What changes? Who does it affect? How does the world look different if you're right?

0:45
6
(The Curator) What do you see as the next step for this argument? What question does your claim open up?

The most generous question: what's the future? If you're right, what does that mean for the next phase of thinking on this topic?

0:45

After the Vote: Reflection & Language Reference

Reflection Questions

What surprised you about arguing a position you don't hold?▼

Often, students discover that the "opposing" side has stronger arguments than they expected, or that their own position has weak points they hadn't considered. This is productive — it builds intellectual humility and nuance.

Which debate move felt most powerful to you?▼

Was it the opening frame-setting? The evidence + warrant combination? The rebuttal that dismantled a specific claim? Or the weigh that showed why your points mattered more? Recognising which moves worked for you helps you use them more consciously next time.

What would you do differently in the next debate?▼

Did you spend too much time on evidence and not enough on impact? Did you concede too much or not enough? Did you fail to weigh? Learning from what didn't work is how debaters improve.

Formal Debate Language Reference

Opening & Signposting

"This house believes [X]. I'll argue in three parts: first, [part 1]; second, [part 2]; third, [part 3]. The burden of proof requires [specific standard]."

"I define [key term] as [definition that favours my case]."

"Turning now to the second point: [transition into next argument]."

Evidence & Warrant

"According to [credible source], [fact]. This matters because [impact chain]. Therefore, [claim proven]."

"The implication is clear: [argument]. The world looks different if we accept this."

Rebuttal Language

"The opposition claims [their argument]. However, their evidence actually shows [counter-interpretation]."

"Even if we concede [their point], this doesn't undermine our case because [explain why]."

"This is a logical fallacy: they conflate [X] with [Y], but [explanation]."

Point of Information

"Point of information — when you say [their claim], do you mean [clarification]? Because the data actually shows [counter]."

Weighing Arguments

"The opposition's benefit is [scope]. Ours is [scope]. On the scale of [metric], our argument outweighs because [explanation]."

"We concede that point, but even their best case is overcome by [our argument]."

Hedging (Appropriate Caution)

"The evidence suggests..." (weaker claim, defensible)

"We can confidently say..." (when evidence is strong)

"Most scholars agree..." (when consensus exists)

"This suggests, though doesn't prove..." (acknowledging limits of evidence)

Closing Summary

"The clash was [identify main point of disagreement]. On this clash, we won because [brief explanation]. The motion passes."
