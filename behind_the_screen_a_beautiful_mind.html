A Beautiful Mind

A brilliant mathematician's mind turns against him — and his real story of illness, love and a Nobel Prize is even more remarkable than the film.

Today: you'll tell the story of A Beautiful Mind — a mathematical genius whose own mind turns against him — discover how much of his extraordinary real story the film keeps and how much it invents, and master the grammar English uses to add information about a person or thing: defining and non-defining relative clauses.
Before we begin — talk it through: What do you think makes someone a genius? Would you want to be exceptionally brilliant if it came at a cost? Do you think a serious illness can change who a person really is underneath — or not?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. At just eight years old, the boy solved calculus problems that stumped his university-educated tutors; everyone agreed the child was a genuine prodigy.
a prodigy (n) — a young person with an exceptional, seemingly innate talent, far beyond their years.
2. Convinced the shadows on his wall were spies sent to watch him, the man could not be talked out of his delusion, however much evidence his family showed him.
a delusion (n) — a fixed false belief that a person holds firmly, even when it clearly contradicts reality.
3. He wore odd, mismatched socks, greeted the pigeons in the park by name, and refused to own a telephone; his neighbours found him harmlessly eccentric.
eccentric (adj) — unconventional or strange in behaviour and habits, in a way that is usually harmless.
4. As the evidence piled up against him, his carefully constructed alibi began to unravel, one loose thread of a lie at a time.
to unravel (v) — to come undone or fall apart, so that what was hidden or held together is gradually exposed.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. paranoid is closest in meaning to: (a) cheerful and relaxed   (b) irrationally suspicious and fearful   (c) simply forgetful
(b) irrationally suspicious and fearful — paranoid (adj) describes an unfounded, often extreme, fear that others intend to harm you.
6. lucid is closest in meaning to: (a) confused and rambling   (b) extremely loud   (c) clear-headed and easy to follow
(c) clear-headed and easy to follow — lucid (adj) means expressed or thinking clearly; free of confusion.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. Even years after recovering fully, she found the stigma attached to her diagnosis meant colleagues still treated her differently.
A stigma is: (a) a badge of honour; (b) a mark of shame or disapproval that society attaches to something; (c) a form of medical treatment.
(b) — a stigma (n) — a mark of social disgrace or disapproval attached to a person, condition or circumstance, often unfairly.
8. After a decade of failed experiments, one overlooked variable finally gave the team their breakthrough, and the whole problem suddenly made sense.
A breakthrough is: (a) a sudden, important discovery that solves a problem; (b) a complete failure; (c) a piece of laboratory equipment.
(a) — a breakthrough (n) — a sudden and important discovery or development that overcomes a difficulty and moves things forward.

D. Infer from context — two bigger ideas

9. Convinced no one could be trusted, he began to isolate himself — cancelling plans, avoiding calls — until months passed without him seeing another soul.
to isolate (v) — to separate yourself (or be separated) from other people, becoming cut off and alone.
10. She had lost her job, her home and nearly her health, yet she rebuilt her life piece by piece — the kind of resilience that quietly inspires everyone who hears her story.
resilience (n) — the capacity to recover from difficulty, setbacks or hardship; toughness of spirit.

The film, in three parts

Ron Howard's A Beautiful Mind (2001). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it. This story touches on serious mental illness, and we treat it with the seriousness it deserves.)

Part 1 — A brilliant, awkward mind

John Nash arrives at Princeton in 1947 as a brilliant but painfully awkward graduate student, determined to have one truly original idea before he ever teaches a class. Socially clumsy and often mocked by classmates who admire his mind more than his manner, he finds an unlikely, exuberant roommate, Charles, who becomes his closest friend. Watching a group of friends compete for the same woman in a bar one evening, Nash has a flash of insight that overturns over a century of thinking on competition and cooperation — the breakthrough that will one day make his name.

Years later, married to Alicia, a brilliant physics student, and lecturing at MIT, Nash is approached by a secretive government handler named Parcher, who recruits him into a covert mission: to find hidden Soviet codes buried inside ordinary newspapers and magazines. Thrilled to feel essential to something vast and important, Nash begins delivering sealed reports to a locked mailbox in an abandoned building, telling no one — not even Alicia — what he believes he is really doing.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What insight does Nash have watching his friends in the bar, and why does it matter?

2. What secret task does Parcher recruit Nash for?

1. He realises that if everyone in a group competes for the very same thing, they can all end up failing — but if each person considers what the others will do, everyone can do better. This becomes the basis of his celebrated equilibrium theory.

2. He is recruited to decode hidden Soviet messages he believes are concealed inside ordinary newspapers and magazines, dropping the results into a locked mailbox.

Discuss:
  • Nash hides his "secret work" even from his wife, believing it protects her. Is keeping someone in the dark ever a genuine act of love — or is it always, eventually, a betrayal?

Part 2 — The mind that couldn't be trusted

As the secret missions grow more dangerous in Nash's mind, he becomes convinced he is being hunted by enemy agents, terrifying Alicia and endangering their infant son. At a lecture, armed men and a psychiatrist intervene, and a devastating truth emerges: Charles the roommate, Charles's young niece Marcee, and Parcher the agent have never been real. Nash is diagnosed with paranoid schizophrenia and hospitalised for insulin coma therapy. Released, he is prescribed medication that controls the hallucinations but dulls his thinking and strains his marriage. Desperate to feel like himself again, he and Alicia secretly stop the drugs against medical advice — and the illness comes roaring back, with terrifying consequences.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What shocking truth is revealed to Nash about Charles, Marcee and Parcher?

2. What choice does Nash face between medication and his own mind?

1. None of them exist — they are vivid hallucinations his mind has produced for years; he has been living, in part, inside a delusion.

2. The medication controls the hallucinations but leaves him unable to think clearly or connect with Alicia; coming off it restores his mind but also his illness, with dangerous results.

Discuss:
  • Nash must choose between a clear mind that cannot work, and a working mind he cannot fully trust. Which would frighten you more — losing your abilities, or losing your grip on what's real?

Part 3 — Living with the ghosts

Rather than return to hospital, Nash resolves — with Alicia's fierce and unwavering support — to manage the illness through will and reason rather than medication alone, slowly learning to recognise Charles, Marcee and Parcher whenever they reappear, and to consciously refuse to engage with them, however real they still feel. Years pass. A once-wary Princeton lets him quietly sit in the library and audit classes, and an old rival discreetly arranges for him to teach again.

Decades after his original breakthrough, a delegation of scholars arrives to tell him, quietly, that he has been awarded the Nobel Prize. In his acceptance speech, Nash credits it all, above every equation he ever wrote, to Alicia — the woman who never gave up on him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What technique does Nash use to manage his illness, instead of relying only on drugs?

2. What does Nash say matters most in his acceptance speech?

1. He trains himself to recognise the hallucinations that recur and to deliberately ignore them, refusing to act on what he now knows is not real.

2. He thanks his wife, saying she has given his life meaning, and that love, in the end, is his only rational explanation.

Discuss:
  • The film suggests Nash never fully loses his hallucinations — he simply learns not to act on them. Is choosing to ignore what your mind tells you the same thing as being "cured"? What do you think recovery really means?

Grammar — defining & non-defining relative clauses

This story is full of people and things that need extra explanation — a roommate who never existed, a wife who refused to leave. English has two kinds of relative clause for exactly this job, and mixing them up changes the meaning.

Defining relative clauses tell us which person or thing we mean — the information is essential, and there are no commas: "The roommate who never existed haunted him for years." "The equation that changed economics fit on a single page." Use who/that for people, which/that for things, whose for possession, where for places. When the pronoun is the object of the clause, it can often be dropped: "The friend (that) he trusted most turned out to be imaginary."
Non-defining relative clauses add extra, non-essential information — the sentence still makes complete sense without it — and they are always marked off by commas: "His wife, who stood by him through the worst of it, refused to give up." "Princeton, where he began his career, later welcomed him back." You cannot use that here, and the pronoun can never be dropped.
Feel the difference: defining = essential, no commas, that allowed, pronoun droppable if it's the object. Non-defining = extra, commas required, no that, pronoun never droppable.

1. Which pronoun fits? (who / which / that / whose / where)

These are all defining clauses — no commas needed.

1. The mathematician idea reshaped economics was only twenty-one when he had it.

2. Princeton is the university Nash returned decades after his illness first appeared.

3. The theory he developed in a single afternoon later won a Nobel Prize.

1. whose (shows possession — "his idea")

2. where (a place)

3. that / which (a thing, defining — no comma)

2. Defining or non-defining? Choose the pronoun the punctuation demands

Look carefully at the commas already in the sentence.

1. Alicia, never stopped believing in her husband, later remarried him decades after their divorce.

2. The illness doctors diagnosed him with in the 1950s had no reliable cure at the time.

3. The Nobel committee, decision came decades after his recovery began, praised his "pioneering analysis."

1. who (non-defining — the commas rule out "that")

2. that / which (defining — no commas, "that" is fine)

3. whose (non-defining, showing possession — "its decision")

3. Complete the story — mix both types

Fill each gap with the relative pronoun the sentence needs. Read it aloud when you're done.

The mailbox, he believed held secret messages for the government, was in reality just an ordinary box. The delusion frightened Alicia most was Parcher, an agent orders Nash felt bound to obey. Princeton, he had once been a rising star, eventually became the place he quietly rebuilt his career.

which (non-defining) · that/which (defining) · whose (possession) · where (non-defining — Princeton is already identified) · where (defining — "the place")

Notice the pattern: a comma signals extra, non-defining information; no comma means the clause is essential to identifying who or what we mean.

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

Here's what makes A Beautiful Mind unusual: the extraordinary parts are largely true, and it's the film's most vivid device — the hallucinated characters you can actually see — that is its biggest invention.

🧮 A real mathematician, a real prize

John Forbes Nash Jr. (1928–2015) really did have a breakthrough insight, as a Princeton graduate student in his early twenties, that became the Nash equilibrium — a way of predicting the outcome of situations where people or companies compete and cooperate at the same time. It came not from a single flash of insight in a bar, as the film dramatises, but from his 1950 doctoral thesis — a mere 27 pages long. In 1994, Nash shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with John Harsanyi and Reinhard Selten "for their pioneering analysis of equilibria in the theory of non-cooperative games." The Nash equilibrium remains one of the most-used ideas in modern economics, applied everywhere from auctions to evolutionary biology.

The illness was real — but the hallucinations looked different

Nash really was diagnosed with paranoid schizophrenia in the late 1950s and endured years of institutionalisation, delusions of conspiracy, and — as the film shows — insulin coma therapy. But here is where the film takes its boldest liberty: the real Nash's hallucinations were overwhelmingly auditory — voices, not vivid, walking, talking characters. The roommate Charles, his niece Marcee, and the secret agent Parcher were all invented for the screen, a vivid way to let the audience see, rather than only hear, what Nash's mind was doing to him.

A messier, and in some ways more remarkable, real marriage

The film shows one continuous marriage; the truth is more complicated, and arguably more moving. John and Alicia Nash married in 1957, divorced in 1963 as his illness worsened and life together became close to unbearable, though Alicia never fully let go — she took him back into her home again in 1970 — and the two eventually remarried in 2001, decades after the events dramatised at the end of the film. The book behind it all is Sylvia Nasar's 1998 biography, also called A Beautiful Mind, which the film only loosely adapts.

One further real detail the film couldn't have shown: in 2015, John and Alicia had just travelled to Norway, where Nash received the Abel Prize — one of mathematics' highest honours — for work entirely separate from game theory, done decades earlier. Flying home, the taxi carrying them from the airport crashed on the New Jersey Turnpike, killing them both instantly. He was 86; she was 82.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, did you assume the "ghosts" John Nash saw were close to reality? Does it change the story to learn his real hallucinations were mostly voices?
  • Does the messier, real timeline of divorce and remarriage make Alicia's loyalty more or less remarkable than the film's version?
  • Nash and his wife died together, days after a fresh honour, decades after the story the film tells. Does that ending change how you feel about everything that came before?
