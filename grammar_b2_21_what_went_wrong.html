Warm-up

Have a go, out loud, before anything else — messy is fine.

SPEAK

Think of the last time something small went wrong — a delivery, an appointment, the weather, a journey. In one sentence, what happened? Say it now, however rough it sounds.

Listen to this one going wrong

Jo: You will not believe my morning.

Sam: What happened?

Jo: I was standing at the bus stop when it started pouring with rain. The bus was supposed to come at quarter past nine — typical, it got cancelled with no warning at all.

Sam: No umbrella, I'm guessing?

Jo: Worse — I'd left it at home. So I got soaked walking to the station instead.

Jo uses four different tools to tell this story well. A scene, a failed plan, a piece of bad luck, and a little twist revealed partway through. Can you spot all four? Say them before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "I was standing at the bus stop when it started pouring" — the scene (was + -ing) interrupted by the event (past simple).
2. "The bus was supposed to come... it got cancelled" — the plan that failed, and the bad luck that replaced it.
3. "I'd left it at home" — the twist: something that had already happened before the story moment.
4. "I got soaked" — misfortune, spoken style.

Master these four and any "you won't believe what happened" story is yours to tell properly.
Today: three tools for telling a brilliant disaster story — the scene (I was standing... when...), the twist (it got cancelled / stuck / charged), and the failed plan (it was supposed to be easy) — so you can tell anyone about the day everything went wrong.

Setting the Scene

Every good disaster story needs a calm moment right before it all goes wrong.

Spot the pattern

  • "I was queuing for coffee when the fire alarm went off."
  • "We were driving to the airport when the car broke down."
  • "She was cooking dinner when the power went out."

What's doing which job here? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
was/were + -ing paints the calm background scene — the thing already in progress. when + past simple is the sudden event that breaks into it. The scene doesn't stop the story; it just gives the disaster somewhere to land.
was / were + verb-ing  +  when  +  past simple verb
One trap: the interrupting event is never continuous — "when it started" (not "when it was starting"). The scene is the background; the event is the sudden thing.

Quick check

1. You're sitting in the waiting room. Then the dentist calls your name. Use the continuous form of sit:

"I in the waiting room when the dentist called my name."

2. Your friends are watching a film at the cinema. The fire alarm suddenly goes off. Use the continuous form of watch:

"They the film when the fire alarm suddenly went off."

3. You're carrying a tray of drinks across the room, and you drop it. Use the continuous form of carry:

"I a tray of drinks when I dropped it on the floor."

Now say it

SPEAK

Describe three moments from your own life using was/were + -ing... when... — real or invented. Fast, out loud:

  1. Something ordinary you were doing when your phone rang with unexpected news…
  2. Something you were doing when the weather suddenly changed…
  3. Something you were doing when someone interrupted with bad news…

And Then It Got Worse

The everyday way to share bad luck — nobody cares who's to blame.

Spot the pattern

  • "My flight got delayed by four hours."
  • "The parcel got left with a neighbour I've never even met."
  • "I got charged twice for the same coffee."

What do all three have in common, and why "got" rather than "was"? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
got + past participle is the spoken passive for things going wrong — the doer is unknown or simply doesn't matter. "Was" would be equally correct, but in everyday talk, "got" is the sound of bad luck: got delayed, got lost, got mixed up, got left behind.
got + past participle  (spoken misfortune — the thing first, the doer forgotten)
Watch the irregular participles: got taken (not taked), got given (not gived), got shown (not showed).

Quick check

1. A delivery driver leaves your parcel outside in the rain, and the box falls apart. Use get + ruin:

"My parcel in the rain — the box completely fell apart."

2. Roadworks close your usual road, and you're trapped in traffic for a whole hour. Use get + stick:

"We in traffic for a whole hour because of the roadworks."

3. The card machine at the café accidentally charges you more than the menu price for your coffee. Use get + overcharge:

"I for my coffee, so I asked for a refund."

Now say it — the bad-luck round

SPEAK

Give me five pieces of bad luck — real or invented — every one starting with the thing, not the person, using got:

  1. Something of yours that got lost, broken, or stolen…
  2. A journey that got delayed or cancelled…
  3. Something that got left in the wrong place…
  4. A bill or a booking that got mixed up…
  5. Something that got ruined by the weather…

It Was Supposed To Be Easy

The plan that didn't survive contact with reality — and the twist revealed a beat later.

Spot the pattern

  • "The plumber was supposed to arrive at nine — he turned up at two."
  • "I was meant to catch the earlier train, but I'd already missed it by the time I got to the platform."
  • "The cake was supposed to take forty minutes — after an hour it still hadn't set."

Two jobs are happening here. What are they? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
was/were + supposed to / meant to + base verb states the plan — and signals it's about to fail. had + past participle is the kicker: a fact from even further back, revealed partway through the story, usually the reason everything unravelled.
was / were + supposed to / meant to + base verb  (the plan)
had + past participle  (the twist — what had already happened)
Two traps: never drop "was/were" ("I supposed to..." is wrong), and after "supposed to / meant to" always use the base verb, never the past form ("supposed to came" is wrong — "supposed to come").

Quick check

1. The electrician is expected at midday. He never shows up at all. Use supposed to + turn up:

"The electrician at midday — he never showed up at all."

2. You run to the platform, but the train has already departed. Use had + already go:

"By the time I reached the platform, the train ."

3. You were expected to finish the report by Friday. By Thursday night you hadn't even started. Use meant to + finish:

"I the report by Friday — and by Thursday night I hadn't even started."

Now say it

SPEAK

Tell me about a plan that failed — real or invented. State the plan with was/were supposed to / meant to, then add a twist with had + past participle:

  1. What was supposed to happen…
  2. What actually happened instead…
  3. The twist — something you'd already realised, or that had already happened, before you knew the plan was ruined…

The Story

Everything together, out loud. This is the whole point of the lesson.

Round 1 — you talk

A friend asks: "Go on then — what happened? Tell me everything."

Talk for two to three minutes about a day that went wrong — real or invented. Try to include:

  • a scene, interrupted — I was... when...
  • at least one piece of bad luck — got cancelled / stuck / soaked / charged
  • a plan that failed — was/were supposed to / meant to
  • a twist revealed partway through — I'd already...
02:00

Round 2 — you ask

SPEAK

Flip it: now interview me about my worst journey ever. Build your questions out loud, using today's grammar:

  1. "What were you doing when...?" — set the scene of my disaster.
  2. "What got cancelled / delayed / lost?" — dig for the bad luck.
  3. "What was supposed to happen instead?" — find the failed plan.
  4. "Had you already...?" — try to find the twist.

Round 3 — it keeps getting worse

SPEAK

Retell your Round 1 story from the start — but each time, add one more disaster on top of the last. Keep the scene-setting, the bad luck, and the failed plan all in the mix as the story grows:

  1. Tell it once, exactly as before.
  2. Tell it again, adding one new thing that went wrong.
  3. Tell it a third time, adding yet another disaster — how far can it go before it's completely absurd?

Round 4 — pin it down in writing

Write the 3–5 sentence message you'd send a friend afterwards ("You will not believe my day — ..."). Use all three tools from today; the checker will tell you which ones it finds.

Mix It Up

Real stories never tell you which tool to reach for. Here everything's shuffled.

Which tool fits?

1. "I ___ my umbrella at home, so I got completely soaked."

2. "The train ___ for twenty minutes with no explanation."

3. "We ___ arrive by six, but the traffic was terrible."

Fix the mistakes

Each one has a single grammar slip. Say the correct version out loud first, then type the whole sentence.

4. "While I waited for the bus, it suddenly began to snow."

5. "I supposed to meet him at six, but he never came."

6. "My phone got smash when I sat on it by accident."

7. "When I got to the cinema, the film already started."

Say it better

SPEAK

Each sentence below is correct but flat. Say a more natural version out loud using today's grammar, then compare:

8. "I was at the bus stop. Then it started raining really hard."

Compare with a natural version CLICK
"I was standing at the bus stop when it started pouring with rain." — two flat sentences collapse into one scene-and-event story.

9. "The plan was for the engineer to fix the boiler in the morning. It didn't happen until the evening."

Compare with a natural version CLICK
"The engineer was supposed to fix the boiler in the morning, but he didn't turn up until the evening." — the failed plan carries the frustration on its own.

10. "Someone cancelled my dentist appointment without telling me."

Compare with a natural version CLICK
"My dentist appointment got cancelled without any warning." — the appointment leads, and nobody needs to know who's to blame.

What Do You Remember?

First, further back

1. Tell someone what you and your friends did last Friday. Use the past simple of go:

"We to the cinema last Friday."

2. A storm broke a window in your house last night. Tell someone, starting with "The window":

"The window during the storm last night."

Say first, then check — fresh situations, no looking back

3. You're browsing in a shop when the till suddenly breaks down. Use the continuous form of browse:

"I in the shop when the till suddenly broke down."

4. Bad weather means your flight is put off until further notice, with no new time given. Use get + postpone:

"Our flight with absolutely no warning."

5. The dentist is expected to see you at three. By half past, nobody has called your name. Use supposed to + see:

"The dentist me at three — by half past, nobody had called my name."
