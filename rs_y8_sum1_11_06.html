Question 1: Name three major ethical theories and briefly explain how each one decides if an action is right or wrong.

🔒Think about this first!

Answer: (1) Consequentialism: actions are right or wrong based on their outcomes or consequences. (2) Deontology: actions are right or wrong based on rules and duty, regardless of outcomes. (3) Virtue Ethics: actions are right if they come from good character traits like honesty, courage, and compassion.

Question 2: What are the Four Noble Truths in Buddhism, and how do they connect to ethics?

🔒Try to remember first!

Answer: (1) Suffering exists. (2) Suffering has a cause (craving/attachment). (3) Suffering can end (Nirvana). (4) The Eightfold Path leads to the end of suffering. Ethical actions are part of Right Speech, Right Action, and Right Livelihood — they reduce suffering and help you progress toward enlightenment.

Question 3: What is Humanism, and how do Humanists approach ethics differently from religious traditions?

🔒Try to explain it first!

Answer: Humanism is a worldview that doesn't require belief in God or the supernatural. Humanists believe we can work out right and wrong through reason, empathy, and evidence about human wellbeing. Ethics are based on how actions affect real people and society, not on divine commands or religious texts.

Question 4: How do Christian and Islamic ethics both use rules, but understand morality differently?

🔒Try first!

Answer: Both traditions have clear rules (the Ten Commandments in Christianity; Shariah in Islam) based on God's will. Christians often ask "what would Jesus do?" — focusing on virtue, compassion, and love. Muslims follow Shariah, which sets rules for prayer, justice, family life, and society. Both combine rules (deontology) with religious consequences (heaven/hell) and character development.

Encounter: All Approaches Together

Here's a brief summary of all the ethical approaches you've studied. Read carefully — you'll need to sort examples and compare these frameworks in the next tabs.

Consequentialism

Actions are right if they produce good outcomes. The most famous version is utilitarianism: "the greatest good for the greatest number." Focused on results, not intentions or rules.

Deontology

Actions are right or wrong based on rules and duties, regardless of outcomes. The rule "don't steal" is always wrong, even if stealing would save someone's life.

Virtue Ethics

Focuses on character. A good person develops wisdom, courage, honesty, and compassion. Right action comes from cultivating these qualities over time, not from calculating outcomes or following rules blindly.

Buddhist Ethics

Based on the Eightfold Path and the Five Precepts. Actions that increase suffering (lies, theft, violence) are wrong. Good actions support enlightenment and reduce karma. Emphasis on mindfulness and intention.

Humanist Ethics

No God or supernatural authority. Right and wrong are based on reason, empathy, and evidence about human wellbeing. Focus on promoting happiness and reducing suffering for all people.

Christian and Islamic Ethics

God sets the rules (Ten Commandments, Shariah). Christians emphasise love and compassion — "what would Jesus do?" Muslims follow Shariah law on justice, family, charity. Both combine rules with character development and divine consequences.

What They Share
  • All care about reducing suffering and harm
  • All value fairness and justice
  • All ask: "How should we live?"
  • All combine outcomes, rules, and character in different proportions
  • All agree that some actions are definitely wrong
Where They Differ
  • Source of authority: reason, God, virtue, results, tradition
  • What matters most: outcomes vs. rules vs. character
  • Role of duty and obligation vs. personal choice
  • Whether rules can ever be broken for better outcomes
  • Focus: individual happiness vs. community vs. enlightenment

Evaluate: One Framework or Many?

Three positions are debated below. Which makes most sense to you? Consider evidence from the whole unit.

Position 1: We need all of them

No single framework handles every situation well. Real life requires rules (deontology) to protect people, consequences (consequentialism) to guide choices, and character (virtue ethics) to navigate complexity. Religious and humanist approaches remind us that ethics serve different purposes: reducing suffering, respecting rights, and building community.

Position 2: One framework is enough

If you commit to one strong approach, everything becomes clear. Consequentialists focus on reducing suffering. Deontologists protect rights absolutely. Virtue ethicists develop good judgment. Religious followers have clear guidance. Humanists use reason and empathy. Mixing frameworks creates confusion — pick the strongest one and apply it consistently.

Position 3: Ethics is too complex for frameworks

Real dilemmas are messy. Frameworks are too rigid — they ignore context, relationships, and culture. Each situation is unique. Wise people don't follow frameworks; they develop judgment through experience. Ethics comes from wisdom and empathy, not theory. Rules and calculations miss what matters most: human connection.

Your evaluation:

Which position do you agree with most? Can you explain why, using examples from the unit? Are there situations where one position works better than the others?

Review: Key Knowledge

Question 1: Name the six major ethical approaches covered in this unit.

🔒Try to list them first!

Answer: Consequentialism, Deontology, Virtue Ethics, Buddhist Ethics, Humanist Ethics, and Christian/Islamic Ethics.

Question 2: What is the difference between ethics and morality?

🔒Try to explain it first!

Answer: Morality is the beliefs about right and wrong that guide a person's or society's behaviour. Ethics is the study and philosophy of morality — it asks why certain actions are right or wrong, and how we should decide.

Question 3: How do Buddhist ethics and Christian ethics both use consequences, but in different ways?

🔒Think about karma and heaven/hell first!

Answer: Buddhism uses karma — your actions have consequences across lifetimes. Good actions lead to better rebirths; bad actions lead to suffering. Christianity uses divine judgment — you face eternal consequences (heaven or hell) based on your choices. Both mix rules with consequences.

Question 4: What is a moral dilemma, and why are they important for testing ethical frameworks?

🔒Try first!

Answer: A moral dilemma is a situation where you must choose between two or more options, and each option involves some harm or moral cost. Dilemmas are important because they test whether a framework actually works — they show us when rules conflict with outcomes, or when different ethical approaches disagree.

Key Terms

Ethics
The study of right and wrong — the branch of philosophy that asks what we ought to do and how we should live.
Consequentialism
The ethical theory that actions are right or wrong based on their consequences. Good outcomes = right action.
Deontology
The ethical theory that actions are right or wrong based on rules and duties, regardless of consequences.
Virtue Ethics
The ethical theory that focuses on developing good character traits (virtues) like honesty, courage, and compassion.
The Middle Way
Buddha's ethical teaching: avoid extremes of self-indulgence and self-denial. Balance and moderation guide right living.
Five Precepts
Buddhist ethical rules: avoid killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, intoxication, and lying. They reduce suffering and build karma.
Humanism
A worldview based on reason and empathy, not supernatural beliefs. Humanists believe we can work out ethics through evidence and care for human wellbeing.
Divine Command Theory
The idea that actions are right because God commands them, and wrong because God forbids them. Used in Christianity and Islam.
Shariah
Islamic law based on the Qur'an and Hadith. Sets rules for justice, family life, finance, prayer, and society. Combines rules, consequences, and divine will.
Moral Dilemma
A situation where you must choose between two or more options and each one involves some harm or moral cost. Tests whether frameworks actually work.
