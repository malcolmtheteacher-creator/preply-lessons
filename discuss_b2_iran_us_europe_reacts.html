The Case Study

Tap to reveal the case click anywhere on the blurred area

In February 2026, the US and Israel launched major military strikes on Iran. European leaders responded — but their statements were carefully worded and deliberately ambiguous.

France's president called it "an outbreak of war" — but also told Iran to negotiate. The EU called for "maximum restraint" — but didn't condemn the strikes directly. Nobody said "we support this." Nobody said "we oppose this."

This pattern happens every time a major ally does something controversial. We're going to use this crisis as a case study to understand HOW and WHY leaders choose their words so carefully — and to practise doing it yourself.

The Language of Diplomacy

European leaders are very careful about word choice. Let's look at WHY certain expressions are used — and practise using them yourself, one strategy at a time.

What Are They Actually Doing?

Listen to these real quotes. Before I explain anything — what do you think each one is trying to achieve?

"The situation is greatly concerning."

— EU Leadership, February 28

SPEAK
What does this actually mean? Think about what they COULD have said instead. They could have gone stronger: "This was wrong." "We condemn this." Or they could have gone the other way: "We support our ally's decision." They chose "concerning" — a word that sits right in the middle. Why? Some possibilities:
a) They genuinely have mixed feelings — worried about method and consequences, but not necessarily opposed to the goal
b) They're buying time while they figure out their actual position
c) They need a word that works for multiple audiences (their own voters, the US, Iran, the UN)
d) Something else you've noticed?
HEDGING
"greatly concerning" — this word does a lot of work. It communicates genuine worry (about escalation, about consequences, about not being consulted) without having to declare whether the action itself was right or wrong. Maybe they think regime change is the right goal but the method is reckless. Maybe they're not sure yet. "Concerning" holds all of those positions at once.

The pattern: Use evaluative language that expresses feeling without requiring a verdict → concerning, troubling, deeply worrying, regrettable, alarming

What this lets you avoid: Having to choose between "This was wrong" and "We support this." You can be worried about HOW something happened and WHAT might follow without opposing the thing itself.

"France was neither warned nor involved in this operation."

— Emmanuel Macron, February 28

SPEAK
Why does Macron specifically mention that France wasn't warned? Think about the AUDIENCE — is he talking to the French public ("don't blame us"), to the US ("you should have asked us"), to Iran ("this wasn't our choice"), or to all three at the same time? And notice what he does NOT say — he doesn't say "we disagree" or "we oppose this." Why not?
DISTANCING
"neither warned nor involved" — Macron is saying: this wasn't our decision. We are not responsible for the consequences.

The pattern: Make clear you had no role → We were not consulted... / This decision was taken without... / We had no part in... / This does not reflect our position...

Why this matters: If things go well, you weren't involved — so no one can blame you for not helping. If things go badly, you weren't involved — so no one can blame you for the consequences. Either way, you've protected your position without having to say whether you agree or disagree.

"We call for maximum restraint from all parties and urge Iran to return to negotiations."

— EU Joint Statement, February 28

SPEAK
Notice the structure: "restraint from all parties" (= directed at the US/Israel) AND "urge Iran to return to negotiations" (= directed at Iran). They put both demands in ONE sentence. Why not just pick a side? Think about what would happen if they ONLY criticised the US — or if they ONLY criticised Iran. What would each version cost them politically?
BALANCING
"calls for restraint... AND urges Iran to negotiate" — criticising both sides equally. Nobody can accuse you of taking sides.

The pattern: Criticise/request from BOTH sides in one statement → While [X] must..., [Y] should also... / We call on all parties to... / Both sides need to...

Why this works: You might actually think one side bears more responsibility than the other — but by addressing both, you keep your relationships intact and your future options open. It also reflects the reality that most international situations involve shared responsibility, even if unequally.

"We remain committed to regional stability and the resumption of negotiations."

— France, Germany & UK Joint Statement

SPEAK
Try to translate this into plain, honest English. If a friend said "I remain committed to regional stability and the resumption of negotiations" — you'd say "what do you ACTUALLY mean?" So what do they actually mean? Something like "please stop fighting"? Or "we want talks, not war"? Or "we're not going to do anything but we want to sound constructive"? What's the plain version?
SOFTENING
"commitment to regional stability" — says "we want peace" without saying "stop the war." Sounds constructive without making demands.

The pattern: State what you support rather than what you oppose → We are committed to... / We support the path of... / Our priority remains... / We believe in...

Why this works: Everyone can agree on "stability" — even people who disagree about how to get there. By framing your position around a shared goal, you avoid the accusation of taking sides while still signalling what outcome you want.

"The United Kingdom played no role in these strikes. But we have long been clear — the regime in Iran is utterly abhorrent."

— Keir Starmer, February 28

SPEAK
This one is different from the others. Read those two sentences again — what's happening between them? The first sentence distances the UK from the strikes. The second sentence attacks Iran. He's doing two things at once. What effect does this combination create? Some options:
a) "We didn't do it — but we're not sorry it happened"
b) "We're staying out of this — but just so you know, we're not on Iran's side"
c) "We want to look neutral — but we're actually closer to the US position than we're admitting"
d) Something else?
COMBINING
DISTANCING + implicit support — Starmer distances the UK from the METHOD ("played no role") while signalling agreement with the GOAL ("utterly abhorrent" regime). He then goes further: "Iran can end this now" — putting the responsibility entirely on Iran, not on the US. This is the most sophisticated version of the goal-vs-method split we've seen. He never says "we support the strikes." He never says "we oppose them." But the overall message leans clearly in one direction.

The pattern: Combine distancing from the action with strong language about the target → you separate yourself from HOW it was done while making clear you understand WHY.

Why this is worth studying: This is the clearest example of a leader holding two positions simultaneously — and it's the pattern that best illustrates the difference between opposing an action and opposing a goal.

Translate Direct → Diplomatic

The Direct → Diplomatic Machine

Here are four things someone might WANT to say. Together, let's transform each one using a specific strategy. I'll do the first one, then you try.

Direct: "We're worried this could spiral out of control and nobody asked us first."

Strategy: HEDGING →

"The recent military developments are deeply troubling and raise serious questions about the path forward."

Notice: the worry is real and genuine — but the diplomatic version doesn't force you to say who's to blame or whether the action was justified. You can hold that concern while still leaving room for the possibility that the outcome might be one you wanted.

Direct: "Nobody told us this was happening and we want everyone to know that."

Strategy: DISTANCING →

SPEAK
Transform this using the distancing pattern. Make clear you had no role in the decision — without saying whether you agree or disagree with it. Try starting with something like: "This operation was conducted..." or "Our government was not..." — focus on what you DIDN'T do rather than what you think.

"This operation was conducted without our knowledge or consultation. Our government was not party to this decision."

Key move: focus on PROCESS (we weren't consulted) rather than JUDGMENT (we support/oppose). You leave your actual opinion open.

Direct: "Everyone needs to calm down — both sides are making this worse."

Strategy: BALANCING →

SPEAK
Transform this using the balancing pattern. Address BOTH sides equally in one or two sentences. Try starting with "We urge all parties to..." or use the structure "While [X] must..., [Y] should also..." — the key is: nobody gets singled out.

"We urge all parties to exercise maximum restraint and return to the negotiating table. Military de-escalation and a credible commitment to non-proliferation must go hand in hand."

Key move: "all parties" — no names. "Must go hand in hand" — both obligations are equal.

Direct: "Stop the war now."

Strategy: SOFTENING →

SPEAK
Transform this using the softening pattern. Instead of saying what you're AGAINST ("stop the war"), say what you're FOR. Try starting with "We remain committed to..." or "Our priority is..." — then add a positive noun like "peace," "stability," "dialogue," or "a diplomatic resolution."

"We remain firmly committed to a diplomatic resolution and the restoration of regional stability."

Key move: "committed to" + positive noun (resolution, stability). Zero criticism, zero demands.

Three Perspectives

In any international crisis, the same event looks completely different depending on where you're standing. Three positions, three sets of logic.

🇺🇸 The US Position

"Major combat operations" — framed as a necessary action against a nuclear threat. The argument: diplomacy was tried (Geneva talks failed), so force is the next step.

SPEAK
What's the strongest version of this argument? Even if you disagree — try to make the BEST case for it. You could think about it from angles like: "Iran was developing nuclear weapons and that's dangerous for everyone," or "the US tried diplomacy for years and it didn't work," or "sometimes military action prevents a worse outcome later." Which of these feels strongest to you? Can you add anything?

🇮🇷 Iran's Position

Accused the US and Israel of violating the UN Charter. Launched retaliatory strikes and closed the Strait of Hormuz to shipping — a move that directly affects global energy supply.

SPEAK
From Iran's point of view, closing the Strait of Hormuz is... what? Here are some possible readings — pick one and argue for it, or combine them:

a) Self-defence — "you attacked us, so we're using the tools we have to fight back"
b) A bargaining chip — "stop the strikes and we'll reopen the Strait"
c) An attempt to drag other countries in — "now China and Europe are losing money too, so they'll pressure the US to stop"
d) A desperate act — "we can't match US military power, so this is the only leverage we have"

🇪🇺 Europe's Complicated Position

This isn't simple opposition. Consider what European leaders are juggling:

  • They've spent years trying to stop Iran's nuclear programme — so the GOAL may not be the problem
  • But they weren't consulted — their ally made a massive decision without them
  • They worry about escalation — what if this doesn't work? What if it triggers a wider war?
  • They depend on Middle Eastern energy — the Strait of Hormuz closure hits Europe directly
  • They want to uphold international law and the principle that diplomacy comes first
  • But they also need the US as an ally and don't want to damage that relationship

Their concern may be less "this was wrong" and more "this is risky, and we had no say in it."

b) Pride — "our ally made a massive decision without even calling us"
c) Fear — "what if this escalates into a wider war that reaches Europe?"
d) Economics — "oil prices are about to destroy our economies"
e) Uncertainty — "we don't actually know yet if this will work or backfire, so we're buying time"

Or is it a combination? Which two matter most?

Evidence for Hypothesis A (they oppose it): Macron called it an "outbreak of war." Calling for a UN Security Council meeting publicly challenges the US. No European leader endorsed the strikes.

Evidence for Hypothesis B (they're worried, not opposed): Macron ALSO pressured Iran to negotiate — if he opposed the goal, why do that? Starmer called Iran "utterly abhorrent" and said "Iran can end this now" — that's closer to supporting the US position than opposing it. The EU said "greatly concerning" — not "wrong" or "illegal." The nuance: European reactions seem to focus more on process (we weren't consulted), risk (this could escalate), and consequences (energy, wider war) than on opposing the objective itself. Different European leaders likely sit at different points on this spectrum — and their public statements are designed to avoid committing to either position clearly.

Your Position — Extended Speaking

Two options — pick one to do first.

OPTION A — THE PRESS CONFERENCE

You're a European foreign minister. You've just come out of an emergency meeting. Journalists are waiting. Give a 2-minute statement. Here's a structure to help:

  • Opening: Acknowledge the gravity — try a hedging or softening opener. E.g., "The events of the past 48 hours are deeply..."
  • Your position: Explain where your country stands — are you distancing? Balancing? Be clear but measured. E.g., "Our government was not consulted on..." or "While we recognise the threat posed by..."
  • The human cost: Mention what this means for ordinary people — energy prices, security, uncertainty. This is where you can be more direct.
  • Your call to action: End with a specific demand — ceasefire? Emergency summit? Return to talks? Humanitarian corridors? Pick one and commit to it.
OPTION B — THE PODCAST ANALYSIS

You're a political analyst on a podcast. The host asks you: "So, is Europe basically powerless here, or do they actually have influence?"

Give a 2-minute answer. Think about these angles:

  • What Europe CAN'T do: they can't match US military power, they weren't consulted, they depend on the US as an ally
  • What Europe CAN do: economic sanctions, energy policy, UN voting power, diplomatic mediation, public opinion pressure, controlling the narrative
  • A specific example: use something from today — Macron's UN Security Council move, the joint statement, the energy crisis angle
  • Your verdict: powerless, influential, or somewhere in between? Say it clearly at the end.
