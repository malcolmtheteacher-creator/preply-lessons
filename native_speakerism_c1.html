The Native Speaker Fallacy

What does it actually mean to be a "native speaker"? And why does ELT treat it as the gold standard?

In 1992, linguist Robert Phillipson identified what he called the "native speaker fallacy" - the unexamined assumption that native speakers make inherently better teachers. Three decades later, this ideology still shapes hiring practices, course materials, and professional identity in ELT.

~75% of English speakers worldwide are non-native Crystal, 2008
72% of ELT job ads still require "native speaker" TEFL Equity Advocates, 2023
80% of English teachers globally are non-native Canagarajah, 2005

The Academic Critique

Robert Phillipson - "Linguistic Imperialism" (1992)

Oxford University Press

Argued that the "native speaker" construct serves commercial and political interests more than pedagogical ones. The British Council and US State Department historically promoted native-speaker teachers as part of cultural diplomacy - not because research showed they were more effective.

Adrian Holliday - "Native-speakerism" (2006)

ELT Journal, Oxford Academic

Coined the term "native-speakerism" to describe a pervasive ideology in ELT that frames non-native teachers as permanently deficient. He argues this creates a "professional pecking order" that has nothing to do with actual teaching competence.

Real Cases

Dr. Marek Kiczkowiak

DELTA-qualified, PhD in Applied Linguistics, founder of TEFL Equity Advocates

"I was rejected from jobs in the UK purely because of my Polish passport. My qualifications didn't matter. My experience didn't matter. What mattered was where I was born - something I had zero control over."

Silvana Richardson

Head of Teacher Development, Bell Educational Services

Her 2016 IATEFL plenary challenged the industry directly: "We accept that skin colour shouldn't determine career prospects. Why do we accept that birthplace should?"

The paradox: 80% of English teachers are non-native, yet the industry's default assumption is that native speakers are preferable. The majority is treated as second-best.
Consider: CELTA and DELTA don't distinguish between native and non-native teachers in their assessment criteria. So why does the job market?

Academic Discourse (1-3)

Language for nuanced academic discussion - click each card to explore.

"What's often overlooked is..." +
Function

Introducing a counterpoint or neglected aspect of an argument. Signals that you're adding intellectual depth, not just disagreeing.

Form

What's often overlooked is + noun phrase / that-clause

Variations: "What tends to be overlooked..." / "What frequently gets missed..."

Register

Academic/formal. Common in journal articles, conference talks, and professional discussion. Shows you're engaging critically, not superficially.

"The debate focuses on native-speaker intuition, but what's often overlooked is that this 'intuition' varies enormously across dialects and regions."
NOW YOU TRY: Complete: "People focus on accent in hiring decisions, but what's often overlooked is..."
"The evidence would suggest..." +
Function

Grounding your position in research rather than opinion. The conditional "would" adds appropriate academic caution - you're not claiming certainty.

Form

The evidence would suggest (that) + clause

Variations: "Research would indicate..." / "The data would suggest..."

Register

Highly academic. The hedge ("would") is crucial - removes the phrase from personal opinion territory into evidence-based reasoning.

"The evidence would suggest that students taught by qualified non-native speakers perform equally well on standardised tests."
NOW YOU TRY: Use this phrase to discuss: What makes a teacher effective - native status or training?
"To some extent, this represents..." +
Function

Acknowledging partial validity while leaving room for qualification. Essential for nuanced academic discussion - avoids black-and-white thinking.

Form

To some extent, + statement

Often followed by "however" or "but" to add qualification.

Variations: "To a certain degree..." / "In part, this reflects..."

Register

Academic/professional. Demonstrates intellectual maturity - you can hold complexity rather than demanding simple answers.

"To some extent, the preference for native speakers reflects genuine student demand. However, that demand itself is shaped by marketing and ideology."
NOW YOU TRY: Use this phrase: "To some extent, native-speakerism in ELT represents..."

Academic Discourse (4-5)

Two more expressions for sophisticated argumentation.

"One could argue that..." +
Function

Presenting a position without necessarily committing to it. Useful for exploring arguments, playing devil's advocate, or introducing controversial perspectives diplomatically.

Form

One could argue (that) + clause

Variations: "It could be argued..." / "The case could be made..."

"One" = generic/impersonal (more formal than "you")

Register

Formal academic. The distance created by "one" and "could" allows you to raise provocative ideas without being accused of personally holding them.

"One could argue that the native-speaker preference is a form of discrimination comparable to other protected characteristics."
NOW YOU TRY: Complete: "When it comes to pronunciation teaching, one could argue that..."
"This raises the question of..." +
Function

Signalling that a logical implication demands further examination. Moves discussion forward rather than closing it down. Shows analytical thinking.

Form

This raises the question of + noun phrase / wh-clause

Variations: "This brings us to the question of..." / "This begs the question..." (though note: "begs the question" technically means something different in logic)

Register

Academic/analytical. Demonstrates you can identify implications and drive inquiry deeper rather than accepting surface-level answers.

"If native-speaker status doesn't predict teaching effectiveness, this raises the question of why it remains the default hiring criterion."
NOW YOU TRY: Complete: "If 80% of English teachers are non-native, this raises the question of..."
Pronunciation note: In these academic phrases, stress falls on the content words, not the function words. "What's often overLOOKED..." / "The EVidence would sugGEST..." / "This RAISES the QUEStion..."

The Native Speaker Fallacy

Student Preferences

Professional Identity

The Core Argument

Native-speaker status doesn't predict teaching effectiveness. The preference for native speakers reflects ideology and commercial interests rather than evidence-based pedagogy. This has measurable impacts on hiring, professional identity, and the field's credibility.
