Why this matters

Governance language is about credibility under pressure. As audit committee chair, you're the board's voice on control, risk, and integrity. Your job isn't to protect management. It's to serve shareholders and the company. When control breaks, your language has to signal that you understand the severity, have assessed the impact, and have a path to remediation — all without admitting to catastrophic failure.

Six governance moves

1 Fiduciary duty language +
Your fiduciary duty is to shareholders and the company, not to management. Use this language to establish independence and integrity. It shields you legally and establishes the principle that governance comes first.
How it sounds

"As audit committee chair, my duty is to shareholders. We have identified a material weakness. We have a remediation plan. I'm confident in management's commitment to fixing this, but I wouldn't be serving shareholders if I didn't flag this openly."

2 Audit committee reporting +
Audit committee reporting is formal, documented, and on the record. You're not having a conversation — you're making a formal report. Use full governance language. Every word may be in the minutes and disclosed.
The move

"The audit committee has identified and reported to management and the external auditor a material weakness in the design and operation of internal controls over revenue recognition. We have required management to develop a detailed remediation plan with milestone dates."

3 Material weakness framing +
Material weakness has a legal definition. Use it precisely. A material weakness means internal control cannot be relied upon to prevent material misstatement. This is serious. Don't minimize it, but don't catastrophize it. You found it. You're managing it.
The move

"A material weakness exists where a deficiency, or combination of deficiencies, could reasonably result in a material misstatement not prevented or detected timely. This is what we have identified. This is what we are remediating."

4 Shareholder communication +
Material weaknesses must be disclosed in regulatory filings. Frame disclosure as transparency, not failure. You're telling shareholders you found a problem and are fixing it. That's governance working.
The move

"We have disclosed a material weakness in our control environment. This reflects our commitment to transparency and our confidence in management's remediation efforts. Detailed disclosure is contained in our filing."

5 ESG governance language +
ESG governance includes integrity, transparency, and ethical culture. A control weakness is an ESG issue. Use ESG language to frame remediation as a positive commitment to governance standards, not just compliance.
The move

"Our governance standards require robust internal controls. This material weakness reflects where we fell short. Our remediation plan demonstrates our commitment to governance excellence and ethical culture."

6 Remuneration committee register +
Control failures have compensation implications. Executive bonuses may be at risk. Use formal language when discussing compensation consequences. This isn't punishment — it's governance accountability.
The move

"The remuneration committee will assess whether the control weakness impacts executive compensation outcomes for the period. We expect management to achieve full remediation before bonus vesting."

High-stakes scenario

It's Wednesday. Audit committee meeting. The external auditor has identified the material weakness. You now need to brief the CEO and CFO, get their proposed remediation plan, and schedule the full board presentation for tomorrow.

But the CFO has just said: "This is being overblown. We've had no material misstatements. The risk is theoretical." The auditor has just responded: "The definition of material weakness doesn't require a misstatement to have occurred. It's about the control design."

You're in the middle. The CFO is minimizing. The auditor is being technically precise. You need to guide both toward reality.
