Welcome to the Real World

Read these real situations where people had to "prove themselves" for surprisingly ordinary things:

The Restaurant That Requires a CV
A famous restaurant in London has a three-month waiting list. When you finally get through, they ask you to complete a questionnaire: What is your dining history? Can you describe your palate? Do you have references from other fine dining establishments? One reviewer joked: "I felt like I was being shortlisted for a Michelin star, not just trying to eat dinner."

The Allotment Waiting List
In many British cities, the waiting list for a small garden allotment is longer than for some university courses. Applicants must demonstrate their commitment to gardening, provide evidence of their track record with plants, and sometimes attend an interview. One applicant said: "They wanted to validate that I was serious. I had to bring photos of my windowsill herbs as proof of my credentials."

The Dog Adoption Process
Adopting a rescue dog can involve home visits, background checks, reference letters, and multi-stage interviews. Some shelters set benchmarks for garden size, working hours, and previous pet experience. One couple reported: "The accreditation process for adopting a Labrador was more intense than buying our house."

Vocabulary: Proving Yourself

These words are used in professional contexts — but they appear everywhere in daily life too. Click each card to see the definition:

credentialsYour qualifications, training, and experience that prove you can do something
validationThe process of officially confirming that something meets the required standard
equivalentEqual in value, level, or meaning — even if not identical
accreditationOfficial recognition that something or someone meets certain standards
benchmarkA standard or point of reference used to measure quality or performance
track recordYour history of achievements and results over time
shortlistedSelected as one of a small group of finalists being considered
red tapeExcessive official rules and procedures that make things slow and frustrating

When Proving Yourself Goes Too Far

Read each scenario. Discuss: Is this reasonable, slightly over the top, or completely ridiculous? What would you do?

Scenario 1

The Exclusive Book Club

You want to join a neighbourhood book club. They send you a five-page application form asking about your reading history, your favourite genres, and three "book references" — titles that shaped your thinking. They also ask you to write a 200-word essay on why you deserve a place. There are only 8 spots and 35 applicants.

Would you complete the application? What "credentials" would you include?

Is there a point where gatekeeping becomes snobbery? Where is the line?

Scenario 2

The Flatmate Interview

You find the perfect flat to rent. The current flatmates invite you for a "casual chat" which turns into a 90-minute interview. They ask about your cleaning standards, your noise levels, your cooking habits, your views on overnight guests, and whether your lifestyle is "equivalent to theirs." They say they will get back to you within two weeks after reviewing all candidates.

Have you ever experienced anything like this? How would you react?

When people set these kinds of benchmarks for everyday things, is it careful or controlling?

Scenario 3

The Gym Membership

A trendy new gym opens in your area. To join, you need to attend a "fitness assessment," provide a letter from your doctor, agree to train at least three times per week, and complete a one-month trial period before they grant you full membership. A friend says: "You basically have to go through an accreditation process just to use a treadmill."

Would this make you more or less interested in joining? Why?

Some people say exclusivity creates value. Others say it creates frustration. What do you think?

The Bigger Question

Think about any area of life — not just work — where you have felt that the process of proving yourself was more exhausting than the thing itself. What was it? Was the "validation" worth it in the end?

Your Ridiculous Application

C. You are applying to adopt the world's most spoiled rescue cat. The shelter requires extensive proof that your home meets their standards.

D. Your own idea — what is something ridiculous you would like to "apply" for?

Prepare your application

Which challenge did you choose?

What are your "credentials" for this? (Your experience, background, or skills that make you qualified)

What is your "track record"? (Evidence of past success)

Why should they pick you over other applicants?

How would you respond to this challenge: "We have concerns about your suitability. What would you say?"

Useful phrases for your pitch

"I believe my credentials speak for themselves..."

"My track record clearly demonstrates that..."

"I understand you need to validate my suitability, and I welcome the process."

"While I may not have formal accreditation, my practical experience is equivalent to..."

"I meet every benchmark you have set, and then some."

"I was thrilled to be shortlisted — it shows you recognise quality."

Your Application Notes

Make Your Case

Now present your application out loud. Be persuasive, be confident, and try to use at least 5 of the 8 vocabulary words. Remember: the more serious you sound about something ridiculous, the funnier it is.

Your pitch should include:

  • An introduction — what you are applying for and why
  • Your credentials and track record
  • Why you meet (or exceed) their benchmarks
  • A confident response to a potential objection
  • A closing statement that leaves no doubt
