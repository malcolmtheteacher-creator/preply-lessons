At the Edge of Knowing

Black Holes, Cosmology & the Language of Uncertainty

Where shall we start?

You don’t need a textbook to have an opinion about black holes. Most people who think about them at all have some image in their head — usually wrong, occasionally beautiful, sometimes both.

Before we read anything, let’s find out what’s already in yours.

1. If you had to describe a black hole to someone who’d never heard the term, what would you say in three sentences?

A black hole is a region of space where gravity is so strong that nothing — not even light — can escape once it crosses a certain boundary. It forms when a massive star collapses at the end of its life, or, in much larger cases, sits at the centre of a galaxy. We can’t see them directly; we infer them from how they affect the matter and light around them.

2. Which of these claims do you think is true? (More than one may be.)

(a) A black hole would “suck” the Earth in if one appeared in our solar system.

(b) Light can curve around a black hole without falling in.

(c) Time runs more slowly near a black hole than far away from it.

(d) Black holes last forever.

(a) False — gravity at a distance is the same as it would be from any object of the same mass. If the Sun became a black hole tomorrow, Earth would keep orbiting (we’d freeze, but we wouldn’t fall in).
(b) True — bent light from objects behind a black hole produces a “ring” effect known as gravitational lensing.
(c) True — gravity slows time. The closer you get to the event horizon, the more dramatic the effect.
(d) Probably false — Stephen Hawking proposed that black holes slowly evaporate. The evidence is theoretical, not yet observed, but most physicists accept it.

Eight words to take into the reading

Four of these are the vocabulary of cosmology itself. The other four are the vocabulary of careful thinking — words you’ll need any time you discuss something where the evidence is incomplete. All eight appear in the passage on the next tab.

Click each card to flip it.

event horizon The boundary around a black hole beyond which nothing — not even light — can return.
singularity A point at which our equations break down; in a black hole, the centre, where matter is crushed to (theoretically) infinite density.
accretion disk The flat, swirling band of superheated gas spiralling toward a black hole. It glows brightly — which is, paradoxically, how we “see” something invisible.
spaghettification The (real, official) name for what tidal gravity does to an object falling into a black hole — stretching it into a long, thin string.
to warp To bend or distort. In Einstein’s picture, mass warps the shape of space and time itself.
counterintuitive Going against what your gut tells you. Most of modern physics is counterintuitive — that’s part of the difficulty, and part of the appeal.
a paradox A claim or situation that seems to contradict itself but may still be true. Hawking’s “information paradox” is one of the deepest puzzles in physics.
to defy To resist; to refuse to obey. Black holes defy intuition, defy easy description, and — in some sense — defy classical physics altogether.

Imagine, for a moment, that you are falling toward a black hole feet-first. Long before you reach it, something strange happens to your body. The gravitational pull on your feet is so much stronger than the pull on your head that you begin to stretch. Astrophysicists, with rare humour, call this spaghettification. It is gravity, and gravity alone, that does this — the same force that holds you in your chair right now, simply turned up to an unbearable degree.

Eventually you cross a boundary called the event horizon. Nothing visible happens at this moment. There is no wall, no flash, no announcement. And yet a line has been crossed: from this point on, no signal you send — no light, no radio wave, no message of any kind — can ever reach the outside universe again. According to general relativity, what lies inside is a singularity: a point at which the equations of physics simply stop giving sensible answers. It remains unclear whether the singularity is a real feature of nature or a sign that our theories are still incomplete.

Black holes form, in the simplest case, when a very large star runs out of fuel. Without the outward pressure of nuclear fusion, the star collapses under its own weight. If it’s heavy enough, nothing can stop the collapse. The result is an object whose mass is so concentrated that it begins to warp the fabric of space and time around it. Light passing nearby is bent. Clocks run more slowly. The very geometry of the surrounding region is changed.

For decades these objects were a theoretical curiosity. Today we have considerably more evidence. What changed our picture most dramatically was the Event Horizon Telescope project, which in 2019 produced the first image of a black hole at the centre of the galaxy M87, and in 2022 imaged Sagittarius A* — the supermassive black hole at the heart of our own Milky Way. The orange ring you may have seen in the news is not the black hole itself but its accretion disk: superheated gas spiralling inward, glowing fiercely just before it disappears forever. The dark patch in the middle is, in effect, a shadow cast on the universe.

Black holes are counterintuitive in almost every respect. They are not, despite what films suggest, cosmic vacuum cleaners. From a safe distance their gravity behaves like that of any other object of the same mass. Time near them does not feel slow to you — but to a friend watching from far away, your clock would appear to tick more and more slowly the closer you got to the horizon, until finally it seemed to stop.

Stranger still is what Stephen Hawking proposed in 1974. Combining quantum theory with gravity, he argued that black holes are not entirely black. They may well radiate a faint stream of particles — Hawking radiation — and over enormous timescales, they could even evaporate completely. This raises one of the deepest puzzles in modern physics, known as the information paradox: if a book falls into a black hole and the black hole eventually evaporates, where did the information in the book go? The laws of quantum mechanics insist that information cannot simply vanish. The evidence suggests that something subtle must preserve it — but precisely what, no one yet knows.

This is why physicists keep returning to black holes. They are not merely exotic objects far from Earth. They are places where our two most successful theories — general relativity and quantum mechanics — meet, disagree, and defy us to find something deeper. It is at the event horizon, more than anywhere else, that the limits of our understanding become visible. Arguably, that is also where the next great theory of physics will be found.

Reading checks

1. Why does spaghettification happen?

2. What does the orange ring in the famous photographs actually show?

3. What did Hawking propose in 1974, and why is it troubling?

4. Why does the writer say black holes are “not cosmic vacuum cleaners”?

1. Because gravity pulls more strongly on the part of your body closer to the black hole than the part further away — the difference stretches you.
2. The accretion disk — superheated gas spiralling in, glowing just before it crosses the event horizon. The black hole itself is the dark patch.
3. That black holes radiate particles and could eventually evaporate — which creates a paradox, because the information in anything that fell in shouldn’t simply vanish.
4. Because their gravity at a distance is no greater than any other object of the same mass — they don’t “suck things in” from far away.

One thing worth remembering

The first photograph of a black hole was taken in 2019 not by one telescope but by eight, on four continents, working together as a single Earth-sized instrument. The image you saw was assembled over two years from five petabytes of data — too much to send over the internet; the hard drives had to be flown.

How careful writers say uncertain things

Cosmology is a field where almost everything is partial. The evidence is excellent for some claims, suggestive for others, and theoretical for the rest. Writers handle that by hedging: signalling exactly how certain they are.

Six phrases worth owning:

Hedging phrases — from cautious to almost confident

It remains unclear whether… — we don’t yet know.

The evidence suggests that… — data points this way, but not conclusively.

X may well… / X could… — possible, perhaps even likely, but not proved.

X appears to… / X would appear to… — this is what we observe; the deeper reason is open.

According to current models… — this is what theory predicts.

Arguably,… — a defensible claim, but I’m flagging that someone could disagree.

In an exam, an interview, a science talk, or a thoughtful conversation, hedging marks you as a serious thinker. Overclaiming (“X is definitely true”) makes you sound naïve. Underclaiming (“I don’t really know anything”) wastes your knowledge. The phrases above let you say exactly how sure you are.

Cleft sentences — putting the spotlight where you want it

A cleft sentence splits one sentence into two parts so that you can stress one piece of information. Compare these:

Plain version vs cleft version

Gravity stretches you into spaghetti.

It is gravity that stretches you into spaghetti. — emphasis on gravity (not anything else).

The Event Horizon Telescope changed our picture.

What changed our picture was the Event Horizon Telescope. — builds suspense; saves the punch for the end.

It was at the event horizon that the puzzle became visible. — emphasis on where.

Your turn — rewrite each sentence as a cleft

1. Hawking proposed the radiation idea, not Einstein.

It was Hawking, not Einstein, who proposed the radiation idea.

2. The shadow in the middle of the image is the most striking feature.

What strikes you most about the image is the shadow in the middle. or It is the shadow in the middle that strikes you most.

3. Quantum theory and general relativity disagree at the event horizon.

It is at the event horizon that quantum theory and general relativity disagree. or Where the two theories disagree most clearly is at the event horizon.

4. The information paradox interests me more than anything else in modern physics.

What interests me more than anything else in modern physics is the information paradox.

Hedge it

Rewrite each over-confident claim using a hedging phrase from the list above.

1. Black holes definitely evaporate.

According to current models, black holes may well evaporate over enormous timescales.

2. Inside the event horizon, time stops.

From the outside, a clock falling toward the horizon would appear to slow, and arguably to stop altogether.

3. The singularity is real.

It remains unclear whether the singularity is a real feature of nature or a sign that our theories are incomplete.
