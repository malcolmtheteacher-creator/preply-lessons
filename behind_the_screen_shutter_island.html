Shutter Island

A U.S. Marshal crosses to a hospital for the criminally insane to solve a disappearance — and uncovers a truth about himself. The medicine and the fears underneath are real.

Today: you'll tell the story of Shutter Island — a lawman's hunt for a missing patient that becomes a reckoning with his own mind — discover the real medical history hiding behind the fiction, and master emphatic do/does/did: the way English insists a thing is true, even when reality is quietly falling apart.

The words for today's story

The film, in three parts

Martin Scorsese's Shutter Island (2010), based on the 2003 novel by Dennis Lehane. Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it. The film deals with grief, mental illness and loss, so we'll keep the details respectful rather than graphic.)

Part 1 — Arrival at Ashecliffe

It's September 1954. U.S. Marshal Teddy Daniels and his new partner, Chuck Aule, take a boat out to Shutter Island, home to Ashecliffe Hospital — a high-security institution for the criminally insane, built on a remote outcrop off the coast of Massachusetts. They've come to investigate an impossible disappearance: Rachel Solando, a patient committed after drowning her own three children, has vanished from a locked room, seemingly through a wall.

As Teddy questions the hospital's staff, he senses something is being hidden from him. Migraines blur his vision, and the watchful doctors give careful, evasive answers. Teddy admits to Chuck that he has a second reason for taking the case: he believes a man named Andrew Laeddis — who he blames for a fire that killed his wife, Dolores — is being held somewhere on the island, and he intends to find him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is Teddy and Chuck's official reason for visiting Shutter Island?

2. What personal reason does Teddy admit to Chuck?

1. To investigate the disappearance of Rachel Solando, a patient who vanished from a locked room after drowning her three children.

2. He believes Andrew Laeddis, the man he holds responsible for killing his wife in a fire, is hidden on the island — and he means to find him.

Discuss:
  • Teddy is hiding his real motive from the hospital staff. Is it ever right to take on a job for reasons you don't admit to the people you're working for?

Part 2 — Visions and a gathering storm

As a hurricane closes in and cuts the island off from the mainland, the case grows stranger. Teddy discovers a coded note hidden by another patient, and slips into a forbidden ward where he glimpses evidence that unsettles him deeply. He becomes convinced Ashecliffe is a front for something far worse: secret, dangerous experiments carried out on patients without their consent.

At the same time, Teddy's own mind seems to be turning against him. Splitting migraines bring on vivid, painful visions: memories of the day he and other American soldiers liberated a Nazi concentration camp near the end of the war, and images of his wife Dolores, soaked and grieving, appearing to speak to him. When Chuck vanishes during a climb across the cliffs, Teddy is left alone, increasingly disoriented, unsure who on the island he can still trust.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Teddy come to believe is really happening at Ashecliffe?

2. What two kinds of visions trouble Teddy through this part of the story?

1. He becomes convinced the hospital is secretly performing dangerous, non-consensual experiments on its patients.

2. Flashbacks to liberating a Nazi concentration camp during the war, and haunting visions of his dead wife, Dolores.

Discuss:
  • Teddy's grief and guilt seem to be reshaping what he sees and believes. Can you think of a time a strong emotion — fear, guilt, grief — changed how you perceived a situation?

Part 3 — The lighthouse and the truth

Teddy finally reaches the lighthouse he believes hides the hospital's darkest secret. Instead, he is met by Dr Cawley, who delivers a devastating truth: Teddy is not a marshal. He is Andrew Laeddis — a patient at Ashecliffe. Two years earlier, his wife Dolores, suffering from a severe mental illness the family had missed, took the lives of their three young children at their lake house. When Andrew found her, in his horror and grief, he shot her.

Unable to bear what he had done, Andrew's mind built an entire alternative story to hide from it: a heroic marshal called "Teddy Daniels," hunting a man called "Andrew Laeddis" for a crime that was really his own, investigating a missing patient called "Rachel Solando" — an anagram of his wife's name, Dolores Chanal. "Chuck," his partner throughout, was in reality his psychiatrist, Dr Sheehan, taking part in a last, radical form of therapy: letting Andrew act out his delusion in full, in the hope that living it through to its end might finally let him face the truth and avoid a lobotomy.

For a moment, Andrew seems to understand and accept it all. But the next morning, he speaks to Sheehan again as "Chuck," and the delusion appears to have reclaimed him. As orderlies lead him away — toward the lighthouse, and the operation that awaits him there — he turns and asks one last, quiet question about what kind of man he would rather be.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is Teddy really, and what did he do to his wife?

2. Who was "Chuck," and what was the hospital trying to do for Andrew?

1. He is Andrew Laeddis, a patient; after his wife Dolores took the lives of their three children, he shot her.

2. Chuck was really Dr Sheehan, Andrew's psychiatrist; the staff were attempting an experimental therapy — letting Andrew fully act out his delusion — to help him face the truth and avoid a lobotomy.

Discuss the film's central question:
  • Would you rather live as a monster, or die as a good man? What do you think Andrew chooses at the end, and why?

Grammar — emphasis with do/does/did

No film needs this structure more than Shutter Island. Normally we don't add do/does/did to a simple affirmative sentence — we just say "I saw her," not "I did see her." But when someone insists a thing is true, especially against doubt or denial, English lets us add a stressed do/does/did before the base verb. It's the sound of a man clinging to a version of reality that is quietly falling apart.

The structure: do/does/did + base verb, for emphasis
• Used to insist or contradict a doubt: "You think I imagined it. I DID see her." "Everyone says she's gone. She DOES exist." "He DID come home that night."
• Often strengthened with intensifiers of insistence: really, honestly, actually, truly. "I really did try to save them." "She honestly does remember everything."
Feel the difference:
• Plain statement: "I saw her." "He knows the truth." — no emphasis, nothing is being contested.
• Emphatic insistence: "I did see her — I'm not imagining it." "He does know the truth, whatever he says." — the speaker is pushing back against disbelief, denial or forgetting.

1. Add the emphasis — do/does/did + base verb

Complete each sentence with do, does or did, matching the tense given.

1. Everyone insists he never came home that year, but he (come) home — for one single night, before he vanished again.

2. His doctors say the burnt cottage is nothing but invention, yet the file shows it (exist) — the fire really happened.

3. She always claimed nobody warned her about her own illness, but her sister (warn) her, more than once.

1. did come (insisting against the claim that he never returned)

2. does exist (insisting the record is real, not invented)

3. did warn (insisting the warning genuinely happened)

2. Fix the form

Each sentence has the right idea but the wrong form. Write the corrected verb phrase.

1. "I did tried to save them," Andrew says, "even if no one believes me."

2. "She does believes every word of her own story," the doctor writes in his notes.

3. "The nurses did noticed the change in him, weeks before the doctors did."

1. did try (do/does/did + base verb, never the -ed form)

2. does believe (base verb, not "believes")

3. did notice (base verb, not "noticed")

3. Complete the story — insist on the truth

Fill each gap with emphatic do/does/did + the verb in brackets. Read it aloud when you're done.

"You think none of it was real," Andrew says quietly, staring at the sea. "But I (love) my children — more than anything. I (try) to reach them in time. And whatever this island tells you about who I am now, I (not / invent) all of it. Some of it truly happened."

did love · did try · did not invent (didn't invent)

Notice the pattern: to insist on a plain truth against doubt or denial, add stressed do/does/did before the base verb — and reach for really, honestly, actually or truly to push the insistence further.

The Real Story — the history behind the fiction

Shutter Island is entirely fiction, from a novel by Dennis Lehane — but Scorsese set it at a real hinge point in the history of psychiatry, and its Cold War fears are drawn from things that genuinely happened. Here's where the story is invented, and where it is not.

🏥 1954 — the year an old treatment was giving way to a new one

The film is set in September 1954 — precisely the year the first effective antipsychotic drug, chlorpromazine (marketed in the U.S. as Thorazine), reached the American market after FDA approval. Within eight months, it had already been given to over two million patients, and the population of U.S. mental hospitals began the long decline that would follow. Thorazine's arrival marked the beginning of the end for an older, far more drastic treatment: the surgical lobotomy — which is exactly the operation hinted at in the story's lighthouse.

The lobotomy era, and the ice-pick doctor who symbolised it

The American neurologist Walter Freeman developed a fast, brutal version of the operation in 1945: the transorbital lobotomy, performed by tapping a thin, pick-like instrument through the eye socket and into the brain — no operating theatre required. Freeman performed more than 3,000 such procedures between the 1930s and 1960s, and for years he was a celebrated, influential figure in American medicine. But by the mid-1950s, long-term studies were exposing how badly patients fared, and the arrival of drugs like Thorazine offered doctors a safer alternative. Freeman's reputation, and the lobotomy itself, declined rapidly. Shutter Island places its story at exactly this turning point — a hospital where the old, violent answer and the new, chemical one exist side by side.

Secret experiments, real fears — and the code written to stop them

The film's dread of secret, non-consensual experiments on the mind is not paranoid invention. In 1953, CIA director Allen Dulles approved a real, top-secret program known as MK-Ultra: for two decades, the agency tested LSD, hypnosis and sensory deprivation on subjects — sometimes without their knowledge or consent — searching for ways to control the human mind at the height of Cold War anxiety. The public only learned of it in 1975, through a congressional investigation.

That fear of doctors experimenting on people without consent has a darker, earlier root: the Nuremberg Code of 1947, written by American judges after the trial of Nazi physicians who had performed torturous, lethal experiments on concentration camp prisoners. It was the first major statement of the principle now central to all medical research — that a patient must give voluntary, informed consent. Andrew's flashbacks to liberating a concentration camp are grounded in a real event, too: on 29 April 1945, U.S. Army divisions liberated Dachau, freeing roughly 32,000 prisoners and discovering horrors that shocked the soldiers who found them.

A trauma with no name yet

One more honest detail: in 1954, what we now call PTSD had no settled diagnosis. It would not be formally recognised until 1980, when it entered the American Psychiatric Association's diagnostic manual, following research into Vietnam veterans and Holocaust survivors. Before that, the language available was older and cruder — "shell shock" from the First World War, "combat fatigue" from the Second — and, briefly, "gross stress reaction," added to the very first diagnostic manual in 1952 and then quietly dropped in 1968. A man like Andrew, carrying what he saw at war and the loss of his family, would have had almost no framework, and little real support, for what he was living through.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Which of these facts surprised you most — the lobotomy, MK-Ultra, or the missing diagnosis for trauma?
  • The plot itself is entirely invented. Does knowing that the medicine, the fear of secret experiments, and Dachau's liberation were all real change how you watch the film?
  • Why might a story about mental illness be more powerful when it's anchored to real history, rather than pure invention?
