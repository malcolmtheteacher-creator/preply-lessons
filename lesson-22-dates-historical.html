The schoolmaster's strange words

England in the 1720s. A clergyman and schoolmaster, Richard Grey, has a problem every history teacher knows. His pupils can recite the story of the Norman Conquest, but they cannot tell you the year. They can name the kings, but not the order. Dates, he finds, are the first thing to fall out of a pupil's head.

A date is a number, and we saw in the second part what the mind does with numbers: it lets go. Grey decided to do something about it.

What problem did Richard Grey notice among his pupils?
They could not hold on to dates. Numbers are the first thing to fall out of memory.
Talk about it. Which historical dates can you remember? How did you learn them?

In 1730 he published a small book called Memoria Technica, 'the technical memory'. In it he gave a code in which letters stood for digits, so that a date became a word, or a made-up word, which a pupil could learn like any other. The date of an event was hidden in a nonsense word that sounded a little like its name.

The strange words were never popular. They were clever but hard to learn: the code itself had to be memorised, and the words were dull. But the idea was sound, and it was the same as ours: turn a number into something you can say and see.

What was the Memoria Technica?
A 1730 book that used a code of letters for digits to turn dates into words, so pupils could learn them like words.
Talk about it. Why do you think the nonsense words were not popular?

We can do better with what we have. A date has two halves. The first two digits name the century; the last two name the year. There are only a few centuries that matter, and they can be rooms. There are a hundred possible year-endings, and they are the hundred pictures you already have from the numbers part.

So a date becomes a place and a picture. The place is the century's room. The picture is the year-ending, joined to the event itself. For 1066: the room of the eleventh century, a judge (66), and a battlefield. You will see it, and you will not need to learn a nonsense word.

How does the timeline palace store a date?
The century is a room, the last two digits are a picture, and the event is the scene they act in.
Talk about it. How many centuries would you need rooms for, to cover the dates you care about most?

Rooms for centuries, pictures for years

A year has two parts: the century (which room) and the last two digits (which picture).

  1. Build a timeline palace. A building with a room for each century you need, in time order from the front door. A twelve-room house covers the eleventh to the twentieth centuries and a bit more.
  2. Know your two-digit pictures. Use your Major System words (lesson 7): 66 = judge, 15 = doll, 92 = bone, 89 = fib, 69 = ship.
  3. Make the event a scene. A few strong facts: who, where, what.
  4. Join the year picture to the event. In the century's room, the year picture acts in the scene.
  5. Walk the timeline. From the oldest room to the newest. The order of events is the order of rooms.

Tips

  • Only the last two digits need a picture. The room carries the century.
  • Keep the event simple. One strong image of what happened.
  • Learn the sequence as a story as you walk from room to room: each event leads to the next.
  • Check by number sense. If it is the 1900s, you know the first two digits already.

Columbus sails across the Atlantic in 1492. The ending is 92 (bone or pen). Make a scene in the fifteenth-century room.
A tiny wooden ship, with a giant bone for a mast, sails into the room, and a flock of seagulls tries to eat the mast.

1789 and 1989

1789: the French Revolution begins. 1989: the Berlin Wall falls. Both end in 89 (fib). Make two scenes that are very different from each other.
1789 (18th-century room): a man in a powdered wig tells a huge fib at the Bastille, and his nose grows until it knocks over the gate. 1989 (20th-century room): people pull down a wall of cardboard bricks, laughing, while each brick says 'FIB' on one side.

1969 and 1815

1969: the Moon landing (69 = ship). 1815: Waterloo (15 = tail). Make a scene for each in its own century's room.
1969: a silver ship lands in the moon-dust, with an enormous flag. 1815: Napoleon steps on a lion's tail on the battlefield, and the lion roars.
