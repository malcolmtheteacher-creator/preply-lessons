Three Engines, Thirty Seconds Each

No re-teaching — just the shape of each engine, then straight into using it.

Engine 1: have been + -ing

It's the doing that matters, not the result — carrying on, or only recently stopped.

have / has + been + verb-ing
Trap: state verbs — know, love, believe — never take been + -ing.

1. Your brother started saving for a car eighteen months ago — he's still at it. Use the continuous form of save:

"He for a car for eighteen months."

2. You and your sister have barely stopped texting each other about the wedding this week. Use the continuous form of message:

"We about the wedding all week."

Engine 2: the everyday passive

The thing goes first because the doer doesn't matter.

is / are being + participle  (now)
has / have been + participle  (recent)
got + participle  (casual — usually mishaps)

3. Right now, workmen are repaving the car park next to your gym — you can't park there this week. Start with "The car park":

"The car park at the moment — you'll have to park round the corner."

4. Bad luck: your neighbour's car got scratched in the car park yesterday, and she's furious. Use get:

"Her car in the car park yesterday — she's furious."

Engine 3: which future?

The form you choose tells your listener how fixed the plan is.

-ing  (arranged)  ·  going to  (intended)
will  (decided now / offer)  ·  will be + -ing  (happening anyway)

5. Your friend's laptop just died right before an online call. Offer to lend yours — decided this second, with lend:

"Here — I you mine."

6. "I can't do lunch Thursday — I ___ the dentist. It's been in the diary for weeks." Fill the gap with the arranged form of see:

"I can't do lunch Thursday — I the dentist. It's been in the diary for weeks."

The Call

Two rounds. First she rings you — then you ring her back.

Round 1 — Aunt Jean rings you

Aunt Jean: Hello love! Been ages. So — what have you been up to?

Tell her about your evening photography course, which started in September and is still going. Use have been + -ing:

"I an evening photography course since September."

Aunt Jean: Ooh, lovely. And is it true the old cinema down your road finally reopened? Someone told me it's a restaurant now.

Confirm her news: it's genuinely been turned into a gastropub, and it only opened last month. Use the passive:

"Yes — it into a gastropub. It only opened last month."

Aunt Jean: Well, you'll have to come and show me sometime. Any chance of a visit this summer?

You haven't booked anything yet, but you intend to visit her this August — decided before now, not yet arranged. Use going to:

"I haven't booked anything yet, but I you this August."

Keep Talking

She doesn't pick up. Leave her a message — then the plans start moving.

Leave the voicemail

Aunt Jean doesn't answer. Talk for two full minutes, as if you're leaving a voicemail. Get all three engines in there:

  • how your life's been lately — the good bits and the tiring bits
  • anything different nearby — what's new, gone, or not the same anymore
  • what's ahead — the certain, the maybe, and the "we'll see"

The plans keep changing

SPEAK

She calls back. You've arranged to meet for coffee on Saturday at 11 — then the plan keeps moving. Read each line and speak your response out loud before going to the next one. Let the future form match how fixed things are getting:

  1. She texts: "Can we make it half past two instead?" — agree, keeping the arranged form.
  2. Ten minutes later: "Actually, I might be busy Saturday after all — how about Sunday morning?" — respond with an on-the-spot decision.
  3. Just as you're heading out on Sunday: "Something's come up — could we push it to next week?" — respond however feels natural, and say out loud why your form changed.

Now put it in writing

Draft the voicemail message you'd actually leave — or the text confirming your final plan. 3–5 sentences, and try to fit in all three engines; run the checker afterwards to see which ones showed up.
