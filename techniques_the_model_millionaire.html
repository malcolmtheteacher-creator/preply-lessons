The Model Millionaire

Oscar Wilde's sly little tale of a poor young man, a ragged beggar, and a kindness that pays for itself.

Today: you'll read one of Oscar Wilde's sharpest short stories — about a kind but penniless young man who gives his last coin to a beggar, and gets a surprise in return — and you'll master the English of comparison, from richer than to the most generous.
Warm up — before we meet the words. Talk these through out loud with your teacher; there are no right answers yet.
  • Think of a time someone was kinder to you than you expected. What did they do?
  • Can you tell how rich or poor a person is just by looking at them? When might appearances be misleading?
  • Is a small gift from someone with very little worth more than a large gift from someone with plenty? Why?

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. He had charm and good looks, but not a single coin to his name; he was, in a word, thoroughly penniless.
penniless (adj) — having no money at all; extremely poor.
2. She would only agree to the marriage once he could show a steady income of ten thousand a year — until then, he had no chance of an allowance from her father.
an allowance (n) — a sum of money given to someone regularly, often by a relative, to live on.
3. The old man's coat was torn and patched, and his boots were falling apart; he looked like a beggar who slept in doorways.
a beggar (n) — a very poor person who lives by asking others for money or food.
4. His clothes were ragged — full of holes, frayed at the edges, and grey with age and dust.
ragged (adj) — old and torn; in a poor, worn-out condition.
5. The artist asked the old man to pose for him — to stand quite still by the window while he painted his portrait.
to pose (v) — to hold a fixed position so that an artist or photographer can capture your image.
6. The picture was so lifelike that visitors gasped; it was a remarkable portrait, every wrinkle and shadow exactly right.
a portrait (n) — a painting, drawing or photograph of a person, especially of the face.
7. Moved by the sight of such poverty, Hughie felt a wave of pure pity and reached into his pocket.
pity (n) — a feeling of sympathy and sadness for someone else's suffering.
8. He was the most generous man in London — always giving, never counting the cost, happy to share whatever he had.
generous (adj) — willing to give money, help or time freely; not mean.
9. Behind the rags stood one of the wealthiest men in Europe — a baron, no less, with a title and estates across three countries.
a baron (n) — a nobleman of high rank; here, an extremely rich and important man.
10. When Hughie learned the truth, his face turned red with embarrassment — he had offered a coin to a millionaire.
embarrassment (n) — the uncomfortable feeling of shame or awkwardness when you have done something foolish in front of others.

Talk it through before we read:
  • Using penniless, beggar, ragged and pity, predict: what do you think this story will be about?
  • Which of these words describe money and rank, and which describe feelings? Sort them out loud and say why.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A poor young man in love

Hughie Erskine was, everyone agreed, the most charming young man in London — and also one of the poorest. He had tried everything: the Stock Exchange, tea-selling, sherry-selling, even a spell as a clerk. Nothing lasted. What he had instead was a fine profile, a warm heart, and not a penny of his own. He lived on a small allowance from an aunt, and he owed money everywhere.

To make matters harder, Hughie was in love. He adored Laura Merton, the daughter of a retired colonel, and she adored him back. But her father was blunt about it. "Come to me, my boy," the old man said, "when you have ten thousand pounds of your own, and we'll see." So Hughie went about his days looking rather miserable, in love and utterly penniless.

One morning he called on his friend Alan Trevor, a painter, at his studio. Trevor was a clever, brilliant fellow, a little rough in manner but genuinely fond of Hughie — partly, he admitted, because Hughie was so agreeable and so completely useless with money. "The only people a painter should know," Trevor liked to say, "are people who are beautiful and people who are amusing. Hughie is both."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Hughie's situation in life, and what jobs had he tried?

2. Why couldn't Hughie marry Laura?

1. He was charming and kind but very poor; he had tried the Stock Exchange, tea-selling, sherry-selling and clerking, but nothing worked out.

2. Laura's father, a retired colonel, said Hughie could marry her only when he had ten thousand pounds of his own.

Discuss:
  • Laura's father sets a price on the marriage: ten thousand pounds. Is he being sensible or cruel? Would you set conditions like that?

Part 2 — The beggar in the studio

When Hughie came in, Trevor was finishing a portrait of a most extraordinary model. In the corner of the studio stood an old man, thin and bent, in the most ragged clothes Hughie had ever seen. His coat was torn, his boots were patched, and one hand leaned on a rough stick while the other held out a battered hat for coins. His face, though, was wonderful — worn, weathered, and somehow noble. It was the very picture of misery, and Trevor was painting it beautifully.

"What an amazing model!" Hughie whispered, as he shook hands with his friend. "An amazing model?" cried Trevor. "I should think so! You don't meet beggars like him every day. A find, my boy — a Velázquez in rags!" "Poor old man," said Hughie. "How miserable he looks. But I suppose, to you painters, his face is a fortune?" "Certainly," replied Trevor. "You don't want a beggar to look happy, do you?"

Trevor stepped out for a moment to speak with someone. Left alone with the old man, Hughie looked at the pitiful figure and felt his heart ache. He searched his pockets. All he could find was a single sovereign — a gold coin, and very nearly all the money he had in the world. He hesitated only a second; then, quietly, so as not to embarrass the man, he pressed the coin into the beggar's hand. The old man smiled faintly. "Thank you, sir," he said. "Thank you."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the model look, and why was Trevor so pleased with him?

2. What did Hughie do when he was left alone with the old man, and why was it a real sacrifice?

1. The model was an old man in ragged clothes, thin and miserable, but with a wonderful, noble face; Trevor called him "a Velázquez in rags" — a perfect subject.

2. He gave the beggar a gold sovereign — almost all the money he had — quietly, out of pity, even though he could barely afford it himself.

Discuss:
  • Hughie gives away almost his last coin to a stranger. Was that generous or foolish? Where is the line between the two?

Part 3 — The truth behind the rags

That evening, Hughie mentioned the beggar again, half-laughing at himself for giving away a coin he could not spare. Trevor stared at him, then burst out laughing. "That old beggar, as you call him," he said, "is Baron Hausberg — one of the richest men in Europe. He could buy all of London tomorrow if he chose. He's a friend of mine, collects my pictures, and asked me last week to paint him — as a beggar, for his own amusement." Hughie went scarlet. "You mean," he stammered, "I gave Baron Hausberg a sovereign?" "You did indeed," roared Trevor, "and I'm sure he'll treasure it more than any picture I could paint."

Hughie was mortified. He had offered charity to one of the wealthiest men alive. "Why on earth didn't you tell me?" he groaned, and went home feeling more foolish than he had ever felt in his life. The Baron, meanwhile, had been told the whole story — and had found it delightful.

The next morning, a servant brought Hughie a small parcel and a note. It was from Baron Hausberg. Inside was a cheque for ten thousand pounds — exactly the sum the colonel had demanded — made out as a wedding present to Hughie and Laura. "Millionaire models," the Baron had written, "are rare enough; but model millionaires are rarer still." Hughie married Laura, and Alan Trevor was best man at the wedding. As Trevor put it afterwards, one kind sovereign, given without a thought of reward, had turned out to be the best investment a penniless young man ever made.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was the "beggar" really, and why was he dressed in rags?

2. How did the Baron reward Hughie's kindness, and what happened in the end?

1. He was Baron Hausberg, one of the richest men in Europe; he had asked Trevor to paint him dressed as a beggar, for his own amusement.

2. He sent Hughie a cheque for ten thousand pounds as a wedding present, with a witty note; Hughie was then able to marry Laura.

Discuss:
  • Wilde's title has two meanings — a "model millionaire" and a "millionaire model". Which reading do you think Wilde wanted us to enjoy most?

Grammar — comparatives & superlatives

This whole story turns on comparison: Hughie is poorer than he'd like, the Baron is the richest man in Europe, and a small gift can be worth more than a large one. English has a neat system for measuring one thing against another — and for crowning the one at the very top.

Comparatives — comparing two things:
• Short adjectives add -er + than: rich → richer than, poor → poorer than, kind → kinder than.
• Longer adjectives use more + than: generous → more generous than, miserable → more miserable than.
• Irregulars: good → better than, bad → worse than, far → further than.
Superlatives — the top of the group:
• Short adjectives add the …-est: rich → the richest, poor → the poorest, kind → the kindest.
• Longer adjectives use the most: generous → the most generous, charming → the most charming.
• Irregulars: good → the best, bad → the worst.
Equality — saying two things are the same:
• Use as … as: "He is as kind as his friend." "The gift was as generous as it was surprising."
• Make it negative for "less than": "Hughie is not as rich as the Baron."

3. Hughie was (poor) than his friend Trevor, but far (kind). (comparing two)

2. more (more … than — comparing two)

3. poorer … kinder (both comparatives with -er)

Fill each gap with the correct comparative or superlative form of the adjective in brackets. Read it aloud once you're done.

Going deeper

Wilde tells a light, funny story — but underneath it he is playing with something serious: the gap between how a person looks and who they really are, and the quiet idea that kindness given for its own sake tends to come back around.

The heart of the tale. Hughie can't tell a millionaire from a beggar, because he judges only by appearance — as almost everyone does. Yet his mistake is a generous one: he gives freely, expecting nothing, precisely at the moment when giving costs him most. Wilde's gentle irony is that the "foolish" gift is the wisest thing Hughie ever does. The story smiles at both wealth and poverty, and lands on the side of an open heart.

Talk it through:
  • Appearances vs reality: The Baron dresses as a beggar and fools everyone. Where else in life do people misjudge others by how they look — and does it still happen today?
  • The nature of the gift: Would Hughie's kindness "count" for less if he had known the beggar was rich? Does a good act need to be blind to be pure?
  • The ending: Wilde rewards Hughie with exactly ten thousand pounds. Is that a satisfying ending, or does the tidy reward slightly cheapen the kindness? What would the story lose if the Baron had simply said "thank you" and vanished?
  • What would you have done? If you had one coin left and met someone who seemed to need it far more than you, would you give it? What decides such a moment?

Task B · Kindness and reward (4 minutes)

Hughie is rewarded richly for a kind act he did without any hope of reward. Should kindness be rewarded — and does a reward make it less admirable? Build an argument, using examples you know, and decide whether the best good deeds are the ones done with no thought of getting anything back.

Task C · Judging by appearances (4 minutes)

The whole story turns on a man who looks far poorer than he is. Talk about appearances and reality — and lean on the grammar: who seemed richer or poorer than they turned out to be, who was as kind as they looked, who was the most surprising.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • A person or place that turned out to be very different from your first impression.
  • A famously wealthy person who lives simply, or a modest-looking person who was quietly rich — and what that says about them.
  • A time a small, unexpected kindness made a big difference — to you or to someone you know.
  • A story or film where someone is not who they seem, and the surprise changes everything.
  • The idea that "you can't judge a book by its cover" — an example that proves it true, and one that doesn't.
  • How advertising and social media train us to judge by appearances — is it getting better or worse?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Wilde makes Hughie's generosity pay off handsomely. In real life, kindness usually goes unrewarded. Does that make the story a comforting fantasy, or a quiet challenge to be kind anyway?
  2. The Baron dresses as a beggar "for his own amusement". Is it charming of a rich man to play at poverty, or a little heartless? What's the difference between the Baron and Hughie?
  3. The story is more than a century old. Why do you think a tale about appearances, money and kindness still lands with readers today?
