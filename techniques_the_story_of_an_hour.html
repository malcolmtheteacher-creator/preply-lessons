SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B2/C1 · 50 min

The Story of an Hour

Kate Chopin (1894) — a woman is told she is a widow, and in a single hour lives a whole second life

Read in three parts

Chopin tells the whole story in barely a thousand words — and it is one of the most savage in the language. The whole thing takes place in an hour. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The ending arrives in a single sentence — do not skip ahead.

Part 1 — The news

Knowing that Mrs. Mallard was afflicted with a heart trouble, great care was taken to break to her as gently as possible the news of her husband's death.

It was her sister Josephine who told her, in broken sentences; veiled hints that revealed in half concealing. Her husband's friend Richards was there, too, near her. It was he who had been in the newspaper office when intelligence of the railroad disaster was received, with Brently Mallard's name leading the list of "killed." He had only taken the time to assure himself of its truth by a second telegram, and had hastened to forestall any less careful, less tender friend in bearing the sad message.

She did not hear the story as many women have heard the same, with a paralyzed inability to accept its significance. She wept at once, with sudden, wild abandonment, in her sister's arms. When the storm of grief had spent itself she went away to her room alone. She would have no one follow her.

Pause and discuss:
  • Read the first sentence again. Why does Chopin tell us about the heart trouble first, before anything else?
  • Most women in such stories react with shock and disbelief. Mrs. Mallard does not — she weeps at once. What does Chopin make a point of saying about her instead?
  • She wants to be alone, and "would have no one follow her." Why? What is the act of going alone to a room setting up?

Part 2 — The open window

There stood, facing the open window, a comfortable, roomy armchair. Into this she sank, pressed down by a physical exhaustion that haunted her body and seemed to reach into her soul.

She could see in the open square before her house the tops of trees that were all aquiver with the new spring life. The delicious breath of rain was in the air. In the street below a peddler was crying his wares. The notes of a distant song which some one was singing reached her faintly, and countless sparrows were twittering in the eaves. There were patches of blue sky showing here and there through the clouds that had met and piled one above the other in the west facing her window.

She was young, with a fair, calm face, whose lines bespoke repression and even a certain strength. There was something coming to her and she was waiting for it, fearfully. What was it? She did not know; it was too subtle and elusive to name. But she felt it, creeping out of the sky, reaching toward her through the sounds, the scents, the color that filled the air.

When she abandoned herself a little whispered word escaped her slightly parted lips. She said it over and over under her breath: "free, free, free!" The vacant stare and the look of terror that had followed it went from her eyes. They stayed keen and bright.

She saw beyond that bitter moment a long procession of years to come that would belong to her absolutely. And she opened and spread her arms out to them in welcome. There would be no one to live for during those coming years; she would live for herself. And yet she had loved him — sometimes. Often she had not. What did it matter! "Free! Body and soul free!" she kept whispering.

Pause and discuss:
  • The room she chooses faces an open window. What might that window stand for?
  • It is spring; there is rain in the air; sparrows are twittering; someone is singing. Two paragraphs ago she wept like a storm. What has Chopin done with the weather of the story?
  • The word she finally whispers is "free." Three times. Why three?
  • "She had loved him — sometimes. Often she had not." Read it aloud. Where is the courage in that line?

Part 3 — The door

Josephine was kneeling before the closed door with her lips to the keyhole, imploring for admission. "Louise, open the door! I beg; open the door — you will make yourself ill."

"Go away. I am not making myself ill." No; she was drinking in a very elixir of life through that open window. She breathed a quick prayer that life might be long. It was only yesterday she had thought with a shudder that life might be long.

She arose at length and opened the door to her sister's importunities. There was a feverish triumph in her eyes, and she carried herself unwittingly like a goddess of Victory. She clasped her sister's waist, and together they descended the stairs. Richards stood waiting for them at the bottom.

Some one was opening the front door with a latchkey. It was Brently Mallard who entered, a little travel-stained, composedly carrying his grip-sack and umbrella. He had been far from the scene of accident, and did not even know there had been one. He stood amazed at Josephine's piercing cry; at Richards' quick motion to screen him from the view of his wife.

But Richards was too late.

When the doctors came they said she had died of heart disease — of joy that kills.

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • "Life might be long" appears twice — yesterday and today. What changed in twelve hours?
  • "But Richards was too late." A whole paragraph of one line. Why does Chopin set it on its own?
  • "Of joy that kills." Whose interpretation is that? Is it correct?
  • Was she killed by her husband's return, or by being asked, in that moment, to give the freedom back? What is the most honest reading?
  • Your turn: Read the first line of the story again, then the last. What has the whole story done in between?

How Chopin's word-choice works

Chopin's power is in pairing an ordinary, calm sentence with a single charged word that detonates inside it. Notice the contrast — the same idea in plain English loses everything.

The grammar this story teaches itself

Chopin narrates a future that never arrives. From a single moment in the armchair, Mrs. Mallard looks forward into years she imagines — and backward into the day's events. Two structures do that work: future-in-the-past ("would" + base verb) and the past perfect for everything that happened before the story's "now."

1. Future-in-the-past — imagining a life from a past viewpoint

When we report or narrate a future seen from a moment in the past, "will" becomes "would". This is the engine of the whole window scene: she sees years that would belong to her, knows she would weep again.

Pattern: (past viewpoint) + would + base verb. For an imagined but uncertain future: might + base verb.

1. She knew that she (weep) again when she saw his face.

2. She saw a long procession of years that (belong) to her absolutely.

3. There (be) no one to live for; she (live) for herself.

4. She breathed a prayer that life (may / be) long.

5. Yesterday she had shuddered that life (may / be) long — the same words, the opposite hope.

1. would weep

2. would belong

3. would be / would live

4. might be

5. might be (identical words, recoloured by everything between them)

2. Past perfect — the events behind the "now"

The story's present is the hour in the armchair. Everything that led to it is set one step further back, in the past perfect (had + past participle). This is how Chopin keeps the timeline clear without stopping to explain it.

1. Richards (be) in the newspaper office when the news came.

2. He (take) only the time to assure himself of its truth.

3. By the time the storm of grief (spend) itself, she went away to her room.

4. Brently Mallard (be) far from the accident and (not / know) there had been one.

1. had been

2. had taken

3. had spent

4. had been … had not known

3. Put them together

Finish this mini-narration about a turning point of your own, keeping the same viewpoint — a moment in the past, looking both back and forward:

"By the time I made the decision, I already ________ . I knew my life ________ , and I told myself it ________ ."

Say it aloud first; then write it. There is no single right answer — but the tenses must hold.

What Chopin does — and the verbs to describe it

Five moves to spot. Together they are why a thousand words written in 1894 still ambush a reader today.

1. Foreshadowing — the gun on the table

The very first words tell us Mrs. Mallard "was afflicted with a heart trouble." Chopin loads the ending into line one. Every reader who finishes comes back to that sentence and feels the trap snap shut — and "heart trouble" quietly symbolises something far larger than a medical fact.

2. Symbolism — the open window

She sinks into a chair facing an open window, and what pours through it is spring, rain, song, blue sky breaking through cloud. The window symbolises the life outside the marriage — the future she suddenly sees as hers. It is also the window she will, in effect, be made to turn away from.

3. Repetition — the naked word

"free, free, free!" — then, louder, "Free! Body and soul free!" Chopin withholds the word "freedom" through the whole build-up, then lets her whisper it three times. The repetition intensifies and confesses at once.

4. Irony — the ending that reframes everything

"Of joy that kills." The doctors are right about the cause and wrong about the joy. The reader knows she died not of happiness at her husband's return but of having freedom offered and withdrawn in one hour. The closing line ironises the whole story — and the gentle title, "The Story of an Hour," turns sharp.

5. Syntax — the one-line paragraph

"But Richards was too late." A whole paragraph of one short sentence, set alone on the page. Chopin undercuts the rising triumph with a door slamming. We hear it before we fully know what has happened.

Task A · Unexpected news (4 minutes)

Talk about a time you got news that everyone expected you to feel one way about, but privately you felt something more complicated. Cover three things:

  1. how you heard the news;
  2. what you really felt in private;
  3. how you behaved once you were back with other people.

Try to use at least one future-in-the-past form ("I thought my life would…") and a few effect verbs from the Technique tab.

Task B · The Open Window (3 minutes)

In the story, the open window stands for the freedom Louise cannot yet name.

Choose an object, a view, or a place in your own life that stands for a freedom or a future you want. First describe it in detail, then explain why it represents that for you. Try to describe it clearly enough that I can picture it before you explain what it means.

Task C · The wrong reason (4 minutes)

The doctors end the story by giving the wrong reason for Louise's death — a cause that is neat, respectable, and false.

Talk about a time someone was sure they knew why you felt or did something, but got the reason wrong. Explain what they thought had happened, then what had actually happened. End with one clear sentence giving the real reason.
