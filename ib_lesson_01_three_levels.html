The Three Levels of Analysis

What separates a 5 from a 7 — and how to climb the ladder

The single biggest secret

If you remember one thing from this whole course, make it this:

An IB 7 isn't about spotting more techniques than other students. It's about explaining why the author's choices matter — what effect they have on the reader, and what they reveal about the text.

What Criterion A actually rewards

The IB rubric for Criterion A (Analysis & Evaluation) measures one thing above all: whether you can explain the relationship between authorial choices and their effects on the reader.

"The response demonstrates perceptive analysis and evaluation of the ways in which authorial choices shape meaning."
— IB Criterion A, Band 7 descriptor

Three words to underline forever

  • Authorial choices — every technique the writer used (diction, imagery, structure, voice, irony, symbolism…)
  • Effects — what those choices do to the reader (position them, unsettle them, invite them to question…)
  • Shape meaning — how the choices and effects, together, create the larger point of the text
Translation: Stop telling the examiner what happened in the text. Start telling them how the author made it happen, and why it works on you as a reader.

The ladder of analysis

Every sentence you write about a text sits on one of three rungs. Your goal is to climb to the top rung as often as possible.

1

Identification Band 3–4

"The writer uses a metaphor."

Naming only
2

Description Band 5

"The writer uses a metaphor that compares the leader to a disease."

Naming + what
3

Analysis Band 6–7

"The writer's metaphor of the leader as a disease exposes authority as inherently corrupting, positioning the reader to distrust all claims to power before any wrongdoing is named."

Naming + what + effect

How to recognise which rung you're on

Ask yourself If "no" — you're on rung…
Did I name a specific technique? Below 1 (just summary)
Did I quote or describe what the author actually wrote? 1 (Identification)
Did I explain the effect on the reader? 2 (Description)
Did I link the effect to the bigger meaning of the text? 3 — but only halfway up
The full top-rung sentence has all four: technique → quote/description → effect on reader → meaning.

Weak vs strong, side by side

Three real moments from Orwell's Animal Farm. Same evidence — but watch what changes when we climb the ladder.

⚖️

Example 1 — The pigs name Napoleon "Leader"

"…our Leader, Comrade Napoleon…"

✗ Weak (rung 1)

"Orwell shows Napoleon being called 'Leader'."

Just retells. No technique, no effect.

✓ Strong (rung 3)

"Orwell's capitalised honorific — 'our Leader, Comrade Napoleon' — ritualises authority through language alone, conscripting even 'Comrade' into the machinery of hierarchy. The reader is positioned to distrust the farm's new vocabulary before any violence has been named, because the words themselves have already done the work."

Names technique (capitalised honorific / loaded vocabulary), quotes evidence, explains effect on reader, links to meaning.

⚖️

Example 2 — Boxer's motto

"Napoleon is always right."

✗ Weak (rung 1)

"Boxer believes in Napoleon. This is bad for him."

Plot summary plus a verdict. No analysis.

✓ Strong (rung 3)

"Orwell's deliberate reduction of Boxer's thought to a four-word motto — 'Napoleon is always right' — strips the horse of moral agency and invites the reader's sympathy rather than blame, redirecting criticism upward to the system that rewards such simplicity with loyalty."

Names technique (reduction / motto), quotes evidence, explains effect on reader (sympathy, not blame), links to bigger meaning (the system is the villain).

⚖️

Example 3 — The final scene

"The creatures outside looked from pig to man, and from man to pig, and from pig to man again; but already it was impossible to say which was which."

✗ Weak (rung 1)

"The pigs look like humans now. This is scary because they used to be different."

Describes content. No technique named.

✓ Strong (rung 3)

"Orwell's chiastic pattern — 'pig to man, and from man to pig' — mimics the other animals' scanning gaze and exhausts the reader's ability to distinguish oppressor from oppressed, reinforcing the moral that moral collapse is not an event but a process slow enough for its witnesses to miss it."

Names two techniques (chiasmus, mimetic syntax), quotes both, explains the sensory effect on reader, links to the moral message.

Notice the pattern: the strong column always names a specific technique, quotes the evidence directly, and finishes with what the choice does to the reader. That's the full rung-3 move.

Sample sentences, climbed up the ladder

Below are three sample sentences of the kind students often write. For each one, diagnose which rung it sits on, then try to climb it. The reveal shows one possible upgrade — yours might be different and still excellent.

Sentence 1

"Napoleon is the mighty leader who in fact was greedy to keep the other animals poor and ignorant so he could thrive."

Diagnosis: Rung 1 — Identification only. The writer names what Napoleon does (greedy, keeps others ignorant) but does not name a technique Orwell uses, and does not say what effect Orwell's choice has on us as readers.

What is missing: a named technique + an effect on the reader.

Try rewriting it on rung 3. What technique is Orwell using when he has the pigs adopt words like "Leader", "Comrade", and "equal" for their own purposes?

One possible rung-3 version:

"Orwell's choice to voice the pigs' authority in the revolution's own vocabulary — 'Comrade', 'Leader', 'equal' — uses ironic appropriation to expose tyranny without the narrator ever needing to condemn it. The reader is invited to judge the pigs directly from the hollowed-out words they keep using."

What changed: we named the technique (ironic appropriation / loaded vocabulary), quoted the actual language Orwell uses, and finished with the effect on the reader (invited to judge directly).

Sentence 2

"Boxer's strength made him think his only job was to work harder and harder, and his loyalty has ruined him."

Diagnosis: Rung 2 — Description. The writer describes Boxer's psychology, but does not link this to Orwell's writing choices, and blames Boxer for being ruined, which is not what Orwell's text actually positions us to feel.

What is missing: a named technique Orwell uses + the actual effect on the reader, which is sympathy not blame.

Try rewriting it on rung 3. Hint: think about how Orwell's choice to reduce Boxer's thinking to the two mottos "I will work harder" and "Napoleon is always right" creates a particular feeling.

One possible rung-3 version:

"Orwell's repeated reduction of Boxer to two mottos — 'I will work harder' and 'Napoleon is always right' — frames him as a victim of simplicity rather than a moral failure. The reader is invited to sympathise: Boxer obeys because the system has made him exactly the kind of worker who would, which redirects blame upward toward the pigs."

What changed: we named the technique (characterisation through repeated mottos), pinned it to Orwell's choice, and reframed the effect on the reader (sympathy, not condemnation) — which links to the bigger meaning (the pigs are the villains).

Sentence 3

"This can be seen in Animal Farm, from the 7 commandments which stated 'all animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others', referring to how the pigs take control."

Diagnosis: Rung 1–2 — the writer has the right quote and describes what it means, but does not name Orwell's technique and does not explain its effect on the reader.

What is missing: name Orwell's technique (allegory? gradual revision? satire?) and say what the choice does to us as readers.

Try rewriting it on rung 3. Hint: think about how Orwell shows the change to the commandments — slowly, one rule at a time.

One possible rung-3 version:

"Orwell's gradual revision of the Seven Commandments — until only the contradictory 'all animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others' remains — uses creeping satirical allegory to make the reader complicit in the corruption. We notice each small change too late, mirroring the animals on the farm and amplifying the betrayal."

What changed: we named the technique (gradual revision / satirical allegory), kept the quote, and explained the effect on the reader (complicity, betrayal) — which lifts the sentence onto the top rung.
Notice: in every upgrade, the formula is the same — Author's name + named technique + quoted evidence + effect on reader + bigger meaning.

Effect-verb starter bank

When you write the "effect on the reader" half of your sentence, reach for one of these verbs. Avoid the bland "the reader sees" or "the reader thinks".

positions us to… invites us to question… unsettles the reader… creates a sense of… reinforces the idea that… forces us to confront… undermines our assumption that… implicates the reader in… directs sympathy toward… withholds judgement so that… provokes… amplifies… complicates… makes the reader complicit in…

The four self-check questions

Before you submit any analysis sentence, ask yourself:

  1. Did I name a specific technique?
  2. Did I quote or directly describe what the author wrote?
  3. Did I explain the effect on the reader?
  4. Did I link that effect to the bigger meaning of the text?

If yes to all four, you are on rung 3. If you missed one, you have not yet earned the band you are aiming for.
