Climate Vocabulary

These collocations appear frequently in climate discussions. Explore them below.

carbon dioxide
The main greenhouse gas produced by burning fossil fuels
greenhouse gases
Gases that trap heat in the atmosphere
fossil fuels
Coal, oil, and natural gas formed from ancient plants
polar ice caps
Massive sheets of ice at the North and South Poles
sea levels
The height of oceans, rising due to melting ice
average temperature
Mean global heat, measured over time
land clearance
Removing forests and vegetation for development
severe weather
Extreme events: hurricanes, floods, droughts
human activity
Actions by people driving climate change
gradual rise
Slow, steady increase in temperature or sea level
degree centigrade
Unit of temperature measurement (°C)
climate change
Long-term shifts in global climate patterns

What's at stake?

Rising temperatures threaten ecosystems, agriculture, and human societies. A 1.5°C rise could cause catastrophic flooding, crop failures, and mass migration. Future generations depend on action today.

Consider these angles:
  • Economic cost of climate change vs. cost of prevention
  • Who is most affected? (island nations, developing countries, coastal cities)
  • Should wealthy nations act first?
  • Can technology alone solve this?

Active vs. Passive Voice

Click each pair to see how focus changes. Notice when passive is used in climate writing.

Sunshade Strategy
Passive (Focus on action):

"A giant sunshade would be rocketed into space."

Active (Focus on agent):

"We would rocket a giant sunshade into space."

Why passive here?

The passive emphasizes the technology and its impact, not who does it. Sounds more formal and scientific.

Glass Discs Construction
Passive (Focus on method):

"This could be done by constructing the shade of trillions of small glass discs."

Active (Focus on agent):

"You could do this by constructing the shade of trillions of small glass discs."

Why passive here?

Passive with "could be done" sounds more formal. The agent (you/we) is less important than the method itself.

When to Use Passive
1. Agent Unknown

"The polar ice caps are melting." (We don't need to say "humans are melting them")

2. Agent Obvious

"Forests are being cleared for agriculture." (We know who clears them)

3. Formal/Scientific Writing

"Carbon dioxide is produced by combustion." (More formal than "we produce...")

4. Change Focus

"This proposal was developed over 5 years." (Focus on process, not developers)

Opinion Language

Categorize these phrases. Click to reveal. Notice how C1 speakers hedge, justify, and respond.

Giving Your Opinion

"If you think about it..."
Function:

Invites listener to consider logic; introduces reasoning

"If you think about it, a sunshade would only work if it stays in position—one collision and it fails."
"I'm absolutely convinced that..."
Function:

Strong personal conviction; no doubt

"I'm absolutely convinced that we need to act now, not wait for perfect technology."
"I reckon that..."
Function:

Informal strong opinion; more personal than "I think"

"I reckon that artificial trees are a waste of money compared to protecting real forests."
"As far as I'm concerned..."
Function:

Personal stance; "from my perspective"

"As far as I'm concerned, any solution that doesn't reduce emissions is just delaying the problem."
"No matter how unlikely it seems..."
Function:

Concession + emphasis; "even though X sounds far-fetched"

"No matter how unlikely it seems, we might need radical solutions like this."
"After all..."
Function:

Justification; "don't forget..."

"We should invest in this proposal. After all, the cost of inaction is already billions per year."

Agreeing

"I couldn't agree more"
Complete agreement; emphatic
"The timeline is critical." — "I couldn't agree more. Every year counts."
"Absolutely!"
Quick, emphatic yes
"These proposals need serious funding." — "Absolutely! We're talking billions."
"Well, I suppose so."
Reluctant agreement; some hesitation
"Cloud ships might eventually work." — "Well, I suppose so, but only if we have 20 years."

Disagreeing

"I'm afraid I can't agree with you at all."
Polite but firm disagreement
"This technology is ready now." — "I'm afraid I can't agree with you at all. The data shows it's years away."
"There may be some truth in that, but..."
Acknowledge point, then counter
"The sunshade is low-cost." — "There may be some truth in that, but maintenance would be astronomical."
"You can't be serious!"
Incredulous disagreement
"We should wait another decade." — "You can't be serious! Every degree of warming multiplies the damage."

The Funding Panel

You are a panel of environmental experts. The government has €1 million to fund the top 3 climate proposals. Evaluate each. Choose your top 3. Use the timer—you have 10 minutes to decide and justify your choices.

The Proposals

1. Giant Sunshade (Space-Based)
Cost
€350M–500M (development)
Timeline
15–20 years to deployment

How it works: Trillions of tiny glass discs would be rocketed into space at the Sun-Earth point. They reflect sunlight, reducing Earth's temperature.

Pros: Addresses warming directly; doesn't require emissions cuts.

Cons: Untested technology; risks chain reactions; maintenance impossible; one collision destroys it; ethical issues (geoengineering).

2. Artificial Plankton Blooms (Ocean-Based)
Cost
€80M–120M (pilot phase)
Timeline
5–10 years to measurable effect

How it works: Iron particles are seeded into oceans to trigger plankton blooms, which absorb CO2 as they grow.

Pros: Scalable; relatively cheap; uses natural processes.

Cons: Unintended ecosystem effects unknown; algal blooms can cause dead zones; carbon sequestration not permanent.

3. Artificial Trees (Carbon Capture)
Cost
€200M–300M (mass production)
Timeline
2–5 years to deployment

How it works: Mechanical devices pull CO2 directly from the air using chemical solvents. Carbon is stored or reused.

Pros: Proven technology; removes emissions; flexible deployment.

Cons: High energy requirements (must be renewable); extremely expensive per ton of CO2; slow compared to emissions reductions.

4. Cloud Ships (Marine Brightening)
Cost
€150M–200M (fleet of prototypes)
Timeline
7–10 years to effectiveness

How it works: Autonomous ships spray seawater into clouds over oceans, making them brighter and reflective. This bounces more sunlight back to space.

Pros: Localized; reversible; technology partly tested; lower cost.

Cons: Unpredictable weather impacts; not permanent; could disrupt monsoons; still experimental at scale.

Evaluation Checklist

Use this to organize your thinking. Click items as you assess them.

Readiness: How mature is the technology? Can it be deployed in 5–10 years?
Cost-effectiveness: Does the budget justify the expected carbon reduction?
Reversibility: Can the impacts be undone if it fails?
Environmental risk: Could unintended side effects be worse than climate change?
Public acceptance: Would citizens and governments support it?
Speed: How quickly would it reduce warming?

Wider Discussion

Choose 2–3 questions that interest you. Speak naturally, pushing yourself to use new language.

1

Your Perspective

How do YOU feel about climate change? Is it real and urgent, or overblown? Has it affected your life directly?

2

The Silver Lining

Are there any positives coming from climate awareness? Green jobs, innovation, community action?

3

Government Action

What is YOUR country doing about climate change? Are you proud of it, frustrated, or want more/less action?

4

Worst Case?

Is climate change the worst thing humanity faces? What else might be equally or more important?

5

Climate Refugees

Climate change will force mass migration (island nations, drought zones, flooded coasts). How should countries respond?

6

Personal Action

What climate action can individuals take? Is it enough, or is systemic change the only real answer?
