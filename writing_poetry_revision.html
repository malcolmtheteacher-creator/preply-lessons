Warm-up — The First Draft Is Not the Poem

Beginners often think a poem is written in one sitting. Real poets know: the first draft is the raw material. The POEM is what you make from it.

Ernest Hemingway is supposed to have said the first draft of anything is garbage. He used a stronger word. The point holds: getting words down is one job; making them into a poem is a different job.

Why first drafts can't be the finished thing

  1. You can't hear it yet. When you're writing, you can't tell which lines land and which don't. You're too close.
  2. You haven't found the centre. You don't know what the poem is really about until you've finished the first draft. Often the real subject only emerges at the end.
  3. You used the easy word. The first word that came to mind was rarely the best one. Revision is the chance to find the right word.
  4. You said too much. First drafts always over-explain. Revision is mostly cutting.

The cold read principle

You can only revise a poem honestly if you read it as a STRANGER would. The way to do that: leave it. Put it away for at least a day. A week if you can. Then come back and read it cold. You will see immediately what works and what doesn't.

If you only ever read your poem the moment after you wrote it, you will only ever hear what you MEANT to write — not what you actually wrote.

The hardest revision skill — naming it

The American writer William Faulkner gave the advice: "In writing, you must kill all your darlings." A "darling" is a line, image or phrase you LOVE — but which is not actually serving the poem. Maybe it's clever but it doesn't fit. Maybe it's beautiful but it's not in the right poem.

The mark of a serious writer is the willingness to cut their best lines if those lines don't earn their place. Save the cut lines — they may belong in a different poem one day. But the poem you're working on now is not a museum for your favourite sentences.

Vocabulary — Eight Revision Tools

Draft
Tap for definition
A version of the poem. First draft, second draft, third draft. Each one is a SEPARATE document — never overwrite, always save the previous version.
A good poem might go through 5–15 drafts.
Revise
Tap for definition
Big-picture changes — cutting stanzas, reordering sections, changing the ending, finding the real subject of the poem. Structural.
"This poem starts in the wrong place" is a revision-level note.
Edit
Tap for definition
Small-picture changes — word choice, line breaks, punctuation. Done AFTER revision, never before.
Changing "very tired" to "exhausted" is an edit.
Cut
Tap for definition
Delete a line, a phrase, a stanza. The single most powerful revision tool. Most poems improve by losing 20%.
If a line doesn't add image, music, or meaning — cut it.
Kill your darlings
Tap for definition
Cut a line you love. The hardest revision skill. If a line is beautiful but doesn't serve THIS poem, it has to go. Save it for another poem.
"It's a great line, just not in this poem."
Compression
Tap for definition
Saying the same thing in fewer words. Almost always the answer to "this line is weak."
"It made me feel really lonely" → "I was alone."
Specificity
Tap for definition
Replacing a general word with a specific one. The single biggest upgrade you can make to any draft.
"flower" → "lavender". "day" → "Saturday in May".
Cold read
Tap for definition
Reading the poem after a deliberate gap (a day, a week, longer) so you can hear it as a stranger would. The only way to find what's actually on the page.
Put it away. Come back. You will know what to do.

Before & After — One Poem, Two Drafts

Here is the same poem in its first draft and its revised version. Read both carefully, then look at the annotations to see what was changed and why.

FIRST DRAFT 
The Garden After

After my father died, the garden was sad.
The flowers seemed to droop.
The grass needed cutting but nobody cut it.
The weeds came up everywhere.
The bench where he used to sit was empty.
It made me feel really lonely.

I went out there one day and looked around.
I thought about how much he loved gardening.
I picked up his old gardening fork.
It was rusty from sitting in the rain.
I thought I should do some weeding.
I did it for an hour.
It made me feel a bit better.
I think he would have been pleased.
REVISED 
The Garden After

After my father died, the lavender bowed
where his hands had stopped tending it.
The grass remembered him as a season,
not a man.

The bench was a held breath.

I went out one Saturday in May
and found his fork in the shed,
red with three winters of rain.
I held it. The handle was the wrong weight in my hand.

I weeded for an hour.
The blackbird watched.
The lavender did not stand up
but it looked at me for the first time.

What changed, and why

1. Specificity — replacing general nouns with specific ones
Before: "the garden was sad" / "the flowers seemed to droop"
After: "the lavender bowed"
One specific flower replaces two vague references. Lavender carries a fragrance the reader almost smells. "The flowers seemed to droop" is a generic sentence.
2. Cut — telling not showing
Before: "It made me feel really lonely."
After: [cut entirely]
The image of the bowed lavender already does this work. Adding "I felt lonely" tells the reader what they should already be feeling. CUT.
3. Compression — one image for many lines
Before: "The grass needed cutting but nobody cut it. / The weeds came up everywhere. / The bench where he used to sit was empty."
After: "The grass remembered him as a season, not a man. // The bench was a held breath."
Three flat descriptive lines replaced with two metaphors. The poem now says MORE in fewer words — and the metaphors carry feeling that the description didn't.
4. Specificity — when, exactly?
Before: "I went out there one day"
After: "I went out one Saturday in May"
"One day" is vague. "One Saturday in May" places the reader in a specific moment. Specificity is almost always free — it costs nothing and adds everything.
5. Better image — exactness
Before: "It was rusty from sitting in the rain"
After: "red with three winters of rain"
"Rusty" is what every garden fork is. "Red with three winters" tells you HOW LONG since anyone touched it — and turns rust into a colour you can see.
6. Kill your darling — cut the explanation
Before: "I think he would have been pleased."
After: [cut, replaced]
The original ending tells the reader what to feel. The new ending — "The lavender did not stand up / but it looked at me for the first time" — shows it. The reader feels the relief without being told to feel it.

The revised version is THREE LINES SHORTER than the first draft. But the second one is a poem. The first was a record of an experience.

Drill — Rewrite the Weak Line

Below are five lines from rough drafts. Each has a specific problem. Use the revision tool named to rewrite it.

1 — Use SPECIFICITY
"He had a really nice old car."
Replace the vague nouns and adjectives. What kind of car? What year? What colour? Pick details that suggest him as well as it.
One way to revise it

"He drove a sand-coloured Volvo that had outlasted two marriages." — specific car, specific colour, and a detail that suggests him as much as the car.

2 — CUT (telling, not showing)
"When she walked into the room I felt so happy and full of love."
Cut the words "happy" and "love". Replace with what you DO or SEE when she walks in. The reader fills in the feeling.
One way to revise it

"When she walked into the room I stood up before I knew I had stood up." — the action of standing up tells the reader everything.

3 — COMPRESS
"I waited at the bus stop for a very long time and it was raining and I started to feel really cold."
Cut this in half or better. Same information, fewer words. Drop "very", "really", and anything redundant.
One way to revise it

"I stood at the bus stop in the rain until my fingers stopped feeling." — three details (place, weather, sensation) in one line. The "long time" is implied by the cold hands.

4 — KILL YOUR DARLING
"The autumn leaves were like dancing acrobats in the wind, swirling and twirling with a beautiful golden grace."
This line is too pleased with itself. Strip it back to ONE clear image. Cut all the dressing.
One way to revise it

"The leaves came down sideways." — three words. The wind is in the word "sideways" — no need for swirling, twirling, or dancing.

5 — SPECIFICITY + COMPRESSION
"My grandmother used to make food for us and it always tasted very good."
Pick ONE specific dish, one specific sense. Cut everything else.
One way to revise it

"My grandmother made a stew that tasted of patience." — one specific dish, one unexpected description. The word "patience" suggests time AND her temperament at once.

Your Turn — Revise a Poem You've Written

Open your notebook (or this lesson series) and find a poem you've written in any of the previous lessons. If you haven't written one recently, write a quick four-stanza poem about anything (10 minutes) and then come back here.

Now apply the revision toolkit. The questions below take you through it in order.

Revision timer
20 minutes.
20:00

Paste your original poem here

Now answer these questions about your draft

1 — Cold read
Read it aloud as if a stranger wrote it. Which line do you LIKE the LEAST? Which line do you secretly already know is weak?
2 — Cut
Which ONE line could you remove and the poem would still work? Cut it. (You can put it back if the poem feels worse without it.)
3 — Specificity
Where in the poem have you used a general word (flower, day, person, place) when a specific one would land harder? Rewrite that line.
4 — Telling not showing
Where do you TELL the reader how to feel (sad, happy, lonely, angry)? Replace with a concrete action or image.
5 — Kill your darling
Which is your favourite line in the poem? Is it ACTUALLY doing work, or is it just clever? If it's only clever, mark it for cutting (you can save it for another poem).

Now write the revised version

Read both versions aloud. Which one is the poem?

Free Write — Draft and Revise in One Sitting

Final exercise of the series. You will write a NEW poem and then revise it — all within this session. This is unusual (revision normally needs a cold gap), but the practice teaches you to revise as a habit, not a special occasion.

Step 1 — Write
Pick any subject you haven't written about in this series. Write a free-verse poem of 4–6 stanzas. Don't think about quality. Just get words down. 15 minutes.
Write timer
15 minutes — write without stopping.
15:00
Step 2 — Revise
Now make yourself wait 60 seconds. Stand up, look out the window, drink water. Anything that gives you a tiny gap. Then come back and revise — using cut, compression, specificity, and the courage to kill your darlings. 10 minutes.

10 minutes.

The end of the series

You've now worked through:

  1. Foundations — what poetry is and the eight foundational tools.
  2. Sound — the music-making toolkit.
  3. Image & Metaphor — going deeper into how images do work.
  4. Forms — when structure helps and when it hurts.
  5. Slam & Spoken Word — writing for the stage.

That's a complete pass through the major tools of writing poetry. Six lessons does not make you a poet. It makes you somebody who has the equipment.

What makes you a poet is keeping going. Write the next poem. Read other poets. Revise. Read your work aloud. Then write the one after that. The skill compounds — every poem you finish makes the next one slightly easier.

You started this series unable to name a metaphor. You finish it knowing how to write one, how to extend one across a whole poem, how to read one aloud for an audience, and how to revise a draft until the poem comes out. That's not nothing.
