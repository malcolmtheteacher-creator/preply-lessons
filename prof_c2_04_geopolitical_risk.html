Categories of Geopolitical Risk

Understanding the distinct ways that global politics and conflict affect business.

1. Trade and Tariff Risk

Changes in tariff regimes, trade agreements, or protectionist policies can dramatically alter the economics of global supply chains. A 25% tariff on imports from a key supplier nation, or the collapse of a trade agreement, can reduce margins or force costly geographic reallocation.

Example: A US automotive manufacturer's reliance on Mexican suppliers became riskier during periods of trade treaty uncertainty.

2. Sanctions and Asset Freezes

Governments impose sanctions to punish geopolitical actors. Companies with operations in, or customers in, sanctioned regions face enforcement actions, frozen assets, and reputational damage. Compliance requires rigorous screening of customers and supply chains.

Example: Oil and financial services companies faced severe restrictions when new sanctions were imposed on certain regions.

3. Supply Chain Concentration Risk

Over-reliance on suppliers or customers in politically unstable regions creates vulnerability. Wars, coup d'états, natural disasters, or policy shifts can abruptly cut off critical inputs. Diversification across geographies is a defensive strategy.

4. Currency and Macroeconomic Volatility

Political crisis or conflict often triggers currency collapse, inflation, or capital flight. Companies with revenue in foreign currencies face margin compression. Central bank policies shift unpredictably during political turmoil.

5. Regulatory and Expropriation Risk

Some governments have nationalised or heavily regulated foreign-owned assets. Changes in government ideology, election outcomes, or policy can result in renegotiated contracts, higher taxes, or forced divestitures.

6. Reputational and ESG Risk

Operating in countries with poor human rights records or environmental practices creates reputational damage risk. Investors and customers increasingly screen companies for exposure to geopolitically sensitive or ethically problematic regions.

Reflection: How does a multinational company balance profitability in growing markets with geopolitical risk? Is the profit sufficient reward for the risk?

Assessing and Communicating Geopolitical Risk

How companies systematically identify, quantify, and manage exposure to geopolitical events.

A Framework for Risk Assessment

Step 1: Identify Exposure
Map all customer, supplier, and operating exposures by geography. Categorise by criticality: Are these essential inputs or ancillary? Can they be substituted?

Step 2: Score Country Risk
Assess political stability, governance quality, corruption levels, and track record of government respect for contracts. Use indices like the Fragile States Index or Worldwide Governance Indicators. Rate each region from low to critical risk.

Step 3: Model Financial Impact
For each critical exposure, quantify the downside: If supply was cut off for 90 days, what revenue loss would result? If currency depreciated 30%, what margin impact? Build stress scenarios.

Step 4: Develop Mitigation Strategies
Options include: geographic diversification, dual-sourcing, inventory buffers, hedging contracts, political risk insurance, or selective divestment. Each has trade-offs in cost and execution.

Language for Risk Reporting

Disclosing geopolitical exposure to the board:

"We have material revenue concentration in Region X, representing 23% of group EBITDA. This exposure is moderately elevated given recent political volatility. We are executing a two-year geographic diversification plan and have secured political risk insurance for our critical assets. We will report quarterly on progress."

Responding to shareholder concern about political risk:

"Geopolitical risk is inherent in global operations. Rather than retreat, we mitigate through diversification, scenario planning, and active risk management. Our resilience is demonstrated by our performance through recent geopolitical events."

Building Resilience Through Scenario Planning

Best-in-class companies develop three to five geopolitical scenarios (e.g., "renewed trade war," "regional conflict," "supply chain collapse") and stress-test business plans against each. This allows rapid decision-making if a scenario unfolds in reality.

Key insight: Companies that anticipate and plan for geopolitical shocks outperform those that are surprised. Resilience is a competitive advantage.

Case Study: Responding to Trade Sanctions

A global technology company faces unexpected sanctions on a major market.

The Scenario

Your company manufactures semiconductor components. Region X represents 18% of annual revenue. New trade sanctions prohibit sales of advanced semiconductors to companies in Region X, effective immediately.

  • Immediate impact: £85 million in contracted revenue is now legally prohibited.
  • Supply chain: 12% of your semiconductor inputs come from suppliers in Region X; alternative sourcing will add 6% to cost and delay by 4 months.
  • Customer concentration: Five major customers in Region X; they account for 60% of regional revenue and cannot be easily replaced.
  • Stock impact: Share price has fallen 8% on the news; analysts downgrade the stock, citing "geopolitical headwind."
10:00

Your Task: Crisis Response Strategy (10 minutes)

You are presenting to the board's crisis committee. Prepare a structured response covering:

1. Immediate Actions (Days 1-7)

What legal, operational, and communication steps will you take immediately? How will you ensure full compliance with sanctions while protecting employees and operations?

2. Medium-Term Mitigation (Months 1-6)

How will you replace the lost revenue? Accelerate sales in other regions? Reallocate supply capacity? What supply chain adjustments are feasible and cost-effective?

3. Financial Impact and Guidance

Model the FY impact on revenue and EBITDA. Should you withdraw or downgrade guidance? How will you maintain investor confidence and prevent further share price decline?

Follow-up: Six months later, sanctions are partially eased for humanitarian goods. Should you re-enter Region X, or is the relationship damaged? What does this teach you about long-term geopolitical resilience?
