The CRO's dilemma

You're Chief Risk Officer. Geopolitical tensions are rising. Your company has significant operations in three countries that are now experiencing political instability. The board is asking you to present a geopolitical risk assessment. One board member thinks you're being alarmist. Another says the company should exit the markets immediately. You need to present risk with precision and clarity, not panic or complacency.

Initial Assessment Take 2 minutes. You're the CRO. Lay out the geopolitical risks the company faces — political instability, sanctions risk, supply chain exposure, currency risk. Don't minimize. Don't catastrophize. Speak like a risk professional.
2:00
Scenario Planning Now take 2 minutes. Walk through three scenarios: base case (current trajectory), stress case (tensions escalate), and tail risk (sudden rupture). What's the company's exposure in each? What can we control? What can't we?
2:00

Why this matters

Geopolitical risk is inherently uncertain. As CRO, you need to speak with precision about uncertainty itself. You can't predict geopolitical events. But you can articulate scenarios, quantify exposures, and recommend mitigation. Your language signals to the board that you understand the limits of prediction while taking risk seriously.

Six geopolitical moves

1 Risk taxonomy (political, regulatory, operational) +
Geopolitical risk manifests across three categories. Political risk is regime change or civil instability. Regulatory risk is sanctions, capital controls, or nationalization. Operational risk is supply chain disruption or talent departure. Distinguish them. They require different responses.
How it sounds

"We face three distinct geopolitical risks in Country X. Political risk from potential regime change. Regulatory risk from potential sanctions escalation. Operational risk from supply chain vulnerability. Each requires different mitigation."

2 Scenario planning language +
Scenarios are tools for thinking about uncertainty, not predictions. Use language that signals this — "if/then," "in the event of," "assuming." Never claim to predict geopolitical events. Claim to assess exposures in scenarios.
The move

"In our base case, current trajectory continues. In our stress case, sanctions are escalated. In our tail risk case, regime change occurs suddenly. We've modeled EBITDA impact in each scenario."

3 Sanctions and compliance framing +
Sanctions risk is regulatory risk. You're not making a geopolitical judgment — you're assessing exposure to government action. Use language that separates your political view from your risk assessment. Compliance with sanctions is non-negotiable.
The move

"Regardless of the geopolitical backdrop, our sanctions compliance is absolute. We've assessed which transactions could trigger OFAC or EU sanctions exposure. Our legal team has determined we can mitigate that risk through restructuring supply contracts."

4 Communicating uncertainty with precision +
Uncertainty doesn't mean vagueness. It means clarity about what you know and don't know. Quantify exposures even when you can't predict triggers. This is precision in the face of uncertainty.
The move

"We cannot predict whether geopolitical escalation will occur. We can quantify our exposure: 12% of EBITDA, 23% of supply chain. This is our exposure to geopolitical events we cannot forecast."

5 The risk appetite conversation +
Risk appetite is ultimately a board decision, not a CRO decision. You're supposed to articulate risk, not decide what's acceptable. Frame geopolitical risk conversations as enabling the board to make an informed choice about appetite.
The move

"The company's appetite for geopolitical risk in Country X is ultimately a strategic choice. We assess our exposure at 150 million euros of EBITDA at risk in stress scenarios. The question for the board is: are you comfortable accepting that risk in exchange for the current returns?"

6 Country exit language +
If the board decides to exit, use language that frames it as a risk management decision, not a failure or panic. Market exits are legitimate strategic responses to geopolitical risk. Make them sound prudent.
The move

"We recommend a structured exit from Country X over the next 18 months. This is driven by our risk assessment, not by current headlines. A planned, orderly exit protects shareholder value better than being forced to exit in crisis."

High-stakes scenario

You're presenting the geopolitical risk assessment to the board. You've laid out the three scenarios and quantified exposure. Now the questions come.

Board member A says: "You're overblowing this. Geopolitical risks are overblown all the time. We've been in that market for 20 years. I think the risks are overstated."

Board member B says: "I disagree. This is serious. I think we should exit immediately. We shouldn't be taking this kind of tail risk."

The CEO looks uncomfortable. She likes the cash flows from that market. You need to ground both board members in your actual analysis, without taking a personal position.

Preparation: 2 minutes

2:00

Think about how you'll respond to both. You're not here to advocate for a position. You're here to help the board make an informed decision. How do you do that?

Your response: 3 minutes

3:00

Speak now. You're the CRO. You need to validate both perspectives while keeping the focus on actual exposures and options. Navigate this tension.

Scaffold: How to structure this

Acknowledge (20s)

Real Both concerns have merit. Historical stability doesn't guarantee future stability. Risk is genuine.

Evidence (60s)

Data Point to specific exposures, scenarios, quantified impacts. This is what we can control.

Options (40s)

Action What can we do? Hedging? Diversification? Structured exit? Maintain current exposure?

Five geopolitical pressure moments

You've presented your assessment. Now you face escalating pressure from different directions. Respond to each.

The CEO pulls you aside: "Your risk assessment is making investors nervous. The stock dropped 3% after your presentation. Can you soften your language for the next board meeting?" She's not asking you to lie. Just to be less alarming.
1:30

How do you handle this? Your job is risk assessment, not communications management. But your language does matter.

A sanctions lawyer tells you: "Based on my analysis, one of our key suppliers has 60% ownership by a sanctioned entity, concealed through shell companies. We're in violation. What do we do?" This is a legal risk, not just a geopolitical risk.
1:30

Respond. This is no longer theoretical. You're dealing with a potential compliance violation. What do you do? Who do you tell?

The board decides to stay in the market, accepting the geopolitical risk. Six months later, a political event occurs that you'd modeled in your stress scenario. The stock drops 15%. A shareholder sues, claiming the board ignored risk warnings. What's your position?
1:30

Respond. You're being pulled into litigation. You need to protect the company and yourself. What do you say?

The CFO says: "We're hedging the currency risk, but we can't hedge the geopolitical tail risk cost-effectively. So we're just accepting it. But I want to make sure the board is making an informed choice. Can you quantify the expected value of that tail risk?"
1:30

Respond. This is a genuinely hard question. You can't predict geopolitical events. How do you quantify risk when predictability is low?

A year in: The political situation in Country X has stabilized. The market is profitable. The board member who pushed for immediate exit says to you: "I was wrong. You were right to present it as manageable risk." The other one says: "See? We handled this well." You're wondering if your assessment was correct or just lucky.
2:00

Reflect. How do you know if your risk assessment was good if nothing bad happened? How do you learn from geopolitical forecasts that didn't materialize?
