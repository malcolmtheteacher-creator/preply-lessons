The Untouchables

A small team of honest police officers tries to stop the most powerful criminal in America. The team was real — but the real story is very different.

The film, in three parts

Brian De Palma's The Untouchables (1987). Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one. (There are spoilers — that is fine, we want to talk about it.) Some people die in this story. They were real people, so we will talk about it carefully.

Part 1 — One man owns the city

Chicago, 1930. In America at this time, alcohol is illegal. But people still want it, so criminals sell it, and they become very rich. The richest and most famous is Al Capone. He is a gang boss. Everybody knows about his crimes, but nobody can stop him, because he pays a lot of police officers and politicians.

At the beginning of the film, one of Capone's bombs kills a little girl in a small shop. Nobody goes to court for this. Then a young government agent arrives: Eliot Ness. He wants to stop Capone. His first police action is a disaster — his men break open a building in front of the newspapers, and inside there is nothing.

Ness meets an old police officer, Jim Malone. Malone is poor and honest: he never takes bad money, so he never gets a better job. Malone helps Ness choose two more men: George Stone, a young police student who shoots very well, and Oscar Wallace, a quiet accountant with glasses. The newspapers give the four men a name: "the Untouchables", because nobody can buy them. And Wallace has an important idea: Capone never pays his taxes.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is Al Capone so rich and so difficult to stop?

2. What is the accountant's idea?

1. Alcohol is illegal, so people buy it from his gang and he makes a lot of money. He pays many police officers and politicians, so nobody stops him.

2. Capone does not pay his taxes. So they can take him to court for money, not for murder — because no witness wants to talk about the murders.

Talk about it:
  • Malone is honest, so he stays poor and never gets a better job. Is it always easy to be honest at work? What do you think?

Part 2 — The team pays a high price

The four men start to win. They stop a big lorry of illegal whisky near the border with Canada. They also catch one of Capone's accountants — a man who understands all the money. To make him talk, Malone does something very ugly and frightening. It works, but now the "good men" are not really clean any more. Ness watches, and says nothing.

Capone's gang answers with violence. First, Oscar Wallace, the quiet accountant, is shot and killed in a lift with his witness. Then a man comes to Malone's home at night and kills him too. Before he dies, Malone tells Ness one last thing: the other accountant is leaving the city tonight, on a train.

So Ness and Stone wait at the big train station at midnight. A young mother is pulling a baby's pram up the long stone steps. Ness helps her — and then the shooting starts. The pram falls and goes down the steps, step by step, very slowly, while the two men shoot and try to catch it. They save the baby. And they take the accountant alive, because he can be their witness in court.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which two men in the team are killed?

2. Why do Ness and Stone go to the train station?

1. Oscar Wallace, the accountant, and Jim Malone, the old police officer.

2. To catch Capone's accountant before he leaves the city. He is the witness who can explain the money in court.

Talk about it:
  • Malone says: to win against a bad man, you must break the rules too. Do you agree? Is that ever right for the police?

Part 3 — In court

Capone goes to court, but he is not worried, because he has paid the people who decide — the jury. Then Ness finds Capone's top man, Frank Nitti, in the court building with a gun. Nitti also has the address of Malone's home in his pocket. Ness runs after him, up to the roof, and throws him off it. Nitti dies. The film shows this as a good moment for the hero.

Then Ness shows the judge some evidence: the judge's own name is in Capone's money book. The judge is frightened, and he changes the whole jury for a different one. Now Capone cannot win. His lawyer says "guilty" without asking him, and Capone becomes very angry in front of everybody. The court says he is guilty of not paying his taxes, and he must go to prison for eleven years.

At the end, a journalist tells Ness some news: soon alcohol will be legal again. So all this — the police work, the deaths — was for a law that is going to disappear. The journalist asks Ness what he will do then. Ness gives a short, tired answer about having a drink, and he goes home.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Ness stop Capone from winning in court?

2. What news does the journalist give Ness at the end?

1. He shows the judge that the judge's name is in Capone's money book, so the judge changes the jury. Capone had paid the first jury.

2. That alcohol will soon be legal again — so the law that Ness was fighting for is going to end.

Talk about it:
  • Ness throws a man off a roof, and the film says this is good. Do you think that is a problem? Why, or why not?

Grammar: must & have to

This film is all about rules: who follows them, who breaks them, and who does not care about them. English has two easy ways to talk about things that are necessary.

must + verb = the speaker feels it is very important, or it is a written rule: "A police officer must tell the truth in court." "You must show your ticket."
have to / has to + verb = another person, a job or a law makes it necessary: "Police officers have to obey the law." "A journalist has to check the facts." Past: had to.
Careful — these two are very different:
• mustn't = it is not allowed. "You mustn't sell alcohol in 1930." (It is illegal.)
• don't have to / doesn't have to = it is not necessary. "The agents don't have to wear a uniform." (They can if they want.)

1. must, mustn't, have to or don't have to?

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence.

1. Malone says to the team: "We stay together. It is the most important thing." (the speaker feels it is important)

2. Agents write a report after every action, because that is the rule of their job. (a rule from outside)

3. You take money from criminals. It is never allowed. (not allowed)

4. Capone is very rich, so he work. (not necessary)

1. must (the speaker feels it is important)

2. have to (a rule from their job)

3. mustn't (it is not allowed)

4. doesn't have to (it is not necessary)

2. have to, has to or had to?

Choose the correct form. Look at the person and the time. Then read each sentence out loud.

1. Ness choose his own men, because the city police are not honest.

2. The four agents work at night, when nobody can see them.

3. Last year, Malone work alone, and nobody helped him.

4. Capone pay a lot of money to his lawyers every month.

1. has to (he = one person, now)

2. have to (they = more than one, now)

3. had to (last year = past)

4. has to (he = one person, now)

The Real Story — is any of this true?

Eliot Ness was a real person. The Untouchables were a real team. But the most famous fact about them is not true: they did not catch Al Capone.

⚖️ The team was real — but they did not win the case

Ness was a young government agent. He read the files of many agents and chose about ten men who had never taken bad money. When Capone's gang tried to buy them, the men said no, and a Chicago journalist called them "the untouchables". From March 1931 they broke into Capone's beer factories. In about six months they destroyed machines worth about $500,000, and Capone lost about $2 million. That is real. But it is not a court case, and it did not put him in prison.

The men who really caught Capone worked in an office

The real work was done by tax men. Frank J. Wilson and his boss Elmer L. Irey worked for the tax office. From 1930, their team read more than two million papers and talked to hundreds of people. They found an old money book with an entry about money paid "for Al", and they found the accountant who could explain it in court. On 17 October 1931 the court said Capone was guilty of not paying his taxes. A week later, on 24 October, the judge sent him to prison for eleven years. Eliot Ness was not part of this case.

Big parts of the film never happened

Jim Malone is not a real person. The writer invented him. There was no shooting at the train station — no baby, no pram, no steps. And Frank Nitti did not fall from the roof. He lived for twelve more years. On 19 March 1943, one day before he had to go to court about a different crime, he walked to a railway yard and shot himself. The jury was changed in the real trial — but the judge did it, not Ness. Also, this was not a game: between 1919 and 1933 there were 729 gang killings in Capone's part of Chicago. In one of them, on 14 February 1929, seven men were shot dead in a garage. Capone was never taken to court for it.

And Ness? He lost an election, had money problems, and died of a heart attack on 16 May 1957, at only 54. A few months after he died, his book The Untouchables was published. It sold about 1.5 million copies, then there was a TV programme, and then this film. So Ness became famous for something he did not do — and he never knew about it.

Talk about it:
  • Before this lesson, who did you think caught Al Capone?
  • The film gives the credit to the wrong man. Is that a big problem, or is it "just a film"?
  • Frank Wilson read two million papers. Eliot Ness broke down doors. Why do films always choose the second man?
