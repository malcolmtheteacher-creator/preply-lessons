The Cask of Amontillado

Edgar Allan Poe (1846) — a polite, friendly man who is hiding a cold plan

Original version available: read this lesson at B2/C1 level →

Read the story in three parts

This is a famous old story, told here in simple English. Read each part, then stop and talk about the questions. The man who tells the story is friendly and polite — but watch carefully, because he has a secret plan.

Part 1 — The angry man

Montresor was angry with a man called Fortunato. In the past, Fortunato was unkind to him many times. Montresor said nothing, but inside he was very angry. He wanted to pay Fortunato back.

It was carnival time. There were parties in every street. People wore colourful clothes and laughed and danced. Everyone was happy.

That night, Montresor met Fortunato. Fortunato had a red hat and bells, and he had had a lot of wine. He was happy and a little loud.

Montresor knew one important thing: Fortunato loved good wine very much. So Montresor had a plan.

Stop and talk:
  • Why is Montresor angry with Fortunato?
  • What time of year is it? What are people doing?
  • What does Fortunato love very much?

Part 2 — Down into the cellar

Montresor smiled and spoke in a friendly way. "My friend! I have a special bottle of rare wine. It is a cask of Amontillado. But I am not sure it is real. Can you come and tell me?"

Fortunato wanted to see the wine. "Yes! Take me to it now," he said.

So Fortunato followed Montresor down, down into the cold cellar under the house. It was deep and dark. The walls were old. They walked a long way.

Fortunato coughed in the cold air. "You are not well," said Montresor kindly. "We can go back." But Fortunato did not want to go back. He wanted the wine. "No, no. Let's go on," he said.

Montresor was very polite and friendly the whole time. He gave Fortunato more wine to drink as they walked.

Stop and talk:
  • What does Montresor say to bring Fortunato into the cellar?
  • What is the cellar like? (Use words like cold, dark, deep.)
  • Montresor offers to go back. Why do you think he says this?

Part 3 — The wall

At the end of the cellar there was a small space in the wall. "The Amontillado is in here," said Montresor. Fortunato walked in to look.

Then, quickly, Montresor trapped him there. He took bricks and began to build a wall across the front of the small space. Brick by brick, the wall grew higher.

Fortunato could not get out. At first he thought it was a joke. Then he understood. But it was too late. Montresor was calm. He did not stop.

Finally, Montresor put in the last brick. The wall was finished. Then he turned and walked away.

Montresor kept his secret for fifty years. The friendly, polite voice in this story was hiding a cold plan all the time.

The end — stop and talk:
  • What does Montresor build across the front of the small space?
  • Montresor was friendly and polite all night. Was he really friendly? What was he really doing?
  • Read Part 1 again. Were there any clues that Montresor had a plan?

Past simple and sequence words

This story tells about the past. We use the past simple to tell what happened. We use sequence words to show the order of events.

Past simple = a finished action in the past. Montresor smiled. Fortunato followed. They walked.
Sequence words show order: first, then, next, after that, finally. We often put them at the start of a sentence: First, he smiled. Then, they walked down. Finally, he built the wall.

How the writer surprises you

A good story is more than just words. The writer uses tricks to make you feel something. Here are some simple ones from this story.

1. The cold surprise

Montresor is friendly and polite all night. He smiles, he gives Fortunato wine, he says "We can go back." We almost like him. Then, at the end, he traps Fortunato and builds a wall. The surprise is cold and quiet — and that makes it stronger.

2. The friendly voice hides a plan (irony)

Montresor says kind words, but he is doing a terrible thing. His friendly words and his real plan are opposites. When words say one thing but mean another, we call it irony. The "nice" voice is the most frightening part.

3. The man tells his own secret

Montresor is the person telling the story. He is telling us about his own bad plan, fifty years later. We see everything through his calm eyes. This makes us feel close to him — even though he did a terrible thing.
