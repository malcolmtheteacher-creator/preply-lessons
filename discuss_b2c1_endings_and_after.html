Hook

Three findings that contradict the story we usually tell

Films, songs and novels agree on one thing: when something big ends, you are broken for a long time, and then — usually because of a montage — you are fine. Psychologists who actually followed people through endings found something different.

MOST
Across studies of people facing a major loss, the most common pattern was resilience — real distress, then a return to normal functioning — not long-term breakdown.
Bonanno, American Psychologist, 2004
WRONG
We are systematically wrong about how long bad news will hurt. Psychologists call this the impact bias.
Gilbert & Wilson, affective forecasting research
SOONER
People tracked through a real break-up recovered sooner than they themselves had predicted — the prediction was wrong, not the pain.
Eastwick, Finkel, Krishnamurti & Loewenstein, JESP, 2008

🎙 Which of the three is hardest to believe? Say why.

🎙 If all three are true, why do you think our films and songs almost never show it that way?

Looking Back at It

Here are two people describing the same event — a business that closed. Read both. Don't scroll past them yet.

Version A

"The shop closed in 2019. I was very sad. Then I found a new job and now I am OK. It was a bad time but now it is finished."

Version B

"The shop closed in 2019. At the time it felt like the end of everything. Looking back, I can see that I'd been unhappy there for two years. It wasn't until much later that I understood the closure did something I'd never have done myself."

🎙 Before you look: what exactly is Version B doing that Version A isn't? Name two things. reveal ▾

1. It separates two viewpoints. Version A has one voice. Version B has two: the person then ("at the time it felt like…") and the person now ("looking back, I can see that…"). That gap between the two is where a listener hears maturity.

2. It reports a change of understanding, not just a change of mood. "Now I am OK" reports feelings. "I understood the closure did something I'd never have done myself" reports a new interpretation of the same facts. C1 speakers do the second.

The distance phrases

Four ways to stand at a distance from something that happened to you. Notice that each one signals to the listener: I have already thought about this.

At the time, it felt like… → the view from inside the event

Looking back, I can see that… → the view from outside it

With the benefit of hindsight, … → slightly more formal; often introduces a judgement

It wasn't until much later that I realised… → marks a delay between the event and the understanding

That last one is a cleft structure. The normal sentence is "I realised much later." The cleft version pushes "much later" into the spotlight. English does this when the timing is the interesting part.

🎙 Take a decision you made at least three years ago — a small one is fine, a course, a purchase, a place you moved to. Describe it twice: once as the person you were then, once as the person you are now. Use at least two of the four phrases.

Sharpen it

Which is stronger, and why?

Two sentences about a friendship that faded:

(i) "With the benefit of hindsight, we'd stopped having anything to say to each other long before either of us admitted it."

(ii) "With the benefit of hindsight, it was sad."

🎙 Both are grammatically correct. One of them wastes the phrase. Which, and what is the rule?

Reveal the rule after you've said your version out loud reveal ▾

(ii) wastes it. "It was sad" was equally true at the time — so hindsight adds nothing.

The rule: a hindsight phrase must be followed by something you could only know later. If the statement was just as true on the day, drop the phrase.

Bit by Bit

Endings almost never end on a date. They end in a process. English has a rich set of tools for slow, unglamorous change — and most learners at B2 have only "slowly".

I do — watch the model

A model, annotated

Someone describing the year after a redundancy:

"It took a while before I could talk about it without getting angry. Then, bit by bit, I stopped rehearsing the argument in my head. I found myself applying for things I'd never have considered before, and before I knew it I'd built something that didn't look anything like the old job."

Four moves, in a deliberate order: it took a while before (a long, resistant phase) → bit by bit (incremental change) → I found myself + -ing (change the speaker didn't plan) → before I knew it (the moment they look up and notice how far they've come).

Why "I found myself applying" and not "I decided to apply"? Because "I found myself" removes the intention. It says: the change happened to me before I chose it. That's an extremely useful C1 structure and almost no B2 learner uses it.

We do — build it together

Rank the speed

Six expressions, all describing change over time. Put them in order from slowest to fastest. Two or three of them are genuinely arguable — those are the interesting ones.

  • before I knew it
  • it took years before
  • within weeks
  • little by little
  • overnight
  • gradually

🎙 Say your order out loud and defend the two you were least sure about.

One defensible ordering — argue with it if you disagree reveal ▾

it took years before → little by little → gradually → within weeks → before I knew it → overnight

The contested pair is little by little vs gradually. They are close, but "little by little" emphasises the steps (and often the effort), while "gradually" emphasises the curve and is more neutral. That's why "little by little, I stopped" sounds more personal than "gradually, I stopped".

Before I knew it is the trap: it doesn't actually mean fast. It means the speaker wasn't paying attention. Real change of six months can still be "before I knew it".

What the Research Actually Found

Four paths, not one

George Bonanno spent years following people through major losses and disruptions. Instead of one universal path, he found several distinct trajectories:

Resilience — a short period of genuine disruption, then a return to normal functioning. The largest group in most of his studies.

Recovery — a longer dip, months of real difficulty, then a return.

Chronic — sustained distress that doesn't lift on its own.

Delayed — apparently fine at first, difficulty arriving later.

His argument that caused the most trouble: because the resilient group looked "fine" quickly, researchers had long assumed they were in denial. The follow-up data didn't support that. They were, mostly, just fine.

🎙 Why do you think a resilient response gets read as coldness, or denial, rather than health?

The prediction problem

Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson's work on affective forecasting found a consistent error: we are decent at predicting which events will upset us, and bad at predicting how long the effect will last. We routinely overshoot.

Gilbert's explanation is what he calls the psychological immune system — a set of unconscious processes that reframe, rationalise and absorb bad events. We can't see it working from the inside, so we leave it out of our forecasts.

"We are consistently surprised by how well we cope — because the machinery that helps us cope is invisible to us while we are predicting."
— a summary of Gilbert & Wilson's affective forecasting argument

🎙 If this is true, it has an awkward consequence: our own predictions about our own future feelings are unreliable evidence. Does that change how much weight you'd give them?

The contested bit

Does hardship actually make people grow?

Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun named the idea of post-traumatic growth: that people can come out of a hard chapter reporting deeper relationships, clearer priorities, more strength.

Supporters argue the reports are consistent across cultures and too widespread to dismiss. Critics counter that almost all the evidence is retrospective — people are asked afterwards how much they've changed, which measures the story they now tell, not the change itself. Studies that measured people before and after have found much weaker growth than the retrospective ones.

🎙 Which position do you find more convincing, and what evidence would change your mind?

Cruel or kind?

Recall & Reflect

Recall Zone — from earlier work

Nothing from today. Nothing to look up. Say each one out loud, then check.

1. What's the difference between "I used to work there" and "I would work there on Saturdays"? Which one can describe a state? check ▾

used to works for both states and repeated actions — "I used to live in Berlin" (state), "I used to swim every morning" (action).

would only works for repeated actions. "I would live in Berlin" is wrong for a past state. That's the whole distinction.

2. Give three ways to soften a strong opinion so you sound careful rather than uncertain. check ▾

Any three of: I'd say it depends on… · I'm not entirely convinced that… · It's hard to say, but… · I wouldn't go so far as to say… · There's an argument that… · To some extent, yes, but…

The tell is what follows: hedging plus a reason sounds careful; hedging plus nothing sounds evasive.

3. Turn this into a third conditional: "I didn't apply, so I didn't get the job." check ▾

"If I had applied, I would have got the job."

Both halves are unreal past: had + past participle in the if-clause, would have + past participle in the other.

4. What's the difference between "I stopped to talk to her" and "I stopped talking to her"? check ▾

stopped to talk = you stopped doing something else in order to talk. The talking started.

stopped talking = the talking ended.

Same verb, opposite outcomes, decided entirely by infinitive vs -ing.
