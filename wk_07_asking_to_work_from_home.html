Asking to Work From Home

You want two days a week at home. Nobody has said no — nobody has said anything. Which means this comes down entirely to how well you build the case.

The Scene 🎬

Nothing has been announced. There was no email, no new policy, no meeting. And yet somehow the office is fuller than it was six months ago — a few more desks taken on a Wednesday, one manager who's suddenly always in, a comment about "energy in the building". The drift back to five days a week is happening the way most things at work happen: quietly, without anyone deciding it out loud.

You have twenty minutes with your manager on Thursday. You want two fixed days at home — Tuesday and Thursday — and you're going to ask for them. Here's the thing you already half know: you will not win this with how tired the commute makes you, or how much you'd like your mornings back. Those things are true. They're also entirely your problem, and your manager cannot take your problem upstairs and defend it.

So the whole lesson lives in one question: whose problem is this? An argument built on your life asks for a favour. An argument built on the work — with the objection already answered before anyone raises it, and a small, safe way to test it — hands your manager something she can say yes to and still look sensible on Monday. That's the difference between wanting something and building a case for it.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of something you once asked for at work — time, money, a change, a tool. Did you frame it around what you needed, or around what it would do for the work? What happened?
  2. Some things at work are never announced; they just start being true. Has that happened where you work — a rule nobody wrote down but everybody follows? How do you argue with something that was never said out loud?

Who's who

You
The Case-Builder 📋

You're good at your job and you know exactly when you do your best work. Today isn't about how much you'd like this. It's about proving it serves the work — and making it easy for someone else to agree with you.

The other voice
Rhea, your manager 🪑

Fair, straight, and genuinely on your side. But she doesn't decide in a vacuum: whatever she agrees to, she has to justify — to her own boss, and to the four people who'll hear about it by Friday.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't just follow the argument — ask, at every single line, who is this argument for? And watch for the moment where one of them raises Rhea's objection before Rhea gets the chance. That move is the whole lesson.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Notice how little of it is about wanting, and how much of it is about the work, the team, and Rhea's own position.

RheaRight — twenty minutes, you said. What's on your mind?
YouThere's something I'd like to put to you — nothing dramatic. And just so we start in the right place: I want to talk about how I work best, not about doing less.
RheaAlright. Go on.
YouWhat I'd like to propose is two fixed days at home — Tuesday and Thursday.
RheaAh. Right. You've noticed which way the wind's blowing at the moment, I assume.
YouI have, which is exactly why I've come with evidence rather than a feeling. Over the last quarter, almost all the deep work has happened on the days I've been at home — the spec rewrite, the migration plan, both client reports. The office days are the days things get answered. They're not the days things get written.
RheaThat's… uncomfortably accurate. But you can see my problem. If I say yes to you, I'm having this same conversation with four other people by Friday afternoon. And it starts to look like I'm quietly running my own policy down here.
YouI know the worry is the team, so let me get ahead of that. Monday and Friday stay office days precisely because that's when we plan and hand over. I'd be in for every stand-up, every handover, every planning session. And it's two fixed days, not two protected days — if something lands on a Tuesday, I'm in. Nothing the team needs from me moves.
RheaMm. My real difficulty is upstairs, though. I'd have to defend this to Dev, and Dev's the one nudging everybody back to five.
YouThen let's make it small enough to defend. Could we trial it for a month? Then you're not defending a principle, you're pointing at four weeks of actual output. And if it slips, we drop it. No argument.
RheaI like that a great deal more than I expected to. But honestly? Not this month. Dev's got the headcount review running and I'm not opening a second front while that's live.
YouThat's fair — what would need to be true for it to work?
RheaThe review closed. And me being able to point at something that isn't just "they asked nicely and I liked them".
YouCan I put a short note together and we look at it properly next time? One page — what I'd do on those days, what the team keeps, and how we'd both know if it wasn't working.
RheaYes. Do that. And keep it to one page — if it's three, I'll read it on the train and I'll hate it.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Rhea says no. But she says no in a way that leaves the door open — she names two conditions that would have to change first. What were they?

2. Monday and Friday stay office days. What reason is given for that — and, more importantly, who is that argument for? Whose problem does it solve?

3. Having just been told no, the last thing You asks for is not the two days. What is it — and what does asking for that achieve that arguing again wouldn't?

1. The headcount review has to be closed — and Rhea has to be able to point at something more solid than "they asked nicely and I liked them". Notice both conditions are about her position, not about you. A no like that is really "not yet, and here's the shape of a yes".
2. The reason given is that Monday and Friday are when the team plans and hands over. But the argument isn't for You at all — it's for Rhea. It answers the "what about the team?" objection before she has to say it out loud, so she never has to be the one raising a problem. That's why she ends up agreeing rather than defending.
3. A one-page note, and a proper look at it next time. It doesn't ask Rhea to change today's answer, so there's nothing for her to refuse — it just moves the conversation to a day when the answer could be different, and hands her the very thing she said she was missing: something to point at.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that turn a want into a case. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. This has to land as a proposal — not a plea, not an ultimatum. Two things give you away, and both are in the voice, not the words. Let your pitch rise at the end of a sentence and a case turns into begging: you've made every line a question, and you're asking permission to have had the thought at all. Go too flat and hard, and the same words become a threat — something you've decided and are now informing her of. Aim for the middle: steady, unhurried, ending each sentence down — the tone of someone who has thought this through and is genuinely interested in what the other person makes of it. Then stop talking. The silence after a good proposal is doing your work for you; don't rush in and fill it.

🚪 Opening it

Phrase 1

You've got twenty minutes with Rhea. Your first sentence sets the temperature for everything after it. Which opener flags "this is a proposal worth hearing" — without making her brace for bad news, and without apologising for existing?

A. "I need to talk to you about something. It's quite important."
B. "There's something I'd like to put to you — nothing dramatic."
C. "I don't know if you'll go for this, but I wanted to ask about working from home."
B — "There's something I'd like to put to you — nothing dramatic."
To put something to someone means to offer an idea for them to consider and respond to — it's what you do with a proposal, not a complaint. It quietly says: I've thought about this, and your view matters. The dash and "nothing dramatic" then take the fear out of the room — when a manager hears "I need to talk to you", the first thing they think is are you resigning?, and you've lost thirty seconds of their attention to that. A is heavy and vague; C hands her the "no" before you've made the case.
Phrase 2

She's braced for "I want to be in the office less". You want to redraw the subject before she's finished framing it — this is about the conditions you do good work in, not about quantity. Complete the chunk: "I want to talk about how I ___ ___, not about ___ ___."

"I want to talk about how I work best, not about doing less."
The "not about…" half is the working part. You name her suspicion out loud and set it aside before she can settle into it — and you can't argue with someone who has already agreed with you. "How I work best" also plants the frame you'll use for the rest of the conversation: this is a conversation about output, and every later line will refer back to it.

📊 The ask, and the evidence

Phrase 3

Time to say the actual thing — clearly, with no hedging and no wriggle room. Vagueness here reads as embarrassment, and it invites her to define the ask for you. Complete the chunk: "What I'd like to ___ is two ___ days at home — Tuesday and Thursday."

"What I'd like to propose is two fixed days at home — Tuesday and Thursday."
Two words carry it. Propose puts this in the register of business — you propose an idea, you beg for a favour, and the verb alone tells her which of the two is happening. Fixed is doing even more: it means the same two days every week, so the team can plan around you and nobody is ever wondering where you are. "Two days a week, flexible" sounds easier to grant but is far harder to defend — and naming the actual days makes it real and checkable rather than a wish.
Phrase 4

Now your evidence — and remember, Rhea has to repeat this argument to someone else, without you in the room. Every one of these is true. Which one can she actually use?

A. "I get so much more done at home, honestly — it's a completely different day."
B. "The commute is nearly two hours and I'm exhausted before I've started."
C. "Over the last quarter, almost all the deep work has happened on the days I've been at home."
C — "Over the last quarter, almost all the deep work has happened on the days I've been at home."
Ask of each one: can Rhea say this to her boss? B is real and it's honest, but it's your problem — she cannot walk upstairs and defend a decision on the grounds that your journey is long. A is your opinion, and everyone's opinion of their own productivity is generous. C is the only one that has a time frame ("over the last quarter"), a measure ("the deep work"), and a claim about the work rather than about you — so it's the only one that survives being repeated by somebody else. Then name the pieces: the reports, the plan, the rewrite. Specifics are what turn a claim into evidence.

🛡️ Pre-empting the objection

Phrase 5

You know precisely what she's about to say: what about the team? You have about one sentence before she says it. Which line takes the objection off her — rather than leaving it sitting there for her to pick up?

A. "I hope that wouldn't be a problem for the team."
B. "If the team has an issue with it, we can talk about it then."
C. "I know the worry is the team, so let me get ahead of that."
C — "I know the worry is the team, so let me get ahead of that."
To get ahead of something is to deal with a problem before it reaches you — you go and meet it rather than waiting for it to arrive. Say it, and the objection stops being hers and becomes something you're both looking at together. Never make your manager be the one who raises the problem: the moment she says "but what about the team?" she owns that position and has to defend it, and now you're on opposite sides of a table. A is a hope, not an answer. B pushes the hard part into the future, which is exactly where she can't afford to leave it.
Phrase 6

Now answer it. The office days aren't what's left over — they're chosen, and chosen for the team's sake. One word does the heavy lifting: it insists the choice is deliberate. Put it in order:

we plan and hand overstay office daysMonday and Fridaythat's whenprecisely because
"Monday and Friday stay office days precisely because that's when we plan and hand over."
Precisely because is the word that turns leftovers into a design. Without it you have two days at home and two days not at home; with it, the office days exist for a reason you can name — and the reason is the team, not you. "Stay" matters too: nothing is being taken away, the week keeps its shape. You've now answered the objection so completely that agreeing costs her nothing.

🧪 Making it a trial, not a demand

Phrase 7

She's not against it — she just can't sign off on something permanent that she'd have to argue for upstairs. So shrink what you're asking her to agree to. Complete the chunk: "Could we ___ it for a month?"

"Could we trial it for a month?"
Trial as a verb means to test something for a set period before deciding properly. It changes what she's saying yes to: not a new arrangement, just a look at one. That's a far smaller thing to defend — "we're testing it for four weeks" is almost impossible to object to, because objecting means arguing against finding out. And a month from now you're not asking again; you're discussing results.
Phrase 8

One fear remains: that once you have this, taking it back would be a horrible conversation she'll have to have. Kill that fear. Which line actually reassures her?

A. "I'm sure it'll be fine."
B. "It won't slip — you can trust me on that."
C. "If it slips, we drop it. No argument."
C — "If it slips, we drop it. No argument."
If work slips, it starts arriving late or gets worse — deadlines slide, quality drops. You're naming the exact failure she's imagining, and handing her the exit in advance: "no argument" promises she won't have to fight you to end it. That costs you almost nothing and removes the last real reason to say no. A and B are promises about the future, and promises are precisely what she can't take upstairs — she needs a way out, not your confidence.

🚧 Handling a no

Phrase 9

She says no — a fair no, for reasons that have nothing to do with you. Arguing now burns her goodwill; accepting it politely ends the whole thing. There's a question that does neither: it accepts today's answer while asking her to describe the conditions for a different one. Put it in order:

trueneed to befor it to work?that's fair —what would
"That's fair — what would need to be true for it to work?"
This is the most useful sentence in the lesson. "That's fair" is the sound of you not fighting, so she can relax. Then the question quietly moves her: instead of defending her no, she's now describing the conditions for a yes — and the moment she starts listing them, she's building your case for you, out loud, in her own words. A dead conversation becomes a to-do list. Note the shape: not "why not?" (which attacks) but what would need to be true (which invites her to design it).
Phrase 10

She's told you what's missing. Don't push the ask again — instead, ask for the smallest possible next step: something you'll write, and a moment to look at it together. In your own words, say it out loud now — what exactly do you ask for?

"Can I put a short note together and we look at it properly next time?"
Nothing here is refusable: you're not asking her to change today's answer, only to read one page later. And it hands her the thing she said she lacked — something to point at. Note "we look at it properly" — not "I'll send it", which she can leave unread, but a shared moment already in the diary. The word short is not modesty either; a one-page note gets read, a five-page case gets postponed forever, and a postponed case is a no with better manners.

The Twist

🎯 You won. Congratulations.

Any you couldn't tick? That's the one to run again — and it's almost certainly the one that decides the real conversation.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Strip it back and this was never an argument about geography. It's an argument about trust — and the awkward fact that trust is hard to see, while a person at a desk is extremely easy to see.

So we measure the visible thing. Not because attendance is what we want — nobody has ever wanted attendance — but because it's the thing that can be counted without effort, and the counted thing quietly becomes the real thing. Ask what an office is genuinely for and the honest answers are small and specific: the handover, the argument that needed a whiteboard, the new person learning by overhearing. None of those take five days.

Which is why the case you built today works. It didn't ask anyone to trust you more. It gave them something to look at instead.

Two bigger questions

  1. We measure work by attendance largely because attendance is easy to measure. What else in your working life gets judged by whatever is easiest to count rather than by what actually matters — and who benefits from it staying that way?
  2. If the good things about an office are real but occasional — the handover, the accident, the new person overhearing an argument — then who should decide which days you're there: you, your manager, or the work itself? And what would go wrong with each answer?
