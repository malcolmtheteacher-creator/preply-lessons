Check your idea CLICK
"Sisters" just follows the normal rule: sister + s. But "children" and "men" change completely — child → children, man → men. English has a small group of these irregular plurals, and they're some of the most common words you'll ever use.
Today: plural nouns for people and things (one → many), subject pronouns — I, you, he, she, it, we, they — and object pronouns — me, him, her, it, us, them — so you can talk about the people and things in your life without repeating names.

One or Many? Plural Nouns

For people and things — when there's more than one, most nouns change in a regular way. A few don't.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "I have two brothers."
  • "She has three boxes in her room."
  • "My feet are cold!"

Before you check: what's different about "feet" compared to "brothers" and "boxes"?

Check the pattern CLICK
"Brothers" just adds -s. "Boxes" adds -es, because "box" ends in -x (words ending in -s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x, -o usually need -es). But "feet" isn't built from "foot" with an ending at all — the whole word changes. That's an irregular plural.
most nouns: + -s → brother → brothers
after -s, -ss, -sh, -ch, -x, -o: + -es → box → boxes, watch → watches
irregular (no rule — just learn them): child → children, man → men, woman → women, person → people, foot → feet
Plurals are everywhere in daily life: "Two coffees, please." "Three tickets to the museum, please."

Who Does It? Subject Pronouns

Instead of repeating a name again and again, use a short word for the person or thing doing the action.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences, said twice:

  • "My brother works in London." → "He works in London."
  • "My parents live near the beach." → "They live near the beach."
  • "This phone is new." → "It's very fast."

Look at the bold words. What job do they do in the sentence?

Check the pattern CLICK
Each bold word replaces the naming words before it ("my brother", "my parents", "this phone") and sits right before the verb, doing the action. These are subject pronouns: I, you, he, she, it, we, they.
I / you / he / she / it / we / they + verb

From two names to one word

"My sister and I are twins."
→
"We are twins."

Once it's clear who you mean, English almost always shortens it to a subject pronoun.

Who Gets It? Object Pronouns

Now the short word comes after the verb — for the person or thing that receives the action, not the one doing it.

Spot the pattern

  • "My brothers live in Rome. I call them every week."
  • "This cake looks amazing. Can I try it?"
  • "The teacher gave us homework."

Where do the bold words sit — before the verb, like in the last tab, or after it? What are they doing?

Check the pattern CLICK
They come after the verb (or after a word like "for", "to", "with") — receiving the action instead of doing it. These are object pronouns: me, you, him, her, it, us, them.
verb (+ for / to / with) + me / you / him / her / it / us / them

From subject to object

"I saw my teacher this morning."
→
"I saw her this morning."

"My teacher" is female, so once it's clear who you mean, it becomes "her" — never "she" after the verb.
