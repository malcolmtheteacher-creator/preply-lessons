Insisting & Requiring: "We insist that she be informed"

Formal and written English — official statements, memos, contracts.

Spot the pattern

Three formal statements:

  • "The board insists that the CEO resign immediately."
  • "It is essential that she be informed before the announcement goes out."
  • "The contract requires that the tenant not sublet the property without written permission."

Look at the bold verbs next to "the CEO", "she" and "the tenant". What would you normally expect there instead — and what's happened to the negative in the third one? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
You'd expect "resigns", "is" and "does not sublet" — but the -s disappears, and the negative loses "does" and becomes simply "not sublet". After verbs of insisting, recommending or requiring (and phrases like it's essential/vital/important), the pattern is that + subject + BASE FORM, whatever the subject is. Negative = subject + not + base form.

Honest note: in everyday British speech, people usually just say "insists that the CEO should resign", "that she should be informed", "that the tenant shouldn't sublet" — completely natural and very common. The bare subjunctive is more typically American; in British English it survives mainly in formal writing, legal and contract language.
insist / recommend / require / demand / suggest / propose + that + subject + BASE FORM
it is essential / vital / important + that + subject + (not) + BASE FORM
everyday alternative: ...that + subject + should + BASE FORM
Everyday: "The landlord says the new tenant should pay a deposit."
→
Formal: "The landlord requires that the new tenant pay a deposit."

As It Were: The Were-Subjunctive

For hypothetical, unreal situations — and one fixed idiom that never changes.

Spot the pattern

  • "If I were you, I'd get a second quote before agreeing to anything."
  • "He's become the unofficial mentor of the team, as it were, guiding everyone through their first year."
  • "Were it not for a generous grant, the museum would have closed years ago."

Look at "were" in the first and third sentences — what's unusual about it, given the subjects "I" and "it"? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
In both, "were" is used instead of "was" — even with I/he/she/it. This is the were-subjunctive: for something hypothetical, unreal, or for effect, English keeps "were" whatever the subject. "As it were" is a fixed phrase meaning roughly "in a manner of speaking" — it never changes form. "Were it not for X" is a formal, inverted way of saying "if it were not for X" — meaning "without X".

Honest note: in relaxed everyday speech you'll very often hear "If I was you..." — extremely common, and most people won't blink at it. But "were" is still the form that careful and written English expects, and the one exams and formal writing reward.
if + subject + WERE ... (all persons: I were, he were, it were)
as it were = so to speak (fixed, no change)
were it not for + noun = if it were not for + noun (= without)

Should You, Had We, Were This: Formal Inversions

Emails, contracts and polished speech — conditionals with no "if" at all.

Spot the pattern

  • "Should you require any further information, please do not hesitate to contact us."
  • "Had we known about the delay, we would have booked a later train."
  • "Were this policy to change, employees would be notified in writing."

Each sentence is a conditional with no "if" at all. What's moved to the front instead? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
The auxiliary — should, had, were — jumps in front of the subject and does the job "if" normally does. "Should you require..." = "If you should require..."; "Had we known..." = "If we had known..."; "Were this policy to change..." = "If this policy were to change...". It's a formal, slightly old-fashioned-sounding pattern you meet constantly in emails, contracts and polished speech — never in casual chat.

Careful with the Had-inversion: if the base sentence already has "had" as its main verb (not just as an auxiliary), that "had" doesn't disappear — only the fronted auxiliary is new. "If I had had all the facts, I would have decided differently" inverts to "Had I had all the facts, I would have decided differently" — both "had"s stay. "Had I the facts" sounds like something from a costume drama, not modern standard English.
Should + subject + base form, ... (= If + subject + should + base form)
Had + subject + past participle, ... (= If + subject + had + past participle)
Were + subject + (to) + base form, ... (= If + subject + were (to) + base form)

One more formal alternative worth knowing: but for + noun phrase replaces a whole "if it weren't for..." clause. "But for the fire alarm test, the office would have been silent all afternoon." = "If it hadn't been for the fire alarm test, ..."
