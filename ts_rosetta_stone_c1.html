The Man Who Made the Stones Speak

For fourteen centuries, not one person on earth could read ancient Egypt's writing. Then soldiers rebuilding a fort unearthed a broken black stone — and two of Europe's finest minds raced to crack history's greatest code.

Today: you'll follow the greatest detective story in the history of scholarship — and you'll master the full grammar of deduction: this must be a royal name; it can't have been pure symbolism; he would have known the sound from Coptic.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — A stone in a fort wall

Begin with the silence. The last dated hieroglyphic inscription was carved at the temple of Philae in 394 AD; within a few generations the knowledge of reading them was extinct, and Egypt spent fourteen hundred years surrounded by its own unreadable autobiography. Europe filled the vacuum with confident nonsense: on the authority of a late Greek writer, the signs were held to be mystical symbols — an eye meaning providence, a goose meaning son — and generations of scholars "translated" temple walls into philosophical poetry that the walls simply did not say. The theory's grip is worth respecting: it was ancient, elegant, well-sourced — and wrong from its foundations.

The key surfaced through war. In July 1799, soldiers of Napoleon's Egyptian expedition, demolishing a ruined wall to extend a fort near the delta town of Rashid — Rosetta, to Europeans — pulled out a broken slab of dark stone, densely carved in three distinct bands. The officer on site, Pierre-François Bouchard, had the wit to see what he was holding: the lowest band was ancient Greek, which any classicist could read; above it ran two Egyptian scripts — the everyday cursive called demotic, and hieroglyphs proper. If the three bands carried the same text, the unreadable had just been shackled to the readable. The Greek confirmed it: a priestly decree of 196 BC honouring the boy-king Ptolemy V — tax relief, temple honours, administrative gratitude — with instructions that it be published in all three scripts. Bureaucracy, by pure luck, had built a bilingual key and buried it in a wall.

Then the key changed pockets. Britain defeated the French expedition, and under the surrender terms of 1801 the Stone passed — over the bitter protests of France's scholars, who reportedly threatened to burn their research rather than yield it — into British hands. By 1802 it stood in the British Museum, where it stands today, its side still bearing the painted boast: captured in Egypt by the British Army. But ink had already done what armies could not contain — copies and rubbings of the inscription circulated across Europe. The race to read it would be run on paper, between an Englishman and a Frenchman, and their two nations have quarrelled about the result ever since.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the symbolic theory of hieroglyphs so durable — and what was it worth?

2. What exactly made the Stone the perfect key, and how did it reach London?

1. It rested on an ancient, elegant, apparently authoritative Greek source — and produced fourteen centuries of confident mistranslation. Well-sourced is not the same as true.

2. One decree of 196 BC carved in three scripts — readable Greek shackled to unreadable Egyptian: a built-in parallel text. Found by French soldiers in 1799, surrendered to Britain in 1801, in the British Museum since 1802 — though copies had already spread the text across Europe.

Part 2 — The duel

The English champion was Thomas Young — physician, physicist, the man who demonstrated the wave nature of light, a prodigy who read at two and handled a dozen languages; contemporaries called him the last man who knew everything. Attacking the Stone in the 1810s as one interest among many, he made genuine advances: he showed the demotic script mixed sign-types, matched several of its words to the Greek, and — crucially — argued that the cartouches, the oval rings in the hieroglyphic band, must contain the royal name Ptolemy, spelled out phonetically, sign for sound. He extracted several correct sound-values. And there, at the threshold, he stopped: like nearly everyone, Young held that phonetic spelling was a special concession for foreign names like Ptolemy's — the system as a whole, he assumed, remained symbolic. The old theory, even cracked, kept its grip.

The French champion had been preparing his whole life without knowing for what. Jean-François Champollion, a tradesman's son from Figeac, was consuming Hebrew, Arabic, Syriac and Persian as a schoolboy; at roughly eleven, shown hieroglyphs and told no one could read them, he is said to have announced that he would. The legend may be polished; the preparation is documented — above all his mastery, rare in Europe, of Coptic, the language of Egypt's Christians, which he correctly judged to be the direct descendant of the pharaohs' own speech. Where Young brought a magnificent general intelligence, Champollion brought the one specific weapon the lock actually required: he alone could guess what the words sounded like.

The breakthrough came on 14 September 1822, and it arrived through fresh evidence: copies of cartouches from Abu Simbel — a temple far older than the Greek-ruled era, meaning any name inside must be native Egyptian, where the "foreign names only" theory forbade phonetic spelling. One cartouche ended in two signs Champollion knew spelled s; it began with a circle — the sun. In Coptic, the sun is Ra. Ra…s…s — the middle sign, known from other contexts, gave m: Ra-m-s-s. Ramses — a great pharaoh's name, spelled by sound, deep in native antiquity. The forbidden conclusion followed instantly: the phonetic principle wasn't the exception; it ran through the entire system, woven together with the symbols. Champollion ran through the streets to his brother's office at the Institute, flung his papers on the desk, cried "Je tiens l'affaire!" — I've got it! — and collapsed, spending, the family story insists, the better part of five days in bed. Within the fortnight he presented his famous Lettre à M. Dacier; within two years, a full grammar of the system: part sound, part symbol, wholly readable. The silence was over.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Young genuinely achieve, and precisely where did he stop?

2. Reconstruct the Ramses deduction, step by step.

1. Real demotic progress, the cartouche-equals-royal-name insight, and correct sound-values for Ptolemy — but he kept the assumption that phonetic spelling was only for foreign names, leaving the system itself "symbolic". The old theory survived its own crack.

2. Abu Simbel predates Greek rule, so its cartouche must hold a native name; the final signs read s-s; the opening sun-disc, via Coptic, must sound as Ra; the middle sign gives m → Ra-m-s-s → Ramses. Part 3 — Three thousand years, returned

What the key opened exceeds easy description. Within a generation, Egypt stopped being a landscape of mute spectacle and became a documented civilisation with the longest continuous written record on earth: king-lists and tax receipts, medical papyri and court cases, love songs, schoolboys' copybooks with teachers' corrections, a worker's excuse note for missing work — brewing beer — three thousand years of human dailiness handed back across the silence. Champollion himself finally reached Egypt in 1828, walked the temples reading walls no one had read in millennia, and reported, with the satisfaction of a man completing a childhood vow, that the system held everywhere he tested it. He died four years later, aged forty-one, worn through; his brother published the grammar posthumously. Vindication was total and partly unwitnessed — the fate this series keeps meeting.

The quarrels, being human, outlived the discovery. British accounts long championed Young's priority; French ones crowned Champollion alone; the sober verdict — Young opened the door a crack, Champollion understood there was a door and walked through the building — satisfies neither nation entirely, which is how you know it is roughly fair. And the Stone itself became the emblem of a larger argument: Egypt has formally requested its return, and the British Museum's showpiece now stands at the centre of the world's repatriation debate — alongside the Parthenon marbles and the Benin bronzes. The museum's case: universal institutions preserve and display for all humanity, and the Stone's story is genuinely entangled with Europe's scholarship. Cairo's case: it is an Egyptian object, taken as war booty between two foreign empires on Egyptian soil, and a nation's right to its own record does not expire. Both cases are serious; you will be making them shortly.

For the traveller, the story leaves a permanent gift and a discipline. The gift: every readable label beside every artefact in every Egyptian gallery on earth descends from one September morning in 1822 — the past does not speak; it is made to speak, by preparation meeting luck. The discipline is the method itself, portable to any mystery: collect what is known, treasure the parallel text, respect the clue that doesn't fit, guess boldly and test ruthlessly — and remember the fourteen centuries lost to a theory that was elegant, authoritative and wrong. The stones are still speaking; the question the story hands you is simply which silence, in your own world, deserves a Champollion.

Grammar — deduction, present & past

Decipherment is deduction under discipline, and English grades certainty with surgical precision — about the present, and about the past. This is the grammar Champollion's notebooks were effectively written in.

Deduction about the present:
• must be — the evidence allows one conclusion: "Three scripts, one decree — the bands must be the same text."
• may / might / could be — open possibilities: "This sign might be a sound, or could be a symbol."
• can't be — ruled out: "It can't be an alphabet — there are far too many signs."
• will / would be — confident assumption, no checking needed: "That will be the Greek at the bottom." · "You would be the colleague Dacier mentioned."
Deduction about the past — add have + participle:
• "The knowledge must have died out within a few generations."
• "Young can't have realised what the Abu Simbel copies implied."
• "A native scribe would have found our struggles hilarious."
• And the softer pair: "The boy's vow may well have been polished by retelling."
Remember the polarity rule from this series: the opposite of must have is can't have — never mustn't have. And bound to upgrades certainty about outcomes: "With Coptic in hand, he was bound to get there first."

Going deeper

A note on the record: the childhood vow and the five-day collapse come from family memoir and have surely been polished; the priority dispute is genuinely contested territory where national pride still leans on the scales; the decipherment itself, and its 1822 date, are as solid as history gets.

What the story actually teaches. First, the anatomy of a wrong consensus. The symbolic theory persisted not through stupidity but through pedigree: an ancient source, elegant readings, and no method of disproof — every mistranslation was unfalsifiable poetry. What broke it was not a better opinion but a constraint: the parallel text made wrongness visible for the first time. The general lesson travels well beyond Egyptology: fields stagnate where error carries no cost, and the fastest way to progress is rarely a new theory — it is a new way of being checkably wrong.

Second, prepared luck, this series' oldest theme in its purest form. The Stone was luck; Bouchard's recognition, Young's cartouche insight and Champollion's Coptic were three layers of preparation queuing to meet it. Note the precise shape of the final advantage: not intelligence — Young had a surplus — but the one deeply specific competence the problem secretly required. Worth asking of any hard problem you care about: what is its Coptic, and who is quietly learning it?

Third, the ownership question the key cannot answer. Decipherment gave Egypt's voice back to the world — through institutions that simultaneously carried Egypt's objects away. Both facts are true; neither cancels the other; and the repatriation debate now runs through every great museum a traveller will ever visit. The mature position is not a slogan on either side but a discipline: know the provenance of what you're admiring, hear both cases, and accept that "who owns the past?" is a question each generation must answer again — preferably before, not after, it buys the ticket.
