The Last Lap

In 1908 a small Italian runner entered the Olympic stadium in first place. He did not finish the way he started — and the world has never forgotten it.

Today: you'll follow a true race story and tell stories of your own with confident narrative tenses, so you can describe any event — a race, a match, a hard day — clearly and with feeling.
Before we start — talk these through:

  • Why do people choose to do things that are hard on purpose — races, climbs, long challenges? What do they get from it?
  • Think of a sporting moment you remember vividly. Where were you, and what happened?

Dorando Pietri and the 1908 Olympic Marathon

Read each part. Answer the quick check from memory, then discuss.

Part 1 — A very hot afternoon

On 24 July 1908, the Olympic Games were being held in London, and the marathon was about to start. The route began at Windsor Castle and finished inside the new stadium at White City, so that the royal family could watch the beginning and the crowd could watch the end. The distance was about 26 miles and 385 yards — roughly 42 kilometres. The usual explanation is that this odd measurement came from the exact length of that route, and it was later adopted as the standard marathon distance.

More than fifty runners set off in heat that was severe for the time. Many of them started too quickly. As the afternoon went on, they began to fall away, one after another, and ambulances collected the ones who had stopped at the side of the road.

Among the runners was Dorando Pietri, a twenty-two-year-old from the small Italian town of Carpi, who worked in a sweet shop. He was short and light, and few people had heard of him. Instead of racing at the front, he had run at a careful pace for most of the way. While the leaders were fading, he was quietly moving up through the field.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did the race start and where did it finish, and why was that route chosen?

2. What happened to many of the runners as the afternoon went on?

3. How did Dorando run for most of the race, and what was he doing while the leaders faded?

1. It started at Windsor Castle and finished in the stadium at White City, so that the royal family could see the start and the crowd the finish.

2. They had started too fast in the heat, so they slowed down, fell away and some had to be taken off the road by ambulance.

3. He ran at a careful, steady pace and was moving up through the field while the leaders were getting weaker.

Discuss:
  • Which is the better plan for a long race — a fast start or a careful pace? What do you do in other situations, like exams or long projects?

Part 2 — The stadium

By the time the leaders were approaching the stadium, Dorando had overtaken most of them and had taken the lead. He entered the arena at White City in first place, to the cheers of about 75,000 people. But he looked terrible. He had been running for nearly three hours, and he had clearly hit the wall.

Then he turned the wrong way. Officials ran to him and pointed him in the right direction. A few steps later his legs gave way and he collapsed on the track. He got up, staggered forward, and fell again. Most accounts say he fell five times in the last few hundred metres.

Doctors and officials crowded round him. The spectators were shouting. And at some point, while Dorando was lying on the track, someone took his arm and lifted him to his feet. Supported on both sides, he staggered towards the line. He crossed it in 2 hours 54 minutes and 46 seconds, with the stadium roaring.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the situation when Dorando entered the stadium?

2. What mistake did he make, and how did the officials respond?

3. How did he get across the finishing line?

1. He was in first place and 75,000 people were cheering, but he was completely exhausted — he had hit the wall.

2. He turned the wrong way; officials ran to him and turned him round to face the right direction.

3. Officials and doctors helped him up and supported him on both sides, so he staggered across the line with their help.

Discuss:
  • You are one of the officials standing next to him. Do you help him? What would you do in that moment, and why?

Part 3 — The decision

The crowd had been watching a man win the Olympic marathon. But almost at once, the American team lodged a protest. Their runner, Johnny Hayes, had come in about half a minute later, and they argued that Dorando had been helped to the finish. The rules said that he should have run the race without assistance.

The judges agreed. Dorando was disqualified, and the gold medal went to Hayes. Dorando was taken to the medical room.

That might have been the end of the story. Instead, the public had already decided who the real hero was. The newspapers wrote about him for days — the writer Arthur Conan Doyle, the creator of Sherlock Holmes, reported on the race and described the final lap in detail. The next day Queen Alexandra presented Dorando with a special gilded silver cup. A song, “Dorando”, was written about him. Later he made a living by running exhibition races in several countries.

Hayes kept his gold medal, and few people argue that the decision was legally wrong. And yet more than a century later, it is Dorando's name that most people remember.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the American team protest?

2. What was the judges' decision, and who received the gold medal?

3. What happened to Dorando afterwards, even though he had been disqualified?

1. They argued that Dorando had been helped over the finishing line, which the rules did not allow.

2. The judges upheld the protest: Dorando was disqualified and the gold medal went to Johnny Hayes.

3. He became famous: newspapers praised him, the Queen gave him a silver cup, a song was written about him, and he ran exhibition races for money.

Discuss:
  • The judges applied the rules correctly, yet most people remember Dorando, not Hayes. Why? What does that say about what we admire?

Grammar — Narrative Tenses

A good story keeps different times in order — what was happening in the background, what happened next, and what had already happened. Four tenses do that job. Look at how the story above used them.

1 · Past simple — the main events, in order. “Dorando entered the stadium. He turned the wrong way. Officials pointed him in the right direction.” Each verb moves the story forward.
2 · Past continuous — the background, or an action in progress. “The spectators were shouting while the doctors were helping him.” Use it for the scene, or for a longer action that something else interrupts: “While he was lying on the track, someone lifted him up.”
3 · Past perfect — what had already happened before the main events. “He had started carefully, so he still had some energy.” “The leaders had run too fast.” It takes us one step back in time.
4 · Past perfect continuous — the long action leading up to a moment. “He had been running for nearly three hours when he entered the stadium.” It stresses how long something went on, and often its effect.
5 · Choosing between them. Ask: Is this a step forward in the story (past simple)? Is it the scene or an action in progress (past continuous)? Did it happen before the story's moment (past perfect)? Did it go on for a long time before it (past perfect continuous)?
6 · The traps. When he had entered the stadium, the crowd cheered — if the events happen one after the other, use past simple for both: “When he entered the stadium, the crowd cheered.”  He was falling five times → “He fell five times” (a completed series of events, not a scene in progress).

Going deeper — the race, the rules and the numbers

The story of Dorando is about more than one man's bad luck. It raises questions about fairness, effort and what we count as winning — and it also explains something about the distance runners train for today.

Where do 42 km and 21 km come from?

The marathon distance was not fixed until after 1908. After the London Games it was gradually standardised at 42.195 km — 26 miles 385 yards — which is the length of that Windsor-to-stadium route. A half marathon is simply half of it: 21.0975 km, or 13.1 miles.

Today a half marathon is one of the most popular distances in the world. It is long enough to demand real training, but short enough that many ordinary runners can complete it within a few months of preparation.

1. What is the exact length of a half marathon in kilometres?

2. Which event in the story is the usual explanation for the marathon's odd length?

3. Say the sentence in the past perfect: “They ran too fast, and then they became tired.”

1. 21.0975 km (about 13.1 miles).

2. The 1908 London Olympics: the route from Windsor Castle to the stadium.

3. They had run too fast before they became tired.

Was it fair?

People have argued about the 1908 decision for over a century. Both sides have a real case.

The case for disqualifying himThe rule was clear: no assistance. If officials are allowed to help the leader but not the others, the race is no longer equal. Johnny Hayes had run the whole distance on his own legs.
The case for keeping his victoryHe was the first to arrive. The officials were trying to save him from harm, and he had not asked for help. Punishing him for being rescued seems harsh.
