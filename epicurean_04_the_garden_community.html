Today: We're diving deeper into how Epicurus's community actually functioned — the real people who lived there, what their days looked like, how friendship held it together, and how it compared to rival philosophical schools.

A Living Philosophy

The Garden wasn't a school in the modern sense — no formal curriculum, no lectures to crowds. It was a way of life. People lived together, shared meals, discussed philosophy during and after eating, and supported each other in pursuing tranquility and simple living.

This lesson reveals the real people who made up this community and how their daily practices embodied Epicurean philosophy.

Key Members of the Garden

We know very little about most Epicureans because they chose to live quietly, away from public life. But some names survived. Here are the most significant figures:

Metrodorus of Lampsacus (d. 307 BCE)
Epicurus's closest friend and successor

Metrodorus was the intellectual heart of the Garden alongside Epicurus. He joined Epicurus in Lampsacus before the move to Athens. Sources suggest he was Epicurus's closest confidant — they spent decades together. When Epicurus died, Metrodorus was expected to lead the community, but he had died just a few years earlier.

What we know: Metrodorus wrote prolifically (though his works are lost). He was mentioned as equal in authority to Epicurus. The two friends exemplified the Epicurean ideal of deep, lifelong friendship based on philosophical agreement.

Hermarchus of Mitylene (c. 325-250 BCE)
Successor to Epicurus; led the Garden after his death

Hermarchus was likely younger than Epicurus and represented a bridge to the next generation. He became Epicurus's chosen successor and headed the Garden for decades after Epicurus's death (270 BCE), maintaining Epicureanism through the Hellenistic period.

What we know: Hermarchus came from a poor background (his family was impoverished), which meant he had everything to gain from joining a community devoted to simple living and friendship. He authored works on rhetoric and ethics. Unlike Epicurus, Hermarchus was willing to engage politically when necessary — showing that Epicureanism was flexible enough to accommodate different personalities.

Polyaenus of Lampsacus
Early member; wealthy benefactor

Polyaenus was another figure from Epicurus's early teaching days. He was a man of wealth who gave it up to join the Garden — embodying the Epicurean principle that wealth itself brings no true pleasure if it comes with anxiety or desire.

What we know: Ancient sources mention him as one of the school's pillars. His choice to abandon wealth for simple living and philosophy made him an early proof of the Epicurean ideal: a rich man who found more peace in a garden than in estates.

Before you read about Leontion: Why might it have been revolutionary for a woman to join a philosophical school in ancient Greece? What barriers did women face?

The barrier: In ancient Greece, respectable women were confined to the home. Women were the property of fathers, then husbands. Educated women who appeared in public were assumed to be courtesans (hetaerae). Plato's Academy excluded women entirely. A woman doing philosophy publicly was a scandal.

Leontion (dates unknown, flourished c. 300 BCE)
Philosopher; likely head of the women's section

Leontion is extraordinary: the only woman Epicurean whose name survived antiquity, she was a full philosophical member of the Garden — not a servant, not a wife, but a thinker in her own right.

What we know: Leontion openly criticized the physicist Theophrastus (Aristotle's successor) on scientific grounds — an act of public intellectual engagement virtually unthinkable for a woman of her time. She likely led teachings for women in the Garden (ancient sources suggest the community had separate spaces for different groups). Her very existence in this role proves that Epicureanism's inclusiveness wasn't theoretical — it was practiced.

⭐
The radical fact: A woman philosopher in 300 BCE who published work, criticized male colleagues, and lived as an intellectual equal. In Plato's Academy or Aristotle's Lyceum, this would have been impossible. In the Garden, it was normal.
Mys (dates unknown)
Enslaved person; philosophical member of the Garden

Mys is mentioned in ancient sources as a member of the Garden — a person formerly enslaved (or born enslaved) who became part of the philosophical community. This is almost without precedent in ancient philosophy.

What we know: Very little is recorded, but the bare fact of inclusion is remarkable. In a society where enslaved people had no legal rights, Epicureanism offered a radical idea: the capacity for philosophy and friendship is not determined by legal status. If Epicureanism is the pursuit of a calm mind and good friendships, these are equally available to all humans regardless of wealth or birth.

🔗
What this meant: Mys ate at the same table as Epicurus. Participated in the same discussions. Was called a friend, not a servant. In a slaveholding society, this was not just philosophically radical — it was practically revolutionary.
Your reflection: Notice who gets remembered: a few men, one woman, one formerly enslaved person. What does this tell us about historical record-keeping? What voices are lost?

Lost voices: Most Epicureans — likely women, enslaved people, and working-class members — were never named in surviving texts. The fact that Leontion and Mys are remembered at all is accidental: they appear in a few sources, often mentioned dismissively.

What this means: The Garden was probably MORE radical and MORE inclusive than surviving records suggest. Epicureanism may have appealed precisely to those excluded from other philosophy schools. But we only know this from silence — from the very fact that women and enslaved people could disappear from history while being part of the community.

A Day in the Garden

🌅

A Simple Routine

Philosophy was practice, not study. The Garden's rhythm centered on communal meals, conversation, and the care of the garden itself.

The Epicurean Day

  • Morning: Tending the garden (literal gardening — vegetables, herbs). Physical work as practice.
  • Midday: Shared meal of bread, vegetables, water. Sometimes cheese or wine.
  • Afternoon: Study and discussion of texts. Reading Epicurus's letters. Memorizing key doctrines.
  • Evening: Communal supper and philosophical conversation. This was the heart of daily life.

Philosophy Through Conversation

Dinner in the Garden wasn't entertainment — it was education and spiritual practice combined. People sat together, discussed philosophy, asked questions, challenged interpretations. There's a famous anecdote that Epicurus taught through conversation while eating with friends, correcting misunderstandings, deepening understanding.

Compare this to Plato's Academy or Aristotle's Lyceum, where the teacher lectured and students listened. The Garden's model was peer learning — everyone contributed, everyone learned from everyone else.

When we gather, let us not speak of what brings indulgence but of what brings peace. For as we eat simple food, so let our conversation be simple and true.

— Paraphrased from Epicurean practice (not a direct quote, but the spirit of community meal practice)

Marriage and Family in the Garden

A common misconception: Epicureanism was anti-marriage or anti-family. The truth is more nuanced.

Some Epicureans married (within the community or with outsiders). Some didn't. Some had children who were raised in the Garden. Metrodorus had a wife, Batis, who is mentioned in sources as part of the community. The key principle was: pursue relationships that bring tranquility and genuine friendship, whether married or single.

Marriage for wealth, status, or social obligation — that was rejected. But marriage based on genuine compatibility and friendship — that fit naturally into Epicurean life.

Your turn: Why do you think critics claimed Epicureanism opposed marriage and family? What misunderstanding does this reveal?

The misunderstanding: Critics heard "avoid excess" and "reject worldly ambition" and assumed this meant rejecting family life. But Epicurus's actual teaching was more subtle: some desires are natural and necessary (food, shelter, companionship); others are natural but unnecessary (luxury goods); still others are vain (fame, power).

Companionship is natural and necessary: The desire for friends and loved ones is built into human nature. Rejecting family entirely would be rejecting a natural good. So some Epicureans married, some didn't — what mattered was freedom from the anxiety and competition marriage could bring.

Friendship as the Heart of Epicureanism

If pleasure is the highest good in Epicureanism, and pleasure means freedom from pain and disturbance, then friendship is the means to achieve it. Friendship wasn't peripheral to Epicurus's teaching — it was central.

Of all the things which wisdom acquires to produce the blessedness of the complete life, far the greatest is the possession of friendship.

— Epicurus, Principal Doctrine 27

Why Friendship Matters

  • Security: In the Garden community, you had people who would help you, protect you, support you. This removes a major source of anxiety.
  • Philosophical growth: Friends challenge you, correct your mistakes, help you understand truth. Learning happens in dialogue.
  • Pleasure of conversation: Good discussion, shared laughter, honest exchange — these are among the purest pleasures available.
  • Meaning and purpose: Living for others' benefit (without expectation of repayment) gives life meaning beyond mere individual satisfaction.
Your reflection: Epicurus says "wisdom acquires friendship." What does this mean? Is friendship something you pursue deliberately, or something that happens naturally?

Both are true: As you become wiser (less anxious, clearer about what matters), you attract better friends. And having good friends makes you wiser (through conversation and shared life experience).

The Garden as friendship practice: The community itself was a kind of "friendship school." Living together, working together, eating together, discussing together — these daily practices strengthen the bonds that friendship requires.

Modern parallel: Think of any deep friendship you have. It probably developed through time together, shared experiences, honest conversation. That's what the Garden was designed to create — the conditions where friendship could flourish.

What Friendship Requires

Reciprocity: Not a transaction (you help me, I help you), but mutual care. You care for friends not because you expect something in return, but because their good matters to you.

Honesty: Real friendship requires truth-telling. If a friend is making a mistake, you tell them — gently but clearly.

Acceptance of mortality: Knowing that friends will die (as Epicurus lost friends, and eventually Metrodorus), you value the time together more. This isn't sad — it's liberating. It makes present moments matter.

💚
The paradox of Epicurean friendship: Though Epicureanism is often called selfish (pursuing your own pleasure), the path to your greatest pleasure is caring deeply for others. You find tranquility and joy through genuine friendships. This isn't sacrifice — it's enlightened self-interest.

Why the Garden Was Different

It was a commune: Unlike the Academy and Lyceum (where students came for lessons then went home), the Garden was a residence. Philosophy wasn't a subject you studied — it was a way of life you lived.

It welcomed outsiders: Platonism and Aristotelianism were elite institutions. The Stoa admitted more people than the Academy, but the Garden was uniquely open — to women, to formerly enslaved people, to the poor.

It rejected political ambition: While Platonists and Aristotelians saw philosophy as training for rulership and public life, Epicureanism offered an alternative: find your good life in withdrawal and friendship, not in public power.

Your analysis: Which approach — engagement with public life (Academy, Lyceum) or withdrawal (Garden) — seems more practical? More ethical? Why might Epicureanism's withdrawal have been controversial?

The tension: Epicureanism looked like a cop-out to its critics. Why would you withdraw from public life when you could improve the state, win power, make a name for yourself?

Epicurus's answer: Public life brings anxiety, competition, envy, fear of enemies. The tranquil life is better. But this stance troubled even sympathetic readers: wasn't there a responsibility to contribute to society?

The irony: By being apolitical, Epicureanism was profoundly political. It challenged the assumption that the good life meant power and glory. It offered a radical alternative. This made it threatening to authorities and appealing to those excluded from power.
