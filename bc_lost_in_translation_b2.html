Lost in Translation

Some feelings are so specific that only one language has a word for them. What can we learn from the words English is missing?

Before anything — you talk

This is a talking lesson. So start with you, before we read a word.

Start here · 2 minutes

Have you ever had a feeling, or been in a situation, that was really hard to put into words — in any language? Try to describe it to me now. What was the feeling, and why was it so difficult to explain?

Keep going:
  • Is there a word in another language you love, that has no good translation in English?
  • When you speak English, is there anything you find hard to express that feels easy in your own language?
  • Do you think the language we speak changes the way we see the world?

The words English is missing

I'll give you the idea in pieces. After each one, you do the talking.

Every language draws the world in a slightly different way. Sometimes a language has a single word for a feeling or a situation that, in English, takes a whole sentence to explain. These “untranslatable” words are interesting because they show us what one culture has decided is important enough to name.

Over to you:
  • Why do you think one culture gives a feeling its own word, while another doesn't? What does that say about them?

Six real examples

Read them, then we'll talk — don't worry about saying them perfectly.

  • saudade (Portuguese) — a deep, gentle sadness; missing someone or something that may never come back.
  • sobremesa (Spanish) — the relaxed time spent talking at the table after a meal is finished.
  • hygge (Danish) — a warm, cosy feeling of comfort and togetherness.
  • iktsuarpok (Inuit) — the restless feeling of waiting, and keeping going to the door to see if someone has arrived.
  • gigil (Tagalog) — the urge to squeeze something that is unbearably cute.
  • jugaad (Hindi) — a clever, simple, low-cost fix for a difficult problem.
React · 3–4 minutes

Which of these six words do you most wish English (or your own language) had? Which one describes a feeling you have actually had — when, and where? And which seems strange or useless to you? Talk me through them.

Some people believe these words are more than charming. They argue that if your language gives a feeling a name, you notice that feeling more often — and if it doesn't, the feeling can almost “hide” from you. Others say that is nonsense: we all feel the same things, with or without a word for them.

A word, in this view, is like a small window — once a language opens one, its speakers start to see what was always there.

Take a side · 3–4 minutes

Does having a word for something change how much we notice or feel it? Argue for one side — give a real example from your own life — then answer the strongest objection to your view. I'll push back.

Grammar focus — I wish…

This topic is full of one structure: “I wish English had a word for that.” We use wish to talk about things we would like to be different. The tricky part is the verb form that comes after it.

1. Wishing about now

To wish a present situation were different, use wish + past simple (even though we mean now).

I wish + past simple (for an unreal present)

I wish I had more time. (I don't have much now)

I wish English had a word for it. / I wish I could explain it better.

After I/he/she/it, both was and were are heard, but were is the “correct” form: I wish I were braver.

2. Wishing about the past

To express a regret about the past, use wish + past perfect (had + past participle).

I wish + had + past participle (for a past regret)

I wish I had started learning languages earlier.

She wishes she hadn't forgotten the word.

3. Wishing other people would change

To complain about other people's habits, or something annoying, use wish + would. We do not normally use this about ourselves.

I wish + (someone/something) + would + verb

I wish people would listen more.   I wish it would stop raining.

For yourself, use the past form instead: I wish I were more confident (not “I wish I would be”).

Practice A — wish about now or the past?

1. I wish English ____ (have) a single word for “saudade”.

2. I wish I ____ (start) learning languages when I was a child.

3. I wish I ____ (can) describe this feeling better.

4. She wishes she ____ (not / forget) the word she learned yesterday.

5. I wish I ____ (know) what that word really means.

1. had (wish about now)

2. had started (past regret)

3. could (can → could, about now)

4. hadn't forgotten (past regret)

5. knew (wish about now)

Practice B — choose the right form

Use wish + would for other people / annoying things; use the past form for yourself or for states.

1. I wish it ____ (stop) raining so we could go out.

2. I wish my neighbour ____ (not / play) loud music at night.

3. I wish I ____ (be) more confident when I speak English.

4. I wish people ____ (listen) more and talk less.

1. would stop (annoying situation we want to change)

2. wouldn't play (other person's habit)

3. were (or: was) (about yourself — not “would be”)

4. would listen (other people's behaviour)

  • I wish I had… (when I was younger)
  • I wish English had a word for…

What's your view? Build a case, then argue the opposite side as well as you can.
