Joan of Arc

A teenage peasant girl who heard voices, crowned a king, and changed the course of a war.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of an unlikely heroine and discuss faith, destiny and how history judges its outsiders — using the language of prophecy and the grammar of "the future in the past".

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. France and England had been fighting a long, exhausting siege: an enemy army had surrounded the city of Orléans for months, hoping to starve it into surrender.
a siege (n) — a military operation in which an army surrounds a town and cuts it off, forcing it to surrender.
2. From the age of thirteen she heard voices she believed were saints, telling her she had been chosen for a great mission.
voices (here, n, pl) — sounds or messages a person believes they hear from a divine or supernatural source; visions.
3. She was utterly certain of her destiny — convinced that God had already decided her future and that nothing could turn her from it.
destiny (n) — the events that will necessarily happen to someone in the future, seen as already fixed or fated.
4. The throne was disputed: the rightful heir, the man who should inherit the crown of France, had not yet been properly made king.
an heir (n) — the person legally entitled to inherit a title, throne or property when its holder dies.
5. At Reims, the new king was anointed and crowned in a sacred coronation that finally made his rule official in the eyes of the people.
a coronation (n) — the formal ceremony at which a person is crowned and officially made king or queen.
6. Her enemies could not simply execute her, so they accused her of heresy — of holding religious beliefs that the Church had condemned as false.
heresy (n) — a belief or opinion that contradicts official religious teaching. (adj/n: a heretic)
7. The court was rigged against her; the verdict was decided long before the trial in Rouen even began.
a trial (n) — a formal examination of evidence in court to decide whether someone is guilty of a crime.
8. Twenty-five years after her death, a second hearing nullified the original verdict, declaring the first trial unjust and her conviction void.
to nullify (v) — to cancel something legally, so that it has no force or effect; to declare it invalid.
9. Centuries later the Church canonised her, formally recognising her as a saint and an example of holiness.
to canonise (v) — (of the Church) to officially declare a dead person to be a saint.
10. To her followers she was a martyr — someone who suffered death for her faith and her cause, and whose memory only grew stronger because of it.
a martyr (n) — a person who is killed because of their religious or political beliefs, and is then honoured for it.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The girl who heard voices

She was born around 1412 in Domrémy, a small village in north-eastern France, the daughter of farming peasants. She could not read or write. By any ordinary measure, her life would be spent in the fields and the home — and history would never have learned her name. But France in those years was a country torn apart. For nearly a century it had been locked in what later generations would call the Hundred Years' War, a long and bitter struggle with England over land and, above all, over the French crown itself.

The crown was the heart of the dispute. An earlier treaty had handed the succession to the English king, pushing aside the French heir — the Dauphin, Charles. Much of northern France, including the city of Reims where French kings were traditionally crowned, was held by the English and their Burgundian allies. Charles, uncrowned and unsure of himself, controlled only the south, and his enemies mockingly called him the "King of Bourges."

Into this hopeless situation walked a teenage girl with an extraordinary claim. From the age of about thirteen, she said, she had been hearing voices — those of Saint Michael, Saint Catherine and Saint Margaret. They told her she had been chosen by God to drive the English out of France and to see the Dauphin crowned as the rightful king. She did not doubt them for a moment. She was certain she would do exactly what they had told her.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the French crown in dispute, and what was Charles's weak position?

2. What did the voices tell her she had been chosen to do?

1. An earlier treaty had given the succession to the English king and pushed aside the French heir, the Dauphin Charles. England and its Burgundian allies held the north, including Reims; Charles, uncrowned, held only the south and was mocked as the "King of Bourges."

2. The voices of Saints Michael, Catherine and Margaret told her she had been chosen by God to drive the English out of France and to have the Dauphin crowned the rightful king.

Discuss:
  • An illiterate teenage girl believed she had been personally chosen by God to save a nation. How do you think the people around her first reacted — and how would we react to such a claim today?

Part 2 — Maid of Orléans

Astonishingly, she got her audience. In early 1429 she travelled to the castle of Chinon and convinced the Dauphin to give her a chance — partly, it seems, because she was able to pick him out from his courtiers even though he had disguised himself to test her. She had cropped her hair, dressed in men's armour, and demanded to be sent to the besieged city of Orléans. To a court that had run out of options, this strange, fearless girl began to look less like a madwoman and more like a sign from heaven.

At Orléans she was less a general than a symbol — but what a symbol. Carrying her white banner rather than a sword, she gave the exhausted French army something it had not felt in years: belief. Within days the siege that had held for months was broken, and on 8 May 1429 the English withdrew. The victory electrified France. She then pressed Charles to march on Reims through enemy territory, insisting he was going to be crowned there as the voices had promised.

And he was. On 17 July 1429, in Reims cathedral, Charles was anointed and crowned King Charles VII, with Joan standing close by, holding her banner. A peasant girl had made a king. For a few short months, everything she had predicted had come true, and she stood at the height of her fame.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did she win the Dauphin's trust, and what was her role at Orléans?

2. What did she insist Charles do after Orléans, and what happened on 17 July 1429?

1. At Chinon she convinced the Dauphin to give her a chance (reportedly picking him out despite his disguise). At Orléans she acted as a symbol and an inspiration, carrying a banner rather than fighting; the months-long siege was broken and the English withdrew on 8 May 1429.

2. She insisted he march to Reims to be crowned. On 17 July 1429 Charles was crowned King Charles VII in Reims cathedral, with Joan beside him.

Discuss:
  • She won battles not by fighting but by giving people belief. Why can confidence and conviction sometimes change the outcome of events as powerfully as weapons or numbers?

Part 3 — The trial and the flame

Triumph did not last. In May 1430, while defending the town of Compiègne, she was captured by Burgundian soldiers. Her own king, the man she had crowned, did nothing to ransom her. Instead the Burgundians sold her to the English for a large sum of money, and her enemies finally had her where they wanted her.

They could not punish her simply for defeating them, so they put her on trial for heresy at Rouen — accusing her, among other things, of falsely claiming to speak for God and of wearing men's clothing. The court was led by clergymen loyal to the English, and the outcome was never really in doubt. After months of questioning, she was condemned. On 30 May 1431, she was burned at the stake in the marketplace at Rouen. She was only about nineteen years old.

But her story did not end there. In 1456, a second hearing examined the case again and nullified the original verdict, declaring the trial unjust and her conviction void. Over the centuries that followed, the illiterate village girl became one of the most powerful symbols France would ever know — and in 1920, nearly five hundred years after her death, the Church canonised her as a saint. The enemies who burned her are long forgotten; the girl they tried to erase echoes louder than ever.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did she end up in English hands, and what was she put on trial for?

2. What happened to her reputation after her death in 1431?

1. She was captured by Burgundian soldiers at Compiègne in 1430; Charles did not ransom her, and the Burgundians sold her to the English. She was put on trial for heresy at Rouen and condemned, then burned at the stake on 30 May 1431, aged about nineteen.

2. In 1456 a second hearing nullified the verdict and declared the trial unjust. She became a great national symbol, and in 1920 the Church canonised her as a saint.

Discuss:
  • The king she crowned abandoned her, yet she is remembered as a saint while he is largely forgotten. Why does history sometimes reward the betrayed and forget the powerful?

The grammar this story runs on: the future in the past

Joan's whole story is about prophecy — about a future she was certain of before it happened. To tell that, English has a special set of forms: the future seen from a point in the past. They let us say what someone, back then, believed or planned would happen next.

The three tools:
• was/were going to — a past plan or prediction: "she told the Dauphin he was going to be crowned king."
• would — a future seen from the past, often a conviction or promise: "she was certain she would drive out the English."
• was/were about to — a future that was on the very edge of happening: "the siege was about to break."

1. was/were going to vs would — plan against conviction

Use was/were going to for a plan or an intention formed in the past, and would for what someone firmly believed or promised the future held.

1. From the start, Joan was certain that she (drive) the English out of France.

2. She promised the frightened court that Orléans (be) saved.

3. She told Charles he (going to / be) crowned at Reims, just as the voices had said.

1. would drive (a conviction about the future, seen from the past)

2. would be (a promise about what the future held)

3. was going to be (a confident prediction / plan)

2. was/were about to — the future on the edge

Use was/were about to + verb for something that was just on the point of happening at that past moment.

1. After months of siege, the city (about to / fall) when Joan arrived.

2. Neither side realised it, but the war (about to / change) forever.

3. As she stood in the marketplace at Rouen, a legend (about to / be born).

1. was about to fall

2. was about to change

3. was about to be born

3. Put them together — narrate a paragraph

Fill each gap with the form the meaning needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

When Joan reached Chinon, she had no doubt that she (would / succeed). She told the Dauphin that he (going to / become) king, and that France (would / be) free. The court did not yet know it, but everything (about to / turn) in her favour — and within months her predictions (would / come) true.

would succeed (conviction) · was going to become (confident prediction) · would be (promise about the future) · was about to turn (on the edge of happening) · would come (a future seen from the past)

Notice how each form points forward in time — but from a vantage point in the past.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. She believed her future was already decided — her destiny fixed. Do you think a person achieves more by believing the outcome is certain, or by accepting that it isn't?
  2. The same court that condemned her as a heretic was, in time, overruled — and she was made a saint. What does her story tell us about who gets to decide what is "true" at any given moment?
  3. She has been claimed by very different causes over the centuries — religious, national, feminist. Why are powerful historical symbols so easy to borrow, and is that fair to the real person?
