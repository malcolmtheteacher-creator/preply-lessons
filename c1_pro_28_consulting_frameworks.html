Consulting Frameworks & Language

C1 Level · Professional Services

What Consultants Actually Do

The stereotype of consultants is arrogant outsiders who arrive with a PowerPoint, recommend obvious solutions, and charge $500K. But good consultants do something different: they bring structure to chaotic problems, surface uncomfortable truths, and help organizations make difficult decisions.

Companies hire consultants for several reasons: they lack internal expertise, they need objective perspective unburdened by internal politics, they need an external authority to justify difficult decisions, or they need extra capacity during a crisis. A CEO struggling with whether to divest a business unit might hire a consultant to validate their intuition — the consultant's blessing makes the difficult decision easier to defend to the board.

The Consulting Mindset

Hypothesis-driven thinking: Consultants don't gather facts and hope an answer emerges. They form hypotheses ("The problem is likely driven by X, Y, or Z") and test them. This focuses the investigation and saves time.

MECE (Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive): When analyzing a problem, break it into categories with no overlap that cover all possibilities. Example: A company's revenue is down. This could be because (1) volume is down, (2) price is down, or (3) both. These are mutually exclusive and cover all possibilities. Now analyze each independently.

Data-driven but humble: Consultants obsess over data but recognize data has limits. You can prove almost anything with the right dataset. The best consultants are skeptical of their own conclusions.

Focus on impact: A brilliant analysis that doesn't change behavior is worthless. Good consultants present recommendations that are (1) actionable, (2) realistic given the client's constraints, and (3) clearly tied to business impact.

Consulting Vocabulary

These terms define how consultants think and speak. Flip each card to reveal the definition.

Framework A structured way of thinking about a problem. Maps all dimensions of an issue. Examples: SWOT, Porter's Five Forces, value chain analysis.
Hypothesis-Driven Starting with assumptions about the problem, then testing them with data. Efficient: avoid analyzing everything; focus on what matters.
MECE Mutually Exclusive, Collectively Exhaustive. Organizing information so categories don't overlap and nothing is missing. Clarifies thinking.
Stakeholder Anyone affected by or involved in the decision. CEOs, employees, customers, board members. Consultants must understand each stakeholder's perspective.
Workstream A parallel project or work area. Large consulting engagements have multiple workstreams (e.g., operations, technology, finance) running simultaneously.
Deep-Dive An intensive analysis of a specific area. Example: "We conducted a deep-dive on customer acquisition to understand why costs are rising."
Bandwidth Organizational capacity. "The team has limited bandwidth for new initiatives" means they are stretched thin. Consultants help by doing the work.
Synthesise Combine information from multiple sources into a coherent conclusion. Synthesis is harder than analysis. It requires judgment.

Diagnosis & Recommendation

A client comes to you with a problem: sales have dropped 25% in the past six months. Your task: structure a diagnostic approach using MECE, develop 3 hypotheses, and present recommendations.

Step 1: MECE Breakdown

Revenue = Price × Volume. If revenue is down 25%, what could be causing it? Use MECE to break down the possibilities.

Step 2: Three Hypotheses

Based on typical business patterns, form three hypotheses about why sales might be down. Each hypothesis should be testable.

Step 3: Recommended Next Steps

You have one week to investigate. What data would you gather? What questions would you ask? Prioritize: what would give you the most insight fastest?

Step 4: Recommendation Structure

After your investigation, you recommend a specific action. How would you present it? Use this structure:

MECE: Sales down means either (1) customers buying same volume at lower price, (2) fewer customers buying same volume, (3) same customers buying less volume, or (4) combination of the above.

Hypothesis 1: A key customer (15% of revenue) is reducing orders. Test: call top 10 customers, ask about their plans.

Hypothesis 2: A competitor launched a lower-price product. Test: win-loss analysis, customer interviews.

Hypothesis 3: Internal issue: product quality dropped, customer service degraded, or sales team is demoralized. Test: NPS survey, customer support ticket analysis, sales team interviews.

Recommendation (if Hypothesis 1 is confirmed): SITUATION: Our largest customer reduced orders by 40% due to their own budget cuts. COMPLICATION: They represent $4M annual revenue. Losing them cascades to 40% annual revenue decline. RECOMMENDATION: Offer a 3-month discount to maintain relationship while they stabilize. BUSINESS CASE: Cost of discount ($300K) is less than the profit impact of losing this customer ($1.2M).

The Consulting Profession: Strengths & Critiques

Consulting is a powerful tool but also faces legitimate criticism.

Consulting Culture & Risks: Consulting firms are high-pressure environments. Consultants are expected to work 60+ hour weeks. Burnout is common. The culture is "eat what you kill" — those who bring in clients get ahead. This incentivizes selling, not necessarily solving problems well.

Over-Reliance on Frameworks: A talented consultant knows which framework to use when. A mediocre consultant applies frameworks mechanically. There is a joke: "A consultant borrows your watch, tells you what time it is, and charges you $200K." The critique is valid — consultants sometimes repackage what the client already knows.

Implementation Gap: Many consulting recommendations fail because the organization doesn't execute. A consultant diagnoses the problem and proposes a solution. But changing behavior is hard. The best consultants stick around during implementation. The worst deliver a report and leave.

Internal vs. External Consulting: Large organizations now employ internal consultants (sometimes called "Business Operations" or "Strategy") to do what external consultants do. This saves cost and builds institutional knowledge. External consultants retain the advantage of objectivity and specialization.
