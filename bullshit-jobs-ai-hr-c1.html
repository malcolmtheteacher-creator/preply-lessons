The Bullshit Jobs Paradox

When AI automates the pointless, what's left for humans?

The Accusation

In 2018, anthropologist David Graeber dropped a bomb on the professional world. His book Bullshit Jobs argued that a huge portion of white-collar work is completely meaningless—and that the workers themselves know it.

"A bullshit job is a form of paid employment that is so completely pointless, unnecessary, or pernicious that even the employee cannot justify its existence."

— David Graeber, Bullshit Jobs: A Theory (2018)

Graeber's Five Categories of Bullshit

1. Flunkies
Status Symbol

Jobs that exist only to make someone else look or feel important. Receptionists with nothing to do, assistants hired to be seen.

2. Goons
Aggressive

Jobs that only exist because competitors have them. Armies of lobbyists, corporate lawyers, PR consultants in an arms race of influence.

3. Duct Tapers
Patch Work

Jobs that exist to fix problems that shouldn't exist. Employees hired to work around bad systems rather than fixing them.

4. Box Tickers
Compliance Theatre

Jobs that exist to allow organisations to claim they're doing something they're not. Compliance officers, diversity consultants, quality assurance—when the goal is documentation, not results.

5. Taskmasters
Management Bloat

Supervisors of people who don't need supervision. Middle managers whose main job is to create work for others and hold unnecessary meetings.

The Robot Defence

Here's the twist: the machines are coming for the bullshit first.

AI and automation are rapidly taking over exactly the tasks Graeber identified as meaningless. If these jobs were truly pointless, should we celebrate their elimination? Or does their automation reveal something more complicated?

What's Already Automated

Each card shows an HR task that AI can now do. Click to reveal the problem companies discovered when they tried it.

📄
CV Screening
AI systems scan thousands of applications in seconds, ranking candidates by keyword matches and predicted performance.
The catch

Amazon scrapped their AI recruiter in 2018 after discovering it penalised CVs containing the word "women's" (as in "women's chess club"). The system had learned bias from historical hiring data.

🎥
Video Interviews
HireVue and similar platforms analyse facial expressions, word choice, and tone to predict job performance. Over a million interviews analysed daily.
The catch

HireVue dropped facial analysis in 2021 after intense criticism. Studies showed the AI consistently rated people with certain accents or disabilities lower. The "science" was never peer-reviewed.

📊
Performance Analytics
Software tracks keystrokes, email patterns, calendar time, and Slack activity to generate "productivity scores" and predict who might quit.
The catch

Microsoft's own research found that surveillance software increased reported anxiety by 56% and caused employees to perform "productivity theatre"—mouse-jiggling software became a bestseller.

💬
HR Chatbots
24/7 bots answer employee questions about leave policies, benefits, and procedures. Some handle initial stages of grievance procedures.
The catch

Air Canada was ordered to compensate a passenger after their chatbot invented a bereavement policy that didn't exist. The company argued the bot was "responsible for its own actions"—the court disagreed.

🎯
Talent Matching
AI systems suggest internal candidates for promotions and lateral moves, theoretically removing favouritism from the equation.
The catch

Research from MIT found these systems often perpetuate existing hierarchies. They're trained on past promotions—which were made by humans with all their biases.

📝
Policy Generation
Large language models can draft employee handbooks, job descriptions, and HR policies in minutes rather than days.
The catch

A New York lawyer was sanctioned after submitting an AI-generated brief citing cases that didn't exist. The model "hallucinated" legal precedents that sounded plausible but were fabricated.

The Paradox

"We automated the bullshit. But it turns out the bullshit was hiding something important."

The uncomfortable realisation: many "pointless" HR tasks served a hidden function. CV screening wasn't just about finding candidates—it was about understanding the market. Performance reviews weren't about the forms—they were about forcing managers to have difficult conversations. Compliance training wasn't about the content—it was about creating shared reference points.

When you automate these tasks, you often automate away the human judgment that was embedded in them—judgment that wasn't visible in the job description but was doing real work.

The Human Case

So what can't be automated? What remains when the administrative layer is stripped away?

Six real HR situations. Click each to read it, then discuss: what would you do? And could an algorithm handle this instead of you?

The Hidden Resignation ▼
A high-performing engineer has been unusually quiet in meetings. Their work is fine, deadlines are met, but something feels off. Their manager dismisses it: "Numbers are good, what's the problem?" But you've seen this pattern before—and you know this person is probably job hunting.

What would you do? Could an algorithm detect this? Should it?

The Brilliant Jerk ▼
Your top salesperson brings in 30% of company revenue. They're also creating a toxic environment—three people have quietly mentioned they're uncomfortable. Nothing documented, nothing actionable. No formal complaints. Just a growing sense that something needs to change.

How do you quantify "toxic"? How do you weigh revenue against culture? What would you advise?

The Restructuring Conversation ▼
The company is eliminating a department. You have to tell someone who's been here 15 years that their position no longer exists. They're 58. The severance package is "competitive." You know they'll struggle to find equivalent work.

Could a chatbot deliver this news? Should it? What does a human bring to this moment?

The Culture Mismatch ▼
A candidate has the perfect CV—right experience, right skills, stellar references. But after three interviews, something doesn't sit right. You can't articulate it. The hiring manager loves them. Your gut says no.

Should you trust instinct over data? How do you separate intuition from bias? What would you do?

The Unspoken Rule ▼
A new employee from another country keeps making small cultural missteps—nothing offensive, just... off. They're being slowly excluded from informal networks. No one has said anything to them. You notice; others might not.

How do you address something no one will admit is happening? Is this HR's job?

The Mental Health Grey Zone ▼
An employee's work quality has declined. They've mentioned "stress" but nothing specific. You suspect there's more—perhaps burnout, perhaps something personal. Company policy says offer the EAP. But something tells you this requires a different approach.

Where does duty of care end and privacy begin? What would you do?

The Pattern

Notice what these scenarios have in common: ambiguity, unwritten rules, competing values, and the need to act on incomplete information. They require reading between the lines, holding contradictions, and making judgment calls that can't be reduced to rules.

This is what HR becomes when the administrative layer disappears: a role that's fundamentally about navigating human complexity.

Wrap-Up

Today we explored the uncomfortable intersection of meaningless work and automation—and what it reveals about the future of HR.

The Big Ideas

Graeber's provocation: Many jobs exist not because they're needed, but because we've built systems that require them. HR is often caught in this trap.

The automation paradox: When we automate "bullshit" tasks, we often discover they were hiding important human judgment that's now lost.

The human case: What remains when administration disappears is work that requires ambiguity tolerance, ethical judgment, and the ability to navigate competing values—exactly what machines can't do.

The Real Question

The question isn't whether AI will change HR. It's whether HR professionals can articulate what they offer that software can't. Those who can will thrive. Those who can't will be replaced—not because their work was bullshit, but because they couldn't explain why it wasn't.

"The paradox of the bullshit jobs thesis is that proving a job isn't bullshit requires doing the kind of reflective, articulate, human work that was never in the job description."
