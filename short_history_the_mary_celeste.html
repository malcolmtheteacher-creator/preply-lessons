The Mary Celeste

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The old ship was a merchant vessel, built not for war or for passengers but to carry barrels of goods across the ocean for profit.
a merchant vessel (n) — a commercial ship that carries cargo (goods) for trade, rather than passengers or weapons.
2. With no one steering and no one on watch, the empty ship simply drifted, carried wherever the wind and current chose to take it.
to drift (v) — to be carried along slowly by water or air, with no one controlling the direction.
3. Below deck, the cargo was untouched — over a thousand barrels of raw alcohol, exactly as they had been loaded in New York.
cargo (n) — the goods carried by a ship, plane or lorry.
4. The crew numbered eight — the captain, his officers and the ordinary sailors who worked the ropes and the sails.
a crew (n) — all the people who work together to operate a ship, plane or other vehicle.
5. The captain brought his wife and their two-year-old daughter aboard, so this was as much a household at sea as a working ship.
a household (n) — a family and the others living together in one home.
6. The only thing missing was the lifeboat — the small boat kept ready so that people can escape if the ship is in danger.
a lifeboat (n) — a small boat carried on a larger ship, used to save people in an emergency.
7. There was no blood, no sign of a fight, no note — the disappearance was completely baffling, and no one could offer a convincing explanation.
baffling (adj) — so confusing or strange that it cannot be understood or explained.
8. At the official inquiry, lawyers and officers questioned witnesses for weeks, trying to establish exactly what had happened at sea.
an inquiry (n) — an official investigation to find out the facts about something.
9. Some suspected mutiny — that the sailors had turned on the captain, seized the ship, and killed those in charge.
mutiny (n) — a revolt in which sailors or soldiers refuse to obey, and often attack, their commanders.
10. Over the years the true account was buried under speculation — endless guesses, wild theories and invented "facts" that no one could prove.
speculation (n) — guessing or forming opinions about something without firm evidence.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The ship that met another ship

On the afternoon of 5 December 1872, a British ship called the Dei Gratia was crossing the Atlantic, roughly halfway between the Azores and the coast of Portugal, when the crew spotted another sailing ship in the distance. Something about it was wrong. It moved strangely, its sails only partly set and hanging loose, and it did not answer their signals. As they drew closer, the captain, David Morehouse, recognised her: she was the Mary Celeste, an American merchant vessel he happened to know — she had left New York only eight days before his own ship, bound for Genoa in Italy.

Morehouse sent men across in a small boat to investigate. What they found was deeply unsettling. The Mary Celeste was in good condition and still afloat, but there was not a single living soul aboard. The cargo — some 1,700 barrels of raw industrial alcohol — sat untouched in the hold. There was food and fresh water for months. The crew's pipes, boots, oilskins and personal belongings were all in place, as though the men had simply stepped away a moment ago. The captain's chart table still held his instruments. Only one thing was clearly gone: the ship's single lifeboat.

Whoever had been aboard the Mary Celeste had left in a hurry — and had not come back. Eight people had vanished into the middle of the Atlantic, and the sea gave no answer.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who found the Mary Celeste, and what was strange about the way she was moving?

2. What did the boarding party find aboard — and what was missing?

1. The crew of the British ship Dei Gratia, led by Captain Morehouse, spotted her in the Atlantic; she moved oddly, with sails only partly set and not answering signals.

2. They found the ship in good order, with cargo, food, water and the crew's belongings all in place — but no people at all. The lifeboat was gone.

Discuss:
  • The crew clearly left in a hurry, yet the ship was not sinking. What could frighten experienced sailors enough to abandon a perfectly good ship in the open ocean?

Part 2 — The crew, and the suspicion

The people who had disappeared were not reckless adventurers. The captain was Benjamin Briggs, a serious, religious and highly experienced New England seaman. With him were his wife, Sarah, and their two-year-old daughter, Sophia — the ship was as much a family home as a workplace. The rest were seven crewmen, said to be careful, capable sailors. These were exactly the kind of people you would not expect to panic.

The Dei Gratia brought the empty ship into Gibraltar, and there an official inquiry began. Under maritime law, a crew who rescued an abandoned ship could claim a reward called "salvage" — a share of the ship's value. That gave the investigating official, Frederick Solly-Flood, a suspicious idea. What if there had been no innocent mystery at all? What if Morehouse and his men had attacked the Mary Celeste, murdered everyone aboard, and invented the whole story to claim the reward?

He looked for evidence and thought he found it: some marks on the hull, an old sword with what looked like stains, a little water in the ship. But under proper examination, none of it held up. The "blood" was not blood; the marks were not made by an attack. In the end, the inquiry cleared everyone. It could prove no crime — but it could not explain the disappearance either. The Dei Gratia's crew received only a small salvage payment, far less than usual, as though suspicion still hung in the air.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was the captain of the Mary Celeste, and who else was aboard with him?

2. Why did the investigator suspect the crew of the Dei Gratia, and what came of that suspicion?

1. Captain Benjamin Briggs, an experienced and religious seaman, together with his wife Sarah, their two-year-old daughter Sophia, and seven crewmen.

2. Because rescuers could claim a salvage reward, the official suspected Morehouse's men had murdered everyone and lied. The "evidence" didn't hold up; the inquiry cleared them but couldn't explain the mystery, and they got only a small payment.

Discuss:
  • The reward for saving an abandoned ship gave the rescuers a motive — and made them suspects. Is it fair that trying to help can turn you into the accused?

Part 3 — The theories, and the legend

With no answer from the inquiry, the theories multiplied. The most sensational was fiction: in 1884, a young unknown writer named Arthur Conan Doyle — later the creator of Sherlock Holmes — published a short story about a similar abandoned ship, full of invented horrors. Many readers took it as fact, and from then on the real events were tangled up with pirates, sea monsters, madness and murder that never happened.

The sober theories are quieter, and more interesting. The cargo of raw alcohol may have leaked fumes; a small, harmless-looking flash or the fear of an explosion could have panicked Briggs into ordering everyone into the lifeboat for safety — meaning to stay tied to the ship by a rope until the danger passed. If that rope then broke, the little boat, with everyone in it, would have been left behind as the Mary Celeste sailed on alone, and the eight of them would have been lost to the sea. Others point to a faulty pump, a sudden fear the ship was flooding, or a violent waterspout. Each theory explains some of the facts; none explains all of them.

And that is why, a century and a half later, the Mary Celeste has never let go of us. It is the perfect mystery: not a horror story, but an ordinary ship, an ordinary family, and a disappearance so complete and so quiet that no one has ever filled the silence. The ship herself sailed on for another dozen years before being deliberately wrecked in an insurance fraud — an unremarkable end for the most famous ghost ship in history.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Arthur Conan Doyle change the way people thought about the Mary Celeste?

2. Describe the "alcohol fumes and lifeboat" theory — and why no single theory fully settles the case.

1. His 1884 short story about an abandoned ship was fiction, but many readers believed it, mixing the real events with invented horrors — pirates, monsters, murder.

2. Fumes from the raw alcohol may have caused a scare of explosion; Briggs may have ordered everyone into the lifeboat, tied to the ship, and the rope broke, leaving them behind. But this and every rival theory explains only part of the evidence.

Discuss:
  • Conan Doyle's fiction became more famous than the facts. Why do dramatic invented stories so often beat the quieter truth?

Grammar — -ed vs -ing adjectives

The Mary Celeste gives us the perfect way to feel this distinction. The mystery is baffling (it does something to us); the investigators were baffled (they felt that thing). Same root, opposite direction — and mixing them up is one of the most common mistakes at every level.

The rule, in one line:
• -ing adjective describes the cause — the thing or situation that produces the feeling: a baffling mystery, a terrifying discovery, a fascinating case.
• -ed adjective describes the person who feels it — their reaction or state: the baffled investigators, the terrified sailors, a fascinated reader.
Two quick tests:
• Ask "Is it doing the feeling, or feeling it?" The empty ship caused shock → the discovery was shocking. The boarding party received it → they were shocked.
• The famous trap: "I am boring" means you send others to sleep; "I am bored" means you feel dull inside. People almost never mean the first one.

1. -ed or -ing? Complete each pair

Put the adjective in the right form. One describes the cause, one describes the person.

1. (baffle) The disappearance was completely , and the investigators were utterly .

2. (terrify) A smell of gas filled the hold, and the captain ordered everyone off.

3. (fascinate) Readers still find the case ; whole books have been written by historians.

1. baffling (cause) · baffled (people)

2. terrifying (the smell causes fear) · terrified (the captain feels it)

3. fascinating (the case causes interest) · fascinated (the historians feel it)

2. Choose the correct one

Only one form fits the meaning. Write the correct adjective.

1. After hours of questioning, the exhausting / witnesses could say no more. (they felt it)

2. The theory about a giant waterspout is genuinely / convinced. (it does the persuading)

3. It was a / disappointed salvage payment — far less than the crew had hoped. (it caused the letdown)

1. exhausted (the witnesses feel tired → -ed)

2. convincing (the theory does the persuading → -ing)

3. disappointing (the payment caused the letdown → -ing)

3. Complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the -ed or -ing form of the word in brackets. Read it aloud once you're done.

The sight of the drifting ship was (alarm), and the (alarm) sailors rowed across carefully. The empty cabins were (frighten). For years the case has remained (puzzle), and even the calmest experts admit they are (puzzle) by it.

alarming (cause) · alarmed (people) · frightening (cause) · puzzling (cause) · puzzled (people)

Notice the pattern: whenever the word describes the ship, the scene or the case, it takes -ing; whenever it describes the people and how they felt, it takes -ed.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The Mary Celeste is famous precisely because it has no ending. Why are we so drawn to mysteries that can never be solved?
  2. The rescuers were suspected of murder simply because they stood to gain a reward. When is suspicion reasonable, and when does it become unjust?
  3. Conan Doyle's invented horrors became more famous than the sober facts. In an age of viral stories, is the dramatic version always going to win?
