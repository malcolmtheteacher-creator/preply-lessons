Luck

Mark Twain (1886) — a famous hero, and an old man who knows the truth about him

Original version available: read this lesson at B2/C1 level →

Read the story in three parts

This is a famous short story, told here in simple English. Read each part, then stop and talk about the questions. The end is a surprise — read slowly and do not jump ahead.

Part 1 — The big dinner

There was a big dinner in London. It was for a famous soldier. He was a national hero. Everybody at the dinner looked at him. People loved him. They thought he was a great man.

I sat at the table and looked at him too. Next to me sat an old man. He was a clergyman — a teacher in a church. He was a good friend of mine. Long ago, he was a teacher at the military school where the hero was a young student.

The old man looked at the famous soldier. Then he said something quietly, very close to my ear:

"This famous man is not clever. He is a fool."

I was very surprised. How could this be true? The old man drank his coffee and said, "Keep it a secret, and I will tell you the whole truth. I know it, because I was his teacher."

Stop and talk:
  • Where are the people, and why are they there?
  • Who is the old man next to the narrator?
  • What surprising thing does the old man say?

Part 2 — The teacher's secret

The old man told his story. "Many years ago, I was a teacher at the military school. This man was my student. He was very kind, and everybody liked him. But he was not clever. He could not pass his exams."

"I felt sorry for him. So I helped him in secret. I gave him all the answers before the exams. With my help, he passed. But I was sure of one thing: in a real war, he would make a big mistake and fail."

The old man drank some more coffee. "Then a war came," he said. "I waited for his big mistake. I waited for everyone to see the truth. But the mistake never came. Something very strange happened instead."

Stop and talk:
  • Why could the young man not pass his exams?
  • How did the teacher help him?
  • What did the teacher think would happen in a war?

Part 3 — Lucky again and again

"In his first battle, he made a silly mistake. He sent his men the wrong way. But by luck, the wrong way went straight to the enemy — and his side won! Everybody said, 'What a clever plan!' They gave him a medal."

"And it happened again and again. Every time he did something silly, by luck it looked like a clever plan. His side always won. People thought he was brilliant. So he went higher and higher. Now he is a great hero, and the whole country loves him."

The old man looked at the famous soldier one more time. Then he said quietly:

"He is a fool — but he is the luckiest man in the world."

The end — stop and talk:
  • What did the soldier do in his first battle? Why did his side win?
  • Why do people think he is clever?
  • The old man says he is "the luckiest man in the world." Do you agree? Is luck the same as skill?

Reported speech

The whole story is one man telling us what another man said. So we use a lot of reported speech. We use it to tell people what someone said before.

When we report words from the past, the verb usually goes one step back:

Direct words: "I am a fool."  →  Reported: He said that he was a fool.
Direct words: "I help him."  →  Reported: He said that he helped him.

Easy rule: am / is → was   ·   are → were   ·   present verb → past verb   ·   I / me → he / him.

1. Change the words to reported speech

1. "I am a fool."  →  He said that he a fool.

2. "I am his teacher."  →  He said that he his teacher.

3. "I help the boy."  →  He said that he the boy.

4. "He is kind."  →  He said that the man kind.

1. was   2. was   3. helped   4. was

2. Now use "told me"

We can also report with told me. Change these the same way.

1. "I like the boy."  →  He told me that he the boy.

2. "The man wins every battle."  →  He told me that the man every battle.

3. "I am sure he will fail."  →  He told me that he sure he would fail.

1. liked   2. won   3. was

3. say or tell?

This is an easy rule. We use tell with a person after it. We use say with no person after it.

tell + person:   He told me…   She told him…   (always a person: me, him, us)
say (no person):   He said that…   She said hello.

So we say: "He told me the truth." But: "He said the truth." (not "He told the truth" to a person, and not "He said me".)

How the writer surprises you

A good short story is more than just words. The writer uses tricks to make you feel something. Here are some simple ones from this story.

1. The big surprise (the "twist")

Everybody thinks the soldier is a clever hero. But the old man tells us the truth: the hero is really a fool. This surprise is called a twist. It makes us see the famous man in a new way.

2. Luck or skill?

The soldier did not win because he was clever. He won because he was lucky. The story asks a big question: when someone is famous and important, is it because of skill — or sometimes just luck?

3. A story inside a story

The writer does not tell us "the hero is a fool." Instead, the old man tells the narrator, and the narrator tells us. So there is a small story (the old man's words) inside the big story. This is called a frame. It makes the secret feel more true, because a real teacher is telling us.
