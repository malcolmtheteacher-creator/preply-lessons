A Job Interview

One chair, one hour, one shot to be the person they remember. Time to talk about yourself without either boasting or hiding.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

You've made it through the CV pile, the phone screen and a nervous night's sleep, and now you're sitting in a bright meeting room with a glass of water you don't dare touch. This is the job you actually want — not a fallback, not a maybe. The kind of place you'd tell your friends about.

Across the table is the person who decides. In the next forty minutes you have to do three things well: introduce yourself so it sticks, talk up your strengths without sounding pleased with yourself, and — the trap everyone dreads — admit a weakness in a way that somehow makes you look better. Do that, ask a sharp question or two, and the room is yours.

The interviewer has read a hundred CVs this month and heard "I'm a perfectionist" from most of them. They're not trying to catch you out. They just want to meet the real person behind the bullet points — so give them one.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of the best "tell me about yourself" answer you could give right now. What's the ONE thread that runs through everything you've done — the thing that's really you?
  2. Some people freeze the moment they have to praise themselves out loud. Where does that come from — and is a little healthy self-promotion honesty, or just showing off?

Who's who

You
The Candidate 💼

Well-prepared, quietly hopeful, and determined not to babble. You want this job — and today the challenge isn't your skills, it's talking about them without shrinking or swaggering.

The other voice
The Interviewer 🪑

Warm but watchful. Has heard every rehearsed answer going and can spot one a mile off — but genuinely lights up when someone gives a real, thoughtful one.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the softeners, the way strengths are backed with proof, and the moment a flaw is turned into a story about growing.

InterviewerCome on in, take a seat. Did you find us alright? The car park's a bit of a maze, I'm afraid.
YouNo trouble at all, thanks — and thank you for making the time today. I have to say, it's lovely to see the place in person after reading so much about it.
InterviewerKind of you to say. Shall we dive straight in? So — tell me a little about yourself. The version that isn't already on your CV.
YouOf course. By way of background, I've spent the last five years in customer operations — but what really gets me out of bed is taking a messy, tangled process and making it run smoothly. That's honestly the thread running through everything I've done.
InterviewerGood — and where would you say your real strengths lie?
YouIf I had to pick one, it's staying calm when everything's on fire. Colleagues tend to say I'm the person they want in the room on a bad day. I'd rather show you than just tell you, though — last winter our whole system went down in our busiest week, and I ended up coordinating the fix and keeping four teams talking to each other.
InterviewerLet me turn that around, then. What's something you find genuinely difficult? And I'd rather not hear "I'm a bit of a perfectionist".
YouFair enough — you must hear that a hundred times a week. If I'm honest, I used to take far too much on myself rather than hand it over. I've had to learn, the hard way, that trusting the team actually gets a better result than doing it all alone. I'm not perfect at it yet, but I'm a world away from where I started.
InterviewerThat's a refreshingly straight answer. So — why us? You could take those skills more or less anywhere.
YouHonestly, what draws me to this role in particular is that you're growing fast but you haven't lost the human side — and that's a rarer combination than it sounds. I'd far rather help build something than just keep a machine ticking over.
InterviewerNicely put. Right — I've been doing all the asking. Is there anything you'd like to ask me?
YouThere is, actually. I'd be curious to know — what would a successful first six months look like in this role? Put another way, what would I need to have got right by then?
InterviewerNow that's the question I always hope someone asks. Six months in, you'd have the team's trust and one real improvement already shipped — nothing flashy, just proof you can land things here.
YouThat's really helpful, thank you — and honestly, it only makes me keener. Whatever you decide, I've enjoyed this enormously; I'd genuinely welcome the chance to be part of it.
InterviewerWell, you've certainly given me plenty to think about — in the best way. We'll be in touch by the end of the week. Mind the maze on the way out.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The candidate says their strength is "staying calm when everything's on fire" — but they don't stop there. What do they add straight afterwards, and why is that second half doing more work than the claim itself?

2. Asked about a weakness, the candidate avoids the "perfectionist" trap. What flaw do they actually admit — and how do they leave the interviewer thinking better of them, not worse?

3. "Why us? You could take those skills anywhere." What is the interviewer really testing with that question — and what makes the candidate's answer land?

1. They back the claim with proof — a specific story: the system crash, coordinating the fix, keeping four teams talking. Anyone can say they're calm under pressure; a concrete example turns a boast into evidence. "I'd rather show you than tell you" is the whole move.
2. They admit they used to take on too much and struggle to delegate. It works because they frame it as something they've grown out of — "I've had to learn… I'm a world away from where I started". A real weakness plus a story of fixing it reads as self-aware, which is exactly what an interviewer wants.
3. It tests whether they actually want this job or just any job. The answer lands because it's specific to the company — "growing fast but haven't lost the human side" — and reveals a value ("I'd rather build something than keep a machine ticking over"), not just flattery.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that let you sell yourself without either boasting or shrinking — in this interview and every one after it. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚪 Getting off on the right foot

Phrase 1

You walk in and want to open warmly — grateful, but relaxed, not stiff. Which greeting sets the right tone for the next forty minutes?

A. "Hello. I am here for the interview."
B. "Thank you for your consideration of my application."
C. "Thanks so much for making the time today — it's lovely to be here."
C — "Thanks so much for making the time today — it's lovely to be here."
A is flat; B is stiff and written, not spoken. C thanks them for the one thing they actually gave up — their time — and sounds like a warm human, which is the first impression you want.
Phrase 2

"Tell me about yourself." You want to open your answer with a short, elegant signpost that means "here's some context first". There's a four-word phrase that does it: "___ ___ ___ ___, I've spent the last five years in…"

"By way of background…"
It frames what follows as scene-setting, so you don't just launch into a list. Useful cousins: "To give you a bit of context…" and "A little about me…".

💪 Talking up your strengths (without swagger)

Phrase 3

You want to name a strength but you're wary of sounding pleased with yourself. The trick is to put the praise in other people's mouths. Which version does that?

A. "I am extremely good at staying calm under pressure."
B. "Colleagues tend to say I'm the person they want in the room on a bad day."
B — "Colleagues tend to say I'm the person they want in the room on a bad day."
A is a bare boast; B is exactly the same compliment, but reported from others — which reads as evidence, not ego. Templates: "People often tell me…", "My last manager said…".
Phrase 4

You've made a claim about yourself — now you want to signal you'll back it with a real example rather than just asserting it. There's a short phrase that promises proof is coming: "I'd rather ___ ___ than just ___ ___."

"I'd rather show you than just tell you."
The golden rule of interviews in one line — always follow a strength with a story. Then reach for: "For example, last year…" or "A good example would be…".
Phrase 5

You're about to single out your single greatest strength, and you want to soften the spotlight so it doesn't sound arrogant. Put the opener in order:

pick one,if Iit'shad to
"If I had to pick one, it's…"
The little conditional ("if I had to") pretends you're being forced to boast — which makes the boast charming instead of pushy. Same family: "If I'm honest about it…", "If there's one thing…".

🩹 The weakness question — gracefully

Phrase 6

The dreaded "what's your weakness?" lands. You want to signal that a real, honest answer is coming — not a humble-brag. What three-word opener buys you that credit before you've even named the flaw?

"If I'm honest…"
It flags a genuine, unguarded moment and lowers the interviewer's guard with it. Close relatives: "I'll be candid with you…", "To be completely honest…".
Phrase 7

The secret to the weakness answer is showing it's in the past — a flaw you've grown out of. Complete the chunk that turns a weakness into a growth story: "I ___ ___ struggle with delegating, but I've since learned to trust the team."

"I used to struggle with delegating, but I've since learned to…"
"I used to… but I've since learned to…" is the whole formula: name the flaw in the past tense, then show what you did about it. The weakness becomes proof that you can grow.

🎯 Asking the questions that win it

Phrase 8

The interviewer asks if you have any questions — never say no. You want to open with something softer and warmer than a blunt "What are the hours?". What gentle four-word lead-in signals genuine curiosity?

"I'd be curious to know…"
It sounds interested rather than interrogating. Equally good: "I'd love to understand a bit more about…" and "Could you tell me a little about…?".
Phrase 9

You want to ask the one question that shows you're already picturing yourself doing the job well. Put this classic in order:

what would alook likefirst six monthssuccessfulin this role?
"What would a successful first six months look like in this role?"
It quietly moves you into the job — you're no longer asking whether you'll do it, but how well. Interviewers love it because it's forward-looking and shows you think in outcomes.

🌟 Closing so they remember you

Phrase 10

The interview's wrapping up. You want a warm final line that reaffirms your interest without begging. Which closing leaves the best taste?

A. "OK. Bye then. I hope I get it."
B. "Please give me the job, I really need it."
C. "I've enjoyed this enormously — I'd genuinely welcome the chance to be part of it."
C — "I've enjoyed this enormously — I'd genuinely welcome the chance to be part of it."
A is limp and B is desperate. C reaffirms real interest with dignity: "I'd genuinely welcome the chance to…" is keen without pleading. Pair it with "Whatever you decide…" to sound gracious either way.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole interview out loud using all ten, in order — warm opening → your story → strengths with proof → the weakness turned into growth → your sharp question → a closing they'll remember. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your interview. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer to keep the pressure of a real room behind you.

Round 1 — you are the candidate 💼

The interviewer speaks — you answer. No looking back at the phrases; trust your ear. Remember: warm openings, strengths with proof, and turn any weakness into a story about growing.

InterviewerCome on in, have a seat. Did you find us alright?
InterviewerGreat. So, to get us started — tell me a little about yourself.
InterviewerAnd what would you say your greatest strength is?
InterviewerNow the flip side — what's a genuine weakness? And please, not 'I'm a perfectionist'.
InterviewerFair enough. And why us — why this job in particular?
InterviewerGood. Now — is there anything you'd like to ask me?
InterviewerWell, that's about all my questions. Anything you'd like to leave me with?

Round 2 — now YOU are the interviewer 🪑

Swap chairs. It's your company, your role, your call. Invent the job, the team, the challenges. Give the interviewer a personality — warm, sharp, a little playful.

CandidateThanks so much for seeing me today — it's lovely to be here.
CandidateBy way of background, I've spent the last few years in marketing, but what really drives me is telling a story people actually remember.
CandidateHonestly, people tend to say I can make almost anything sound interesting — and I'd rather show you than tell you…
CandidateIf I'm honest, I used to say yes to everything and burn out — I've since learned to protect my time.
CandidateBecause you make things people love, and you haven't gone corporate doing it. That's rare.
CandidateI'd be curious to know — what would a successful first six months look like here?
The Twist

🌩️ The curveball question

One more round — no script, no safety net. Halfway through, the interviewer leans back and asks the question you didn't prepare for: "If we don't offer you this job, what will you have learned about yourself from applying?" Start the timer and talk your way through it — stay honest, stay warm, and don't panic. Then trade the awkward questions back and forth: "Where do you see yourself in five years?", "Tell me about a time you failed.", "Why did you leave your last job?" One rule: the moment you bluff or waffle instead of giving a real answer, you lose the room.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1942, physicist Robert Oppenheimer had to fill a secret city in the New Mexico desert with the finest scientific minds on earth — for a job he wasn't allowed to describe. Imagine the interview: "Would you like to move to a town that doesn't officially exist, to work on something I can't tell you about, for reasons you'll only learn once you've said yes?"

And people said yes — persuaded not by a job spec but by the person across the table, and by the feeling of being asked to be part of something bigger than themselves. The whole thing turned on a conversation.

You'll never be recruited quite that mysteriously. But every interview is the same quiet transaction: can this person make me believe I'd be part of something worth joining?

Two bigger questions
