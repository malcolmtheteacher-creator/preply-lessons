A Job Interview

One chair, one hour, one shot to be the person they remember. Time to talk about yourself without either boasting or hiding.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

You've made it through the CV pile, the phone screen and a nervous night's sleep, and now you're sitting in a bright meeting room with a glass of water you don't dare touch. This is the job you actually want — not a fallback, not a maybe. The kind of place you'd tell your friends about.

Across the table is the person who decides. In the next forty minutes you have to do three things well: introduce yourself so it sticks, talk up your strengths without sounding pleased with yourself, and — the trap everyone dreads — admit a weakness in a way that somehow makes you look better. Do that, ask a sharp question or two, and the room is yours.

The interviewer has read a hundred CVs this month and heard "I'm a perfectionist" from most of them. They're not trying to catch you out. They just want to meet the real person behind the bullet points — so give them one.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of the best "tell me about yourself" answer you could give right now. What's the ONE thread that runs through everything you've done — the thing that's really you?
  2. Some people freeze the moment they have to praise themselves out loud. Where does that come from — and is a little healthy self-promotion honesty, or just showing off?

Who's who

You
The Candidate 💼

Well-prepared, quietly hopeful, and determined not to babble. You want this job — and today the challenge isn't your skills, it's talking about them without shrinking or swaggering.

The other voice
The Interviewer 🪑

Warm but watchful. Has heard every rehearsed answer going and can spot one a mile off — but genuinely lights up when someone gives a real, thoughtful one.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Listen for the softeners, the way strengths are backed with proof, and the moment a flaw is turned into a story about growing.

InterviewerCome on in, take a seat. Did you find us alright? The car park's a bit of a maze, I'm afraid.
YouNo trouble at all, thanks — and thank you for making the time today. I have to say, it's lovely to see the place in person after reading so much about it.
InterviewerKind of you to say. Shall we dive straight in? So — tell me a little about yourself. The version that isn't already on your CV.
YouOf course. By way of background, I've spent the last five years in customer operations — but what really gets me out of bed is taking a messy, tangled process and making it run smoothly. That's honestly the thread running through everything I've done.
InterviewerGood — and where would you say your real strengths lie?
YouIf I had to pick one, it's staying calm when everything's on fire. Colleagues tend to say I'm the person they want in the room on a bad day. I'd rather show you than just tell you, though — last winter our whole system went down in our busiest week, and I ended up coordinating the fix and keeping four teams talking to each other.
InterviewerLet me turn that around, then. What's something you find genuinely difficult? And I'd rather not hear "I'm a bit of a perfectionist".
YouFair enough — you must hear that a hundred times a week. If I'm honest, I used to take far too much on myself rather than hand it over. I've had to learn, the hard way, that trusting the team actually gets a better result than doing it all alone. I'm not perfect at it yet, but I'm a world away from where I started.
InterviewerThat's a refreshingly straight answer. So — why us? You could take those skills more or less anywhere.
YouHonestly, what draws me to this role in particular is that you're growing fast but you haven't lost the human side — and that's a rarer combination than it sounds. I'd far rather help build something than just keep a machine ticking over.
InterviewerNicely put. Right — I've been doing all the asking. Is there anything you'd like to ask me?
YouThere is, actually. I'd be curious to know — what would a successful first six months look like in this role? Put another way, what would I need to have got right by then?
InterviewerNow that's the question I always hope someone asks. Six months in, you'd have the team's trust and one real improvement already shipped — nothing flashy, just proof you can land things here.
YouThat's really helpful, thank you — and honestly, it only makes me keener. Whatever you decide, I've enjoyed this enormously; I'd genuinely welcome the chance to be part of it.
InterviewerWell, you've certainly given me plenty to think about — in the best way. We'll be in touch by the end of the week. Mind the maze on the way out.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The candidate says their strength is "staying calm when everything's on fire" — but they don't stop there. What do they add straight afterwards, and why is that second half doing more work than the claim itself?

2. Asked about a weakness, the candidate avoids the "perfectionist" trap. What flaw do they actually admit — and how do they leave the interviewer thinking better of them, not worse?

3. "Why us? You could take those skills anywhere." What is the interviewer really testing with that question — and what makes the candidate's answer land?

1. They back the claim with proof — a specific story: the system crash, coordinating the fix, keeping four teams talking. Anyone can say they're calm under pressure; a concrete example turns a boast into evidence. "I'd rather show you than tell you" is the whole move.
2. They admit they used to take on too much and struggle to delegate. It works because they frame it as something they've grown out of — "I've had to learn… I'm a world away from where I started". A real weakness plus a story of fixing it reads as self-aware, which is exactly what an interviewer wants.
3. It tests whether they actually want this job or just any job. The answer lands because it's specific to the company — "growing fast but haven't lost the human side" — and reveals a value ("I'd rather build something than keep a machine ticking over"), not just flattery.

Your Turn 🎭

No script this time. You'll have a real conversation with a real person. They have their own mind, and you have a goal.

1 · Warm up your voice 🎤

Say each step once, out loud, in your own words. No writing, no peeking at the phrases.

  1. Start well. Thank them and settle in.
  2. Introduce yourself. Give a short background that leads somewhere.
  3. Talk about a strength. Say it, then prove it.
  4. Be honest about a weakness. Be real and show what you did about it.
  5. Ask a good question. Show you are curious about the job.
  6. Close strongly. Leave a clear last impression.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1942, physicist Robert Oppenheimer had to fill a secret city in the New Mexico desert with the finest scientific minds on earth — for a job he wasn't allowed to describe. Imagine the interview: "Would you like to move to a town that doesn't officially exist, to work on something I can't tell you about, for reasons you'll only learn once you've said yes?"

And people said yes — persuaded not by a job spec but by the person across the table, and by the feeling of being asked to be part of something bigger than themselves. The whole thing turned on a conversation.

You'll never be recruited quite that mysteriously. But every interview is the same quiet transaction: can this person make me believe I'd be part of something worth joining?

Two bigger questions

  1. Oppenheimer's recruits couldn't be told what the job really was — yet they said yes anyway. What makes a person accept a role on trust alone? Would you have said yes?
  2. Some of those brilliant people later regretted the work they were hired for. When you take a job, how much responsibility do you carry for what your employer does with your talent — and can a great interview hide a job you shouldn't take?
