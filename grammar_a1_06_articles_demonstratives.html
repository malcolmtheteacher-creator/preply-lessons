A or An?

For the first time you mention something, for jobs, and for "any one" of many things — not one specific thing.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "I need a new phone — mine broke."
  • "She's an English teacher."
  • "There's an apple in my bag if you're hungry."

Two of these use "an", one uses "a". What decides which one we use — is it the spelling, or the sound?

Check the pattern CLICK
It's the sound, not the spelling. "An" goes before a vowel sound (English, apple), "a" goes before a consonant sound (phone). Also — none of these things were mentioned before, so the listener doesn't know which one yet.
a + consonant sound (a phone, a job, a bike)
an + vowel sound (an idea, an apple, an hour)
It's really the sound that counts: "an hour" (the "h" is silent, so it sounds like a vowel), but "a university" (it starts with a "y" sound).

The One We Know

Use "the" when you and the listener both already know exactly which thing you mean.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "Can you close the window? It's cold." (only one window here)
  • "I bought a jacket yesterday. The jacket was really cheap." (already mentioned)
  • "Where's the bathroom, please?" (only one in this house)

What do all three have in common? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
In every case, both people already know exactly which one: there's only one in that place, it was already mentioned once, or it's obvious from the situation. That's the whole idea of "the".
the + noun → both people know exactly which one

From new to known

"I saw a cat in the garden."
→
"The cat was orange and white."

First mention: "a cat" (new information). Second mention: "the cat" (now you both know which one).

This, That, These, Those

For pointing at things — near you or far away, one thing or more than one.

Spot the pattern

Four everyday sentences:

  • "This coffee's gone cold." (in your hand)
  • "How much is that jacket, the blue one over there?" (across the shop)
  • "These shoes are really comfortable." (on your feet, more than one)
  • "Are those your keys, on the table over there?" (far away, more than one)

What decides this/that, and what decides this/these? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Distance decides this/that: this/these = near you, that/those = far away. Number decides this/that vs these/those: this/that = one thing, these/those = more than one.
near: this (one) → these (more than one)
far:   that (one) → those (more than one)
