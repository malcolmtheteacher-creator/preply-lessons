The Unpaid Invoice

He's lovely on the phone. He's always lovely on the phone. He's also ninety days late, and you'd like to keep him as a client and get paid, in that order or the other one.

The Scene 🎬

You did the work in March. It was good work; he said so at the time, twice, and once in writing. The invoice went out on the last day of the month and it is now the middle of July, and the money is not in your account. You've rung twice already. Both calls were friendly. Both calls ended with him saying he'd chase it, and both times you came off the phone feeling oddly grateful, which should probably have told you something.

So here you are with his number on the screen, doing the thing everybody does before this call: rehearsing the opening, deciding you sound aggressive, softening it, softening it again, until what you've got is a sentence that could be about almost anything. That's the trap. It isn't rudeness that loses you the money — you're not going to be rude, you like him. It's that you'll be so careful, so understanding, so willing to say "no rush" that he'll put the phone down genuinely believing there isn't one.

And you want him back next year. That's the whole difficulty. If you never wanted to hear from him again this call would take nine seconds.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Has anyone ever owed you money — a client, a friend, a landlord, an employer? How long did you leave it before you said something, and what were you actually waiting for?
  2. In your country, who is supposed to feel embarrassed in this situation: the person who hasn't paid, or the person asking to be paid? Be honest about which one you'd feel like.

Who's who

You
The One Who's Owed 💷

Self-employed, four months out of pocket, and quite fond of him. You want the money and the relationship. Today's job is to stay exactly as warm as you've always been, and still come off the phone holding a date.

The other voice
Callum Reid 📞

Your client. Delighted to hear from you, every single time. Apologises beautifully, means all of it, and has now been ninety days late without ever once being unpleasant about it. That's what makes him hard.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's a real phone call, so it's messy: people talk over each other, lose the thread, start sentences again. Listen for the moments where the warmth stays and the ask doesn't move.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Callum's explanation changes during the call. What does he seem to be hinting at first, and what does he finally admit — and why does the speaker say that the admission is something they can work with?

2. Halfway through, the speaker says something and apologises for it immediately — but doesn't take it back. What was it, and what's the difference between apologising for a thing and withdrawing it?

3. The call ends with a date. Did Callum offer that date? Where did it actually come from?

1. He starts by letting it sound like an accident in his accounts department, and when the speaker offers to split the payment he suddenly wants it made clear that the business isn't short — it's just chaos since Priya left. Chaos is a much better answer than no money, because chaos has a person in it who can be found and asked, whereas an empty account can't pay you whatever you say.
2. "You said that in May, though, didn't you." They apologise for how sharp it sounded, but they never say it isn't true, and they don't offer him a way to forget it — they go straight on to ask for a date. Apologising for your tone costs you nothing and keeps him comfortable; taking the fact back would have handed him another few weeks.
3. No. Callum never gives a date — he offers to chase it, which isn't the same thing. The date comes out of asking who actually releases the money: Dean runs the payments on the last working day, so the speaker works out the 30th themselves and says it back to him until he agrees to it.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for getting paid without losing the client. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. This call goes wrong in two directions. Apologise too much and he hears the whole debt as optional, because you've told him it is. Go cold and formal and he decides the relationship is finished, in which case he may as well pay you last. What you want is your ordinary voice — friendly, unhurried, a bit bored, as though you were sorting out a delivery date rather than asking for money. The phrases below are all fairly warm, and that only works if you say them warmly, and then stop talking and let the pause sit. Most people fill a silence with a date.

📞 Opening it, instead of circling it

Phrase 1

He's answered, he's cheerful, and you can feel yourself about to spend two minutes on his holiday before you get anywhere near the money. Which opener admits the call is awkward and then gets past it?

A. "Sorry to bother you — it's nothing important, really."
B. "I hate this bit, so I'll be quick."
C. "Right. I'm ringing about the money you owe me."
B — "I hate this bit, so I'll be quick."
Saying the awkwardness out loud is what stops it running the call, because once it's been named you can both get on with things. "This bit" quietly means the asking-for-money part without you having to spell it out, and "I'll be quick" is a promise that he isn't in for ten minutes of discomfort, which makes him relax enough to listen. A tells him it doesn't matter, so he will treat it exactly that way. C is true, but it makes him defensive before you've even asked him anything.
Phrase 2

Now the actual question. You want it short, neutral, and impossible to answer with "lovely to hear from you". Put it in order:

with it?The invoicewhere are wefrom March —
"The invoice from March — where are we with it?"
"Where are we with it" treats the invoice as a shared piece of work that the two of you are somewhere in the middle of, rather than an accusation aimed at him, so it's very hard to be offended by. It's also an open question: he can't get out of it with yes or no, he has to tell you something. And naming the month does a lot on its own — March, in July, sounds like what it is.

📅 Naming the size of it, without an accusation

Phrase 3

He's surprised it's still outstanding. You need to say how late this actually is, so that it stops sounding like an oversight — but you're not accusing him of anything. Which one does that?

A. "It's been a while now, to be honest."
B. "It's ninety days now, which is a bit beyond a slip."
C. "Ninety days. That's just not good enough, frankly."
B — "It's ninety days now, which is a bit beyond a slip."
The number does the work that your tone doesn't have to: "a while" can be argued with or shrugged off, whereas ninety can't be. A "slip" is a small accidental mistake, the kind anybody might make, so telling him this is "a bit beyond a slip" says that it has stopped being an accident — without ever saying he did it deliberately. And "a bit" is doing something clever: it makes the sentence sound mild while the number stays hard, which is why you can say it in a perfectly friendly voice. C says roughly the same thing but hands him a reason to be offended, and now you're arguing about that instead of the money.
Phrase 4

You want to hand him an innocent explanation — that the invoice is sitting unnoticed in his building rather than being refused. Complete the chunk: "Has it got stuck somewhere ___ ___?"

"Has it got stuck somewhere your end?"
"Your end" means your side of the arrangement — your office, your building, your accounts department — and it's the ordinary word for it on the phone, which is why it doesn't sound like an inspection. The kindness is in the word "stuck", because it offers him a version of events in which nobody has done anything wrong and something is simply sitting in the wrong pile. That's a very easy thing for him to agree to, and the moment he agrees the invoice is stuck somewhere, he's agreed there's something to unstick.

⏳ Turning warmth into a date

Phrase 5

He's given you "I'll chase it today", and before that it was "as soon as I can", and before that "you'll have it shortly". All of those are feelings. You want something you could write in a diary. Say your version out loud first — then reveal.

"Can you give me a date rather than 'soon'?"
Putting his own vague word inside your question does the criticism for you, so you never have to make it yourself — you're not accusing him of anything, you're just quoting him back to himself. And notice how small the ask is: you're not demanding the money on this call, only a date, which is a tiny, answerable thing and therefore much harder to refuse than "please pay me". Once he says a date out loud, the conversation is about that date rather than about whether there'll ever be one.
Phrase 9

He's apologetic, he means it, and he has no power. Somewhere in that building is the person whose hands the money actually leaves through, and you'd like their name. Put it in order:

on payments?Whopresses the buttonactually
"Who actually presses the button on payments?"
"Presses the button" is the picture of the last physical step — the moment the money is released — so the question skips past everyone who can be sympathetic and finds the one person who can pay you. "Actually" is the word doing the real work, because it politely admits you both know the person on the phone isn't that person, and it does it without insulting him. It's also the question that turns a sorry into a system: once you know who does it and when they do it, you can work out the date for yourself instead of waiting for him to invent one.

🤝 Keeping him on your side

Phrase 6

You want him to understand there are other roads open to you, while making it clear you'd rather not take them. Complete the chunk — two words meaning the two of us, with nobody else involved:

"I'd rather sort this ___ ___ than go through anybody else."

"I'd rather sort this between us than go through anybody else."
"Between us" means the two of you and nobody else involved, so the sentence offers him something genuinely nice: this stays a conversation. The pressure is entirely in "anybody else", which you never explain, and because you don't explain it he fills the gap himself with whoever he'd least like brought into this — which is worse than anything you could have named, and you didn't have to say it. "I'd rather" keeps the whole thing a preference you're expressing rather than a warning you're issuing, which is what lets you say it warmly.
Phrase 7

There's a chance he hasn't got it this month and is too embarrassed to say so. You'd like the truth more than you'd like the full amount today. Put it in order:

we'll do itIf cash flow's tight,in two payments.tell me and
"If cash flow's tight, tell me and we'll do it in two payments."
"Cash flow" is money moving in and out of a business, and "tight" is the ordinary word for it not quite stretching this month — so "cash flow's tight" says the business is short right now without suggesting it's in any real trouble, which is a far easier thing for him to admit to. Offering the two payments before he's asked for them costs you a fortnight and buys you the truth, and the truth is what you're short of. And if he turns it down, as Callum does, you've learned something valuable: the money exists, and this is about somebody's desk rather than his bank account.

🚧 The line you hold — and the close

Phrase 8

The next job can't start. You have to tell him that, and you want him to hear it as a fact about your life rather than as pressure you've decided to apply. Which one?

A. "If I don't get paid, I'm not doing the next job. Simple as that."
B. "I can't start the next piece until this one's settled — that's not a threat, it's just where I am."
C. "I might struggle to start the next piece, possibly, depending on things."
B — "I can't start the next piece until this one's settled — that's not a threat, it's just where I am."
"Settled" means paid in full and closed, so the condition is exact and there's nothing to argue about. The interesting half is the second one: you name the thing your sentence could be mistaken for and put it aside, and then you tell him what it really is — "where I am" means this is simply your situation rather than a punishment you've chosen, and no reasonable person argues with somebody's situation. A is the identical fact delivered as an ultimatum, so he has to react to your tone before he can think about the money. C hedges so heavily that he won't believe the work will really stop, which means nothing has been said at all.
Phrase 10

You've got the date. Now close it — put it in writing, and let him off the hook in the same breath. Complete the chunk: "Right — the 30th. I'll put that in an email and we'll ___ ___ ___ ___ it."

"Right — the 30th. I'll put that in an email and we'll say no more about it."
Saying the date back to him is what turns his half-promise into something the two of you have agreed, and it takes two words. "We'll say no more about it" is a fixed way of saying the matter is now closed and won't be brought up against him afterwards, and it's a real gift — it tells him he isn't in trouble, which is exactly what he's been afraid of for ninety days. It's also what makes the email land as tidying up rather than as evidence being collected, although of course it is both, and he knows that, and it's still fine.

The Twist

🎯 He rings you first

Before you can make the call, your phone goes and it's him — and he's thrilled. There's a new project, much bigger than the last one, starting Monday, the best money you'd have seen all year, and he wants you and only you and he needs to know today. He talks for four minutes. He does not mention March. He is so pleased that saying the word "invoice" would feel like putting a brick through a window. Start the timer and take the call live, out loud, all the way to a yes or a no. You cannot have both things: you can't take the work and leave March hanging, and you can't tell him no and expect the offer to still be there tomorrow. Decide while you're talking, the way you'd have to in real life.

Before you close the tab…

Run back over what you actually said out loud. Honestly, now — could you tick each of these?

  • I said the awkward part in the first ten seconds instead of waiting for it to come up on its own.
  • I put a real number on how late it was, and I still sounded friendly saying it.
  • He offered me a feeling and I asked for a date anyway — and then asked again.
  • I found out who actually releases the money, and when.
  • I got off the phone with a date, and he'd still want to work with me next year.

Whichever one you couldn't tick is the reason you're still waiting. Run the twist again and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

The awkward thing about a call like this is that Callum isn't a villain. He's disorganised, he means every apology, and he has been quietly relying on you being too polite to make it uncomfortable — which, for ninety days, worked perfectly well.

Most money owed to small suppliers isn't kept back on purpose. It sits in a pile because nobody in that building is uncomfortable enough to move it, and the only person who is uncomfortable is you, on the other end of the phone, saying "no rush, honestly" and then not sleeping.

None of that asks you to be tough, which is just as well, because tough doesn't get you paid any faster and it does lose you the client. It asks you to be slightly less comfortable than usual for about ninety seconds, rather than for another ninety days.

Two bigger questions

  1. In a lot of workplaces, asking about money is treated as rude while paying late is treated as normal. Whose comfort does that arrangement protect, and who ends up paying for it — and is that a rule anybody actually agreed to, or one that simply suits whoever is holding the money?
  2. Callum is charming, apologetic, and ninety days late. Is he a bad client? What exactly would you need to see before you stopped working with someone you genuinely like — and how many people do you know who never get to that point at all?
