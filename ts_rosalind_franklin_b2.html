Photograph 51

The most important photograph in biology was taken by a scientist who never knew what was done with it — and the argument about what she was owed has never ended.

Today: you'll examine science's most argued-about credit dispute — a story that exists almost entirely in who said what — and you'll master reported speech: he said that…, she told them…, they later admitted that…

The vocabulary of this story

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. She fired X-rays through a fibre of DNA for hours; the rays bent around its atoms and drew a pattern of shadows on film. The technique is called X-ray crystallography.
crystallography (n) — reading a molecule's structure from how it scatters X-rays. She was among the best in the world at it — the story stands on that fact.
2. The photograph showed a bold X of black marks — to a trained eye, the unmistakable signature of a helix.
a helix (n) — a spiral, like a spring or a spiral staircase. The double helix — two strands winding around each other — became biology's most famous shape.
3. Wilkins showed her best photograph to a rival without her knowledge — she was never asked, and never told.
without someone's knowledge — they didn't know; compare without permission (they knew the rule and broke it). Which phrase applies here is exactly what historians argue about.

B · Which word means…?

Three words: posthumous · rivalry · to decode. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which word means ongoing competition between people or teams?
rivalry (n) — a fierce / friendly rivalry; rival labs. Here: King's College London versus Cambridge — with the fatal complication that the rivalry inside King's was worse than the one between them.
2. Which word means to work out the meaning hidden in something?
to decode (v) — to decode a signal / an image / a genome. Photograph 51 had to be decoded — and the decoding required exactly the mathematics Franklin had mastered.
3. Which word means happening or awarded after someone's death?
posthumous (adj) — a posthumous award / publication. The Nobel Prize is never posthumous — a rule at the centre of this story's ending.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Watson's memoir described her clothes, her manner, her "unfeminine" seriousness — and barely her science. The portrait was deeply patronising. This means…
  (a) admiring   (b) treating someone as inferior while pretending kindness   (c) accurate
(b) — patronising (adj) — a patronising tone / portrait. His own colleagues objected; the book's epilogue partly apologised. The damage was done — and became, ironically, the engine of her fame.
2. She died at thirty-seven, and the prize rules meant she could never be honoured with the others. Many call that the story's central injustice. An injustice is…

(a) — an injustice (n) — to suffer / right an injustice. Whether this story contains one, two, or none is precisely what you'll debate in the Speak tab.

1. You know credit. Build the set: to ___ credit for something (claim it) · to give credit ___ credit is due · her contribution went ___ (adjective: not recognised).
To take credit · give credit where credit is due · went unrecognised / uncredited. The whole story in three collocations.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — Two labs, one molecule, one misunderstanding

By 1951, biology's biggest question had narrowed to one molecule. DNA was known to carry inheritance; nobody knew its structure, and structure, everyone sensed, would explain everything — how life copies itself. In Britain the problem belonged, by gentleman's agreement, to King's College London, where Maurice Wilkins had been probing DNA fibres with X-rays. That year the lab recruited Rosalind Franklin: thirty-one, Cambridge-trained, and just back from Paris, where she had become one of the world's finest X-ray crystallographers working on the structure of coal.

The collaboration was poisoned before it began. Through a failure of management that historians still wince at, Franklin arrived understanding that DNA was to be her project, with Wilkins away; Wilkins returned believing this precise, formidable newcomer had been hired as his assistant. Neither was ever properly corrected. Their relationship froze into cold formality within months — two scientists on the same corridor, working on the same molecule, barely speaking. Meanwhile in Cambridge, two outsiders with no DNA data of their own — the young American James Watson and the loudly brilliant Francis Crick — decided to skip the measuring altogether and guess the structure by building models, the approach the American chemist Linus Pauling was using to terrifying effect an ocean away.

Franklin, methodical where the model-builders were theatrical, made the field's decisive technical advance: she discovered that DNA took two forms, wet and dry, whose overlapping X-ray patterns had confused everyone, and she worked out how to photograph each form pure. In May 1952, she and her doctoral student Raymond Gosling exposed a fibre of the wet "B form" to X-rays for over sixty hours. The result — labelled simply Photograph 51 — was the clearest image of DNA ever taken: a stark X of black bands that shouted helix to any trained eye, with dimensions readable from the spacing. Franklin, deep in the mathematics of the dry form and rightly suspicious of pretty conclusions, set it in a drawer and continued her systematic analysis.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What misunderstanding poisoned King's College, and how did it happen?

2. What was Photograph 51, and what did Franklin do with it?

1. Franklin was told DNA would be her project; Wilkins believed she had been hired as his assistant. Management never corrected either, and the two froze into barely speaking — rivals inside one lab.

2. The clearest X-ray image of DNA ever taken (with Gosling, May 1952) — the B form's X-pattern, the signature of a helix with readable dimensions. Franklin filed it and continued her systematic mathematical analysis of the other form.

  • One botched introduction froze a partnership that might have won everything. How much of scientific history do you suppose turns on management failures nobody records?

Part 2 — What was shown, and what was built

In January 1953 the story reached its famous hinge. Watson visited King's, argued with Franklin, and on his way out was taken aside by Wilkins — who, wanting sympathy or alliance, pulled out Photograph 51 and showed it to him. Franklin was not asked; Franklin was not told. Watson later wrote that his mouth fell open and his pulse began to race: the helix was obvious at a glance, and the picture's parameters travelled back to Cambridge in his memory. Weeks later, a second channel opened: Franklin's precise measurements, written into an internal report for a funding committee, reached Crick through his supervisor Max Perutz. Nothing was stolen in the criminal sense — Wilkins had come to possess the photograph legitimately through Gosling, the report was not marked confidential — and generations have argued about those two sentences ever since.

What is not in dispute is what the data did. Crick, a trained crystallographer unlike Watson, could read in Franklin's numbers a fact she had herself established: the molecule's symmetry meant its two strands ran in opposite directions. With her parameters constraining the guesswork, the Cambridge pair built one final model — and when Watson, playing with cardboard cut-outs of the four bases, saw that the pairs A–T and G–C had identical shapes, the structure clicked shut. The double helix explained inheritance in a single image: unzip the strands, and each is the template for the other. Franklin, shown the model in March, accepted it quickly and without apparent bitterness — her own notebooks reveal she had been closing in on it herself, two steps away.

In April 1953, Nature published the discovery as three papers: Watson and Crick's model first, Wilkins's data second, Franklin and Gosling's photograph and analysis third — arranged so that her evidence read as confirmation of their idea, rather than its foundation. The model paper acknowledged, in a sentence of famous understatement, that the authors had been "stimulated by a knowledge of the general nature" of the King's workers' unpublished results. Franklin never learned how directly her photograph and her report had steered the model. She had, by then, already arranged to leave.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. By what two channels did Franklin's data reach Cambridge, and what is disputed about each?

2. What did her data contribute to the model, specifically?

1. Wilkins showed Watson Photograph 51 without her knowledge; her measurements reached Crick via an internal funding report passed on by Perutz. Neither act was theft in a formal sense — the photograph was legitimately held, the report unmarked — and whether they were wrong is the sixty-year argument.

2. The helix and its dimensions from the photograph; and from her measurements, the symmetry showing the two strands run in opposite directions — the constraints that made the final model buildable.

Part 3 — The five years, the book, and the argument

What the legend forgets is that Franklin's story did not end at King's — it got better. She moved to Birkbeck College, into a happier lab, and did what many consider her finest work: unravelling the structure of the tobacco mosaic virus, foundational to structural virology, and beginning on polio. She became friendly with Crick and his wife, staying with them during her illness — whatever she suspected about 1953, she did not live as a woman betrayed. In 1956 she was diagnosed with ovarian cancer; she worked between operations, publishing to the end, and died in April 1958, aged thirty-seven. Four years later, the Nobel Prize for DNA went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins. The prize is never awarded posthumously and is limited to three names; whether she would — or should — have been included had she lived is a question with no answer, which has never stopped anyone.

It was a book that turned a science story into a moral one. Watson's memoir The Double Helix (1968) was a sensation — candid, gossipy, gripping — and its treatment of "Rosy" (a nickname she never used) was so belittling, so fixated on her appearance and her supposed bad temper, that it accomplished the opposite of its tone: readers began asking who this woman actually was. Crick and Wilkins objected to the manuscript; Watson added an epilogue conceding, too late and in the past tense, that he had realised "years too late" the struggles a woman in her position had faced and that her work had been first-rate. Anne Sayre's answering biography, and later Brenda Maddox's, rebuilt the record. The patronising portrait became the engine of the vindication.

Since then, the pendulum has kept moving — and the newest scholarship complicates the martyrdom too. Historians examining her notebooks and correspondence argue that Franklin was less a victim robbed in the dark than an equal contributor whose work was known and used with a casualness that reflected the era's norms — including its norms about women — and that she likely understood more about how her data had travelled than the legend allows. She has been compensated with the fame of an icon: institutes, universities, a Mars rover carry her name, arguably outshining the three laureates. The last word belongs to the argument itself, which is why the story is taught: every era retells it as a mirror — first as a tale of genius, then of theft, then of sexism, now of credit's genuine complexity — and every retelling says at least as much about the tellers as about four scientists and a photograph.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Franklin do after King's, and what happened in 1958 and 1962?

2. How did Watson's book backfire, and what does recent scholarship add?

1. At Birkbeck she produced landmark virus-structure work (tobacco mosaic, then polio) and remained friendly with Crick. She died of ovarian cancer in 1958 at 37; the 1962 Nobel went to Watson, Crick and Wilkins — the prize being neither posthumous nor open to a fourth name.

2. Its belittling portrait of "Rosy" provoked the biographies that restored her record — the insult built the icon. Recent scholarship argues she was an equal contributor, aware of more than the legend claims, used with the era's casual norms rather than robbed in secret — a harder, truer story than either myth.

Grammar — reported speech

This story survives in memoirs, letters and interviews — everything in it is someone reporting what someone said. Master the machinery and you can retell any dispute accurately.

The backshift: when the reporting verb is past, the original tense usually steps one back.
• "The project is mine." → She said the project was hers.
• "I have seen the photograph." → He admitted he had seen the photograph.
• "We will build a model." → They announced they would build a model.
No backshift needed when the fact is still true: He explained that DNA carries inheritance.
The other changes:
• Pronouns and place/time words shift: "I took it here yesterday" → She said she had taken it there the day before.
• Questions lose their question order: "Where is the data?" → He asked where the data was. · "Did she agree?" → They asked whether she had agreed.
• Commands and requests: "Show me the photograph." → He asked Wilkins to show him the photograph.

Choosing the verb is the analysis (see the Wirecard case for the full C1 scale): said, told, explained, admitted, claimed, denied, insisted, acknowledged. "Watson admitted that his pulse had raced" and "Watson boasted that…" report the same words and different worlds.

Going deeper

A note on the record: this story is unusual in that its primary sources are memoirs and letters written by the participants — partial by nature. Where the accounts conflict, this lesson has said so; where legend has outrun evidence, the newest scholarship is flagged.

What the story actually teaches. First, that credit is an institution, not a feeling. Who is asked, who is told, whose paper goes first, who fits inside a three-name prize — these are rules and habits, and they distribute glory as surely as any payroll distributes money. The 1953 arrangement of the three Nature papers did more lasting work than any single act at King's: it set the reading order of history. Whoever controls the order of the papers controls the story.

Second, the norms defence and its limits. "Everyone shared data casually then" is true, and it is also precisely how norms protect themselves — casualness reliably flowed one way, along the gradients of status and gender. When judging any era (including ours), the question is not "was it normal?" but "normal for whom, at whose expense?"

Third, the martyrdom trap. The corrective legend — Franklin as pure victim — required shrinking her back into someone defined by what was done to her, erasing Birkbeck, the viruses, and the five years of first-rate science after DNA. The newest history restores her as a protagonist: brilliant, prickly, partly aware, unfinished at thirty-seven. It is a harder story to chant, and a better one — and its lesson generalises: honouring someone accurately is more respectful than honouring them conveniently. The mirror question this case leaves is for every workplace: in your own team, whose data travels casually, whose name arrives late on the paper — and would you notice?
