A Bridge to Arthur

In Lesson 3, you learned about Boadicea's revolt (60 AD) and Rome's iron grip tightening. But now we jump ahead to around 410 AD. Rome is leaving Britain. For the next 150 years, in the chaos that follows, a figure emerges in the sources — or does he?

The question we're asking: Was Arthur a real person? A military leader? A legend that grew from a half-remembered war? Or simply what people needed to believe — that someone, somewhere, had fought back against the darkness?

II. Marshall's Arthur — Legend and Light

H.E. Marshall opens her Arthur chapters with romance. She's not writing history here — she's telling the legend as it was believed and told for centuries. And the legend is beautiful.

The Birth of Arthur

Marshall tells us that before Arthur came to the throne, there was a king called Uther Pendragon. And a great wizard:

When Arthur was born, he was kept hidden lest any harm should befall him. After Uther Pendragon's death, there was no king, and the nobles quarreled among themselves. Then Merlin came to the quarreling nobles leading a tall, fair-haired boy by the hand. The boy was Arthur, Uther Pendragon's son. Arthur was only fifteen when he was made king. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XIII

But Arthur was untested. How would the nobles accept a boy of fifteen? Merlin arranged a test — a test that has echoed through legend ever since.

The Sword in the Stone

There was a large stone placed in front of the cathedral doorway. And in the stone stood a sword with a gem-encrusted hilt. The inscription upon it read, 'Whoso can draw me from this stone is the rightful king of Britain.' All the nobles tried to draw the sword, but none could move it. At last Arthur tried. He drew the sword from the stone quite easily. He held the magic sword in his hand, and a cry of wonder went through the crowd, and the nobles fell back in astonishment. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XIII
The historian's note: The sword-in-the-stone test is Geoffrey of Monmouth's invention (12th century), not history. But it reveals what the legend-makers wanted to say: that Arthur's kingship came from something higher than noble birth — from destiny, from magic, from right. A modern general might say the same thing differently. Arthur was chosen because the people believed in him.

Merlin's Promise

Merlin declared:

This boy should be the greatest king who should ever reign in Britain. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XIII

And Marshall adds her own judgment:

And so Arthur grew up. He was the bravest, wisest and best king that had ever ruled in Britain. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XIII

The Round Table

Arthur's greatest creation was not a fortress or an empire, but an idea:

King Arthur, desiring to gather around him the bravest and noblest knights in the land, founded the Round Table. This table was made in such a way that there was no head. Therefore no one could claim a place of honor above the rest. All who sat at the Round Table were equals. Each knight was bound by a solemn oath to be true, loyal, and to protect the weak. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XIII
The historian's note: The Round Table is pure legend. But it's a legend that tells us something about what people valued: equality, loyalty, the protection of the weak. Medieval writers were imagining a better world than the one they lived in — where strength served justice instead of serving itself.

What We Actually Know About Arthur

So much for the legend. But what's the history?

The oldest sources: A 6th-century monk named Gildas mentions a British victory at the "siege of Mount Badon" against the Saxons (around 500 AD). A later historian, Nennius (9th century), names Arthur as the leader. But these sources don't describe him as a king — they call him "dux bellorum" (a war leader, a duke of battles).

What he probably was: Arthur was likely a Romano-British military commander — a war leader who rallied the remaining Celtic Britons against the Anglo-Saxon invasion in the late 5th and early 6th centuries. He may have won a great victory at Mount Badon around 500 AD, halting (for a time) the Saxon conquest of Britain.

Why the legend grew: In the chaos after Rome left, people needed a hero. They needed to believe that once, someone had stood against the darkness and won. The Arthur of history became the Arthur of legend — Excalibur, Camelot, the Round Table — a king who was everything a good ruler should be.

III. The Dark Ages (450–700 AD)

While legends of Arthur were being born, what was actually happening in Britain?

The Saxon Invasion and Settlement

When Rome withdrew around 410 AD, Britain was left to defend itself. The Saxons were not invaders in the sense that Caesar was — they were settlers. Marshall tells it with her usual narrative flair:

Across the sea, called the North Sea, there is a great country called Germany. The people who live in this country are Saxons. They are very brave and valiant fighters. When Rome could no longer defend Britain, the Saxons resolved to come and live there and rule it. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XI

Marshall tells the story of Hengist and Horsa, legendary Saxon leaders who supposedly arrived at Vortigern's invitation:

Then Hengist and Horsa, seeing what a fine country Britain was, resolved never to go away again. They resolved rather to stay and conquer it for themselves. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XI
The historian's note: Hengist and Horsa are probably legendary. But the broad picture is real: Germanic tribes — Angles, Saxons, and Jutes — did settle in Britain over the course of the 5th and 6th centuries. Some came as mercenaries invited to protect Britain. Others came as settlers. Gradually, they conquered or absorbed the remaining Celtic kingdoms. This wasn't a sudden invasion; it was a slow replacement of Romano-British culture with Anglo-Saxon culture.

The Conversion to Christianity

One crucial moment in the Dark Ages is the conversion of the Saxons to Christianity. In 597 AD, Pope Gregory (the Great) sent Augustine to convert the Anglo-Saxons. Marshall tells a wonderful story about Gregory and some beautiful children in the Roman slave market:

When Gregory was in the market place of Rome one day, he saw some fair-haired boys being sold as slaves. 'What is the name of these boys?' he asked. 'They are Angles,' he was told. 'Not Angles,' said Gregory, 'but Angels. They have faces like Angels. When I am Pope, I will send missionaries to convert their people to Christianity.' — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XIV

Marshall emphasizes Gregory's commitment to the mission. When Augustine expressed fear at going to England, Gregory responded with strength:

I am ashamed that you should be so cowardly. Go back again. If the people of England kill you, you die for others, even as Christ did. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XIV
The historian's note: The story about Gregory and the Angles may or may not be true — it comes from a 9th-century source. But Augustine's mission in 597 AD is real. He landed in Kent and converted King Ethelbert. The conversion was gradual, with setbacks, but by the end of the 7th century, most of the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms were Christian. This was hugely significant: it linked the Anglo-Saxon kingdoms to Rome and to the wider Christian world.

The Monks and the Light

Here's what makes the "Dark Ages" label misleading: while violence and chaos were real in lowland Britain, something remarkable was happening in the monasteries.

The great monasteries of Ireland and Scotland — especially Iona and Lindisfarne — became centers of learning and art. Monks copied manuscripts. They preserved the texts of classical authors. They created illuminated manuscripts like the stunning Book of Kells (created around 800 AD in Ireland). These monks were keeping the light of learning alive while much of Europe was in darkness.

The Monasteries: A Timeline

  • 563 AD — Saint Columba founds Iona monastery off the coast of Scotland
  • 635 AD — Lindisfarne monastery founded off the coast of Northumbria
  • c. 800 AD — The Book of Kells created in Ireland; represents the peak of Celtic Christian art

Why were monasteries so important? They were schools, hospitals, scriptoriums (copying centers), and refuges for learning. When the Roman Empire collapsed in the West, most classical texts would have been lost forever — but monks copied them, preserved them, and passed them on to the medieval scholars and eventually to us.

The historian's note: The term "Dark Ages" was invented by Renaissance scholars who thought the medieval period was barbaric and intellectually empty compared to classical antiquity. But it's a misleading label. The period from 450–700 AD saw the transformation of Britain from Roman to Anglo-Saxon, the conversion to Christianity, and the rise of monastic learning. It was dark in some ways — warfare, plague, the loss of Roman cities and literacy. But it wasn't empty. It was a time of change, creativity, and survival.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to think about and discuss.

Why do we need Arthur?

Arthur may or may not have been a real person. But why did people need to believe in him? What does a legend like Arthur do for a culture that history alone cannot?

Legend vs. history

Marshall tells the Arthur story beautifully, but she's not writing history. How do we tell the difference? When do legends help us understand the past, and when do they mislead us?

The Round Table and equality

The Round Table has "no head" so all knights are equals. But the medieval world wasn't equal. Was this a dream? A prophecy? A radical idea for medieval writers?

Why "Dark Ages"?

Monks were copying, creating, building. Yet we call it the "Dark Ages." Who decides what age is dark? When do we misunderstand a period because of the name we give it?

The monastery mission

Iona and Lindisfarne preserved knowledge while Britain fell to invasion. Without monks copying manuscripts, what would we have lost? What ideas might be gone forever?

Rome left, and then what?

The Romans gave Britain roads, cities, order, and language. They took it all away. How does a society rebuild when the structure that held it collapses?
