The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The apprentice from the hills

He was born in 1452 in the Tuscan village of Vinci — "Leonardo of Vinci," Leonardo da Vinci. He was the illegitimate son of a young notary and a peasant woman, and because of his birth he was barred from the professions and the university education his father might otherwise have given him. In an age obsessed with status, that closed door may have been a strange gift: with no Latin schooling and no fixed trade, he was free to learn from the world itself, by looking.

As a teenager he was sent to Florence and placed in the workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio, one of the finest artists of the day. There he ground pigments, cast bronze, and learned to draw — and, the story goes, painted an angel in his master's picture so beautiful that Verrocchio, humbled, put down his brush. Florence at that moment was the beating heart of the Renaissance, a city where money, ideas and rediscovered ancient learning all collided, and the young Leonardo drank it in.

But even then the pattern that would define his life was set. He was endlessly, restlessly curious — and he rarely finished anything. Commissions were begun and abandoned; patrons grew impatient. In 1482 he left Florence for Milan, offering his services to its ruler less as a painter than as a military engineer who could, he promised, design bridges, cannons and war machines.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was Leonardo shut out of a formal education, and why might that have helped him?

2. What did he learn in Verrocchio's workshop, and what pattern in his character was already clear?

1. He was born illegitimate, which barred him from university and the professions; but it left him free to learn by direct observation rather than from books.

2. Painting, drawing, casting bronze and grinding pigments under Verrocchio in Florence; his boundless curiosity — and his habit of leaving work unfinished — was already visible.

Discuss:
  • Leonardo learned by looking rather than from books. Can that kind of self-taught, observation-led learning still compete with formal education today?

Part 2 — The mind that would not stay still

Leonardo spent nearly two decades in Milan, and it was here that his genius spread in every direction at once. He designed pageants and stage machinery, planned an ideal city, and sketched an enormous bronze horse that was never cast. For the wall of a monastery dining hall he painted The Last Supper — a mural of such drama and psychological depth that it changed European painting forever. Yet even this was a kind of failure of method: impatient with traditional fresco, he invented his own mixture, and the paint began to deteriorate within his own lifetime.

All the while he was filling his notebooks. Thousands of pages survive, written right-to-left in mirror script, crammed with studies of water, birds in flight, the movement of the heart, plans for flying machines, tanks, diving apparatus and self-propelled carts. He dissected human bodies to understand anatomy from the inside, drawing muscles and unborn children with an accuracy no one would match for centuries. He studied light so closely that he perfected sfumato, the soft smoky blending that gives his faces their living, mysterious quality.

Almost none of the scientific work was published. He was obsessed with understanding, not with fame or completion, and so his discoveries stayed locked in private notebooks while other men, generations later, made the same findings and got the credit. When the French invaded Milan in 1499, his patron fell, and Leonardo — nearing fifty — took to the road again.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What went wrong with The Last Supper, and why?

2. What kinds of things filled Leonardo's notebooks, and why did so little of it influence his own century?

1. Impatient with normal fresco technique, he invented his own paint mixture for the mural, and it began flaking and fading within his lifetime.

2. Anatomy, water, flight, machines, light — pages of studies and inventions; almost none was published, so the discoveries stayed hidden and others were later credited.

Discuss:
  • Leonardo made discoveries but never published them. Does an idea "count" if you keep it to yourself — or does it only matter once the world can use it?

Part 3 — The smile that outlived him

Somewhere in these wandering years he began a modest portrait of a Florentine merchant's wife, Lisa del Giocondo. He never delivered it. Instead he carried the little panel with him for the rest of his life, returning to it again and again, layering those thin, smoke-soft glazes until the face seemed almost alive. We know it now as the Mona Lisa — and it has been admired, argued over and copied more than any painting in history, her ambiguous smile still refusing to give up its secret.

His last years were quieter. The French king François I, who revered him, invited him to France and gave him a manor house near the royal château, asking for nothing but his company and conversation. There, an old man with a paralysed right hand, Leonardo sorted his papers, and there, in 1519, he died, aged sixty-seven. Legend — probably invented — says he died in the arms of the king himself.

He left behind a strange, lopsided legacy: only around fifteen finished paintings, many of them incomplete, and thousands of pages of notes that the world would not properly read for centuries. Yet that unfinished, scattered body of work has done something almost no one else's has. It has taught us to see the human body, water, light and flight with new eyes — and it has kept the phrase "Renaissance man" alive as the highest praise we can give a curious mind. Five hundred years on, Leonardo da Vinci is still the person we reach for when we try to imagine the limits of what one human being can know.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is unusual about how Leonardo treated the Mona Lisa?

2. Describe his last years and the shape of the legacy he left behind.

1. He never delivered it; he kept it with him for years, endlessly reworking it — and it became the most famous painting in the world.

2. He spent his final years in France under the protection of François I and died in 1519; he left only around fifteen finished paintings but thousands of pages of pioneering notes, and the enduring idea of the "Renaissance man."

Discuss:
  • Leonardo is remembered as much for what he almost did as for what he finished. Is an unrealised idea worth as much as a completed one?

Grammar — present perfect (linking past to present)

Leonardo died in 1519, yet we say his work has inspired generations and the Mona Lisa has been admired for centuries. That's the special job of the present perfect: it takes something from the past and drops it, still warm, into the present. Master it and you can talk about a person's lasting effect on the world.

The form: have / has + past participle. "His notebooks have survived." "The painting has become an icon." Use has for he / she / it, have for I / you / we / they.
When we use it — and how it differs from the past simple:
• Past into present (still true / still relevant now): "His ideas have shaped modern science." The effect reaches into today.
• With for and since (something continuing): "The Mona Lisa has been in the Louvre for over two hundred years." "People have argued about her smile since it was painted."
• Life experience, time unspecified: "Millions have seen it." "No one has ever matched his notebooks."
• Compare — past simple = finished, dated moment: "He painted The Last Supper in the 1490s." Say when, and you need the past simple, not the present perfect.

1. Present perfect or past simple?

Put the verb in brackets into the correct tense.

1. Leonardo (be born) in 1452 in the village of Vinci. (a dated, finished moment)

2. Since then, his notebooks (inspire) engineers and artists alike. (past reaching into now)

3. The Mona Lisa (hang) in the Louvre for more than two centuries. (continuing up to now)

1. was born (a specific past date → past simple)

2. have inspired (effect continues into the present)

3. has hung (a state continuing up to now, with "for")

2. Build the present perfect

Complete each sentence with have or has + the correct past participle of the verb in brackets.

1. His anatomical drawings (become) some of the most accurate ever made.

2. Scholars (study) his notebooks for generations.

3. No other painting (be) copied as often as the Mona Lisa.

1. have become (irregular participle: become)

2. have studied (regular: study → studied)

3. has been (irregular participle: been)

3. Link the past to the present — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with a present perfect (or, where the meaning is a dated moment, a past simple). Read it aloud once you're done.

Leonardo (die) five hundred years ago, but his influence (never / fade). Since his notebooks were rediscovered, engineers (find) designs centuries ahead of their time, and the Mona Lisa (draw) crowds to Paris ever since.

died (a finished moment) · has never faded (still true now) · have found (past into present) · has drawn (continuing up to now)

Notice the pattern: name a fixed date and you use the past simple; connect it to the living present and you switch to the present perfect.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Leonardo's exclusion from formal education may have set his mind free. Does too much schooling ever get in the way of real originality?
  2. He kept his greatest discoveries private and others got the credit. Whose fault is it when a brilliant idea has no effect on the world — the thinker's, or the world's?
