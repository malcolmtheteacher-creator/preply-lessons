At the Consulate

The bag was on your shoulder at the market. Then it wasn't. Passport, cards, the lot — gone in a crowd, in a country where you know nobody. What's left: a phone at 40%, a police report, and a consulate across town that closes at four. This is the conversation that gets you home.

The Scene 🎬

Losing your passport abroad is the adult version of being lost in a supermarket — and there is a desk, in every major city, whose whole job is finding you.

It happened at the market: a crowd, a bump, and twenty minutes later the sick lurch of a hand finding no bag. You've done the first things right — cards frozen from your phone, police report filed, the report's reference number photographed. Now you're at your country's consulate, ticket 41, and the panic is arguing with your English.

Here's what changes everything about this conversation: the person behind the glass, Mr Haddad, has met this exact Tuesday a thousand times. Lost passports are not an emergency to a consulate — they are the day job. There is a procedure, it works, and it moves at the speed of the information you can give it. Which is the real skill here: panic tells the story ("and then I felt the strap and I just KNEW—"); the desk needs the data. Who you are, when you fly, what you still have, what you can get.

Everything you still have is worth more than you think: a phone photo of the passport page, someone at home who can email documents, the police reference. Offer it all. The traveller who arrives as a half-organised problem leaves as a solved one.

Mr HaddadConsular officer, fifteen years of other people's worst Tuesdays. Calm as furniture. Helps fastest when fed facts, not stories.

The Conversation 💬

Ticket 41. The glass window, the little microphone, and Mr Haddad looking up.

Mr HaddadForty-one? How can I help?
YouI've lost my passport — well, it was stolen this morning. What do I do first?
Mr HaddadStolen how — and is anything else gone?
YouMy bag was taken at the central market. Everything went with it — cards, passport, the lot. I've already frozen the cards and filed a police report — here's the reference number.
Mr Haddad*typing* Good — most people arrive without that and we send them straight back out. When do you travel?
YouThat's my worry. I fly home on Friday — is that still possible?
Mr HaddadFriday. Today's Tuesday… tight, but not impossible. A full replacement passport takes weeks. What you want is an emergency travel document — one journey, home only.
YouThen that's what I want. What can you issue me in that time?
Mr HaddadIf your identity checks out and the paperwork's complete today: the emergency document by Thursday afternoon, usually. The "usually" is doing some work in that sentence.
YouUnderstood. On identity — I've got a photo of the passport page on my phone, and a photo of my national ID card too. Does that help?
Mr HaddadConsiderably. Email both to this address while we talk. It's not proof on its own, but it makes the checks quick instead of slow.
YouSending now. And I can get more sent over from home — my sister has keys to my flat. Birth certificate, old passport, whatever helps.
Mr HaddadThe birth certificate, scanned, to the same address — tell her tonight. Now: two passport photos, from the machine in the hall, and the fee.
YouAh — that's my next problem. What will it cost, and how do I pay with no cards?
Mr HaddadNinety-five, and the machine takes cash only, because of course it does. Options: someone at home can pay online against your case number, or there's a money-transfer office two streets over where your sister could send you cash in an hour.
YouShe'll pay online tonight — she's faster than a transfer office. While the checks run — is there anything I can be doing? I'd rather be useful than sit in a hostel worrying.
Mr HaddadThree things. Photos, today. Your airline — tell them the situation and ask them to note it against the booking; they see this weekly. And keep that phone charged — if a check needs a question answered, we call once.
YouPhotos, airline, phone alive. And if something changes — if it slips past Thursday — who do I call? It'll be the weekend by then and my flight's gone.
Mr HaddadThis card. The top number is the desk; the bottom one is the out-of-hours emergency line — that one is answered on Saturdays, I promise. And forty-one? Breathe. You're the most organised stolen-bag of my week. Thursday will probably hold.
YouI was starting to panic this morning, honestly — thank you. Thursday afternoon. I'll be the one sitting by the door from lunchtime.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for any crisis at any official desk — consulates, airlines, insurance lines, hospitals abroad. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Panic speaks in stories; desks run on facts. Every phrase here converts fear into information — what happened, what's left, what's the deadline, what can I do. Calm isn't a feeling you wait for; it's a sentence structure you decide to use.

🧊 Turning panic into information

Phrase 1

The job: the opener — problem plus request for the process. Complete it:

"I've lost my passport — what do I do ___?"

"I've lost my passport — what do I do first?"
Notice what this ISN'T: the story of the market, the crowd, the sick feeling. It's the problem in five words and then the best question in any crisis — "what do I do first?" — which hands you the expert's map instead of your panic's. There is always a first step, and the person behind the glass always knows it.

Take it anywhere: "I've lost ___ — what do I do first?" Passports, wallets, phones, data. Name it, then ask for step one.
Phrase 2

The job: the full damage report, in one line. Complete it:

"Everything went with the bag — cards, passport, the ___."

"Everything went with the bag — cards, passport, the lot."
The lot = everything, all of it — the compact British way to close a list. The officer's first need is the size of the problem, so give the inventory upfront: what's gone, and (next breath) what you've already done about it. "I've frozen the cards and filed a police report" turned you, in one sentence, from a victim into a case that's already moving.

Take it anywhere: "___ went — ___, ___, the lot. Here's what I've done so far." The damage report shape, for burglaries, crashes, and IT disasters alike.
Phrase 3

The job: putting your deadline on the table immediately. Complete it:

"I fly home on Friday — is that still ___?"

"I fly home on Friday — is that still possible?"
The deadline is the single fact that shapes the entire plan — it's the difference between a normal application and an emergency document — and desks can't plan around a deadline they haven't heard. Say it early, as a question, and you get the honest odds ("tight, but not impossible") plus the route that fits inside it. Hide it, or mention it on Thursday, and no procedure on earth can save your seat.

Take it anywhere: "My deadline is ___ — is that still possible?" Repairs, applications, medical results. Deadline first; plan second.
Phrase 4

The job: asking for what CAN be done, not mourning what can't. Which one?

A. "So there's no way to get a real passport by Friday? None at all?"
B. "What can you issue me in that time?"
C. "This is a disaster. What am I supposed to do?"
B — "What can you issue me in that time?"
A argues with reality — the weeks-long passport is not coming, and re-asking won't shrink it. C hands your problem to the officer as an emotion. B is the crisis question that always works: given the constraint, what IS available? The answer here was the emergency travel document — a thing you didn't know existed until you asked what existed. In every bureaucracy there's a faster, smaller version of what you need. This question finds it.

Take it anywhere: "What can you do in that time?" Flights, visas, repairs, print deadlines — ask for the possible, not the perfect.

🧰 Offering everything you've still got

Phrase 5

The job: volunteering your evidence before it's demanded. Complete it:

"I've got a photo of it on my phone — does that ___?"

"I've got a photo of it on my phone — does that help?"
The traveller's cheapest insurance is a photographed passport page in the cloud — and the phrase that cashes it in is this one. "Does that help?" is the right ending: it lets the expert tell you what your scraps are worth ("not proof on its own, but it makes the checks quick instead of slow"). Offer everything — photos, references, screenshots. You are not qualified to know which of your scraps is gold; they are.

Take it anywhere: "I've got ___ — does that help?" And tonight, genuinely: photograph your passport, ID and insurance card, and put them in the cloud.
Phrase 6

The job: mobilising home. Put the pieces in order:

sent overI can get documentsfrom home.
"I can get documents sent over from home."
You are not one person at a window — you're a network, and the desk should know it. Someone at home with your spare keys can scan a birth certificate tonight; someone can pay a fee online against a case number. Naming your resources ("my sister has keys to my flat") lets the officer build them into the plan on the spot. Stranded is mostly a feeling; the network is real.

Take it anywhere: "I can get ___ sent over from home." Documents, money, confirmations — say who, and how fast.
Phrase 7

The job: the money problem, stated without shame. Complete it:

"What will it cost — and how do I pay with ___ ___?"

"What will it cost — and how do I pay with no cards?"
The embarrassing question that must be asked, because the answer always exists — desks that serve robbed travellers have met robbed wallets before. Ask both halves together: the amount AND the mechanism. Out came two routes you'd never have guessed: online payment against a case number, and a cash transfer office two streets over. The people who don't ask sit outside the answer all afternoon.

Take it anywhere: "What will it cost, and how can I pay, given ___?" Name the constraint; let them route around it.

🕐 Using the wait, and covering the risk

Phrase 8

The job: converting helpless waiting into a job list. Complete it:

"While the checks run — is there anything I can be ___?"

"While the checks run — is there anything I can be doing?"
The question that separates the passengers from the crew. There's almost always a parallel track — photos, the airline call, the charged phone — that shaves a day off the critical path, and officials hand these jobs gladly to anyone who asks. Bonus effect, not small: doing something is the actual cure for the panic. Hostel-ceiling-staring is not.

Take it anywhere: "Is there anything I can be doing while we wait?" Visa queues, hospital corridors, job applications — ask for the parallel track.
Phrase 9

The job: the what-if, arranged while everything's still fine. Complete it:

"If something changes over the ___, who do I ___?"

"If something changes over the weekend, who do I call?"
Thursday "usually" holds — and "usually" is exactly why you ask this on Tuesday, while the person who knows the answer is right in front of you. Out came the card with two numbers, including the out-of-hours line that's answered on Saturdays. The moment to arrange the emergency channel is before the emergency; afterwards, you're googling helplines from a hostel at midnight.

Take it anywhere: "If ___ goes wrong, who do I call — and does that number answer at weekends?" The fallback question, asked in the sunshine.
Phrase 10

The job: the human close — naming what they did for you. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "I was starting to panic this morning, honestly — thank you."
The small honesty ("I was panicking") is what makes the thanks real — you're telling him what the last twenty minutes actually changed. Consular officers spend careers being shouted at by the frightened; the ones who say this get remembered, and remembered kindly matters when your file is one of forty on Thursday. Note his gift back: "Thursday will probably hold." That's not in any procedure.

Take it anywhere: "I was starting to panic, honestly — thank you." For everyone who ever turned your worst morning into a plan.

The Echo 🕰️

In April 1789, in the middle of the Pacific, William Bligh was put over the side of His Majesty's Ship Bounty into a seven-metre open boat, with eighteen loyal men, five days' food, a sextant and no charts worth the name. The mutineers kept the ship. Bligh got the definition of stranded: four thousand miles from the nearest help, with a boat, a notebook, and what was in his head.

What followed is still studied: forty-seven days, four thousand one hundred and sixty-four miles, and he lost not one of the men in that boat. Not by heroics — by procedure. Rations weighed with musket balls, positions logged daily, every man given a job, every decision made on the information they had rather than the despair they felt. Bligh was, by most accounts, a difficult man. He was also living proof that in a disaster, the organised survive the odds that swallow the dramatic.

Your consulate window is not the Pacific, and Thursday is not forty-seven days away. But the method crosses centuries unchanged: inventory what's left, name the destination, give everyone a job, and move at the speed of facts. Panic is a story. Home is a procedure.
