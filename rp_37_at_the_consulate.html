At the Consulate

The bag was on your shoulder at the market. Then it wasn't. Passport, cards, the lot — gone in a crowd, in a country where you know nobody. What's left: a phone at 40%, a police report, and a consulate across town that closes at four. This is the conversation that gets you home.

The Scene 🎬

Losing your passport abroad is the adult version of being lost in a supermarket — and there is a desk, in every major city, whose whole job is finding you.

It happened at the market: a crowd, a bump, and twenty minutes later the sick lurch of a hand finding no bag. You've done the first things right — cards frozen from your phone, police report filed, the report's reference number photographed. Now you're at your country's consulate, ticket 41, and the panic is arguing with your English.

Here's what changes everything about this conversation: the person behind the glass, Mr Haddad, has met this exact Tuesday a thousand times. Lost passports are not an emergency to a consulate — they are the day job. There is a procedure, it works, and it moves at the speed of the information you can give it. Which is the real skill here: panic tells the story ("and then I felt the strap and I just KNEW—"); the desk needs the data. Who you are, when you fly, what you still have, what you can get.

Everything you still have is worth more than you think: a phone photo of the passport page, someone at home who can email documents, the police reference. Offer it all. The traveller who arrives as a half-organised problem leaves as a solved one.

  1. What's the worst travel disaster you've had — and looking back, what actually got you out of it: luck, people, or paperwork?
  2. Why does panic make us tell stories instead of giving facts — and what does it cost us at desks like this one?
YouShaken, bagless, flying Friday. Has: a phone at 40%, a police reference, a passport photo in the cloud, and a sister at home who answers fast.
Mr HaddadConsular officer, fifteen years of other people's worst Tuesdays. Calm as furniture. Helps fastest when fed facts, not stories.
First: read the conversation and notice the shape: every answer You gives is one fact plus one offer ("here's what happened — and here's what I've still got"). That shape is the whole lesson.

The Conversation 💬

Ticket 41. The glass window, the little microphone, and Mr Haddad looking up.

Mr HaddadForty-one? How can I help?
YouI've lost my passport — well, it was stolen this morning. What do I do first?
Mr HaddadStolen how — and is anything else gone?
YouMy bag was taken at the central market. Everything went with it — cards, passport, the lot. I've already frozen the cards and filed a police report — here's the reference number.
Mr Haddad*typing* Good — most people arrive without that and we send them straight back out. When do you travel?
YouThat's my worry. I fly home on Friday — is that still possible?
Mr HaddadFriday. Today's Tuesday… tight, but not impossible. A full replacement passport takes weeks. What you want is an emergency travel document — one journey, home only.
YouThen that's what I want. What can you issue me in that time?
Mr HaddadIf your identity checks out and the paperwork's complete today: the emergency document by Thursday afternoon, usually. The "usually" is doing some work in that sentence.
YouUnderstood. On identity — I've got a photo of the passport page on my phone, and a photo of my national ID card too. Does that help?
Mr HaddadConsiderably. Email both to this address while we talk. It's not proof on its own, but it makes the checks quick instead of slow.
YouSending now. And I can get more sent over from home — my sister has keys to my flat. Birth certificate, old passport, whatever helps.
Mr HaddadThe birth certificate, scanned, to the same address — tell her tonight. Now: two passport photos, from the machine in the hall, and the fee.
YouAh — that's my next problem. What will it cost, and how do I pay with no cards?
Mr HaddadNinety-five, and the machine takes cash only, because of course it does. Options: someone at home can pay online against your case number, or there's a money-transfer office two streets over where your sister could send you cash in an hour.
YouShe'll pay online tonight — she's faster than a transfer office. While the checks run — is there anything I can be doing? I'd rather be useful than sit in a hostel worrying.
Mr HaddadThree things. Photos, today. Your airline — tell them the situation and ask them to note it against the booking; they see this weekly. And keep that phone charged — if a check needs a question answered, we call once.
YouPhotos, airline, phone alive. And if something changes — if it slips past Thursday — who do I call? It'll be the weekend by then and my flight's gone.
Mr HaddadThis card. The top number is the desk; the bottom one is the out-of-hours emergency line — that one is answered on Saturdays, I promise. And forty-one? Breathe. You're the most organised stolen-bag of my week. Thursday will probably hold.
YouI was starting to panic this morning, honestly — thank you. Thursday afternoon. I'll be the one sitting by the door from lunchtime.

  3. What are You's three jobs while the checks run — and what are the two phone numbers for?

2. An emergency travel document — one journey, home only. A full passport takes weeks; Friday doesn't.

3. Photos from the machine today; telling the airline so it's noted on the booking; keeping the phone charged for the one verification call. The card's top number is the desk in office hours; the bottom is the out-of-hours emergency line — answered even on Saturdays.

The Echo 🕰️

In April 1789, in the middle of the Pacific, William Bligh was put over the side of His Majesty's Ship Bounty into a seven-metre open boat, with eighteen loyal men, five days' food, a sextant and no charts worth the name. The mutineers kept the ship. Bligh got the definition of stranded: four thousand miles from the nearest help, with a boat, a notebook, and what was in his head.

What followed is still studied: forty-seven days, four thousand one hundred and sixty-four miles, and he lost not one of the men in that boat. Not by heroics — by procedure. Rations weighed with musket balls, positions logged daily, every man given a job, every decision made on the information they had rather than the despair they felt. Bligh was, by most accounts, a difficult man. He was also living proof that in a disaster, the organised survive the odds that swallow the dramatic.

Your consulate window is not the Pacific, and Thursday is not forty-seven days away. But the method crosses centuries unchanged: inventory what's left, name the destination, give everyone a job, and move at the speed of facts. Panic is a story. Home is a procedure.
