IELTS Writing Task 2

Funnel Introductions  ·  Module 1 · Lesson 1

What an introduction has to do

An IELTS introduction is short — about 40 to 50 words, three sentences. Its job is simple: show the examiner you understood the question, and tell the examiner what you are going to argue.

The funnel: wide to narrow

Strong introductions move from a broad statement about the topic, to a focused restatement of the question, to a sharp position. That shape — wide opening, narrow ending — is called a funnel.

1. General statement about the topic
2. Paraphrase of the question
3. Your position + 2 reasons

Worked example

Question: Some people believe that schools should focus on academic subjects only. To what extent do you agree or disagree?

Funnel introduction:

The role of schools in shaping young people has long been the subject of debate.
Some commentators argue that schools should restrict themselves to traditional academic study, while others believe a broader curriculum is essential.
This essay strongly disagrees with the academic-only view, both because non-academic subjects build essential life skills and because they support the wellbeing that academic study itself depends on.

Paraphrasing — say it differently

Copying the question word-for-word is the single most common mistake in IELTS introductions. The examiner wants to see your own English, not theirs. Three techniques will get you most of the way:

The three paraphrase moves:

1. Swap words for synonyms — "children" → "young people"; "important" → "essential"
2. Change the word class — "the government should regulate..." → "regulation by the government..."
3. Restructure the sentence — turn "Do you agree?" into "Some argue that... while others..."

Common paraphrase swaps

Click each card to see useful synonyms for words that turn up in IELTS questions all the time.

people / children young people, individuals, adolescents, the public, citizens
"Many people believe..." → "Many individuals hold the view that..."
important / good essential, crucial, beneficial, valuable, vital, significant
"It is important that..." → "It is essential that..."
think / believe argue, claim, hold the view, maintain, contend, suggest
"Some people think..." → "Some commentators argue that..."
should ought to, are required to, must, have a duty to, need to
"Schools should teach..." → "Schools have a duty to teach..."
nowadays / these days in recent decades, in the modern era, today, in contemporary society
"Nowadays, technology..." → "In recent decades, technology..."
problem issue, concern, challenge, difficulty, drawback
"This is a problem because..." → "This is a serious concern because..."
help support, enable, facilitate, contribute to, assist, foster
"Sport helps young people..." → "Sport enables young people to..."
because since, as, owing to the fact that, given that, due to
"...because it is cheaper" → "...given that it is more affordable"

The thesis sentence — your position, sharply

The third sentence of the introduction tells the examiner exactly what you are going to argue. It does two things: states your position, and signposts the two reasons you will develop in the body. A vague or wishy-washy thesis costs you marks across all four criteria.

The thesis formula:

This essay [position] the view that [paraphrased claim], both because [reason 1] and because [reason 2].

Phrases for stating a position

Avoid "I think" — it sounds informal and weak. Use phrases that sound like an essay, not a chat. Click each card to see usage.

This essay strongly agrees that… Direct, confident, formal. Use when the question asks "to what extent do you agree or disagree?"
This essay will argue that… Useful for opinion essays where you take one side. Sets up a clear position before the body paragraphs.
In my view, … A short, clean way to introduce your opinion. More formal than "I think" but still personal.
It is my firm conviction that… Strong, formal, slightly literary. Use sparingly — once per essay maximum.
While there is some merit in [view], it is clear that… A "partial agreement" opener. Use when you mostly agree but want to acknowledge the other side.
This essay will discuss both views before concluding that… For "discuss both views and give your opinion" essays. Signals the structure you will follow.

Build a thesis

Each transform below gives you a position; your job is to extend it with two reasons. Reveal the model after you've drafted your own.

Question: To what extent do you agree that universities should be free for all students?
→ Write a thesis using "This essay strongly agrees that..."
This essay strongly agrees that university education should be funded by the state, both because it widens access for talented students from poorer backgrounds and because a more educated population produces clear long-term economic benefits.
Question: Some people think working from home is better than working in an office. To what extent do you agree?
→ Write a thesis using "While there is some merit in..."
While there is some merit in the flexibility that home working provides, this essay argues that office-based work remains more effective overall, both because face-to-face collaboration drives stronger team performance and because separating home and work supports better mental health.
Question: Some argue that traffic congestion can only be solved by raising the cost of fuel. Discuss both views and give your opinion.
→ Write a thesis using "This essay will discuss both views before concluding that..."
This essay will discuss both views before concluding that, although fuel pricing has a role to play, the most effective long-term solution lies in serious investment in public transport, both because it offers a genuine alternative to private cars and because it tackles the underlying cause of congestion rather than punishing individual drivers.

Discuss:

What is the difference between a "weak" thesis and a "strong" one? Can a thesis be too long?

Three introductions — rank them

All three introductions below answer the same question. Read them carefully, decide which is strongest, which is weakest, and which is in the middle. Then check the analysis.

Question:

Some people believe that the best way to reduce crime is to give longer prison sentences. Others believe that there are better alternative ways to reduce crime. Discuss both views and give your opinion.

  • "Crime is a problem in many countries today. Some people think long prison sentences are the best way to stop it, but other people think there are better ways. In this essay, I will discuss both views and give my opinion."

    Verdict: Weak. The opening sentence is generic ("Crime is a problem"), the paraphrase barely changes a word ("stop it" replaces "reduce"), and the thesis is hollow — "I will discuss both views and give my opinion" tells the examiner nothing about what your opinion actually is. No reasons are signposted.

  • "In recent decades, governments around the world have struggled to bring down rates of crime. While some commentators argue that the most effective response is harsher custodial sentences, others believe that more constructive alternatives exist. This essay will discuss both positions before concluding that, although prison has its place, rehabilitation and prevention offer a more lasting solution, both because they address the root causes of offending and because they reduce reoffending rates."

    Verdict: Strong. The opening sentence is broader than the question and sets context. The paraphrase swaps multiple words ("longer prison sentences" → "harsher custodial sentences", "alternative ways" → "more constructive alternatives") and restructures into a "while...others" frame. The thesis is sharp: clear position, partial concession, and two specific reasons signposted. Around 70 words — slightly long but defensible for a discussion essay.

  • "There is much debate today about how best to reduce crime. Some people think tougher prison sentences are the answer, while others suggest that different approaches would be more effective. This essay believes that prevention is more important than punishment."

    Verdict: Middle. The opening is competent, the paraphrase is decent (some synonyms, restructured into "Some...while others"), and the thesis at least states a position. But the position is too short — no reasons are signposted, so the examiner doesn't yet know what the body paragraphs will argue. Adding "...both because... and because..." would lift this to strong.

Discuss:

What single change would lift the weak introduction to "middle"? What single change would lift the middle introduction to "strong"?
