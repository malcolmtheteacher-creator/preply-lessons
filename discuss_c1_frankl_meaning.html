The Freedom Nobody Could Take

A doctor lost almost everything — his work, his family, his freedom. He kept asking one question: what is left that is still mine?

Simpler version: Open this story at A2 level →  ·  Read before: The Man Who Decided to Believe

Today: you'll follow a psychiatrist who studied what keeps people going when almost everything is taken — and learn to put the important idea first with cleft sentences: what mattered was… · it was… that…
Before we start. Talk these through out loud — there are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • When things go badly wrong, what usually helps people to keep going — and what does not help, even if it should?
  • How much of what happens to us is under our control? How much is how we respond?
  • Can you think of someone you admire for how they handled something very hard?

The vocabulary of this story

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. He had sewn his only copy of the manuscript — two hundred and fifty handwritten pages for a book — into the lining of his coat.
a manuscript (n) — the original text of a book or article before it is printed, written by hand or typed. Submit a manuscript to a publisher.
2. Nobody could say how long it would last, but some people found a way to endure it, day after day.
to endure (v) — to continue through something painful or difficult without giving up. Noun: endurance. Endure hardship / pain / a long wait.
3. After the war he reconstructed the lost book from memory and from tiny scraps of paper, writing it again from the beginning.
to reconstruct (v) — to build or create something again after it was lost or destroyed. Noun: reconstruction. Reconstruct events / a building / a manuscript.

B · Which word means…?

Three words: attitude · purpose · hardship. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which word means a situation of suffering, especially from lack of food, money or comfort?
hardship (n) — endure great hardship; economic hardship. Countable and uncountable: the hardships of war.
2. Which word means the way you think and feel about something, shown in how you behave?
an attitude (n) — a positive / negative attitude; an attitude to / towards life. Also (informal): She's got attitude = she is confident, sometimes rude.
3. Which word means the reason for which something is done, or a goal that gives life direction?
(a) purpose (n) — a sense of purpose; the purpose of the meeting; on purpose (= deliberately). Adjective: purposeful.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Everything was taken from him, but not his response to it. A response is…
  (a) an answer or reaction   (b) a punishment   (c) a question
(a) — a response (n) / to respond (v) — in response to a crisis; respond calmly. Related: responsible = able to respond for your actions.
2. Only a few people survived to see the end of the war. To survive means…
  (a) to live a quiet life   (b) to give up   (c) to stay alive through a dangerous situation
(c) — to survive (v) — noun: survival; person: a survivor. She survived the accident / survived on very little.
3. He believed that people need meaning in their lives, more than comfort. Here, meaning is…
  (a) the sound of a word   (b) the reason or value that something has   (c) a long explanation
(b) — meaning (n) — find / give meaning to; the meaning of life. Adjectives: meaningful (has value), meaningless (has none).

D · Word building & collocation

1. Build the nouns: free (adj) → ___ · to survive → a ___ (the person) · meaning (n) → ___ (adjective: without meaning).
freedom · a survivor · meaningless. Suffixes: -dom, -or, -less.
2. One verb completes all three: to ___ the truth · to ___ a challenge · to ___ the future.
to face — face the truth / a challenge / the future. Also: face up to something you would rather avoid.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then a question to discuss. The story is told simply and with care.

Part 1 — The doctor who studied hope

Viktor Frankl was born in Vienna in 1905. As a young doctor he worked with people who had lost all hope. Over the years he developed an idea: the deepest human need is not pleasure or power, but meaning. A person who has a reason to live, he believed, can endure almost any hardship.

By 1942 he was writing a book about this idea. He had spent years on it. But he was Jewish, and Vienna was no longer safe. In September 1942, Frankl, his wife Tilly, his parents and his brother were taken to a ghetto in Theresienstadt, and the book became impossible.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Frankl's main idea about human beings?

2. What happened in September 1942?

1. That the deepest human need is meaning, and that a person with a reason to live can endure almost any hardship.

2. Because he was Jewish, he, his wife, his parents and his brother were taken to the Theresienstadt ghetto, and he could no longer work on his book.

Discuss:
  • Is "meaning" a luxury for good times, or most needed in bad times? Explain what you think.

Part 2 — The coat

In October 1944 Frankl was moved to Auschwitz. He had hidden the manuscript of his book — about two hundred and fifty pages — in the lining of his coat. It was taken from him at once. In the months that followed, in several camps, he lost almost everything: his belongings, his work and, eventually, his wife, his parents and his brother.

He was a psychiatrist, and even there he watched people closely. He noticed that those who had something to live for — a person to meet again, or a task still to finish — often endured the hardship longer. He did not say this was a rule. He knew that luck, health and chance decided many lives, and that many strong, good people did not return. But he saw something that stayed with him for the rest of his life. Almost everything could be taken from a person. What could not be taken was the way each prisoner chose to respond.

To keep his mind alive, he imagined himself giving lectures after the war on the psychology of the camps. On 27 April 1945, American soldiers freed the camp where he was held.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to the manuscript, and what else did Frankl lose?

2. What did he notice about the prisoners — and what did he not claim?

1. It was taken from him when he arrived at Auschwitz. He lost his belongings, his work and, in the end, his wife, parents and brother.

2. People with something to live for often endured longer, and the one thing that could not be taken was how each person chose to respond. He did not claim it was a rule — luck and chance decided many lives, and many good people died.

Discuss:
  • He imagined giving a lecture about the experience while still inside it. Why might a future task help in a hopeless present?

Part 3 — Nine days

Frankl returned to Vienna and learned that nearly everyone he loved had died. He was forty, with almost nothing left except the idea. In 1946, he wrote a short book about his experience in just nine days. Its German title meant A Psychologist Experiences the Concentration Camp. It was later published in English as Man's Search for Meaning, and it has been read by many millions of people.

He went on to develop a method of therapy built on meaning, called logotherapy, and he taught and wrote for the rest of his long life. He died in 1997, aged ninety-two.

Frankl never said that suffering is good, or that people should look for it. His point was smaller and harder. When suffering cannot be avoided, people can still ask what it is for, and what they will do with it. Some critics say that his view is too hopeful, and that such a choice is not always possible. His answer was not to deny the horror, but to refuse to let it be the last word.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How and when did Frankl write his book?

2. What did he say — and not say — about suffering?

1. He wrote it in nine days in 1946, after returning to Vienna; it became Man's Search for Meaning.

2. He did not say suffering is good or that people should seek it. He said that when suffering cannot be avoided, people can still ask what it is for and choose how to respond.

Discuss:
  • Critics say the choice to respond is not always open to everyone. What would Frankl say back — and what do you think?

Grammar — cleft sentences

A cleft sentence splits one idea into two parts so that the important information comes first or last. It adds emphasis — useful when you want people to notice what really matters.

Three common patterns:
• What + clause + be + focus: "What surprised me was how quiet the city was."
• It + be + focus + that-clause: "It was a letter from a stranger that changed her mind."
• All / The only thing + clause + be + focus: "All she wanted was a quiet evening." · "The only thing we could do was wait."
Remember:
• After what / all, the verb be agrees with the focus: What I need is time. · What I need are answers.
• In It was… that…, the focus can be a person, a place or a time: It was in Vienna that he began.
• Don't add an extra pronoun: What he wrote was a short book (not what he wrote it was).

1. Rewrite it

Rewrite each sentence as a cleft sentence, starting with the words given. Say it, then reveal a model.

1. The manuscript mattered most to him. → What…
What mattered most to him was the manuscript.
2. Meaning, not comfort, kept some people going. → It was…
It was meaning, not comfort, that kept some people going.
3. He needed a reason to live. → What…
What he needed was a reason to live.
4. Only his attitude could not be taken from him. → The only thing…
The only thing that could not be taken from him was his attitude.

2. Complete the sentence

Write one word in each gap.

1. Frankl wanted to understand was why some people kept going.

2. was the thought of his wife that gave him strength.

3. All he wanted to see his family again.

1. What   2. It   3. was

1: the focus comes AFTER "was", so the first part is "What… wanted to understand". 2: the focus ("the thought of his wife") comes inside, so we need "It was… that". 3: "All he wanted" is the first part; the verb "be" follows.

3. Error hunt

Each sentence has one mistake. Find it, then reveal the correction.

1. "What he wrote it was a short book."
What he wrote was a short book. No extra pronoun.
2. "It was in 1946 when he published it."
It was in 1946 that he published it. We use "that" (not "when") in an It-cleft.
3. "What matters is how do you respond."
What matters is how you respond. After the cleft, the clause uses normal statement word order, not a question.

Take the pattern into the Speak tab — "what really mattered" is where cleft sentences belong.

Two sides

A note on the record: the dates and facts follow the standard accounts of Frankl's life. His ideas are discussed here carefully — he spoke from experience, and he did not offer a rule that explains who survived and who did not.

Would Frankl agree?

Say whether he would agree with each statement, then reveal.

1. "Suffering always makes people stronger, so it is good to look for it."
No. He never said suffering is good. He said that when suffering cannot be avoided, a person can still find meaning in how they face it. If suffering can be removed, he said, it should be.
2. "People who have a reason to live may endure hardship for longer."
Yes. This was his central observation — and he later developed it into a method of therapy.
3. "Those who died simply did not have the right attitude."
No. He knew that luck, health and chance decided many lives, and that many good, strong people died. Using his idea to blame victims would be a serious misreading.
4. "Even when we cannot change a situation, we can still choose how we respond to it."
Yes. This is the heart of his message — a freedom that remains when almost every other freedom has been taken.

The argument in brief

Frankl's strongest point. Say it in your own words, then reveal.
A person is never only a product of circumstances. Even where nothing can be changed, there is a small, real space in which we choose our attitude — and that space gives life a direction.
The critic's strongest point. Say it in your own words, then reveal.
Talk of "choosing your attitude" can sound cruel to people who are exhausted, ill or traumatised, and it can hide the unfairness of what happened. Survival was shaped heavily by luck and by other people's actions; a psychological theory cannot explain it.
The thread from the last lesson. James chose to believe in free will, with no proof. Frankl met freedom where it seems least possible. One began with a decision; the other tested the idea to its limit. Neither claimed that everything is possible — both claimed that something always remains.
Talk it through:
  • Is the freedom to choose your response a comfort, or can it also feel like a burden?
  • What is the difference between accepting what you cannot change and giving up?
  • Why do we need a reason to live — and where do people find one?
  • How should we use Frankl's idea so that it helps people without blaming them?
