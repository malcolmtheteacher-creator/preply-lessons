The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The government held a referendum on the new treaty.
referendum (n) — a vote in which citizens decide a single question. Example: “The government held a referendum on the new treaty.”
2. Campaigners launched a popular initiative.
initiative (n) — a proposal that citizens can put to a public vote. Example: “Campaigners launched a popular initiative.”
3. They collected 100,000 signatures in eighteen months.
signature (n) — a person's name written to show support. Example: “They collected 100,000 signatures in eighteen months.”
4. Turnout was low because of the bad weather.
turnout (n) — the share of eligible people who actually vote. Example: “Turnout was low because of the bad weather.”
5. Voters rejected the proposal by a narrow margin.
proposal (n) — a plan put forward for others to consider. Example: “Voters rejected the proposal by a narrow margin.”
6. Most democracies are representative, not direct.
representative (adj) — involving elected people who decide for others. Example: “Most democracies are representative, not direct.”
7. The result is binding, so the government must act on it.
binding (adj) — legally required to be followed. Example: “The result is binding, so the government must act on it.”
8. The campaign lasted several months.
campaign (n) — a series of activities to win support. Example: “The campaign lasted several months.”

The stereotype: “Switzerland is a perfect democracy: the people decide everything.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The Swiss way

Switzerland has the most developed direct democracy in the world. Any constitutional change must be approved in a mandatory referendum, and it needs a double majority: most voters and most of the 26 cantons. Citizens can also launch an initiative to change the constitution if they collect 100,000 signatures within eighteen months, and challenge a law passed by parliament with 50,000 signatures in one hundred days. The result is a few votes each year on questions from taxes to immigration to army equipment. Because the government knows a proposal can be challenged, it often negotiates with all the main groups in advance, which helps explain Switzerland's long tradition of power-sharing coalitions. Turnout is usually only around forty to fifty percent, which critics cite as evidence of voter fatigue.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is a double majority?

2. How does the threat of a referendum shape Swiss politics?

1. A majority of voters and a majority of cantons.

2. The government negotiates with all the main groups in advance, because a proposal can be challenged.

Discuss:
  • Does the possibility of a referendum make governments more careful, or only slower?

Part 2 — Rare and risky votes elsewhere

Most democracies are representative: voters elect politicians, who decide. Referendums are held less often and for a few kinds of question: changes to the constitution, as in Ireland, where every amendment needs a vote, including those on marriage equality in 2015 and abortion in 2018, or questions that politicians prefer not to decide themselves. In the UK, the 2016 vote on leaving the European Union was won by 52 to 48 percent, and legally it was only advisory, because parliament is sovereign, although no government felt able to ignore it. Referendums can also be used as a weapon: President de Gaulle called one in 1969 on reforming the French Senate and resigned after losing it. In Italy, some referendums are invalid unless over half of the voters take part, so opponents can defeat them simply by staying at home.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was the 2016 UK referendum only advisory in law?

2. What happened to de Gaulle in 1969?

1. Because parliament is sovereign and the result was not legally binding.

2. He lost a referendum and resigned.

Discuss:
  • Should a referendum result be legally binding, or should parliament be free to decide?

Part 3 — Too simple, or just right?

Supporters say that people should have a direct say on questions that change the rules, and that a vote can settle a conflict that parliament cannot. Critics make three arguments: a yes/no question hides complicated trade-offs, campaigns can be won by slogans and money, and majorities can overrule minorities, as in Switzerland, where women gained the right to vote nationally only in 1971, after a referendum of men, and one canton held out until 1990. A third approach is a citizens' assembly: ordinary people, chosen by lottery, study a question for months with expert advice and make a recommendation, as in Ireland in 2016 to 2018. Its proposals on abortion later went to a referendum, combining deliberation with a final vote.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What are the three criticisms of referendums?

2. What is a citizens' assembly?

1. Questions are over-simplified, campaigns can be won by slogans and money, and majorities can overrule minorities.

2. A group of randomly chosen citizens who study a question and make a recommendation.

Discuss:
  • Would a citizens' assembly be a better way to decide hard questions? Why or why not?

Grammar — Modals of Obligation and Permission

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
Modal verbs for rules and choices.
• must / have to — it is required: “campaigners must collect signatures”.
• needn't / don't have to — it is not required.
• can / may — it is allowed.
• should / ought to — advice or opinion. shouldn't — it is not a good idea.

1. Choose the right form

Complete each sentence about Should Everyone Vote on Everything?.

1. Campaigners collect 100,000 signatures: the law requires it. (must / may / needn't)

2. Swiss voters vote on every proposal; taking part is optional. (needn't / must / should)

3. Voters be asked to decide every technical detail, some critics say. (shouldn't / can / must)

1. must

2. needn't (don't have to)

3. shouldn't

2. Now try these

1. In the UK, parliament decide whether to hold a referendum. (can / needn't / mustn't)

2. For a vote to count, enough people turn out. (have to / don't have to / mustn't)

3. A referendum on a complex law be easy to understand. (ought to / needn't / can't)

1. can

2. have to

3. ought to

Going deeper

The case for and against referendums depends on the question and the context. A vote on a clear constitutional change after a long public debate is very different from a vote called to settle a party quarrel or to confirm a leader's popularity. The more complicated the question, the more the framing of the question and the quality of the information matter. Many political scientists therefore suggest that referendums work best when they are rare, well prepared and combined with deliberation.

Worth noticing: Modals: must / have to (obligation), needn't / don't have to (no obligation), can / may (permission), should / ought to (advice).
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Ask what a vote actually decides. When you read about a referendum, check whether the result is binding, how many people must take part for it to count, and who is allowed to vote. These details matter as much as the final percentage.
Dig into it — talk these through:

  • Should a minority have special protection against a majority vote?
  • How can voters be given reliable information before a referendum?
