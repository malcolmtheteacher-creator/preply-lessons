The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — The sickness comes to Europe

The story began far from Europe, in the dry grasslands of Central Asia. There, a tiny living thing — a kind of bacteria — lived in the blood of wild animals and in the small fleas that lived on them. A flea is a very small insect that bites animals and people. On its own, the sickness could not travel fast.

But in the 1300s, the world was already connected. Long roads carried silk, spices and food from Asia to Europe. These same roads also carried rats, and the rats carried fleas, and the fleas carried the disease.

In 1347, ships from Italy sailed home from the Black Sea. The sickness travelled with them, hidden in the rats on the ships. Sailors who looked healthy went home, but in a few days they were dead. The disease spread very fast, from the ports into the towns. People died of an illness that no one understood, and no cure helped.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the sickness travel from Asia to Europe?

2. What did the rats and fleas do?

1. It travelled west along the trade roads from Asia to Europe, and then came to Italy on ships from the Black Sea in 1347.

2. The disease lived in the fleas, the fleas lived on the rats, and the rats travelled with people and on the ships — so trade carried the sickness everywhere.

Talk about it:
  • Trade made Europe rich, but it also carried the disease. Can you think of something today that brings us good things but also problems?

Part 2 — Fear, death, and blame

The disease worked fast. First, people felt very tired and ill with a fever. Then big, dark, painful swellings grew on the neck and under the arms. Many people who suffered from these swellings were dead in three or four days.

So many people died, so fast, that there was no time to bury them one by one. Dead bodies lay in the streets. In many towns, people dug one big grave and put many bodies in it together. Across Europe, about one person in three died. Whole villages became empty. People who had money fled to the countryside. People who could not, stayed and prayed.

People were very afraid of the sickness, and they wanted to know why it came. Some blamed the stars. Some blamed bad air. Sadly, many blamed other people. In some places, Jewish communities were blamed for the disease and attacked, with no good reason — only fear. Fear led to panic, and panic led to cruelty.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to the body when a person got the disease?

2. When people were afraid, who did some of them blame?

1. First a fever, then big dark painful swellings on the neck and under the arms. Many people died in three or four days.

2. Some blamed the stars or bad air, but many blamed other people. In some places, Jewish communities were wrongly blamed and attacked.

Talk about it:
  • When people are afraid, they often look for someone to blame. Why do you think fear makes people do this?

Part 3 — Forty days, and a new world

No one in the 1300s knew about bacteria or fleas. But some towns slowly learned one thing: if you keep sick people away from healthy people, fewer people die. The port of Ragusa — today the city of Dubrovnik, in Croatia — made a new rule. Ships and travellers had to wait alone, away from the town, before they could come in.

They had to wait forty days. In the Italian language, "forty" is quaranta. This is where we get the English word quarantine. It is one of the oldest health rules we still use today, and it comes from the time of the Black Death.

The worst wave ended around 1351, but the disease came back again and again for years. The world after it was not the same. So many workers had died that the ones who lived could ask for more money. The old way of life, where poor farmers worked for rich lords, began to break. People trusted the Church less, because it could not stop the sickness. Out of this terrible time came big changes — in work, in money, and in how ordinary people saw their lives.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where does the word "quarantine" come from? What is special about the number forty?

2. Name two ways the world changed because so many people died.

1. The port of Ragusa (now Dubrovnik) made ships and travellers wait alone for forty days. "Forty" in Italian is quaranta, which gives us the word "quarantine."

2. Any two of: workers who lived could ask for more money; the old farmer-and-lord way of life began to break; people trusted the Church less; there were big changes in work, money and society.

Talk about it:
  • After the disaster, ordinary workers got more money and more power. Do you think bad times can sometimes make a country better in the end?

Grammar: verb / adjective + preposition

In English, some verbs and adjectives always take a small word after them — a preposition like of, from, with, at. You cannot guess these. You learn them together, as one phrase. This story uses a lot of them.

The pairs to learn, from this story:
• die OF — "Millions of people died of the disease."
• suffer FROM — "People suffered from the swellings."
• afraid OF — "People were afraid of the sickness."
• full OF — "The streets were full of dead bodies."
• ill WITH — "Many sailors were ill with a fever."
• good AT — "The doctors were not good at stopping it."
One easy way to remember:
• We say afraid OF and full OF — both use of.
• We say suffer FROM — for an illness.
• We say good AT — for something you can do well.
