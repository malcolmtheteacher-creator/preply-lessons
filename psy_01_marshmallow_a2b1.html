A four-year-old can wait for a second marshmallow. Will that child do better in life? Choose one.

The marshmallow test: real or made up?

In the 1960s, scientists put a child in a room with a marshmallow. Here are six things about the test. Some are real and some are made up for this game. Which are real?

1. The scientists watched the children through a window.
Real. The children thought they were alone.
2. The children had to wait for two hours.
Made up. They had to wait about 15 minutes. That is long for a four-year-old!
3. All the children were twelve years old.
Made up. They were between three and five.
4. Some children sang songs or covered their eyes to help themselves wait.
Real. Some children also turned their chairs round.
5. A child could have one marshmallow now, or two if they waited.
Real. That was the whole test.
6. Nearly all the children waited and got two marshmallows.
Made up. Only some children waited the whole time.
Talk about it:
  • What would you do at four years old: eat it or wait?
  • What tricks can help a person wait for something they want?

The evidence, in three steps

Before each part, make a prediction out loud. Then read, and check yourself.

Part 1 — The experiment

Before you read: How long can a four-year-old wait with a marshmallow in front of them: one minute, five minutes or fifteen minutes?

In the 1960s, a scientist called Walter Mischel did an experiment at a university in the USA. He worked with children who were three to five years old.

A child sat alone in a small room. On the table there was one marshmallow, a soft white sweet. The adult said: "You can eat this marshmallow now. But if you wait for me to come back, you will get two marshmallows." Then the adult left the room for about fifteen minutes.

New words in this part — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

Which word means 'to test something to learn about it'? (e__________)
experiment (n) — a test that helps people learn something. Example: "The scientist did an experiment."
I want to wait for the bus. It comes in five minutes.
wait (v) — to stay in one place until something happens.
Your call:
  • Would you wait for the second marshmallow? What would you do to help yourself?

Part 2 — What the children did

Before you read: The scientists found the children again years later. What do you think they discovered?

Some children ate the marshmallow in a few seconds. Other children waited. They sang songs, covered their eyes, or turned their chairs round. One child smelled the marshmallow, then put it down quickly!

About one third of the children waited the whole fifteen minutes and got two marshmallows. The scientists watched them through a window.

New words in this part — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

She has a lot of self-control. She never eats the cake before dinner.
self-control (n) — the power to stop yourself doing something you want to do.
This is a reward for your hard work: a free coffee!
reward (n) — something good you get because you did something well.
Your call:
  • Do you think waiting at four years old can tell us about a person's future? Why or why not?

Part 3 — Years later

Before you read: Can you think of a reason why a child might not wait, which is not about self-control?

The scientists followed many of the children for years. They said that the children who waited were often more successful later. They did better at school and often had better jobs.

But new research says the story is not so simple. Children from richer families often waited longer, and they also did better at school. Maybe family money, not only self-control, helped them. So what do you think is the real reason?

New words in this part — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

Later in life, she was successful: she had a good job and a happy family.
What does successful mean? (a) very tired   (b) doing well in life   (c) very young
successful (adj) — answer (b). Doing well; getting what you want in life.
Your call:
  • Is it wrong to take the marshmallow if nobody is watching?

If + will

Use if to say what will happen in the future when something happens first.

The pattern:
• If you wait, you will get two marshmallows.
• If she eats it now, she won't get another one.
The if part uses the present simple (not will!). The result uses will or won't.
Question form: What will you do if you feel hungry?
You can put the if part first or second: I'll wait if I can. (no comma in the second position).

Build it

Join the two facts so they mean the same as the model will show. Type your sentence, say it, then reveal.

1. You wait. You get two marshmallows. Join with if. if
If you wait, you will get two marshmallows.
2. You eat it now. You don't get a second one. Join with if. if
If you eat it now, you won't get a second one.
3. You save money. You can go on holiday. Join with if. if
If you save money, you will be able to go on holiday. (or: you can go on holiday)

Wait or not?

Five choices. Choose what you will do, then say why in one sentence with If I …, I will …. Then check an idea.

1. A friend offers you £10 today, or £15 next week. Which one do you choose?if
Idea. Many people take £10 now. Taking £15 is a bit like the second marshmallow: you must wait.
2. You can watch one episode tonight, or save the whole season for the weekend. What do you do?if
Idea. If you wait, the weekend will be more fun. If you don't wait, you will have a short happy time tonight.
3. You can buy new shoes now, or save money for a holiday. What do you do?if
Idea. Saving is waiting for a bigger reward. Which reward is more important to you?
4. A child sees a marshmallow and nobody is looking. Does it matter if the child eats it?if
Idea. Perhaps it matters less than people say. The child is only four!
5. Can you teach a person self-control, or are some people born with more?if
Idea. Many scientists think you can practise it, a bit like a muscle.

Now it is your turn to talk. Say as much as you can, and give reasons.

Later studies say the link is smaller than the first news said. Money and family life matter too. Some children do not wait because they do not trust that the adult will really come back with the second marshmallow. That is not a lack of self-control.

What do we use after if in a sentence about the future?
The present simple: If you wait, you will get two. We do not say if you will wait.
