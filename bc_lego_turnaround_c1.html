Saved by Fewer Bricks

In 2003 the world's most loved toy company was nearly bankrupt — because of everything it had added. The turnaround came from subtraction.

Easier version: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll work through the most successful turnaround in consumer business — and you'll master the conditionals that connect past decisions to present results: if they hadn't cut, they wouldn't exist today.

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Theme parks, watches, clothing, video games, a television arm — none of them profitable, all of them draining cash from the core. The company had overreached.
to overreach (v) — to attempt more than your resources or competence can support. Noun: overreach. Its strategic sibling: diversification drift — expansion that quietly abandons what you are good at.
2. Cash was running out faster than it came in, lenders were nervous, and the family owners faced the question no owner wants: sell, or fund another year of losses. The company was close to insolvency.
insolvency (n) — the state of being unable to pay your debts. Adjective: insolvent. Distinguish from illiquid (assets exist but cash doesn't) — the argument in every crisis meeting.
3. Each new set added new specialised pieces; the moulds multiplied, the warehouse choked, and almost nobody asked what one extra piece cost the whole system. Complexity had become the silent product.
complexity (n) — here in its operations sense: the hidden cost of variety. Complexity costs, to strip out complexity. The turnaround's central enemy.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: SKU · turnaround · core business. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means the rescue and recovery of a failing company?
a turnaround (n) — a turnaround plan / specialist / story; verb: to turn a company around. The genre this case defines.
2. Which term means the central activity a company actually wins at, as opposed to its sidelines?
the core business — back to core, core competence, non-core assets (the polite name for things about to be sold).
3. Which term means a single distinct product variant in a catalogue or warehouse?
a SKU (stock-keeping unit) — pronounced "skew". SKU count, SKU rationalisation = the unglamorous work of deciding what to stop making. Here, literally counted in bricks.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. The new chief executive began not with a vision but by stemming the bleeding. This means…
  (a) launching new products fast   (b) stopping the cash losses before anything else   (c) cutting the dividend
(b) — to stem the bleeding (also stop the rot) = halt the losses first; strategy comes second. Standard turnaround sequencing: survive, stabilise, then grow.
2. The theme parks were sold — not because they were worthless, but to free up capital for the core. This means…
  (a) release money tied up in assets so it can be used elsewhere   (b) borrow more   (c) reduce taxes
(a) — to free up capital. Related: to divest non-core assets, asset-light, and the harder verb underneath them all: to let go.

D · Collocation & word building

1. You know discipline as a noun. Complete the turnaround collocations: cost ___ · capital ___ · pricing ___ . And what is the adjective for a company that has none?
Cost discipline · capital discipline · pricing discipline. Without them a company is undisciplined — or, in analyst-speak, sprawling. This case is the story of discipline recovered.
2. Complete the two fixed phrases the analysts used about this rescue: the company went "back to ___" (= returned to its fundamentals) · and the cuts "restored the ___ line" (= profit). Two different words.
Back to basics · restored the bottom line. The case in one sentence: going back to basics is what repaired the bottom line. Also useful: the top line = revenue.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — Death by a thousand additions

By 2003 the Lego Group — family-owned since 1932, maker of the century's most admired toy — was losing money at a rate that threatened its existence. Sales had fallen sharply, debt had climbed towards the billion-dollar mark, and reported losses were running to hundreds of millions in a single year. Inside the Danish headquarters in Billund, the owners confronted advice that would have been unthinkable a decade earlier: the family might have to sell.

The strange part was how it had happened. Lego had not been lazy; it had been frantic. Convinced that children were abandoning physical toys for screens — the phrase of the era was that kids were "getting older younger" — the company had diversified in every direction at once: theme parks, clothing lines, watches, jewellery for girls, video games, television tie-ins, education ventures, its own retail stores. Each initiative had a plausible logic. Together they formed a company that did a dozen things adequately and could no longer say what it did best.

The rot ran right down to the plastic. Designers, freed from commercial discipline, had multiplied the number of distinct brick shapes and colours to somewhere around thirteen thousand — bespoke pieces, used once, each requiring its own steel mould at a cost that could run to tens of thousands of euros. Famously, the company found it was producing multiple slightly different versions of essentially the same minifigure police officer. Nobody could say with confidence which products made money. The best-loved brand in toys had buried its own economics under its own creativity.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Lego's position in 2003, and what advice reached the owners?

2. Why is "lazy incumbent" the wrong diagnosis here?

1. Falling sales, debt near a billion dollars, losses in the hundreds of millions — and advice that the founding family might have to sell.

2. Because the crisis came from hyperactivity, not complacency: panicked diversification into parks, clothes, media and more, plus an explosion of brick variety (~13,000 elements) that destroyed the economics invisibly.

Discuss:
  • Every diversification had a plausible business case. What was missing at the level above the business cases?

Part 2 — The thirty-five-year-old and the knife

In 2004 the owner stepped back from day-to-day leadership and handed the company to Jørgen Vig Knudstorp — a thirty-five-year-old former McKinsey consultant and one-time kindergarten teacher's son who had been at Lego barely three years. He was the first chief executive from outside the founding family. His opening message to the organisation was deliberately unheroic: there would be no inspiring vision for a while. Survival first. Cash mattered more than growth; growth would matter again later.

The knife went through the portfolio. The Legoland theme parks — emotionally central, capital-hungry — were sold to a leisure operator, with the family retaining a stake through its holding company. The clothing, the watches, the sidelines: licensed out or shut down. Factories were moved and consolidated. And in the warehouse, the least romantic rescue in toy history: the number of distinct brick elements was roughly halved, from around thirteen thousand towards six or seven thousand. Designers now had to justify every new mould against its full system cost; a new colour needed a business case. Creativity, the new management insisted, thrives on constraint — the brick itself was proof.

Just as importantly, the company turned back towards the people it had drifted from. It re-embraced the adult fan community it had long ignored, invited master builders into product development, and rebuilt its licensed lines — the Star Wars partnership, begun in 1999, had already shown the model — while pouring energy into the core play themes it had starved: city, castle, space. The product strategy fitted on one line: make the brick the hero again.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was unusual about the new chief executive and his opening message?

2. Name three concrete acts of subtraction in the rescue.

1. He was thirty-five, ex-McKinsey, three years at the company, and the first CEO from outside the family — and he explicitly refused to offer a vision, promising survival and cash discipline first.

2. Any three: selling the theme parks; shutting or licensing out clothing and other sidelines; consolidating factories; roughly halving the brick element count; forcing every new mould and colour through a business case.

Discuss:
  • "No vision for a while" from an incoming leader — refreshing honesty or dangerous vacuum? When would it be each?

Part 3 — The empire of constraint

The recovery compounded for a decade. Profitability returned within two years; growth followed; and by the mid-2010s the company that had nearly died of sprawl had overtaken Mattel to become, by several measures, the largest and most profitable toy company in the world. The franchises that might once have sprawled were now run with iron system discipline — The Lego Movie in 2014 was simultaneously a global hit and a two-hour argument for the product, and licensed themes ran alongside evergreen core lines rather than replacing them. Digital, the threat that had panicked the old management into a dozen ventures, was folded into the brick instead of chased instead of it: video games, apps and sets that talk to each other, all anchored to physical play.

Knudstorp's own summary of the turnaround has been quoted ever since: the company had to shrink to its natural size before it could grow from something solid; and the brand, he argued, gave permission for many things, but manufacturing gave the company its right to win in exactly one. The bricks in a modern set click after decades in a way competitors still struggle to copy — moulding tolerance measured in thousandths of a millimetre. What looked like a romantic return to heritage was, underneath, a hard-nosed bet on the one capability nobody else had.

The case's afterlife is where it gets argued about. "Focus on the core" became the sermon preached from every stage — but survivorship makes the sermon easy. Plenty of focused companies died focused; Kodak, from our previous case, could be described as fatally loyal to its core. Lego's rescue worked because the core it returned to was genuinely defensible — patented precision, a brand children trusted and parents revered, a system whose value grew with every brick sold. The live question the case leaves for any leadership team is brutally simple and usually dodged: is our core a fortress like the brick — or just the thing we happen to have been doing longest?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the recovery look like a decade on?

2. Why isn't "always focus on the core" the real lesson — and what is the harder question?

1. Profitable within two years, then compounding growth to become the world's biggest and most profitable toy company by the mid-2010s, with franchises and digital run under strict system discipline anchored to the brick.

2. Because focus only saves you if the core is genuinely defensible — Kodak stayed loyal to a core that was dying. The harder question: is your core a real fortress, or merely your oldest habit?

Discuss:
  • Apply the final question honestly: what is the fortress test for your own organisation's core — and does it pass?

Grammar — mixed conditionals in post-mortems

A turnaround story lives in two times at once: decisions taken then, results visible now. Standard conditionals keep both halves in one time; the mixed forms — the ones executives actually need — cross the boundary.

The two pure forms, for reference:
• Third conditional — past → past: "If the family had sold in 2004, they would have lost billions in later value."
• Second conditional — present → present: "If the core weren't defensible, focus would be no protection."
Mixed form 1 — past decision → present result (the turnaround sentence):
If + past perfect, would + infinitive.
• "If they hadn't halved the brick count, the company wouldn't be profitable today."
• "If the parks hadn't been sold, Lego would still be servicing that debt."

Mixed form 2 — present quality → past result (the character sentence):
If + past simple, would have + past participle.
• "If the brick weren't genuinely defensible, the focus strategy would have failed."
• "If Knudstorp were a sentimental man, he would never have sold the parks."

Inversion upgrade (C1): "Had they not moved in 2004, the company would not exist in its present form."

1. Which half is past, which is present?

Complete each mixed conditional with the correct forms of the verbs in brackets.

1. If the owners (listen) to the advice to sell, the family (not / control) the company today.

2. If the brick system (not / be) so hard to copy, the back-to-basics strategy (fail) within five years.

3. If designers (be required) to cost every mould from the start, the element count (never / reach) thirteen thousand.

1. had listened / wouldn't control (past → present)

2. weren't / would have failed (present quality → past result)

3. had been required / would never have reached (pure third — both past; not every post-mortem sentence mixes)

2. Build the sentence from the facts

Combine each pair of facts into one conditional. Write the full if-clause and result.

1. FACT: They sold the parks in 2005. FACT: The balance sheet is healthy now. → If

2. FACT: The brand is deeply trusted (a permanent quality). FACT: Customers forgave the crisis years. → If

3. Now invert sentence 1, dropping "if". → Had

1. If they hadn't sold the parks, the balance sheet wouldn't be healthy now.

2. If the brand weren't so deeply trusted, customers would never have forgiven the crisis years.

3. Had they not sold the parks, the balance sheet wouldn't be healthy now.

3. The retrospective — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the form the meaning requires, then read the whole paragraph aloud.

It is tempting to treat the rescue as inevitable, but almost none of it was. (the owners / not / step) back in 2004, a sale (be) the likely outcome — and the buyers of that era (strip) the company for its brand. If the new chief executive (be) a visionary by temperament, he (announce) a grand plan instead of stemming the bleeding, and the cash (run out) before any vision mattered. Going deeper

A note on the figures: debt, losses and element counts here are rounded, and accounts of the turnaround differ in detail between tellings — including the company's own.

What the case actually tests. First, the asymmetry of addition and subtraction. Every addition arrives with its own champion and its own business case; subtraction has no natural constituency — nobody's career is built on the products that were never made. That is why sprawl is a ratchet, and why reversing it needed an outsider with no emotional shareholding in any of the additions. The governance lesson sits above the strategy lesson: the owner's real contribution in 2004 was not a decision about bricks but a decision about who decides.

Second, the sequencing discipline that turnaround professionals preach and companies in trouble resist: survival before strategy, cash before vision. Knudstorp's refusal to inspire anyone for two years looks, in retrospect, like the most sophisticated communication decision in the case — because a vision announced during a cash crisis is heard as denial.

Third, the survivorship trap in the moral. "Return to the core" rescued Lego because the core passed what we might call the fortress test: hard to copy, loved beyond reason, and stronger with scale. The same sermon, preached to a company whose core is undefended, is a eulogy. The case therefore pairs with Kodak as a matched set: one company died of loyalty to its core, one was reborn by it — and the difference was never the loyalty. It was the core.

Talk it through:
  • Subtraction has no champion. What mechanism — a role, a ritual, a budget rule — would give it one in your organisation?
  • The decisive 2004 move was changing who decides, not what to decide. When is that true, and how would you know you're in such a moment?
  • Run the fortress test on three "cores" you know: hard to copy? loved? stronger with scale? Which failed which test?
  • Lego folded digital into the brick rather than chasing it separately. When is "absorb the threat into the core" superior to "build a separate digital unit" — and when is it denial wearing a strategy?
