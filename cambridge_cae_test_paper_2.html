The Science of First Impressions

Within a tenth of a second of seeing someone's face, the human brain has already formed judgements about their trustworthiness, competence and likeability. This astonishing speed, documented by researchers at Princeton University, raises uncomfortable questions about how much of what we consider "getting to know someone" is actually the slow confirmation or revision of snap judgements made before we have exchanged a single word.

The biological roots of this phenomenon are unsurprising. Our ancestors, constantly navigating potential threats, benefited enormously from rapid assessments of strangers. Those who hesitated too long before fleeing or fighting were simply less likely to pass on their genes. What is more surprising is that the same ancient machinery now operates in boardrooms and job interviews, contexts where split-second judgements are manifestly inadequate and often discriminatory.

Researchers have identified specific facial features that disproportionately influence these snap assessments. Individuals with wide-set eyes and slightly upturned mouths are consistently rated as more trustworthy, while those with protruding brows and square jaws are perceived as more competent. These perceptions hold remarkably steady across cultures, suggesting something hardwired rather than learnt.

The practical consequences are considerable, though not always intuitive. Studies of American judicial elections, where candidates' faces often feature prominently on ballot papers, have found that voters can be reliably predicted to favour candidates rated higher on "competence" by uninformed observers shown nothing but their photographs. Remarkably, candidates with allegedly "more competent" faces win elections at rates significantly above chance — even though the observers rating them had no idea who they were.

Such findings are disturbing but not necessarily cause for despair. Awareness of our biases is the first step towards mitigating them. Courts, employers and hiring panels are increasingly adopting structured processes — anonymous CVs, blind auditions and standardised interview questions — specifically designed to counteract the tyranny of the first impression. The fact that such interventions demonstrably improve decision-making suggests that we are not doomed to be governed by our instincts.

Nevertheless, in casual social settings, the first-impression effect remains stubbornly in place. Whether we are meeting a potential partner, a new neighbour or a future colleague over coffee, we have already decided much about them before the conversation has even begun. The challenge is to stay curious — to remain genuinely open to the possibility that our initial reading may be entirely wrong.

For questions 37-40, read the four views on individual climate action.

Text A - Professor Angela Torres, Climatologist

Individual actions, while well-intentioned, are a distraction from the only solutions that will genuinely matter: binding international agreements and large-scale decarbonisation of energy systems. When I see campaigns urging people to take shorter showers or avoid plastic straws, I worry that they give the public a false sense of progress while the real levers — industrial regulation, subsidy reform, carbon pricing — go unpulled. Personal responsibility, emphasised too heavily, can become a clever way for polluters to shift blame onto consumers.

Text B - James Okafor, Environmental Activist

I used to share the fashionable view that individual action was symbolic at best. I've since changed my mind. Not because I think personal choices will solve the climate crisis — they won't — but because people who make those choices tend to become more vocal, more willing to vote for climate-friendly policies, and more likely to pressure their employers and politicians. The "footprint" conversation may be statistically trivial, but psychologically it's enormously powerful. Getting people involved at all is half the battle.

Text C - Dr. Mei Lin Wang, Behavioural Economist

The research is clear: individual lifestyle changes alone cannot achieve the emission cuts required. A single person becoming vegetarian reduces their personal footprint modestly; that same person voting for ambitious policy makes a contribution orders of magnitude larger. This is not to say that personal actions are worthless — they can change norms, and norms matter. But the current obsession with consumer choices risks diverting energy from collective political action where it would be far more effective.

Text D - Rahul Ahmed, Community Organiser

I'm tired of the "systems versus individuals" debate — it's a false dichotomy. In my neighbourhood we've installed community solar, launched a repair café, and reduced food waste through collective buying. None of this replaces national policy, but it builds the social infrastructure that makes ambitious policy possible. People who experience climate action as something positive and communal, rather than as guilt and restriction, become far more effective advocates for larger changes.

The Return of Vintage Shopping

Second-hand clothing, once the refuge of students and those on tight budgets, has transformed itself into one of retail's fastest-growing categories. Sales of pre-owned garments are projected to overtake fast fashion within the decade, a shift few would have predicted a generation ago.

[41]

Environmental concerns have undoubtedly played a part in this change. Consumers, particularly younger ones, have become acutely aware of the fashion industry's staggering environmental footprint. Buying second-hand, quite aside from any aesthetic appeal, offers a way to participate in fashion without adding directly to the problem.

[42]

The rise of online resale platforms has also removed the traditional barriers to entry. Where once a dedicated shopper had to spend weekends trawling physical thrift shops, today a few taps on a smartphone deliver curated selections from sellers worldwide. This convenience has dramatically broadened the appeal of pre-owned clothing.

[43]

Interestingly, the economics of vintage shopping have shifted accordingly. Genuinely rare or desirable items now command prices that would astonish anyone familiar with old-style jumble sales. A well-preserved designer jacket from the 1990s can sell for multiples of its original price — sometimes more than current equivalents.

[44]

Even traditional luxury brands have taken notice. Several have launched their own "authenticated pre-owned" services, effectively legitimising a market they once ignored or actively discouraged. For companies founded on the notion of exclusivity, the strategic calculation is finely balanced but increasingly in favour of engagement.

[45]

Whether the current boom represents a lasting shift in consumer behaviour or a passing trend is hotly debated. Optimists point to consistent growth across demographic groups; sceptics note that previous retail revolutions have often fizzled out. What seems beyond dispute is that the stigma once attached to buying used clothes has essentially disappeared.

[46]

Whether this benefits the planet depends on what would otherwise have been purchased. If a second-hand coat replaces a new one, the environmental win is real. If, however, it merely supplements an already-overflowing wardrobe, the picture becomes distinctly murkier.

Paragraphs:

A. That shift in attitude is arguably the most significant development, because stigma, not price, had been the real barrier for previous generations. Younger shoppers now treat pre-owned clothes as a signal of taste rather than limited means.

B. What began as a reaction against mass production has, perhaps inevitably, become a commercial phenomenon of its own. The question now is whether this growth is truly sustainable, or whether the sheer scale of resale has introduced its own set of problems.

C. Industry observers identify several converging factors behind the trend. Rising prices of new clothing have been one driver, but explanations that focus purely on cost miss something more fundamental about how consumption itself is being redefined.

D. Beyond environmental considerations, many shoppers are also driven by a desire for distinctiveness. In an era of algorithmically recommended products, a rare second-hand find offers something that mass-market retail cannot: genuine uniqueness.

E. This inversion — where used goods outpace new ones in value — would have been unthinkable just a decade ago. It reflects a complete reversal of the assumption that clothes lose worth the moment they leave the shop.

F. The luxury bag market, for example, has seen extraordinary turbulence over the past decade, with investment-grade handbags outperforming many traditional assets. Financial analysts now routinely produce reports on the bag market's behaviour.

G. Not everyone is celebrating, however. Traditional charity shops, which once depended on cheap donations, increasingly find their best items pulled from the shelves and listed online for profit, eroding their fundraising model.

Part 1: Essay (220-260 words)

Your class has recently discussed how technology is changing schools. You have made the following notes:

Should schools invest more in digital technology?
  • Preparing students for the future
  • The risk of reducing face-to-face interaction
  • Equal access for all students

Some opinions:

"Digital fluency is as essential now as literacy was a century ago."

"Children spend quite enough time on screens already."

"Schools without modern technology leave poorer students further behind."

Write an essay discussing two of these points and saying whether, in your view, schools should invest more in digital technology.

Model (247 words):

Few subjects in contemporary education divide opinion as sharply as the role of digital technology in schools. Advocates insist that modern classrooms are unthinkable without it; critics point to children already saturated by screens. In my view, increased investment is justified, provided it is approached thoughtfully.

The strongest argument in favour concerns equality. Students from wealthier homes typically grow up surrounded by tablets, laptops and reliable internet connections; those from poorer backgrounds often do not. When schools fail to provide digital tools, they effectively entrench these differences, handing a long-term advantage to the already advantaged. A well-resourced school, by contrast, can level the field — at least in terms of exposure — and equip every student with the competencies employers increasingly demand.

Critics are right, however, to raise concerns about face-to-face interaction. Much of what makes school valuable — collaboration, discussion, disagreement conducted in person — cannot be replicated on a screen, and there is emerging evidence that over-reliance on devices may erode attention spans and social skills. Any serious technology strategy must therefore begin with the pedagogical question, not the other way around.

The answer, then, is neither wholesale adoption nor blanket resistance. Investment should focus on clearly defined educational goals — bridging access gaps, teaching digital literacy, supporting children with additional needs — while vigorously protecting the human aspects of learning. Used wisely, technology can enhance school; used thoughtlessly, it replaces things we cannot afford to lose.

Model Report (244 words):

Report on the Quality of Campus Food

Introduction
This report examines the food currently available on campus, based on informal conversations with students and staff, and suggests improvements that could be implemented without major expense.

Current situation
Most students take their lunch at the main canteen, with a smaller number using the coffee bar near the library. The overall quality is acceptable but unexciting. Hot meals are prepared in large batches from early morning, meaning that later diners frequently receive dishes that have been kept warm for hours.

Problems identified
Three issues emerged consistently. First, vegetarian and vegan options are limited, usually restricted to a single daily alternative. Second, freshness suffers, particularly after 1.30pm. Third, prices, while not unreasonable overall, climb sharply at the coffee bar, pushing students towards less healthy alternatives off campus.

Recommendations
I propose the following measures. Cooking in smaller, more frequent batches would dramatically improve freshness. Expanding plant-based options to at least three per day would better reflect contemporary diets. Finally, lowering coffee-bar prices on fruit and wholegrain snacks would steer students towards healthier choices without removing existing options.

Conclusion
The campus food service is functional but falls short of what students reasonably expect. The measures suggested above would be inexpensive to implement and are likely to improve both satisfaction and health outcomes across the student body.

Model Email (239 words):

Hi Jamie,

I was really pleased to hear that you're considering a gap year before university. I took one myself, and I can honestly say it was one of the best decisions I've ever made — though I completely understand why you're nervous about it.

A few pieces of advice, if I may. First, try to have at least some structure before you leave. Arriving somewhere with no plan at all sounds romantic but, in my experience, usually means burning through savings within a fortnight. A language course, volunteer placement or seasonal job gives you a foothold from which genuine adventure can follow.

Second, don't underestimate ordinary work. My three months in a café in Vienna taught me more about resilience and human behaviour than any amount of sightseeing could have. Routine, even abroad, turns you into a local rather than a tourist.

Third, keep in reasonable contact with people at home. Not daily — part of the value of the year is the distance — but enough that you don't return feeling disconnected. A weekly email to friends works remarkably well.

Finally, remember that returning to studies afterwards is much easier than people fear. If anything, having experience outside education makes the transition to university more rewarding.

Whatever you decide, I'm sure you'll handle it brilliantly. Let me know how I can help.

All the best,
Sam

Part 2: Long Turn (1 minute each)

Task: Compare two photographs showing people learning in different ways (e.g., a large university lecture and a small online class at home). Say what the advantages and disadvantages of each might be.
Model (about 1 minute): "Both pictures show students engaged in learning, but the environments could hardly be more different. The first shows a vast lecture theatre packed with hundreds of students, while the second shows someone alone at home attending what appears to be a small online seminar. The traditional lecture offers undeniable benefits — access to an expert in person, a shared atmosphere, and the energy that comes from being part of a crowd. The drawback, of course, is that genuine interaction is almost impossible at that scale. The online seminar, by contrast, allows for real conversation and flexibility, and students can participate from anywhere. However, isolation can be draining, and the lack of incidental social contact means learners may lose out on friendships and informal mentoring. Ultimately, I think each suits different subjects and different learners, and a good university probably needs to offer both."

Part 4: Discussion (5 minutes)

"Some people believe school should focus only on practical subjects. What do you think?"
Model: "I don't really agree, although I understand where that view comes from. Employers and parents worry that young people leave school unprepared for real life, and there's some truth in that. But I'd argue that cutting out so-called impractical subjects would be short-sighted. History, literature, philosophy — they might not pay the bills directly, but they teach you to think, to empathise, to understand context. Without those skills, practical knowledge becomes mechanical. The best schools, in my view, combine both."
"Do you think people today depend too much on technology?"
Model: "In some ways, yes, but I want to be careful about sounding old-fashioned. Technology has given us tools our grandparents would have dreamed of — instant translation, medical diagnosis, connection across continents. The problem isn't reliance on technology as such; it's our inability to switch it off. Constant notifications, for instance, fragment our attention in ways that seem to be affecting everything from sleep to relationships. So the real challenge, I think, is learning to set limits rather than rejecting technology altogether."
