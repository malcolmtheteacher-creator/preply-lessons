English has a small toolkit of words for talking about amounts, and each one has its own job: some for positive statements and offers, any for negatives and questions, and much / many / a lot of / a few / a little for saying roughly how much or how little there is. Some or Any?

For saying what you have, what you don't have, and for offering things.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "There's some cheese in the fridge, but we're out of milk."
  • "We haven't got any eggs — can you pick some up?"
  • "Would you like some more rice?"

Two of these use "some", one uses "any". Before you check: what's the rule?

Check the pattern CLICK
some → positive statements, offers and requests ("There's some...", "Would you like some...?").
any → negatives and plain questions ("We haven't got any...", "Do you have any...?").
The tricky exception: offers are questions, but they still use "some" — because you expect the answer "yes".
some + noun → positive statements, offers & requests
any + noun → negative sentences, plain questions

How Much? How Many?

For asking and talking about amounts — the difference is whether you can count it.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday questions and comments:

  • "How much time have we got before the film starts?"
  • "How many tomatoes do we need for the sauce?"
  • "Don't worry, we've got a lot of food for the party."

Which nouns go with "much"? Which go with "many"? And what's special about "a lot of"?

Check the pattern CLICK
much + a noun you can't count one by one (time, money, food, water...).
many + a noun you can count (tomatoes, eggs, people, days...).
a lot of works with both types — and in everyday positive statements, it sounds far more natural than "much" or "many" on their own.
how much / not much + uncountable noun
how many / not many + countable noun
a lot of + either type (especially in positive statements)
Common mix-ups: "much people" (should be many people) and "many money" (should be much money) — the noun decides which word you need, not the other way round.

A Few, A Little

For small amounts that are still enough — not none, just not a lot.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday comments:

  • "I've got a few emails to answer before lunch."
  • "Could you give me a little more time to decide?"
  • "There are a few shops still open near the station."

Which nouns get "a few"? Which get "a little"?

Check the pattern CLICK
a few + a countable noun in the plural (emails, shops, minutes...) — a small number, but some.
a little + an uncountable noun (time, milk, money...) — a small amount, but enough.
a few + countable plural noun
a little + uncountable noun

First, from earlier lessons

2. Say it out loud: what's the difference between a countable noun and an uncountable noun? Give one example of each.

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: countable nouns can be counted one by one and have a plural form ("one egg, two eggs"). Uncountable nouns don't usually have a plural ("milk", never "milks") and can't be counted directly by number — that's exactly why they need different words like "much" and "a little" instead of "many" and "a few".
