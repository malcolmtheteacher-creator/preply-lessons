Lesson 5 · The other 95%

The Dark Universe

Everything you have ever seen, every star, planet and person, is about 5% of the universe. The rest has never been directly detected, and we know it is there only by what it does.

What the universe is made of
5%
That is how much is ordinary matter, the atoms of stars, gas, planets and you. About 27% is dark matter and about 68% is dark energy. We have never made either in a laboratory or caught a single particle of either.

“Dark” here does not mean black. It means it neither gives off nor absorbs light, so we can not see it. We know about it for one reason: its gravity. The story is a detective story with a few big clues, and some of the clues are still open.

In the solar system, Mercury moves at 48 km/s and Neptune at only 5 km/s. Planets farther out move slower, because the Sun's gravity weakens with distance. A galaxy is also mostly mass in the middle. What should the stars at the edge of a galaxy do?
They should orbit more slowly than the stars near the centre. That is the prediction. What Vera Rubin found in the 1970s is that they don't. The orbital speed stays about the same, all the way out. Something unseen is pulling on them.
How can you weigh something that gives off no light?
By what its gravity does to other things. It makes stars and galaxies orbit faster than they should, and it bends the path of light passing through it. Mass tells space how to curve, and curved space tells light where to go. That is exactly what Einstein's general relativity says.
Part 1

Three clues, forty years apart

1933. The Swiss astronomer Fritz Zwicky measured the speeds of galaxies in the Coma Cluster. They were moving much too fast for the cluster to stay together given the mass of its visible stars. He concluded that there had to be a large amount of unseen matter, and called it dunkle Materie, dark matter. Few took it seriously.

1970s. Vera Rubin and Kent Ford measured how fast stars and gas orbit in spiral galaxies, starting with Andromeda. The result was the same in galaxy after galaxy: a flat rotation curve. Rubin, who in 1965 had been the first woman officially permitted to observe at the Palomar Observatory, made the case so carefully that the field had to accept it. She never received a Nobel Prize. In 2025 the world's most powerful survey telescope, the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile, with a 3.2-gigapixel camera, began its work in her name.

2006. The Bullet Cluster is two galaxy clusters that have collided and passed through each other. Most of the ordinary matter is hot gas, and the gas of the two clusters crashed into each other and stayed in the middle. But maps of where the mass is, made using gravitational lensing, show the mass passed straight through, to the outsides. The mass is where the visible gas is not.

Part 2

Gravitational lensing

A mass bends light. Einstein predicted it in 1915 and in 1919 Arthur Eddington led an expedition to photograph stars near the Sun during a total solar eclipse. The stars appeared displaced, by just the amount Einstein had predicted. It made Einstein world-famous overnight.

A big enough mass, such as a galaxy cluster, acts like a lens. If a distant galaxy lies behind it, you see arcs, multiple images, even a perfect Einstein ring. The amount of bending tells you the total mass of the lens, whether it shines or not. When astronomers do this they find far more mass than the stars and gas account for.

Dark matter is a very good description: it explains rotation curves, lensing, the CMB patterns and the way galaxies cluster in a cosmic web. But nobody has found the particle. That is a genuine open problem, and a young physicist could be the one who solves it.

Part 4

Dark energy

By the 1990s, astronomers expected the expansion of the universe to be slowing, because all the matter in it pulls everything together. Two teams set out to measure how much. They used Type Ia supernovae, white dwarfs that explode at nearly the same brightness every time, as “standard candles”. In 1998 both teams reported the same shocking answer: the distant supernovae were dimmer than expected, so farther away than they should be. The expansion of the universe is speeding up. Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess shared the 2011 Nobel Prize.

Whatever drives that is called dark energy. The simplest candidate is a constant energy of empty space itself, the cosmological constant Λ, which Einstein added to his equations in 1917 and later abandoned. A story says he called it his biggest mistake. That quotation is second-hand, and may be a legend. Quantum theory predicts a value for the energy of empty space that is wrong by a huge factor, by one estimate about 10120, which is sometimes called the worst prediction in physics.

A fresh hint. In 2025 results from the DESI survey, which has mapped millions of galaxies in 3D, gave hints that dark energy may not be perfectly constant, and may be weakening. The evidence is not strong enough to count as a discovery. If it holds, it will change cosmology.

How does it end? If dark energy stays constant, galaxies drift apart forever and stars burn out: a cold, dark Big Freeze. Use the lab to explore the other options.

Rabbit holes

NeutrinosNeutrinos are real, almost invisible, and everywhere, but they are too light and fast to be the dark matter. Why does it matter that dark matter must be “cold” (slow) to build galaxies?
The cosmic webComputer simulations that start with dark matter and the CMB ripples grow a web of filaments and voids that matches the real galaxy map. Take dark matter away and no galaxies form in time.
EuclidLaunched in 2023, the European Euclid telescope is mapping the shapes of billions of galaxies to measure how lensing, and so dark matter and dark energy, shaped the universe.
The vacuumWhat is the energy of empty space, and why is the answer from quantum theory so wrong? Solving it might unite quantum theory and gravity.
