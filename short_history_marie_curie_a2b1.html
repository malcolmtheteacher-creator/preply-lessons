Marie Curie

The scientist who discovered new things — and changed the world.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — A girl who wanted to learn

Marie Curie was born in Warsaw, in Poland, in 1867. Her name at birth was Maria Skłodowska. She was very clever and she loved to learn. But there was a big problem: the university in her city did not allow women to study.

So Maria studied in secret. She went to special classes that students called the "Flying University." These classes changed location all the time, so the police could not find them. Maria also worked hard as a teacher for many years. She saved her money, and finally, in 1891, she travelled to Paris to study science.

In Paris, she studied at a famous university called the Sorbonne. She was very poor and often hungry, but she was the best student in physics. There she met a kind scientist called Pierre Curie. They worked together, they fell in love, and they got married.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why could Maria not study at the university in Warsaw?

2. Where did she go to study science, and who did she meet there?

1. The university in Warsaw did not allow women to study, so she studied in secret at the "Flying University."

2. She went to Paris (to the Sorbonne) and met the scientist Pierre Curie, who became her husband.

Talk about it:
  • Maria could not study because she was a woman. Is it always fair who can study and who cannot? What do you think?

Part 2 — Two new elements

Marie and Pierre studied a strange thing: some materials give off invisible rays. Marie made a new word for this — she called it "radioactivity." Then she found something amazing inside a black rock. There were two new elements that nobody knew about!

She gave the first one the name "polonium," after her country, Poland. The second one was called "radium." It even glowed in the dark! To find them, Marie and Pierre worked for four years in a cold, old shed. It was very hard work, but they never gave up.

In 1903, Marie won the Nobel Prize — a very important science prize. She was the first woman in the world to win it. Later, in 1911, she won a second Nobel Prize. Even today, she is the only person who has won this prize in two different sciences.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What two new elements did Marie and Pierre discover?

2. Why are Marie's Nobel Prizes special?

1. They discovered polonium (named after Poland) and radium.

2. She was the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, and she is the only person to win it in two different sciences.

Talk about it:
  • Marie gave her new element the name of her country, Poland. Why do you think this was important to her?

Part 3 — The price she paid

Radium was exciting, but it was also dangerous. At that time, nobody knew this. Marie held the materials in her hands for many years. She did not know that the rays were slowly making her ill.

When a big war came (the First World War), Marie wanted to help. She built special cars with X-ray machines inside. She drove them to the soldiers, and doctors used them to find bullets inside the men. Marie helped more than a million wounded soldiers.

Marie Curie died in 1934. The dangerous rays, which she had worked with all her life, had made her very ill. But her legacy is everywhere today. Doctors still use her ideas to treat cancer. And here is a strange fact: her notebooks are still dangerous! They are kept in special boxes, and you must wear protection to touch them.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Marie help in the war?

2. Why are her notebooks still dangerous today?

1. She built cars with X-ray machines and drove them to the soldiers, so doctors could find bullets inside the wounded men.

2. They are still radioactive from her work, so they are kept in special boxes and you need protection to touch them.

Talk about it:
  • Marie's work was famous and useful, but it also made her ill. Was it worth it? What do you think?

Grammar: relative clauses (who / which / that)

We use relative clauses to give more information about a person or a thing. They join two short ideas into one longer sentence.

who = for people: "Marie was a scientist who discovered radium."
which = for things: "Radium is an element which glows in the dark."
that = for people OR things: "the prize that she won," "the woman that helped the soldiers."

1. Choose who, which or that

1. Marie was a scientist won two Nobel Prizes.

2. Radium is an element glows in the dark.

3. Pierre was the man worked with Marie in the shed.

1. who (or that) — a person

2. which (or that) — a thing

3. who (or that) — a person

2. Join the two sentences

Make one sentence using who or which. Then read it out loud.

1. Marie discovered an element. The element was called radium.
→ Marie discovered an element was called radium.

2. She helped the soldiers. The soldiers were wounded in the war.
→ She helped the soldiers were wounded in the war.

3. She built cars. The cars had X-ray machines inside.
→ She built cars had X-ray machines inside.

1. ...an element which / that was called radium.

2. ...the soldiers who / that were wounded in the war.

3. ...cars which / that had X-ray machines inside.
