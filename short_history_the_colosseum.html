The Colosseum

Rome's giant arena — built to thrill a city, and to control it.

Today: you'll tell the story of the greatest building of the ancient world, and discuss why those in power give the people spectacle — using relative and purpose clauses and the language of arenas and crowds.

Key words — meet them first

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Read each word and try to say what you think it means; then reveal the gloss and check how close you were. Read every example aloud.

1. An amphitheatre is a great oval building with seating rising all the way around a central space, so that thousands can look down on whatever happens below.
amphitheatre (n) — a round or oval open building with rows of seats surrounding a central performance space.
2. Down in the arena, the sand soaked up the blood — this was the floor where the fighting and the dying took place.
arena (n) — the central floor of an amphitheatre or stadium where the action happens. (From the Latin for "sand.")
3. A gladiator was a trained fighter — often a slave or a prisoner — who fought, sometimes to the death, for the entertainment of the crowd.
gladiator (n) — a man trained to fight, with weapons, as a public spectacle in ancient Rome.
4. The games were a spectacle on a scale the world had never seen — a vast, dazzling show designed to overwhelm everyone watching.
spectacle (n) — an impressive, large-scale public display or show, meant to be watched and admired. (adj: spectacular)
5. Every spectator leaned forward as the gates swung open, tens of thousands of faces ringing the arena, hungry for the next contest.
spectator (n) — a person who watches an event or show; a member of the audience.
6. To inaugurate a great building was to open it officially with ceremony and celebration, launching it before the whole city.
to inaugurate (v) — to open or begin something officially, often with a ceremony. (n: inauguration)
7. It was a colossal structure — a vast ring of stone so huge that it seemed to dwarf every street and building around it.
colossal / vast (adj) — extremely large; enormous in size or scale.
8. The games were a populist gesture — a calculated act designed to please ordinary people and win their loyalty.
populist (adj) — designed to appeal to ordinary people and their wishes, often to gain their support.
9. A clever ruler always knew how to appease a restless crowd — to calm them and keep them on his side.
to appease (the crowd) (v) — to calm or satisfy people, especially by giving them what they want.
10. Long after the cheering fades, a great monument leaves its legacy — what later ages inherit and remember it by.
legacy (n) — what is left behind by a person, time or event; a lasting effect or inheritance.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Built on a hated man's lake

At the very heart of Rome stands a colossal ring of stone that the world now calls the Colosseum — though that was never its real name. Properly, it is the Flavian Amphitheatre, and the story of why it was built begins not with celebration but with hatred. The land on which it rises had belonged to Nero, the emperor whose cruelty and extravagance Rome had come to despise. Nero had seized a vast stretch of the city centre for a private palace and an ornamental lake, all for his own pleasure.

When a new emperor, Vespasian, came to power around 72 AD, he made a shrewd and deliberately populist gesture. On the site of Nero's private lake — land the people felt had been stolen from them — he began a gigantic public amphitheatre, a gift to the very citizens Nero had robbed. The message was unmistakable: where a tyrant took, the new dynasty gives back. It was funded largely by the spoils of Rome's conquest of Jerusalem in 70 AD — the building was, in a real sense, paid for by an empire's wars.

The name "Colosseum" probably came later, from a colossal statue of Nero that had once stood nearby. So the arena that became the eternal symbol of Rome carries, in its very nickname, the shadow of the man whose ruin made it possible.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Whose land was the Colosseum built on, and why did that choice matter politically?

2. What was the building's real name, and where does the nickname "Colosseum" probably come from?

1. It was built on the site of a private lake belonging to the hated former emperor Nero. Giving that land back to the people as a public arena was a deliberate, populist gesture by Vespasian.

2. Its real name is the Flavian Amphitheatre; "Colosseum" probably comes from a colossal statue of Nero that once stood nearby.

Discuss:
  • Vespasian turned a hated tyrant's private playground into a gift for the people. How powerful a political move was that — and can you think of a modern leader doing something similar?

Part 2 — A hundred days of blood

Vespasian did not live to see his arena finished. It was completed in 80 AD by his son and successor, Titus, who inaugurated it with games that lasted a hundred days. The scale was staggering. The amphitheatre could hold somewhere between fifty and eighty thousand spectators — an average of around sixty-five thousand — and every one of them had a place fixed by rank, from senators down at the sand to the poor crammed high at the top. (Later the building was modified again under Titus's brother Domitian; together, these three emperors were the Flavian dynasty, which is why its true name is the Flavian Amphitheatre.)

And what those spectators came to see was spectacle of every imaginable kind. There were gladiatorial contests, where trained fighters faced one another, sometimes to the death. There were wild-animal hunts, public executions, and grand re-enactments of famous battles. For a brief, astonishing period, the arena could even be flooded for mock sea battles. Ancient writers claim that at the inaugural games alone, more than nine thousand animals were killed.

It was entertainment as overwhelming display — a machine built to thrill, to shock, and to remind every citizen of the sheer power of Rome and of the emperor who provided it all, free of charge.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who finished and opened the Colosseum, and roughly how many people could it hold?

2. Name three different kinds of event staged in the arena.

1. Titus, son of Vespasian, completed it in 80 AD and inaugurated it with a hundred days of games. It held roughly 50,000–80,000 spectators (an average of around 65,000).

2. Any three of: gladiatorial contests, wild-animal hunts, public executions, re-enactments of famous battles, and (briefly) mock sea battles.

Discuss:
  • Seating in the Colosseum mirrored Roman society exactly — senators at the front, the poor at the top. What does a society's choice of who sits where tell us about its values?

Part 3 — Bread, circuses, and a lasting symbol

None of this was simply about fun. The games were politics by other means. By giving the people spectacle on this colossal scale — and giving it for free — the emperors bought loyalty, distracted discontent, and made themselves indispensable. A satisfied crowd is a quiet crowd; a thrilled one is a grateful one. The Roman poet who coined the phrase "bread and circuses" understood it perfectly: feed the people and entertain them, and they will not trouble you about power. The Colosseum was the greatest circus of them all, a vast instrument for appeasing a whole city.

For centuries the arena roared. Then, as the empire declined, the games ended, the building was damaged by earthquakes, and much of its stone was carried off to construct other parts of Rome. And yet it never disappeared. The shell endured, too vast and too magnificent to erase.

Two thousand years on, the Colosseum still stands at the centre of Rome — battered, half-ruined, and utterly unmistakable. It remains the enduring symbol of the city, and of the Roman appetite for spectacle: a monument to a civilisation that knew, better than almost any since, how to give a crowd exactly what it wanted.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the phrase "bread and circuses" mean, and how did the Colosseum fit it?

2. Why does the Colosseum still stand today, even partly ruined?

1. It means keeping people content and obedient by giving them food and entertainment instead of real power. The free, spectacular games were a tool for appeasing the city and securing the emperors' loyalty.

2. Although it was damaged by earthquakes and stripped of much of its stone, it was too vast and magnificent to be erased, and it endures as the enduring symbol of Rome and its appetite for spectacle.

Discuss:
  • The Colosseum was built to entertain a city, yet it has outlived the empire that made it by two thousand years. Why do you think this particular building became the symbol of Rome?

The grammar this story runs on: relative & purpose clauses

To describe a great building — and to explain why it was built — you need two tools constantly. One tells us which thing or person you mean; the other tells us what something is for. Master both and your descriptions become precise and persuasive.

The two tools:
• Relative clauses — add information about a noun, using who (people), which / that (things), where (places): "the emperor who built it," "the arena where gladiators fought," "the lake which Nero owned."
• Purpose clauses — explain the reason or aim, using to + verb, in order to + verb, or so that + clause: "He built it to appease the people," "in order to win their loyalty," "so that the people would love him."

1. Relative clauses — who, which/that, where

4. The spectators, filled every level by rank, came to see blood.

1. who (a person — the emperor)

2. Purpose clauses — to / in order to / so that

Complete each sentence to explain the aim. Use to or in order to before a verb, and so that before a full clause (with a subject).

1. Vespasian gave the people the arena appease them. (to + verb)

2. The emperor staged free games the people would stay loyal. (so that…)

3. He built it on Nero's land send a clear political message. (in order to + verb)

2. so that (so that the people would stay loyal — note "would" after a past main verb)

3. Put them together — combine the sentences

Join each pair into one sentence using a relative clause and a purpose clause. Then read your version aloud. (Sample answers shown — yours may differ.)

1. Titus inaugurated the building. He wanted to thrill the whole city.

2. The Colosseum is an arena. Emperors used it. They wanted to control the crowd.

2. The Colosseum is the arena where emperors staged spectacle in order to control the crowd.

Notice how the relative clause says which thing you mean, and the purpose clause says what it was for.

To finish — the bigger questions
