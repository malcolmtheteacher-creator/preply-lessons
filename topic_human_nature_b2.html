The Real Lord of the Flies

A famous novel says that without rules we turn on each other. This is the true story of six boys who were shipwrecked for fifteen months — and of the scientists who tested human honesty with 17,000 lost wallets.

Today: you'll read three true stories that test a very old question: are people, deep down, selfish — or good? You'll also practise reporting what other people said: He said he had been there for fifteen months. She asked if I could help.

Part 1 — The real Lord of the Flies

In the 1950s, an English schoolteacher called William Golding wrote a novel about a group of British schoolboys who are stranded on an island with no adults. At first they try to organise themselves. But soon they stop looking after the fire, paint their faces, turn violent — and three children die. When a naval officer finally arrives, he says he would have thought that British boys could "put up a better show than that". The novel, Lord of the Flies, sold tens of millions of copies. For many readers, it showed the truth about human nature: take away the rules, and we become savages.

Many years later, a Dutch historian, Rutger Bregman, wondered whether real children had ever been in that situation. After a long search through old newspapers, he found a story from 1966. In June 1965, six Tongan boys, aged 13 to 16, had run away from their strict boarding school in the capital, Nuku'alofa. They "borrowed" a fishing boat without asking. That night a storm destroyed the sail and the rudder. They drifted for eight days with no food, sharing rainwater equally — one sip in the morning, one in the evening — until they reached a rocky, empty island called 'Ata.

They stayed there for fifteen months. And they did not turn on each other. They made a strict roster and worked in teams of two: garden, kitchen, guard duty. They kept a fire burning the whole time. When they argued, they separated to calm down. They began and ended each day with songs and prayers; one boy, Kolo, made a guitar from driftwood, half a coconut shell and wire from their wrecked boat. When Stephen fell from a cliff and broke his leg, the others carried him back up and set the leg with sticks and leaves.

On 11 September 1966, an Australian sea captain, Peter Warner, saw burned patches on the cliffs and a boy diving into the sea. The first boy to reach his boat shouted that his name was Stephen, that there were six of them, and that they thought they had been there for fifteen months. When Warner radioed the capital, the operator came back on the line in tears: the boys had been given up for dead, and funerals had been held. A doctor later said he was astonished by their strong bodies — and by Stephen's perfectly healed leg.

Part 2 — Seventeen thousand lost wallets

One story is not science. So how could you test human honesty on a big scale? Four researchers — Alain Cohn, Michel Maréchal, David Tannenbaum and Christian Zünd — had an idea: lose a lot of wallets.

Their research assistants visited 355 cities in 40 countries and handed in more than 17,000 "lost" wallets at banks, hotels, post offices, museums and police stations. Each wallet was a transparent card case containing three business cards with an email address, a shopping list and a key. Some contained no money. Others contained money worth about US$13. The assistant put the wallet on the counter and said, "Hi, I found this on the street around the corner. Somebody must have lost it. I'm in a hurry and have to go. Can you please take care of it?" — and left. Then the researchers waited to see who would email the owner.

Most people would expect fewer returns when there is money inside. The opposite happened. On average, 40 percent of the empty wallets were reported, but 51 percent of the wallets with money. This happened in 38 of the 40 countries. In the United States, the UK and Poland, the team also tried wallets with almost $95 inside. Then the rate climbed to 72 percent.

Before publishing the results in 2019, the researchers asked ordinary people and professional economists to predict them. Neither group got it right; many ordinary people predicted that more money would mean fewer returns. The researchers' explanation: people care about the owner — and most people don't want to see themselves as a thief. The more money there is, the more it feels like stealing.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was inside the wallets — and what did the assistant say when handing one in?

2. What happened when there was more money in the wallet?

1. Three business cards, a shopping list, a key, and sometimes money. The assistant said they had found it, were in a hurry, and asked the person to take care of it.

2. More people reported it: 40% with no money, 51% with about $13, and 72% with about $95 in the three countries tested.

Part 3 — What the cameras saw

For fifty years, psychology students have learned about the "bystander effect": the idea that when many people see an emergency, each person feels less responsible, so nobody helps. The most famous example was the murder of Kitty Genovese in New York in 1964. The media reported that 38 bystanders watched for half an hour without helping — although later research has questioned that version of the story.

A team of researchers led by Richard Philpot at Lancaster University asked a different question. Never mind what one person does — if you are attacked in a public place, will anyone help you? To find out, they didn't use a laboratory. They used real footage from street security cameras.

They studied 219 real arguments and fights filmed in three cities: Amsterdam in the Netherlands, Lancaster in the UK and Cape Town in South Africa. In nine out of ten cases, at least one bystander did something to help — for example, by calming the attacker with a gesture, blocking contact between the people, pulling someone away, or comforting the victim. On average, almost four people intervened. The result was similar in all three cities, even though they feel very different in terms of safety.

And the more bystanders there were, the more likely it was that someone would help. The researchers, publishing their work in 2019, argued that it was time to change the story: help is not the exception in public conflicts. It is the norm.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the "bystander effect"?

2. What did the researchers find in the camera footage?

1. The idea that when many people see an emergency, each feels less responsible, so nobody helps.

2. In nine out of ten conflicts, at least one bystander helped — usually several. It was similar in all three cities, and more bystanders meant a greater chance of help.

Discuss:
  • Have you ever stepped in to help a stranger — or seen someone else do it? What made the person act?

Grammar — reported speech

This story is full of people reporting what others said: the officer in the novel, Stephen on the boat, the radio operator. When we report words later, the tenses and pronouns usually move.

Reporting statements — tenses move back
• "We have been here fifteen months." → He said (that) they had been there fifteen months.
• "I am in a hurry." → She said she was in a hurry.
• "We will wait." → They said they would wait. · can → could · past simple → past perfect (or stays the same)
• Other words change too: here → there · today → that day · tomorrow → the next day · this → that
• say or tell? He said (that)… but He told me (that)… — tell needs a person. ✗ He said me…
Reporting questions and requests
• Yes/no questions → if / whether + statement order: "Are you OK?" → She asked if I was OK.
• Wh- questions → question word + statement order: "Where are you from?" → He asked where we were from.
• Requests and orders → ask / tell + person + to + verb: "Can you please take care of it?" → He asked the receptionist to take care of it.

Note: if the situation is still true now, you don't have to move the tense back: The study said that people are more honest than we think.

Going deeper

The events and numbers in this story are real, and come from the people who found them. But the question behind them — what are people really like? — is one of the oldest arguments there is.

Three big ideas from the story.

1. Our stories about people are darker than our behaviour. A novel about boys killing each other became a classic; a real story of boys caring for each other was almost forgotten. Many people predicted that money would make strangers keep a wallet; the opposite happened. Bregman argues that we are surrounded by stories, news and theories that make us more cynical than the evidence supports.

2. Being cynical has a cost. If you believe people are selfish, you design rules, offices and contracts for selfish people — more checks, more control, less trust. The wallet researchers point out that honesty is essential for economies: without it, promises are broken and contracts are not enforced. Trusting too little can be as expensive as trusting too much.

3. But be careful with good stories, too. Six boys on one island are not proof of anything about all humans. The wallet study measured one kind of honesty, at a counter, in a job. The cameras only saw what happened in public. People can be cruel, and history shows it. The fair question is not "good or bad?", but "in what situations do people help — and how do we create more of them?"
Talk it through:
  • Why do you think bad news spreads faster and further than good news? Is that the media's fault, or ours?
  • If you were designing a workplace for people who are mostly honest, what would you change? What would you keep?
  • In the wallet study, the more money there was, the more people returned it. Why might a bigger temptation make people more honest?
  • Is it naïve to believe that most people are good? Or is it naïve to believe that they aren't?
