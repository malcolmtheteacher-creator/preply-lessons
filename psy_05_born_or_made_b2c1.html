Born or Made?

Identical twins raised in different homes can be uncannily alike. What does that tell us about who we are?

The words for today's reading

Ten words, four short activities. Try each one out loud before you reveal.

A · What does the bold word mean here?

Read the sentence aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the context, then reveal.

1. Heritability is a statistic describing how much of the variation in a trait across a population is due to genetic differences.
heritability (n) — the proportion of differences between people in a trait that can be explained by genes. It does not say how much of one person's trait is genetic.
2. Researchers have long argued about nature versus nurture: biology on the one hand, upbringing and experience on the other.
nature versus nurture — the debate about whether genes (nature) or environment (nurture) shape a person more.
3. The environment includes what happens before birth, as well as upbringing and culture.
environment (n) — all the external conditions and influences that affect a person. In twin studies, shared and non-shared environment are separated.

B · Which word means…?

Say the word before you reveal. The first letter is given.

4. Which word means "two babies from one egg, with almost identical DNA"? (m__________)
monozygotic (adj) — usually called identical twins; they share almost all their DNA.
5. Which word means "a small difference between people in a trait"? (v________)
variation (n) — Example: "There is wide variation in height."
6. Which word means "to rule out other explanations"? (c_______)
control for (v) — Example: "Researchers controlled for family income."

C · Complete the phrase

These words go with particular partners. Complete each chunk.

7. Critics argued that twins raised apart were often placed in similar homes, so the evidence was ______ . (c_________)
compromised (adj) — weakened or damaged. Compromised evidence can't be fully trusted.
8. A trait that is partly ______ can still be changed by experience. (i________)
inherited (adj) — passed from parents to children through genes. The noun is inheritance.
9. The twins were ______ at birth and reunited as adults. (s_________)

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The two Jims

In 1979, two men in Ohio met for the first time. Jim Lewis and Jim Springer were identical twins, adopted by different families when they were four weeks old, and they had grown up about forty miles apart without knowing it. When they were reunited at thirty-nine, the reports were astonishing. According to the press, both had married a woman called Linda, divorced, and then married a woman called Betty. Both had named a son James Alan, and both had owned a dog called Toy.

Some of these coincidences are probably accidents of the telling: any two people will share a few odd facts if you look hard enough. But the case attracted the attention of the psychologist Thomas Bouchard at the University of Minnesota, who began to study separated twins systematically.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was surprising about the two Jims?

2. How did Bouchard respond?

1. Although they had been raised apart, they appeared to share many unusual habits and life events.

2. He began to study separated twins systematically.

Discuss:
  • Which coincidences in the story do you find convincing, and which do you think are just chance?

Part 2 — What twin studies show

The Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart tested more than a hundred sets of twins and triplets on intelligence, personality and interests. The central result was that identical twins, who share nearly all their DNA, were considerably more alike than non-identical twins, who share about half, even when they had been raised apart. Estimates of heritability in these and similar studies are high for traits such as height, and moderate, around 40 to 50 per cent, for many personality traits.

Genes do not act alone, however. Heritability is a statistic about differences across a population, not about any one person. A figure of 50 per cent does not mean that half of your personality comes from your genes and half from your upbringing. It means that, in the group studied, about half of the differences between people can be linked to genetic differences.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the key finding?

2. What does heritability actually mean?

1. Identical twins were more alike than non-identical twins, even when raised apart.

2. The share of differences in a trait across a group that can be linked to genetic differences; it says nothing about one individual.

Discuss:
  • Why is it so tempting to misread a heritability figure as a statement about an individual?

Part 3 — Why 'nature versus nurture' is the wrong question

The studies have been criticised. Many separated twins were placed in similar adoptive homes, and twins also share a womb, so some similarities may come from early environment rather than genes. A deeper difficulty is that the two influences interact. A child with an inherited love of music may be given lessons; the lessons then strengthen the talent. This is how genes can shape the environment that a person seeks out.

More recently, epigenetics has shown that experiences, from diet to stress, can switch genes on and off, and that some of these changes can even persist for a time. For that reason most researchers now reject the idea of a simple contest between nature and nurture. The more useful question is how the two work together, and how much room there is for change.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Give one criticism of twin studies.

2. Why do researchers now reject nature versus nurture?

1. Separated twins were often placed in similar homes, and twins share a womb.

2. Because genes and environment interact, and experience can switch genes on and off.

Discuss:
  • If genes and environment constantly interact, what would it mean to 'be yourself'?

Grammar — contrast: whereas, while, although and despite

Contrast linkers let you set two findings side by side, and show how they pull in different directions. They are essential for balanced argument.

Four linkers:
• whereas / while + clause, to compare two things: "Height is largely inherited, whereas language is learned." (Both parts are full clauses.)
• although / even though + clause, to show an unexpected result: "Although the twins were raised apart, they were very alike."
• despite / in spite of + noun or -ing: "Despite growing up apart, they were very alike."
Common mistakes:
• Don't use but together with although: "Although they were apart, but they were alike" is wrong.
• Despite is never followed by of. Say despite the evidence or in spite of the evidence.
• While and whereas are used to compare; while can also mean 'at the same time'.

Genes, environment or both?

Five claims. Say how far you accept each one and what you would want to know. Then reveal.

1. "Intelligence is 50% genetic, so half of your ability is fixed."
A misreading. Heritability describes variation in a group, not the 'amount' of a trait in a person, and it changes with the environment studied.
2. "Identical twins reared apart prove personality is inherited."
Too strong. They share genes but also a womb, and were often placed in similar homes; the evidence is suggestive, not conclusive.
3. "If a trait is heritable, it can't be changed."
False. Short-sightedness is highly heritable, but glasses correct it. Heritable does not mean fixed.
4. "Parents have almost no influence on their children's personality."
Debated. Some researchers argue shared family environment explains little of the differences in adult personality, but parents still affect safety, language, values and opportunities.
5. "We should stop asking whether it is nature or nurture."
Largely right. The interesting question is how they interact, for instance when a child's genes lead them to seek out a particular environment.
