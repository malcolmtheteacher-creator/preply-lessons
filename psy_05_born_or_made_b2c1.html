The two Jims: true or made up?

In 1979 two identical twins, adopted by different families at four weeks old, met for the first time at thirty-nine. Press reports listed the things they had in common. Some of the list below is what was reported and some is invented for this game. Decide which, one at a time.

1. Both had named their first son James Alan.
Reported, though the spelling of the middle name differed.
2. Both had taught themselves to play the violin at the age of six.
Made up for this game. Nothing like this was reported.
3. Both had owned a dog called Toy.
Reported.
4. Both had left school at fourteen to work as fishermen.
Made up for this game.
5. Both had married a woman called Linda, divorced, and then married a woman called Betty.
Reported by the press, and the detail everyone remembers.

Part 1 — The two Jims

Before you read: Two identical twins are adopted by different families and never meet until they are 39. How many things in common would you expect: almost none, a few, or dozens?

In 1979, two men in Ohio met for the first time. Jim Lewis and Jim Springer were identical twins, adopted by different families when they were four weeks old, and they had grown up about forty miles apart without knowing it. When they were reunited at thirty-nine, the reports were astonishing. According to the press, both had married a woman called Linda, divorced, and then married a woman called Betty. Both had named a son James Alan, and both had owned a dog called Toy.

Some of these coincidences are probably accidents of the telling: any two people will share a few odd facts if you look hard enough. But the case attracted the attention of the psychologist Thomas Bouchard at the University of Minnesota, who began to study separated twins systematically.

Part 2 — What twin studies show

Before you read: Identical twins share almost all their DNA. Non-identical twins share about half. If genes matter for personality, what should a study of twins find?

The Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart tested more than a hundred sets of twins and triplets on intelligence, personality and interests. The central result was that identical twins, who share nearly all their DNA, were considerably more alike than non-identical twins, who share about half, even when they had been raised apart. Estimates of heritability in these and similar studies are high for traits such as height, and moderate, around 40 to 50 per cent, for many personality traits.

Genes do not act alone, however. Heritability is a statistic about differences across a population, not about any one person. A figure of 50 per cent does not mean that half of your personality comes from your genes and half from your upbringing. It means that, in the group studied, about half of the differences between people can be linked to genetic differences.

Part 3 — Why 'nature versus nurture' is the wrong question

Before you read: Name one way a study of twins raised apart could give a misleading result.

The studies have been criticised. Many separated twins were placed in similar adoptive homes, and twins also share a womb, so some similarities may come from early environment rather than genes. A deeper difficulty is that the two influences interact. A child with an inherited love of music may be given lessons; the lessons then strengthen the talent. This is how genes can shape the environment that a person seeks out.

More recently, epigenetics has shown that experiences, from diet to stress, can switch genes on and off, and that some of these changes can even persist for a time. For that reason most researchers now reject the idea of a simple contest between nature and nurture. The more useful question is how the two work together, and how much room there is for change.

Setting one finding against another

A good argument rarely says only 'genes' or only 'upbringing'. It sets findings side by side. Three linkers do the job.

whereas / while + clause: compares two things. Height is largely inherited, whereas language is learned.
although / even though + clause: an unexpected result. Although they grew up apart, they were alike.
despite / in spite of + noun or -ing. Despite growing up apart, they were alike. Never despite of, and never although … but.

Build it

Join the two facts so they mean the same as the model will show. Type your sentence, say it, then reveal.

1. Identical twins share nearly all their DNA. Non-identical twins share about half. whereas
Identical twins share nearly all their DNA, whereas non-identical twins share about half. (or: Whereas identical twins share nearly all their DNA, non-identical twins share about half.)
2. The twins grew up apart. They turned out very alike. although
Although the twins grew up apart, they turned out very alike. (Even though also works.)
3. The study was large. It could not settle the question. despite
Despite being large, the study could not settle the question. (or: Despite its size, …)

Take a side

Five claims people really make. Choose I agree, I disagree or It depends, then defend your choice out loud in one sentence using the linker shown. Check the evidence last. You do not have to match it.

1. "Intelligence is 50% genetic, so half of your ability is fixed."whereas
A misreading. Heritability describes differences across a group, not the amount of a trait inside one person, and it changes with the environment studied.
2. "Identical twins reared apart prove that personality is inherited."although
Too strong. They share genes but also a womb, and many were placed in similar homes. The evidence is suggestive, not conclusive.
3. "If a trait is heritable, it cannot be changed."despite
False. Short-sightedness is highly heritable, yet glasses correct it. Heritable does not mean fixed.
4. "Parents have almost no influence on their children's personality."whereas
Debated. Some researchers argue that shared family environment explains little of the differences in adult personality, but parents still affect safety, language, values and opportunities.
5. "We should stop asking whether it is nature or nurture."although
Largely right. The interesting question is how they interact, for instance when a child's genes lead them to seek out a particular environment.
