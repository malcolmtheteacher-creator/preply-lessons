Muhammad Ali

The boxer who called himself the greatest — and then spent a lifetime proving that the fight outside the ring mattered just as much.

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. From his first professional fight he was a natural heavyweight, tall and powerful, sharing a ring only with the biggest men in the sport.
heavyweight (n/adj) — the class for the heaviest boxers; also, more generally, someone of great importance or power.
2. He was the reigning champion, the man everyone else had to beat if they wanted the belt.
a champion (n) — the winner of a competition; the person or team that holds the title.
3. He danced around his opponent, light on his feet, refusing to stand still and trade blows — his footwork was as famous as his fists.
an opponent (n) — the person you are competing against or fighting.
4. He would taunt his rivals for weeks before a fight, mocking them, predicting the round they would fall, getting inside their heads.
to taunt (v) — to say cruel or mocking things to provoke or upset someone.
5. He refused to fight in the war on grounds of conscience, saying his beliefs would not let him take part, whatever it cost him.
conscience (n) — your inner sense of right and wrong that guides how you act.
6. When he refused to serve, they stripped him of his title, taking away in an afternoon the belt he had bled for years to win.
to strip someone of something (v) — to take away officially a title, right or honour they held.
7. The draft called young men into the army whether they wished to fight or not; a letter arrived, and you were expected to go.
the draft (n) — a system that legally requires citizens, usually young men, to join the armed forces.
8. After years in the wilderness he staged a stunning comeback, returning to the top of a sport that had written him off.
a comeback (n) — a return to success or fame after a period of decline or absence.
9. His conviction that he was right never wavered, even when standing by it cost him his career and his freedom.
conviction (n) — a strong, firmly held belief; the certainty that you are right.
10. He became more than a boxer — a true icon, a face known on every continent and a symbol of far more than sport.
an icon (n) — a person widely admired and instantly recognised, who stands for something bigger than themselves.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Cassius Clay, and the loudest boy in Louisville

He was born Cassius Marcellus Clay Jr. in Louisville, Kentucky, in 1942, into a segregated America where a Black child grew up knowing exactly which doors were closed to him. The story goes that at twelve he had his bicycle stolen and told a policeman he wanted to "whup" whoever took it. The officer, who ran a boxing gym, told the furious boy he had better learn to fight first. He did — and within a few years it was obvious the world had a rare talent on its hands.

He was fast in a way heavyweights were not supposed to be, dancing around opponents, hands low, head snapping back out of range. At the 1960 Rome Olympics, still a teenager, he won gold. Turning professional, he did something no boxer had done before: he talked. He rhymed, he boasted, he predicted the round his rivals would fall — and, maddeningly, he was usually right. "I am the greatest," he announced, long before anyone else agreed. He said it, and he meant it.

In 1964 he got his shot at the world title against Sonny Liston, a fearsome champion whom almost everyone expected to destroy the loud young challenger. Almost everyone was wrong. Clay danced, jabbed and taunted; Liston, battered and exhausted, failed to answer the bell for the seventh round. At twenty-two, the boy from Louisville was heavyweight champion of the world — and he made sure the world knew it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How, according to the story, did the young Cassius Clay first come to boxing?

2. What was surprising about his fight with Sonny Liston in 1964?

1. After his bicycle was stolen at twelve, he told a policeman he wanted to fight the thief; that officer, who ran a boxing gym, told him to learn to box first.

2. Liston was the feared champion expected to destroy him, but Clay outboxed and taunted him, and Liston quit before the seventh round; at twenty-two Clay became world champion.

Discuss:
  • Ali announced he was the greatest before he had proved it. Is that confidence, arrogance, or a clever strategy — and does it matter which, if he was right?

Part 2 — A new name, a new faith, and the fight he refused

The morning after beating Liston, the new champion confirmed what had been rumoured: he had joined the Nation of Islam and rejected "Cassius Clay" as a "slave name". He would be known, he said, as Muhammad Ali. Much of white America refused to use the name; many sportswriters would not either. He insisted on it anyway — his name, his faith, his choice.

Then, in 1967, at the height of the Vietnam War, Ali was called up by the draft. He refused to go. As a Muslim minister, and on grounds of conscience, he would not fight in a war he did not believe in — "I ain't got no quarrel with them Viet Cong," he said. The response was swift and severe. He was arrested, convicted of refusing military service, and stripped of his heavyweight title. Boxing commissions took away his licence. He was, at twenty-five, the finest heavyweight alive — and he was not allowed to fight.

For more than three years, in what should have been his physical prime, Ali did not box at all. He faced prison, gave lectures on college campuses, and became a lightning rod in a divided country — hated by some as a traitor, admired by others as a man who had sacrificed everything for his principles. In 1971 the Supreme Court finally overturned his conviction. He was free, and he could fight again — but the years, and the peak, were gone.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What two big changes did Ali announce around the time he became champion, and how did much of America react?

2. Why did Ali refuse the draft in 1967, and what did it cost him?

1. He joined the Nation of Islam and changed his name from Cassius Clay to Muhammad Ali; much of white America, and many sportswriters, refused to use the new name.

2. On grounds of religious conscience he refused to fight in the Vietnam War; he was convicted, stripped of his title and licence, and lost more than three years of his career.

Discuss:
  • Ali gave up his title, his money and nearly his freedom rather than fight. Would you sacrifice your career for a belief? Where is the line between principle and stubbornness?

Part 3 — The Rumble in the Jungle, and the icon

When Ali returned, a new champion ruled the heavyweight division: George Foreman, younger, enormous and terrifyingly hard-hitting, a man who had flattened everyone in his path. In October 1974, the two met in Kinshasa, in what was then Zaire, in a fight promoted as the "Rumble in the Jungle". Almost no one gave the older Ali a chance. Almost no one, that is, except Ali himself.

What he did that night became legend. Instead of dancing, he leaned back on the ropes and let Foreman hit him — round after round, absorbing punishment, whispering taunts, letting the younger man punch himself into exhaustion. Commentators later called it "rope-a-dope". In the eighth round, with Foreman spent, Ali struck. A flurry of punches, and the giant went down. Ali had done the impossible: he was champion of the world again. The little phrase that captured his style — "float like a butterfly, sting like a bee" — had never looked so true.

He fought on, through brutal contests like the "Thrilla in Manila" against Joe Frazier, perhaps too long. In his later years he developed Parkinson's disease, and the voice that had never stopped talking grew quiet. Yet he became something larger than any athlete: a global icon of faith, courage and conviction. In 1996, his hands trembling, he lit the Olympic flame in Atlanta, and the whole world rose. When he died in 2016, he was mourned everywhere — remembered not only as perhaps the greatest boxer who ever lived, but as a man who had stood by what he believed, whatever it cost.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the "Rumble in the Jungle", and what tactic did Ali use to win it?

2. How is Ali remembered beyond his boxing — and what happened to him in his later years?

1. His 1974 fight against George Foreman in Kinshasa, Zaire; Ali leaned on the ropes ("rope-a-dope"), let Foreman tire himself out, then knocked him down in the eighth round to become champion again.

2. He is remembered as a global icon of faith, courage and conviction; in later life he developed Parkinson's disease, lit the 1996 Olympic flame, and was mourned worldwide when he died in 2016.

Discuss:
  • Ali is remembered as much for what he stood for as for what he did in the ring. Can a sportsperson be "great" for their character alone — and should we expect athletes to stand for anything at all?

Grammar — Ellipsis & Substitution

Ali was a master of saying a lot with a little — "I said it, and I did." Good English does the same. Rather than repeat words the listener already has, we leave them out (ellipsis) or swap in a short word (substitution). Get this right and your speech sounds natural, quick and native, instead of clunky and repetitive.

Ellipsis — leave words out when they can be understood:
• After an auxiliary/modal, drop the repeated verb phrase: "He said he would win, and he did [win]." "She can box, and so can he [box]."
• In answers and short additions: "Did he win? — He did." "The crowd roared, and so did the whole stadium."
• After and / but / or, drop a repeated subject or verb: "He danced, [he] taunted and [he] won." "Ali could dance and [could] punch."
Substitution — swap in a short word instead of repeating:
• so / neither / nor for a whole idea: "Foreman was tired, and so was Ali." "Ali wouldn't quit; neither would Frazier."
• do / did / does for a repeated verb: "He fights better than he ever did." "She trains harder than he does."
• one / ones for a repeated noun: "That was a great fight — the best one of the decade." "Old belts and new ones."
• so / not after think, believe, hope, expect: "Will he win? — I think so." / "I'm afraid not."

1. Leave it out — complete with an auxiliary (ellipsis)

Finish each sentence with the missing short verb (do / did / can / would / was, etc.) so you don't repeat the whole phrase.

1. Almost no one thought Ali could beat Foreman, but he .

2. Liston expected an easy night, but Clay didn't give him .

3. The crowd rose to its feet, and so the commentators.

1. did ("but he did [beat Foreman]" — the verb is left out)

2. one (substitution: "an easy night" → "one")

3. did ("and so did the commentators [rise]")

2. Substitute — replace the repeated words

Rewrite the underlined idea using so, neither/nor, one/ones, or do/does/did. Write only the short replacement.

1. Ali believed he would win. Will he win? — I think .

2. Foreman couldn't keep up the pace, and could most of Ali's rivals. (= also could not)

3. He trained harder in his comeback than he as a young man. (replace "trained")

1. so ("I think so" replaces "I think he will win")

2. neither ("and neither could most of his rivals")

3. did ("harder than he did" replaces "than he trained")

3. Tighten the paragraph — cut the repetition

Fill each gap with a single short word (an auxiliary, so, one, etc.) so nothing is needlessly repeated. Read it aloud once you're done.

Everyone said Ali couldn't beat Foreman, but he . He'd claimed he was the greatest, and now he'd proved he really . It wasn't his first great fight, and it certainly wasn't his last . Foreman was exhausted — and, by the end, so Ali, though he never showed it.

did (ellipsis: "but he did") · was (ellipsis: "he really was [the greatest]") · one (substitution: "his last one") · was (substitution: "so was Ali")

Notice how much lighter it reads: each short word carries a whole phrase the listener already has.

  1. Ali called himself "the greatest" before he had proved it — and then proved it. When is self-belief a strength, and when does it tip into arrogance?
