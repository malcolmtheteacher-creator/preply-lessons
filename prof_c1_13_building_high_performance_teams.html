The Post-Mortem

The project is over. What actually happened? What do you change?

Warm-up: The Hard Question

A project just finished. It was 4 weeks late, over budget, and the client was unhappy. You're running the post-mortem. Your first question to the room is...? 60 seconds.

1:00
What you just did: A post-mortem is where high-performance teams separate from average ones. Most teams ask "Who was responsible?" The best teams ask "What in our system allowed this to happen?" The first question blames people. The second question improves the system. People learn. Systems prevent failures.

5 Post-Mortem Toolkit Moves

Click each card to see the move.

1. The Blameless Frame

This is non-negotiable. Without it, people lie in post-mortems.

Say this: "We're not here to find who was responsible. We're here to find what in our system allowed this to happen."

What it does: It tells people the meeting isn't a blame session. They can be honest. They won't protect themselves. They'll help you solve the problem.

Why it works: When people fear consequences, they hide. When they're safe, they teach you everything you need to know.

2. The Five Whys

Keep asking why until you reach the root cause. Most teams stop one level too early.

Example chain:

Why 1: "Why was the project late?" — "The handoff from design to dev was delayed."

Why 2: "Why was the handoff delayed?" — "There was no shared deadline visibility."

Why 3: "Why was there no shared deadline visibility?" — "We weren't using a shared project tool."

Why 4: "Why weren't we using a shared project tool?" — "Nobody agreed on process at the start."

Why 5: "Why didn't anybody agree on process at the start?" — "We didn't have a kickoff meeting. We just started building."

The fix: Now you see the real problem. Add a structured kickoff meeting to every project. You've prevented this from happening again.

3. The Positive First

Start with what went well. Not as polite throat-clearing, but as genuine learning.

Bad version: "Well, we delivered eventually. Now let's talk about everything that went wrong."

Good version: "Despite the delay, we nailed the client communication. Every week, before problems escalated, we gave them an update. They stayed confident in us. What did we do right there? How do we replicate that?"

Why it works: You're building on strengths, not just fixing weaknesses. And you remind the team that they did some things right. That matters for morale.

4. The Systemic vs Individual Distinction

Was this a process failure or a performance failure? The response is completely different.

Process failure: "We didn't have a kickoff process." → Fix: Create a kickoff template. Use it every time. The same person who would fail again will now succeed because the system catches them.

Performance failure: "Sarah missed every deadline." → Fix: Conversation with Sarah. Training? Different role? Does she need support? If Sarah leaves and is replaced, the next person will fail the same way without a process to guide them.

Why it matters: 90% of project failures are systemic. Fix the system. Most people want to do good work. You've just made it harder for them.

5. The Commitment Close

Never end a post-mortem without action items. And make them specific and owned.

Weak: "We should do better with deadline communication."

Strong: "By Friday, Sarah will create a shared project dashboard template. Starting with the next project, every team uses it. Every Monday, we review it in team standup."

Why it works: You've named the action (dashboard template). The owner (Sarah). The deadline (Friday). The enforcement mechanism (weekly review). Now something changes. Without that specificity, nothing changes and the same problem happens again.

Run Your Post-Mortem

Pick a scenario. Facilitate a mini post-mortem aloud. You're running it. You ask the questions. You draw out the learning. 6 minutes total (2 min prep, 4 min facilitation).

Pick your scenario:
  • Project failure: Launch delayed 3 weeks. Team worked nights and weekends. Client frustrated. What actually happened?
  • Team conflict: Two departments blamed each other when work fell between the cracks. How do you get to the real systemic issue?
  • Missed opportunity: You saw a market opening but moved too slowly. Competitor got there first. What were the process failures?

Prep (2 minutes)

Think through the scenario. What are the obvious answers? What are the real root causes? What questions will you ask?

2:00

Facilitation (4 minutes)

Run the post-mortem. Open with the blameless frame. Ask Five Whys. Get to root cause. Commit to action. Go.

4:00
What just happened: You separated blame from learning. You asked why multiple times. You found systemic issues. You didn't end with vague commitments. You did what high-performance teams do. That's how they get better.

Post-Mortem Language Bank

Blameless Framing

"We're not here to find who to blame. We're finding what in our system allowed this to happen."

"Assume good intent. We're all doing our best. Let's find the gaps."

Five Whys

"Why did that happen?" ... "And why did that?" ... "What was underneath that?"

"Help me understand the chain of events."

"Keep going. What's the real root cause?"

Positive First

"Despite the challenges, we did [specific thing] really well. How do we keep doing that?"

"What are we proud of from this project?"

"What should we definitely replicate next time?"

Systemic Analysis

"Is this a process issue or a people issue?"

"How would we prevent this with a better system?"

"What structure could have caught this?"

Commitment Language

"By [date], [person] will [specific action]. Starting with [when], we'll [enforcement]."

"Let me make sure this is clear: we're committing to..."

"Here's what changes. Here's who owns it. Here's how we'll check."

Closing a Retrospective

"We came with three problems. We're leaving with three fixes. Here's who's doing what."

"Let's check in in three weeks to see how these changes are landing."

"Thank you for being honest. That's how we get better."
