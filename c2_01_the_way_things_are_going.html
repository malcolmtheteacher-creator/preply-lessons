The Language of Trends

These verbs and phrases describe how data moves. Look at each one and think about what kind of movement it represents — direction, speed, and intensity.

1 to shoot up — to increase very quickly and suddenly Line A (steep rise)
2 to plateau — to reach a level and stay flat Line E (flat middle section)
3 to rocket — to increase extremely fast (even faster than "shoot up") Line A (steepest rise)
4 to slump — to fall suddenly and heavily Line B (sudden sharp drop)
5 to creep up — to increase very slowly and gradually Line D (gentle upward slope)
6 to tail off — to gradually decrease and become smaller Line C (gradual decline)
7 to plummet — to fall very quickly and dramatically Line B (steepest drop)
8 to stabilise — to stop changing and become steady Line E (flat section)

Collocations & Nuance

Dramatic increase: shoot up, rocket, soar, surge

Dramatic decrease: plummet, slump, plunge, nosedive

Gradual change: creep up, edge up, tail off, taper off

No change: plateau, stabilise, level off, remain flat

Notice how the intensity differs within each group. "Rocket" is stronger than "shoot up"; "plummet" is more dramatic than "slump."

Hedging & Speculating

When interpreting data, we rarely state things as absolute fact. Instead, we hedge — we signal that we're making an interpretation rather than stating a certainty. What expressions can you use when you're not 100% sure of something?

To the best of my knowledge...
"To the best of my knowledge, the spike in 2008 was triggered by changes in reporting methods."
It looks like / It looks as if...
"It looks as if there was a correlation between the two variables during this period."
I am / was under the impression that...
"I was under the impression that the figures had been adjusted for inflation."
I take it that / I took it that...
"I take it that the data doesn't account for seasonal variation?"

Can you think of other expressions for hedging?

As far as I'm aware...
As far as I know...
I'm not exactly sure, but...
From what I can gather...
If I'm not mistaken...
It would appear that...
One might argue that...

Stronger vs. Weaker Hedging

Weaker hedge (more certain): "It's widely accepted that..." / "It's generally understood that..."

Moderate hedge: "It looks as if..." / "I was under the impression that..."

Stronger hedge (less certain): "I'm not entirely sure, but..." / "From what I can gather..."

C2 speakers adjust their hedging to signal exactly how confident they are. This is a key feature of sophisticated academic and professional discourse.

Interpreting the Data

Now look at this data set and the summary below. Read the synopsis, then discuss the questions that follow.

This data comes from the US General Social Survey, which has asked Americans "Taken all together, how would you say things are these days — would you say that you are very happy, pretty happy, or not too happy?" since 1972. Comparable studies from other industrialised countries reflect similar patterns.

Women reported being happier than men in the early 1970s, but this gap has gradually reversed. The percentage of women reporting they are "very happy" has declined over the decades, while men's self-reported happiness has remained relatively stable. Both genders saw a downturn around 2008–2010, widely attributed to the financial crisis.

Based on: Stevenson & Wolfers (2009), "The Paradox of Declining Female Happiness"

What specific movements can you identify? Try to use the trend language from Tab 2.

Now consider these possible explanations. Which do you find most convincing? Why?

Explanation 1
Women's lives have become more like men's over the last 35 years. Men have historically been less happy than women. So it may not be surprising if the things in the workplace that always made men unhappy are affecting women in the same way.
Explanation 2
There was enormous social pressure on women in earlier decades to report that they were happy even if they weren't. Now society allows women to express their feelings more openly when they are dissatisfied.
Explanation 3
Female happiness may have been artificially inflated in the 1970s and 80s because it was a very hopeful period for women's rights. Progress has happened more slowly than anticipated, so relative to those expectations, things have been something of a disappointment.
Explanation 4
Self-reported happiness measures are so heavily influenced by other factors that they are essentially meaningless. It's impossible to say whether women are truly less happy than men now compared to then.
