Today's aim: Today we're working on separating three ideas that are easily confused (determinism, predictability and free will), so you can argue a precise, carefully hedged position on whether the future is already fixed.

Words you'll need

determinism (n) — the idea that every event is fully caused by earlier events and the laws of nature. Example: "Determinism says that today's state of the universe fixes tomorrow's."
predictable (adj) — possible to forecast accurately before it happens. Example: "The tides are predictable years in advance."
chaotic (adj) — extremely sensitive to tiny changes, so long-term outcomes become impossible to forecast. Example: "Weather is a chaotic system."
trajectory (n) — the path that something follows through space or time. Example: "The two trajectories start together and then drift apart."
diverge (v) — to separate and move in different directions. Example: "Two nearly identical starting points can diverge rapidly."
random (adj) — happening by chance, with no pattern or cause we can identify. Example: "A single radioactive atom decays at a random moment."
inevitable (adj) — certain to happen and impossible to avoid. Example: "If the future is fixed, is every outcome inevitable?"
causation (n) — the relationship in which one event brings about another. Example: "Physics is largely the study of causation."
negligible (adj) — so small that it is not worth considering. Example: "A rounding error of one part in a million seems negligible."
compatible (adj) — able to exist together without contradiction. Example: "Some philosophers argue that free will is compatible with determinism."

The hook: a demon that knows everything

In 1814 the French mathematician Pierre-Simon Laplace imagined an intellect that knew the position and speed of every particle in the universe, together with all the forces acting on them. For such a mind, he wrote, "nothing would be uncertain and the future, just like the past, would be present before its eyes." Later writers called it Laplace's demon.

It sounds like a thought experiment about a superhuman mind. It is really a question about the universe: if the laws are fixed, is everything that will ever happen already settled?

Say it aloud: Before you read on, give your honest first reaction in 30 seconds. Does the idea unsettle you, comfort you, or leave you cold?

Roughly: a universe can obey deterministic laws and still be impossible to predict, and being predictable is a different matter from being fixed. Whether any of this touches free will is a further, separate question. The next tabs pull those three ideas apart.

Three ideas, in small steps

Idea 1 · Determinism is about laws, not forecasts

A system is deterministic if its present state and the laws together fix its future state. Newton's mechanics is deterministic: give it the same starting conditions and it returns the same result every time.

Idea 2 · Chaos separates "fixed" from "forecastable"

In 1961 the meteorologist Edward Lorenz re-entered a number from a printout as 0.506 instead of 0.506127. The tiny difference changed his simulated weather completely. Chaotic systems amplify small differences, so any error in our measurements grows until the forecast is useless.

In principle: fixed. In practice: unpredictable, because perfect measurement is impossible.

Idea 3 · Quantum theory raises a separate question

On the standard reading of quantum mechanics, the theory predicts only probabilities for individual outcomes, such as the moment a radioactive atom decays. Some interpretations accept this as genuine chance. Others restore determinism at a deeper level. Physicists have not settled the matter.

What you just saw

1 · Same rules, no chance
Gravity and bounces are the only things at work. Drop the same ball from exactly the same spot and you get exactly the same path every time. That is what deterministic means.
2 · Almost the same start
The red ball began a tiny distance from the blue one, far too small to measure.
3 · Different futures
Each bounce magnifies the tiny difference, so the balls end in different slots. Nobody could forecast it from a measurement of the start. That is what unpredictable means here.

So the board is deterministic and unpredictable. Those are two different things.

Where does that leave free will?

Suppose the laws really are deterministic. Then your decisions, too, follow from earlier causes, going back to the early universe. Does that mean you could never have chosen otherwise? Philosophers have argued about it for centuries. Three families of answer are on the table:

PositionSupporters argue…Critics counter…
A · Hard determinismEvery choice follows from earlier causes, so the sense that we could have done otherwise is an illusion.It clashes with how deliberation and responsibility actually work, and seems to make blame meaningless.
B · CompatibilismBeing free means acting on your own reasons without coercion, and that is compatible with determinism.It redefines "free" to fit the conclusion, and dodges the real question.
C · Genuine opennessIf some events are truly undetermined, the future is not yet settled and real choice becomes possible.A random event is not a free choice either: luck is not control.

Precision toolkit: separating ideas that get confused

Calibrating the claim
· In principle, …; in practice, …
· To all intents and purposes, …
· On the standard reading of …
· There is no fundamental law against …, but …
Challenging an inference
· It doesn't follow that …
· That conflates … with …
· That hinges on whether …
· Even granting that, …
