The Better Deal

Eight years a customer, and the bill has crept up again — quietly, the way it does. Twenty minutes on hold has delivered you to the one department with the power to fix it. They're called 'retentions'. Retain you they will try.

The Scene 🎬

The strange modern ritual: to get a fair price from a company you've paid for years, you must first offer to leave it.

Here's the open secret of broadband, phone and insurance pricing: the best deals go to strangers. New customers get the shiny offer; loyal ones get a quiet three-pound rise every spring, on the theory that you won't notice, and that if you notice you won't call.

You noticed. And you've done the ten minutes of homework that changes everything: the competitor's price for the same service, on the screen in front of you. Without that number this call is begging. With it, it's shopping.

The person you'll reach is Darren, and Darren is not your enemy — he's a decent man with a headset, a script, and a screen of discounts he unlocks in stages. The stages only unlock in one direction: towards the word "cancel". Your job is to walk there calmly, mean it enough to say it, and check every offer for the trapdoor — the price that "jumps again after a year".

  2. Could you genuinely walk away — switch provider, switch bank, switch gym? What actually stops you: effort, or the conversation?

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for any renewal, any provider, any creeping bill — broadband, insurance, the gym, the streaming pile. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. One sentence in this call carries everything: "then I'd like to cancel." It only works if it's true — so before you dial, decide honestly that you'll switch if they don't move. People can hear a bluff down a phone line. They can also hear the absence of one.

📋 Opening with the facts

Phrase 1

The job: your standing and the trigger, one sentence, no anger. Complete it:

"I've been with you ___ years, and my bill's just ___ ___ again."

"I've been with you eight years, and my bill's just gone up again."
The two facts that define the whole call, delivered before any feelings: how long you've paid, and what they've just done. "Again" is the quiet sting — it names the pattern, not just this year's rise. No accusation needed; the sentence is the accusation, wearing a suit.

Take it anywhere: "I've been with you ___ years, and ___ has just gone up again." Insurers, gyms, landlords, software subscriptions.
Phrase 2

The job: setting the tone — this is a negotiation, not a moan. Which version?

A. "Honestly, it's disgraceful how you treat loyal customers."
B. "I'm not ringing to complain — I'm ringing to see what you can do."
C. "I suppose there's probably nothing you can do about the price…?"
B — "I'm not ringing to complain — I'm ringing to see what you can do."
A puts Darren on the defensive, and defensive agents reach for the script, not the discounts. C answers itself — ask whether nothing can be done and nothing will be. B is the businesslike middle: no grievance to soothe, just a question with money in it, aimed at the one person whose screen can answer it.

Take it anywhere: "I'm not ringing to complain — I'm ringing to see what you can do." Turns every service call from therapy into commerce.
Phrase 3

The job: the competitor's number, with proof attached. Complete it:

"Vodafone are offering the same thing for twenty-eight — I've got it in ___ of me."

"Vodafone are offering the same thing for twenty-eight — I've got it in front of me."
The engine of the entire call. A vague "I've seen cheaper deals" moves nobody; a named rival, a real number and "in front of me" tells the agent you're one browser tab from leaving. Do the ten minutes of homework before dialling — it's the best hourly rate you'll ever earn.

Take it anywhere: "___ are offering the same thing for ___ — I've got it in front of me." The sentence that converts research into money.

🪜 Walking down the ladder

Phrase 4

The job: receiving the first offer — credit it, and refuse it. Complete it:

"That's better — but it's still ___ than I'd pay next door."

"That's better — but it's still more than I'd pay next door."
The first offer is never the last — it exists to catch the callers who just wanted to be heard. This sentence banks the progress ("that's better") while keeping the comparison alive ("next door"). Warm, unhurried, and unmistakably not done yet. Then wait; silence does the rest.

Take it anywhere: "That's better — but it's still more than ___." Acknowledge every step; accept only the last one.
Phrase 5

The job: when they defend the price with features you never use. Complete it:

"I don't need the extras — I'd rather ___ them and pay ___."

"I don't need the extras — I'd rather lose them and pay less."
The standard defence of a high price is the bundle: the SIM you never activated, the app you can't use, the magazine nobody reads. This sentence calls the bluff by inviting them to remove it all — and it usually unlocks a price they couldn't reach while the bundle stood. Trading down is a negotiating move, not a defeat.

Take it anywhere: "I don't need the extras — I'd rather lose them and pay less." Phone plans, insurance add-ons, software tiers.
Phrase 6

The job: the sentence the whole call is built around. Put the pieces in order:

then I'd likethe best you can do,to cancel.If that's really
"If that's really the best you can do, then I'd like to cancel."
Calm, conditional, and final — no raised voice, because the words carry the weight. "If that's really the best" gives them one last honest chance, and "I'd like to cancel" opens the door on the screen where the deepest discounts live. Say it slowly. And only say it if it's true — the follow-through ("the install team can come Thursday") is what proves it isn't theatre.

Take it anywhere: "If that's really the best you can do, then I'd like to ___." Cancel, switch, look elsewhere — the calm walk-away.

🔍 Checking the deal before it's a deal

Phrase 7

The job: finding the trapdoor in the shiny price. Complete it:

"Is that the price for ___, or does it ___ again after a year?"

"Is that the price for good, or does it jump again after a year?"
For good = permanently. Teaser pricing is the industry's favourite trick — the discount that quietly expires while the contract doesn't. One blunt question drags the reversion date into daylight, and an honest answer ("fixed for eighteen months, then it reverts") lets you diarise the next call instead of being ambushed by the next rise.

Take it anywhere: "Is that for good, or does it jump later?" Intro rates, first-year discounts, promotional anything.
Phrase 8

The job: the deal exists only when it's been said back to you. Complete it:

"Could you read that ___ to me — price, contract, end date?"

"Could you read that back to me — price, contract, end date?"
After the haggling, the audit. Making them read the deal back catches everything a happy customer misses: the contract length that reset, the exit fee that appeared, the extra that survived. The three-item list tells them exactly what you're checking — and out came a fact nobody had mentioned: twelve months, no exit fee.

Take it anywhere: "Could you read that back to me — ___, ___, ___?" Name the fields you care about; make the deal recite itself.
Phrase 9

The job: evidence before the line goes dead. Complete it:

"And can I get that confirmed by ___ before we ___ ___?"

"And can I get that confirmed by email before we hang up?"
Before we hang up is the whole point. "It'll be in your next bill" and "the system sends it automatically" are where phone deals go to dissolve — and a promised email that never arrives is unprovable by teatime. Stay on the line, watch it land, thank them warmly. Thirty extra seconds; sixteen pounds a month.

Take it anywhere: "Can I get that in writing before we finish?" Every verbal agreement, everywhere, always.
Phrase 10

The job: closing so the human on the headset had a good call too. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "You've made that easy — I appreciate it."
Darren didn't set the pricing policy; he walked you down the ladder as fast as his screen allowed. Naming that — "made it easy" — costs nothing and is simply accurate. And notice what warmth bought at the end of this call: the free masterclass. Ask for retentions. Say the magic words. Most callers don't have the other price ready. People tell things to callers they liked.

Take it anywhere: "You've made that easy — I appreciate it." For every gatekeeper who opened the gate.

The Echo 🕰️

In the East End of London in the 1960s, the Kray twins ran what they called protection. Shopkeepers and club owners paid a weekly amount, and in return nothing happened to their windows. The longer you'd paid, the more you paid — loyalty, in that economy, was measured and billed. And the one question nobody dared ask at collection time was the obvious one: what exactly am I paying for?

Everyone understood the real answer: for things to stay as they were. It worked until people stopped being afraid of the conversation — witnesses talked, the twins went down in 1969, and the weekly envelope turned out to have been optional all along, if enough people said no at once.

Your broadband provider is not the Krays. But any bill that rises quietly each year, for things staying as they are, is charging you for the conversation you haven't had. The difference is that your conversation is safe, takes twenty minutes, and ends with an email and sixteen pounds a month. Have it.
