Warm-Up: Identifying Cultural Preferences

Think about your own work experience. Do you work with people from different cultures, backgrounds, or regions within your own country?

Consider these scenarios:

  • A meeting runs 45 minutes over schedule. Some colleagues seem frustrated; others are comfortable. Why might cultural preferences explain this?
  • Your manager asks the team for ideas before explaining the company direction. Does this feel inclusive to everyone equally?
  • After a business decision, one colleague starts building a personal relationship with you; another keeps things professional. Is one approach better than the other?
  • A colleague challenges your proposed approach immediately in the meeting. Do they lack respect, or do they value direct feedback?

Key insight: Cultural differences aren't about right and wrong preferences — they're about different ways of working effectively. Understanding them helps you adapt and collaborate better.

How can you identify someone's cultural preferences?

Click to reveal observation strategies
Watch for patterns in their communication:
  • Do they speak frequently or wait to be invited to speak?
  • When they disagree, are they direct or diplomatic?
  • Do they emphasize individual achievement or team harmony?
  • How formal are their greetings and interactions?
  • Do they seem to value efficiency, or relationship-building?
  • Are they comfortable making quick decisions, or do they prefer consultation?

The Five Cultural Dimensions

Researchers have identified five key dimensions that shape how people prefer to work. None is better or worse — they're simply different preferences. As you move into multicultural teams, understanding where you and your colleagues sit on these spectrums helps explain misunderstandings and find better ways to adapt.

3. TASK-FOCUSED ↔ RELATIONSHIP-FOCUSED
Task-focused Relationship-focused

Task-focused: People prioritize completing the work efficiently. Small talk before meetings feels like wasted time. Let's get straight to business.

Relationship-focused: People prioritize building trust and connection first. They chat, ask about family, and establish rapport before discussing work. The relationship is the foundation for doing business together.

4. RISK-ORIENTED ↔ RESTRAINT-ORIENTED
Risk-oriented Restraint-oriented

Risk-oriented: People are comfortable with uncertainty and quick decisions. They prefer action and learning from mistakes. "We can figure it out as we go."

Restraint-oriented: People prefer careful planning and minimize risk. They want clear rules, detailed preparation, and certainty before moving forward. "Let's plan this thoroughly."

5. TOP-DOWN DECISIONS ↔ CONSENSUS DECISIONS
Top-down Consensus

Top-down: Senior leaders make decisions and communicate them downward. Decisions are faster. People expect to be told what to do.

Consensus: Leaders consult the team before deciding. It takes longer, but people feel heard and are more committed to the decision.

Case Studies: Where Cultural Preferences Create Friction

Case Study 1: Mahmoud

The Situation

Mahmoud recently joined the merchandising team at an international HQ. He's energetic and wants to contribute, but he's becoming frustrated.

What's bothering him:

  • Meetings go on too long — everyone shares ideas, even people without expertise.
  • Decisions take several meetings to finalise. Why can't the manager just decide?
  • Colleagues spend time chatting casually instead of focusing on work.
  • The team structure feels unclear. Who's actually in charge?

Analysis: Where are the cultural clashes?

Click to reveal the cultural explanation
Mahmoud's likely preferences: Hierarchical, Task-focused, Top-down

His team's preferences: Egalitarian, Relationship-focused, Consensus

The friction:
  • Hierarchical vs. Egalitarian: Mahmoud expects the manager to lead clearly and decide. His team values including everyone's voice equally.
  • Task vs. Relationship: Mahmoud sees chatting as wasted time. His team sees it as building trust, which makes teamwork better.
  • Top-down vs. Consensus: Mahmoud wants quick decisions from above. His team wants consultation and buy-in from everyone.

Strategies for Mahmoud to adapt:

Click for adaptation strategies
  • Reframe "long meetings" as team investment: The time spent building consensus now means fewer delays and miscommunications later. Trust the process.
  • Adjust your pace for the team's style: Instead of pushing for fast decisions, participate in the consultation. Ask thoughtful questions rather than giving immediate answers.
  • Recognize the value of relationship-building: Those casual chats before meetings aren't wasted time — they're how your team builds the relationships needed to work together effectively.
  • Lead differently: If you become a manager, you might naturally want to make decisions from the top. Your team won't respond well. Instead, invite input, explain your thinking, and build consensus.
  • Find the logic in their system: Ask a trusted colleague: "Help me understand — why do we do things this way?" Listen without judgment.

Case Study 2: Gillian

The Situation

Gillian has just been hired as a team manager to replace a much-loved manager, Gregor, who retired after 12 years.

What's happening:

  • Gillian immediately starts asking what needs to change and introducing new processes.
  • The team resists her ideas and complains that she "doesn't understand important issues" or "doesn't respect how we work."
  • She's confused: the old way seems inefficient. Why won't they listen?
  • The team members miss Gregor. They compare everything Gillian does to how Gregor handled it.

Analysis: What's Gillian missing?

Click to reveal the cultural explanation
The cultural clash:
  • Relationship-focused team + Top-down/Task-focused manager: Gillian is focused on efficiency and systems. Her team is focused on relationships and continuity. To them, her quick changes feel disrespectful to Gregor's legacy and indifferent to their feelings.
  • Consensus vs. Top-down: The team likely expects to be consulted about changes. Gillian is imposing changes without asking their input first. This breaks trust.
  • She's also a woman replacing a male manager in a relationship-focused culture: Cultural dynamics may add extra complexity here (beyond the scope of this lesson, but real).

Strategies for Gillian to adapt:

Click for adaptation strategies
  • Slow down and listen first: Before proposing changes, spend time understanding why the team works the way they do. Ask: "What made Gregor's approach effective?" Listen for the deeper values.
  • Acknowledge the transition: Explicitly recognize that the team valued Gregor and is grieving the change. Don't try to replace him — be your own leader.
  • Consult before changing: Instead of announcing new processes, say: "I'd like to improve our workflow. What's working well that I shouldn't change? Where do you see challenges?" Invite ideas.
  • Build the relationship first: Schedule one-to-ones with team members. Learn about them, their goals, their families. Show you care about them as people, not just workers.
  • Make change collaborative: If you do implement changes, have the team help design them. They'll buy in if they've had a voice.
  • Accept that change takes time: In relationship-focused cultures, trust has to be rebuilt. You might not feel "accepted" for 6–12 months. That's normal.

Discussion & Deeper Questions

1. The "No Right Way" Challenge

We've looked at how cultural preferences create friction. But here's the harder question: if there's no objectively "right" way to work, how do teams actually make decisions? How do you find a middle ground when your cultural preference says "be hierarchical and efficient" and your colleague's says "consult everyone and build relationships"?

Click to explore this question

Possible approaches:

  • Hybrid systems: Many international teams create explicit norms. For example: "Big decisions go to the team for input. Small decisions, the manager decides and tells you." This gives both groups what they need.
  • Code-switching: Some people become bilingual in culture. They use one style with Hierarchical colleagues, another with Egalitarian ones.
  • Explicit negotiation: Ask your team: "How should we make decisions together?" Instead of assuming, agree on the process.
  • The "both/and" mindset: Instead of "either fast OR consultative," try "we'll consult quickly." Speed and inclusion aren't always opposites.

2. What about your own culture?

It's easy to see others' cultures as "different from the norm." But what if your culture isn't the norm? How do you feel when you're the one adapting? What's hard about it? What feels unfair?

Click to reflect on this

Some honest truths about adaptation:

  • It's exhausting to constantly adjust your natural style.
  • If you're always the one adapting, that's not equality — that's assimilation. A good team shares the adjustment burden.
  • Sometimes the dominant culture in a workplace gets to stay comfortable while everyone else shifts. That's a power dynamic worth noticing.
  • You shouldn't have to erase who you are to fit in. If a workplace demands that, it's a sign of weak cultural awareness, not your failure.

3. When Cultural Preferences Hide Discrimination

Here's a critical distinction: Sometimes what looks like "cultural preference" is actually discrimination. For example, if women are told they're "too direct" (when men with the same style are called "assertive"), that's not culture — that's bias masquerading as culture.

Click to learn more

How to tell the difference:

  • Is the standard applied equally? If the same behaviour is praised in one person and criticized in another, it's likely bias, not culture.
  • Is the person from a marginalized group? Certain groups face extra scrutiny. Women are criticized for directness; people of color are told they're "angry" for speaking firmly. That's discrimination dressed up as feedback.
  • Is the "cultural preference" actually about power? If only senior people get to break the rules, or only native speakers get to be informal, that's a power structure, not a culture.

4. Building Truly Inclusive Teams

Understanding cultural dimensions is a start. But real inclusion means more than just tolerating difference. What would it look like for a team to genuinely value and leverage cultural diversity?

Click for strategies
  • Explicit cultural negotiation: Talk openly about how the team will work across differences. Don't assume everyone shares the same norms.
  • Flexibility in structure: Allow different people to contribute in different ways. Some prefer writing; some prefer speaking. Some need thinking time; some think out loud.
  • Rotate who sets the norms: In truly inclusive teams, sometimes the egalitarian style wins, sometimes the hierarchical one does. It's not always the dominant culture that decides.
  • Interrogate the default: Ask: "Why do we do it this way?" Often, the "normal" way isn't neutral — it's just the majority culture's way, presented as universal.
  • Build on the strengths cultural diversity brings: Relationship-focused people catch problems that task-focused people miss. Risk-oriented people innovate; restraint-oriented people prevent disasters. A truly multicultural team combines these strengths.
