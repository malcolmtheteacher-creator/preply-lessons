Reports, Proposals & Business Writing

C1 Level · Professional Writing

Why Business Writing Matters

Reality: You'll spend more time writing at work than presenting or speaking. Emails, reports, proposals, documentation — it all matters. And most of it is bad. Poor writing wastes time (people have to ask for clarification), damages credibility (people judge you by your writing), and loses deals (proposals that don't persuade lose business).

Reports That Nobody Reads

The problem: You write a 30-page report. Leadership skims the first page. If it's not immediately clear what matters, they stop reading.

Why this happens:

  • Too much context before the insight
  • Key findings buried in the middle
  • No clear recommendation
  • Dense paragraphs when bullets would be clearer
  • Too many details when executives want the headline

The Purpose of Business Writing

Inform: Get factual information across clearly. Reports, status updates, documentation.

Persuade: Convince someone to act. Proposals, business cases, recommendations.

Record: Create a permanent record. Meeting notes, decisions, approvals.

Most business writing tries to do all three — and fails at all three. Pick your primary purpose and optimize for that.

The Six Rules of Business Writing

  1. Lead with your point, not the context. Say what matters first. Context comes later.
  2. Use short paragraphs and bullets. Walls of text lose readers.
  3. Use short sentences. One idea per sentence. Complex sentences confuse.
  4. Cut every unnecessary word. "At this point in time" should be "now." "In order to" should be "to."
  5. Use active voice. "We delivered the project on time" not "The project was delivered on time."
  6. Make your ask crystal clear. What decision or action do you want? State it explicitly.

Vocabulary: Business Writing & Formal Language

Feasibility
The degree to which something is possible or practical to accomplish
Implementation
The process of putting a plan, decision, or system into action
Milestone
A significant event or achievement in a project; a marker of progress
Stakeholder
A person or group with an interest in or affected by a project or decision
Executive summary
A brief overview of a longer document, highlighting the key points
Scope
The extent or boundaries of what is included in a project or analysis
Methodology
The system of methods and principles used in an approach or study
Cost-benefit
Analysis comparing the costs of an action against its expected benefits or advantages

Matching: Informal to Formal Register

Convert these informal phrases to professional business register:

Informal/Casual

1. "We're gonna do this"
2. "This is really important"
3. "People don't like this idea"
4. "We kinda messed up the timeline"

Formal/Professional

A. "Stakeholder feedback indicates concerns regarding this approach"
B. "This initiative directly supports organizational priorities"
C. "We plan to implement this by [date]"
D. "The project timeline has experienced unforeseen delays"

1→C | 2→B | 3→A | 4→D

Formal vs. Jargon

Formal: Professional, measured, appropriate. "We have completed the analysis and recommend proceeding."

Jargon: Overdone corporate speak that obscures meaning. "We have undertaken a comprehensive strategic assessment and would recommend implementation of said recommendations per stakeholder alignment protocols."

Aim for formal, not jargon. Formal is clear and professional. Jargon is unclear and pretentious.

Your Task: Write a Business Proposal Opening

10:00

Scenario

A consulting firm is proposing to help your company improve customer retention. The company has been losing 8% of customers annually. The consulting engagement would cost $250k over 6 months.

Your task: Write the opening section of the proposal (Background, Objective, Scope). This should be about 200-300 words and set up the rest of the proposal.

Model Proposal Opening:

BACKGROUND

Company Name has experienced customer attrition of 8% annually over the past three years, representing a loss of approximately $5M in annual revenue. While product quality and market position remain strong, analysis indicates that customer retention strategy has not evolved to address changing expectations around engagement, support, and personalization. This represents both a risk to current revenue and an opportunity for significant growth through improved retention economics.

OBJECTIVE

This engagement aims to diagnose the root causes of customer churn, identify high-impact retention opportunities, and develop a prioritized roadmap for implementation. Success will be measured by developing a retention strategy that, if implemented, is projected to reduce annual churn to 5% or below — generating $2.5M+ in retained revenue.

SCOPE

This 6-month engagement will include:

  • Customer interviews and churn analysis (weeks 1-4)
  • Competitive benchmarking and best practices research (weeks 3-6)
  • Retention strategy development and roadmap (weeks 5-20)
  • Implementation planning and stakeholder alignment (weeks 20-24)

Deliverables include a final strategy report, implementation roadmap, and transition support for your team.

Why this works: Leads with business impact (not just process). Clear objective with measurable success. Scope is specific enough that readers know what to expect. Formal but clear language. No jargon.

Plain English vs. Corporate Jargon

The Jargon Problem

Jargon: "We need to leverage synergies across the verticals to drive value-add in the customer journey ecosystem."

Plain English: "We should get Sales and Product to work together to improve how customers interact with us."

Jargon sounds important but obscures meaning. It also makes you sound unsure — like you're hiding behind words. Plain English is more confident and persuasive.

When Formality Helps

To your boss in email: "I wanted to flag a potential issue with the timeline. Would you like to discuss?"

To leadership in a report: "The current implementation timeline presents schedule risk due to [reasons]. We recommend [action]."

Formality signals that you're serious and professional. Informality works with people you know well. Know your audience and adjust.

The Persuasion Principle

The best business writing is clear, not fancy. It uses evidence, not emotions. It appeals to the reader's interests, not just your own.

Weak: "We should invest in training because it's important."

Strong: "Training ROI is 5:1. For every dollar spent, we see five dollars in productivity gains. We recommend allocating $100k annually."

Knowing Your Reader

Read what your boss writes. How long are their emails? How formal? How much detail do they want? Write to match. Some leaders want data-heavy documents. Others want one-page summaries. Some like formal tone. Others prefer direct and casual. Observe and adapt.

Reflection questions:

  • What feedback have you received on your writing?
  • Who writes well at your organization? What do they do that works?
