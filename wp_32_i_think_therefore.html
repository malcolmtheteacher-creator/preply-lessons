A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XXXII of L

I Think, Therefore

René Descartes: Methodic doubt, the cogito, and the birth of modern philosophy

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet the father of modern philosophy. Descartes sat alone and decided to doubt everything — not to deny it, but to see what could not be doubted. What he found was his own thinking. This one insight, "I think therefore I am," became the foundation of philosophy. And in finding it, he also created a problem that philosophy is still wrestling with: if thought is the only certainty, what about the body? What about the world? How do we know anything outside our minds? You will encode three dates, place Descartes at the threshold of modernity, and grapple with the mind-body problem he gave us.

A problem to carry with you

If thought is the only certainty, how do we know the world outside our minds is real? This is the deepest question Descartes asked. And he had an answer: God guarantees it. But once you remove God from the picture, you're left with a puzzle philosophy has not solved: the mind seems trapped inside itself.

II. The Story — The Man in the Furnace

René Descartes (1596–1650) lived during a time of intellectual chaos. The medieval synthesis was broken. The Reformation had shattered Christian unity. New sciences were emerging. And Descartes wanted to rebuild philosophy from scratch, on a foundation that could not be doubted.

Methodic Doubt

Descartes decided to doubt everything. Not because he was skeptical about all things — but because he wanted to find what remained undoubtable.

He began by doubting his senses. Can you trust your eyes? Can you trust your ears? You've been deceived before. Perhaps you're being deceived now. Perhaps a demon is deceiving you right now, making you see a world that doesn't exist.

I will suppose therefore that not God, who is supremely good and the source of truth, but rather some malicious demon of the utmost power and cunning has employed all his energies in order to deceive me. I shall think that the sky, the air, the earth, colours, shapes, sounds and all external things are merely the delusions of dreams which he has devised to ensnare my judgement. — René Descartes, Meditations on First Philosophy, Meditation I

But notice: even if a demon is deceiving me, I am still thinking. The very act of doubting requires a thinker. You cannot think away your own thinking.

The Cogito

And so Descartes arrived at his foundation:

Cogito, ergo sum.

I think, therefore I am.

This is the one thing that cannot be doubted. Not God. Not the world. Not the body. But thinking itself — the fact that I am in the act of thinking right now. That is certain.

From this one certainty, Descartes tried to rebuild. If I think, then there must be a "me" that thinks. But what am I? I am a thinking thing. A res cogitans — a mind.

The split he created: By making the mind the only certainty, Descartes created a problem. The world is made of matter — res extensa, extended substance. The mind is made of thought — immaterial, unextended. How do these two utterly different things interact? If the mind is not matter, how can it move the body? And if the body is not the mind, how can it cause sensations in the mind? This is the mind-body problem. And it has haunted philosophy ever since.

God as Guarantor

Descartes needed a way out of this trap. If my mind is trapped in itself, how do I know the world is real? His answer: God.

I have the idea of God — of a perfect, infinite being. But where did this idea come from? I am finite and imperfect. I could not have created the idea of the infinite by my own powers. Therefore, this idea must come from God himself. And God, being perfect and good, would not deceive me. So the world I perceive — the world God created and guarantees — must be real.

I am, I exist, is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind. But if I am certain of this, could I not be uncertain of what is necessary for my being certain of anything? It is certain that in the strict sense I am not just the understanding, which is to say, I am not merely the intellect. For although perhaps (as I shall say in a moment) I do have a body that is closely joined to me, still, on the one hand, I have a clear and distinct idea of myself, in so far as I am simply a thinking thing and not extended. — René Descartes, Meditations on First Philosophy, Meditation II
♦ ♦ ♦

The Price of Certainty

Descartes saved knowledge by making the thinking subject the foundation. But he paid a price: he split the world in half. Mind on one side, matter on the other. And he had to call on God to bridge the gap. Later philosophers would reject the God guarantee. And they would be left with the problem: the mind, certain of itself, seemingly trapped inside itself, with no clear path to the world beyond.

III. The Ideas — The Foundations of Modernity

Idea 1: Methodic Doubt

Systematic doubt reveals what cannot be doubted.

Descartes did not invent skepticism. But he gave doubt a purpose: to find certainty. He doubted everything that could be doubted — not to end in nihilism, but to find bedrock. And the bedrock he found was the thinking subject itself. This method became foundational to modern philosophy: start from what is undeniable and build from there.

Idea 2: The Cogito

Thought proves existence. The mind is the only certainty.

Descartes made consciousness the foundation of philosophy. He did not say "the body is real" or "God is real" as the first principle. He said the thinking thing is real. This was revolutionary. It put the subject — the "I" — at the centre of philosophy. Everything else had to be proven from there.

Idea 3: Mind-Body Dualism

Mind and body are two fundamentally different substances.

The mind is immaterial, unextended, invisible. The body is material, extended in space, visible. They interact, but they are not the same thing. This solved one problem — how the mind can be free and rational while the body follows physical laws. But it created another: how can an immaterial mind move a material body?

♦ ♦ ♦

The Unresolved Inheritance

Descartes said: "I think therefore I am." But he did not say: "I am loved therefore I exist." Or: "I suffer therefore I exist." Or: "I belong therefore I exist." Augustine, centuries earlier, had said: "I love, therefore I am." Augustine's "I" included the capacity for love. Descartes's "I" was the thinking thing. Which is deeper? Which is truer to what it means to be human?

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

The demon's deception

Descartes imagined a powerful demon deceiving him. Do you think something like this could be true? Could everything you see be an illusion?

Is the cogito really undeniable?

Descartes said you cannot doubt your own thinking. But can you doubt that? Some philosophers have. Is there something even more fundamental than thought?

The mind-body problem

If the mind is immaterial and the body is material, how do they interact? How does a thought move your arm? How does pain in your body affect your mind?

What about love?

Augustine said "I love therefore I am." Descartes said "I think therefore I am." Is love less certain than thought? Is it less fundamental to being human?

Is God really necessary?

Descartes used God to bridge the gap between mind and world. But what if we remove God? Are we left with a mind trapped in itself, unable to know anything?

The isolation of the thinking thing

By making the individual mind the foundation, did Descartes isolate philosophy from community, from love, from shared meaning? Is this progress or loss?

VII. Final Review

1. What did Descartes doubt?

Descartes doubted everything that could be doubted: his senses (they deceive), the external world (it could be a dream or demon's illusion), even his own body (if it could be doubted). But he could not doubt his own thinking.

2. The Cogito

3. The Mind-Body Split

What did Descartes say about the mind and body? What problem did this create?

The body is material, extended in space (res extensa).

The problem: How can an immaterial mind move a material body? How can they interact if they are fundamentally different substances?

The Inheritance

Descartes gave modern philosophy its method (systematic doubt), its foundation (the thinking subject), and its deepest problem (mind and body). He said we can trust the mind because it thinks, and thought is undeniable. But he left a question hanging: if the mind is the foundation, how do we know anything outside the mind is real? Later philosophers would wrestle with this. Some would say we cannot. Others would try to find a way beyond the prison of the individual mind.
