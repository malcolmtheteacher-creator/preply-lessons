What's Your Workplace Writing?

Before we explore how to adapt your written style, let's think about what you actually write at work and who you're writing to.

What types of documents do you produce at work?

Think about emails, reports, proposals, meeting notes, project summaries, feedback, instructions, or any other written communication.

Who do you typically write to?

Consider your immediate manager, senior leadership, clients, team members, or cross-functional colleagues.

When do you struggle most in your writing?

Do you ever feel like your reader is frustrated? Do they ask for clarification, or say they don't know what to do with the information?

The Insight

Most workplace writing fails not because of grammar or style, but because it doesn't match how the reader actually thinks and works. Your reader has a communication preference—and when you get it right, everything flows.

Four Reader Types

Every person has a preferred way to receive information. Research in workplace communication identifies four key dimensions:

Action-Oriented

What matters: What needs to happen?

How they work: Fast-paced, focused on outcomes and urgency

They want: The bottom line first, clear next steps

Get to the point

Detail-Oriented

What matters: Are all the facts complete and accurate?

How they work: Systematic, thorough, checking everything

They want: Comprehensive information, data, evidence

Show all the work

Idea-Oriented

What matters: What could this become?

How they work: Creative, exploring possibilities, thinking long-term

They want: Concepts to discuss, opportunities to collaborate

Let's think big

Task-Oriented

What matters: What exactly do I need to do?

How they work: Structured, clear sequences, avoiding confusion

They want: Clear instructions, no buried information

Show me the steps

Reflect

Which one describes you? Most of us have a dominant preference, though we shift depending on the situation. When you receive a document that doesn't match your type, what's your reaction?

  • Action-oriented: "Why are you wasting my time?"
  • Detail-oriented: "Where's the rest of the information?"
  • Idea-oriented: "How is this actually useful?"
  • Task-oriented: "What am I supposed to do with this?"

Meet Your Stakeholders

Here are four real workplace roles with different needs. Click each card to reveal their reader type and how to write for them.

Role
Head of Quality & Risk Management
▼
"Be quick or be gone! I supervise quality and risk for ten projects – I get hundreds of mails, reports, proposals. Highlight what's relevant to me. I don't need the backstory. If people or processes will be impacted, I need to know. Just tell me what approval you require, resources you need, or people you need put in touch with."
Reader Type: Action-Oriented

This person is driven by pace and clarity. They manage multiple projects and need to prioritise ruthlessly. They can't afford to read the whole story.

What They Value:
  • Relevance – highlight what affects quality or risk
  • Brevity – respect their time constraint
  • Impact summary – who or what is affected
  • Actionable asks – be explicit about what you need
How to Write for Them:
  • Start with the headline: What's the core issue?
  • Use a 2-3 line executive summary at the top
  • Structure: Issue → Impact → Your Ask
  • Skip the context; assume they know the background
  • Flag risk or process changes immediately
Role
Financial Controller
▼
"Don't give me things piecemeal – give me all the information I need. I hate having to ask for additional documents. Do your research and make sure your document is comprehensive and detailed. I'll decide what's relevant. Most importantly, include accurate and up-to-date data."
Reader Type: Detail-Oriented

This person needs completeness and accuracy. They want to make decisions based on solid ground, not fragments. They'll scrutinise your work.

What They Value:
  • Comprehensive information – all the data in one place
  • Accuracy – verified figures, sources cited
  • Supporting detail – appendices, charts, evidence
  • Logical structure – easy to follow the argument
How to Write for Them:
  • Do your homework – gather all relevant data first
  • Use tables, charts, and visualisations for complex data
  • Include appendices with source documents
  • Structure: Overview → Detailed Analysis → Conclusion
  • Double-check all numbers and citations
  • Anticipate follow-up questions and address them
Role
Marketing Manager
▼
"Don't just think outside the box! Tear up the box! Every issue is an opportunity to innovate. Come to me with ideas, not fixed plans. Use documents as a launchpad for discussion. Let's identify the best solution by collaborating and thinking holistically about long-term impact."
Reader Type: Idea-Oriented

This person sees documents as starting points for exploration, not final blueprints. They want to collaborate and think creatively about what's possible.

What They Value:
  • Big-picture thinking – how does this fit the vision?
  • Creative possibilities – what could we do?
  • Dialogue – invite their input and collaboration
  • Strategic context – why this matters long-term
How to Write for Them:
  • Frame issues as opportunities, not problems
  • Share emerging ideas and invite collaboration
  • Pose questions: "What if...?" "How might we...?"
  • Structure: Challenge → Possibilities → Discussion
  • Connect short-term actions to long-term goals
  • Suggest next steps as collaborative exploration
Role
Project Manager
▼
"I just spent close to an hour going through a 50-page report and have no idea what I'm supposed to do with it. I've got a lot on my plate. I wish colleagues would clearly outline what tasks I need to do and not hide information in long, dense documents."
Reader Type: Task-Oriented

This person needs crystal clarity about what they're supposed to do. They're juggling multiple workstreams and need clear action sequences, not scattered information.

What They Value:
  • Clear action items – what exactly is the task?
  • Logical sequence – what's the order of operations?
  • Accessible format – easy to scan and follow
  • No buried information – put critical details upfront
How to Write for Them:
  • Lead with a clear task list or action section
  • Use numbered steps, bullet points, and subheadings
  • Structure: Tasks → Details → Background
  • Highlight deadlines and dependencies early
  • Keep paragraphs short and sentences direct
  • Use visual markers (bold, boxes) for key information

Remember

Adapting your style isn't about being fake or changing who you are. It's about respecting how your reader thinks and making it easy for them to act on what you've written. When you do this, your communication becomes not just clear—it becomes powerful.

Broader Thinking

Use these questions to deepen your understanding of how to communicate strategically in a complex workplace.

Why differentiate writing for different stakeholders?

Is there a risk of over-adapting? Can you go too far in tailoring your message? When is it important to maintain a consistent voice or stance, even if it means less buy-in from a particular reader?

Why use a matrix rather than just four separate "styles"?

What advantage does understanding the underlying dimensions (action vs. detail, task vs. idea) give you? Can you think of a reader who doesn't fit neatly into one category?

What happens when you write to multiple stakeholders with different preferences?

You can't restructure a single email five different ways. How do you balance competing needs? What structure would work for a mixed audience? (Hint: Think about layering.)

What about cultural differences?

These four types are rooted in Western business culture. If you work globally or across cultures, how might communication preferences shift? Are there reader types or values common in other cultures that don't appear here?

How does medium affect your approach?

You've seen how an action-oriented person wants brevity in email. But what if they're reading a strategic document? Does the format (email vs. report vs. slide deck) change how you adapt your style?

Can you adapt too much?

Is there such a thing as over-tailoring your writing? When does adaptation become inauthentic or manipulative? Where's the ethical line?
