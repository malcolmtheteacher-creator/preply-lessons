The Moon Landing

How a promise made in 1961 put two men on the Moon.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of the first Moon landing — a promise set, a journey made, a step taken — using the language of space and the grammar of the future perfect and future continuous (will have done / will be doing).

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Reveal the meaning of each one, then say it back in your own words out loud. (Quick warm-up: which two of these ten do you think will matter most in a story about reaching the Moon?)

1. Standing before the cameras, the president named one bold goal for the decade and made a pledge that his nation would reach it.
a goal / a pledge (n) — a goal is an aim you set out to achieve; a pledge is a solemn, public promise to do it.
2. Each new rocket and satellite pushed the two rival nations further into the space race, each desperate to outdo the other beyond the sky.
the space race (n) — the rivalry, especially between the USA and the Soviet Union, to achieve dominance in spaceflight.
3. At dawn, the rocket's engines fired and it began to launch — to rise off the pad and climb into the sky.
to launch (v) — to send a rocket or spacecraft up from the ground into space. (noun: a launch)
4. The spacecraft did not land at once; first it had to orbit the Moon — circling it again and again before any descent.
to orbit (v) — to travel in a curved path around a planet or moon. (noun: orbit)
5. While the main ship stayed up in space, two astronauts climbed into the cramped lunar module and cut it loose to drop towards the grey surface.
the lunar module (n) — the part of the spacecraft designed to carry astronauts down to the Moon's surface and back up again. (lunar = of the Moon.)
6. The pilot eased back the controls and the craft began to descend, the ground rising slowly to meet it.
to descend (v) — to move downwards; to go down. (noun: descent. Opposite: ascend.)
7. The pilot held his breath until the legs settled and a gentle touchdown was confirmed, the engines finally cut.
touchdown (n) — the moment a spacecraft (or aircraft) lands and touches the surface. (verb: to touch down)
8. Some discoveries belong to one country, but a few are gifts to all of mankind, shared by every person on Earth.
mankind (n) — human beings considered as a whole; humanity. (A more modern, neutral equivalent is humankind.)
9. Years later, people still spoke of that day as a milestone, dividing all that came before from everything that followed.
a milestone (n) — an important event marking a significant stage or turning point in a development.
10. It was broadcast live on television, so that hundreds of millions could watch the same moment at the same time.
to broadcast (v) — to transmit a programme or signal by television or radio to a wide audience. (noun: a broadcast)

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A promise no one knew how to keep

In 1961, President John F. Kennedy stood before his nation and set it a goal so bold it sounded almost reckless: the United States, he pledged, would land a man on the Moon and return him safely to Earth before the end of the decade. At the time, this was an extraordinary thing to promise. No one yet knew exactly how it could be done. But the words were spoken, and the clock had started.

Behind the pledge lay a contest. The Soviet Union had already put the first satellite, and then the first human, into space, and the two superpowers were locked in a "space race" — a Cold War fought not with weapons but with rockets, in which getting to the Moon first would prove which system, and which way of life, was the stronger. Kennedy had not chosen the Moon because it was easy. He had chosen it precisely because it was hard.

For eight years, hundreds of thousands of engineers, scientists and workers bent themselves to that single sentence. They would have to build a rocket more powerful than any in history, and a fragile craft that could carry human beings down to another world and back. The promise had been made. Now it had to be kept.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What exactly did Kennedy pledge in 1961, and by when?

2. Why was reaching the Moon about far more than science?

1. He pledged that the United States would land a man on the Moon and return him safely to Earth before the end of the decade (the 1960s).

2. It was part of the Cold War "space race" against the Soviet Union — a contest over which superpower, and which way of life, was the stronger.

Discuss:
  • Kennedy promised something nobody yet knew how to achieve. Is it wise — or reckless — for a leader to make a public pledge before the path to it exists?

Part 2 — The journey to another world

Eight years later, the promise took the form of a single spacecraft: Apollo 11, carrying three astronauts. Neil Armstrong was the commander. Buzz Aldrin was the lunar module pilot. Michael Collins was the command module pilot — the man who would fly them there, and fly them home, but who would never himself set foot on the Moon. On 16 July 1969, from the Kennedy Space Center, the rocket launched, climbing on a column of fire while the world held its breath.

Three days later, on 19 July, the spacecraft slipped into orbit around the Moon, circling the grey, cratered surface that no human had ever touched. Then came the most dangerous moment of all. On 20 July, the lunar module — named "Eagle," and carrying Armstrong and Aldrin — separated from the command module and began its descent towards the surface. Collins stayed behind, alone, orbiting above in the command module, waiting.

Down went the Eagle, towards a plain called the Sea of Tranquility, until at last its legs settled onto the dust of another world. Touchdown. From the silence came Armstrong's voice, crackling across nearly four hundred thousand kilometres of space: "The Eagle has landed." For the first time in history, human beings had brought a craft safely down onto the Moon.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who were the three astronauts, and what was each one's role?

2. What happened on 20 July, and which words marked the landing?

1. Neil Armstrong (commander), Buzz Aldrin (lunar module pilot), and Michael Collins (command module pilot, who flew them but never landed).

2. The lunar module "Eagle," carrying Armstrong and Aldrin, separated and descended to the Sea of Tranquility while Collins orbited alone; Armstrong radioed, "The Eagle has landed."

Discuss:
  • Three men went; only two would walk on the Moon. What kind of person agrees to fly all that way knowing they will stay in the spacecraft alone?

Part 3 — One small step, and the journey home

The Eagle was down, but the greatest moment was still to come. About six and a half hours after touchdown, Armstrong climbed slowly down the ladder, and as his boot pressed into the lunar dust he spoke the words that would outlive everyone who heard them: "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind." Moments later, Aldrin climbed down to join him, and two human beings stood together on the surface of the Moon.

They were not alone in the moment. The whole thing was broadcast live, and an estimated 650 million people — then the largest audience in the history of the world — watched on television as it happened. High above them, Michael Collins kept orbiting, alone in the command module, the only one of the three who would never walk on the Moon, while his crewmates made history on the surface below.

Then came the hardest part to remember and the easiest to forget: getting home. The astronauts lifted off the Moon, reunited with Collins, and turned back towards Earth. On 24 July 1969, their capsule splashed down safely in the Pacific Ocean. Kennedy's pledge — a man on the Moon, returned safely, before the end of the decade — had been kept, with months to spare. The man who made the promise had not lived to see it; the promise itself was kept.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Armstrong say as he stepped onto the Moon, and how many people watched?

2. How and when did the mission end — and was the original promise kept?

1. "That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind." An estimated 650 million people — then the largest audience in history — watched on television.

2. The astronauts returned and splashed down in the Pacific Ocean on 24 July 1969. Kennedy's pledge was kept — with months to spare before the end of the decade.

Discuss:
  • Kennedy never saw the promise kept, yet it was kept anyway. What does it take to finish something that was started by someone who is no longer there?

The grammar this story runs on: future perfect & future continuous

Stand at Kennedy's podium in 1961 and look forward. From there, two future forms do very different jobs: one freezes the action in progress at a future moment; the other marks it finished by a future point. The Moon mission — with its hard 1969 deadline — is the perfect place to feel the difference.

The two forms:
• Future continuous — will be + -ing: an action in progress at a moment in the future. "This time tomorrow, the crew will be orbiting the Moon."
• Future perfect — will have + past participle: an action completed by a future point. "By the end of the decade, America will have landed a man on the Moon."
• Compare with the simple future (will), which just states that something happens: "The rocket will launch in July." The continuous stretches it out; the perfect looks back on it as done.

1. In progress at a future moment — future continuous

Picture the scene unfolding. Each action is happening at the time named — not finished, not yet to start, but underway.

1. This time tomorrow, Armstrong and Aldrin (descend) towards the Sea of Tranquility.

2. While the two men walk on the surface, Collins (orbit) high above them, alone.

3. At nine tonight, 650 million people (watch) the same moment on television.

1. will be descending (in progress at that future moment)

2. will be orbiting

3. will be watching

2. Finished by a future point — future perfect

Set a deadline, then look back from beyond it. The action is already complete by that time. Watch the signpost word by.

1. By the end of the decade, America (land) a man on the Moon.

2. By the time Collins sees them again, the crew (collect) their samples of Moon rock.

3. By 24 July, the capsule (splash) down safely in the Pacific.

1. will have landed (completed by that future point)

2. will have collected

3. will have splashed

3. Choose the right future — continuous, perfect, or simple

Read each situation and decide: is the action in progress (continuous), already finished by a point (perfect), or just a plain fact (simple will)?

1. Don't call at noon — the engineers (run) the final checks then. (in progress)

2. By the time you wake up, the Eagle (separate) from the command module. (finished by then)

3. The mission (cost) billions, but Kennedy believes it is worth it. (a plain prediction)

4. By 1970, the Soviet Union (not / reach) the Moon first. (finished — or not — by then)

5. At the press conference, NASA (explain) the plan while reporters take notes. (in progress)

1. will be running (future continuous — in progress at noon)

2. will have separated (future perfect — done by then)

3. will cost (simple future — a plain prediction)

4. will not have reached (future perfect, negative)

5. will be explaining (future continuous — in progress)

Hold on to the contrast: will be doing for an action underway, will have done for an action already complete by a deadline.
