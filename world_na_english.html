The stereotype: "Americans and Canadians speak exactly the same English." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Webster's revolution

After independence, the American dictionary maker Noah Webster wanted the new nation to have its own English. In his 1828 dictionary he promoted simpler spellings such as "color", "center" and "defense". He said that a national language was a band of national union. Many of his changes stuck, and they are a big reason why American spelling looks different from British spelling today.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Webster publish in 1828?

2. Give an example of his spellings.

1. A dictionary.

2. color, center, defense (any).

Discuss:
  • Should spelling be simple or traditional?

Part 2 — Canadian English: a mix

Canadian English often mixes the two traditions. Canadians usually write "colour" and "centre", like the British, but say "truck" and "gas", like Americans. A Canadian might tell you that a "toque" is a winter hat and that a "double-double" is a coffee with two creams and two sugars. Some Canadians also end sentences with "eh?", though linguists say its use is more varied than the jokes suggest.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which spelling do Canadians usually use?

2. What is a "toque"?

1. British-style, e.g. colour, centre.

2. A winter hat.

Discuss:
  • Which variety of English do you prefer?

Part 3 — More than English

Quebec's Charter of the French Language (1977), often called Bill 101, made French the main language of work, signs and schooling in the province. In the USA, more than 40 million people speak Spanish at home, and many cities have large Chinese, Vietnamese, Tagalog or Arabic-speaking communities. Hundreds of Indigenous languages were spoken before Europeans arrived; many are endangered, but revival programmes are growing.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Bill 101 do?

2. How many people speak Spanish at home in the USA?

1. Made French the main language of work, signs and schooling in Quebec.

2. More than 40 million.

Discuss:
  • Should governments protect languages?

Grammar — Reported Speech

Misunderstandings often begin with what someone said — and what we thought they meant. Reported speech moves the tense back and changes pronouns.

How it works:
• "It's quite good," she said → She said that it was quite good.
• "I will think about it," he said → He said he would think about it.
• Reporting verbs: claimed, suggested, admitted, insisted, explained.

Going deeper

The stereotype that it's "all the same English" comes from the huge reach of American media. Differences are small enough to understand easily, but big enough to matter for identity — especially for Canadians, who use spelling and vocabulary to show they aren't American, and for Quebec, where French is central to identity.

Worth noticing: In reported speech, tenses usually move back (is → was, will → would, have → had), and "tell" needs a person.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Match your audience. When writing for Canadians, consider British-style spelling (colour, centre); for Americans, use American spelling. In Quebec, start with "Bonjour" — even a few words of French are appreciated.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Is spelling part of national identity?
  • Should English learners pick one variety?
  • Which language would you learn in North America?
