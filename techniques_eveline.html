Eveline

James Joyce (1914) — a young woman at a window, one suitcase packed, deciding whether a life can be left behind

Easier version available: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Read in three parts

Joyce tells the whole story in about two thousand words, and almost nothing "happens" — a woman sits at a window, then stands at a quay. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. Watch how the past keeps pulling at her even as the future waits at the dock.

Part 1 — At the window

She sat at the window watching the evening invade the avenue. Her head was leaned against the window curtains, and in her nostrils was the odour of dusty cretonne. She was tired.

Few people passed. The man out of the last house passed on his way home; she heard his footsteps clacking along the concrete pavement and afterwards crunching on the cinder path before the new red houses. One time there used to be a field there in which they used to play every evening with other people's children. Then a man from Belfast bought the field and built houses in it — not like their little brown houses, but bright brick houses with shining roofs.

The children of the avenue used to play together in that field — the Devines, the Waters, the Dunns, little Keogh the cripple, she and her brothers and sisters. Her father used to hunt them in out of the field with his blackthorn stick; but usually little Keogh used to keep watch and call out when he saw her father coming. Still they seemed to have been rather happy then. Her father was not so bad then, and besides, her mother was alive.

That was a long time ago; she and her brothers and sisters were all grown up; her mother was dead. Everything changes. Now she was going to go away like the others, to leave her home.

Home! She looked round the room, reviewing all its familiar objects which she had dusted once a week for so many years, wondering where on earth all the dust came from. Perhaps she would never see again those familiar objects from which she had never dreamed of being divided.

Pause and discuss:
  • The very first thing we are given is dust — "the odour of dusty cretonne." Why might Joyce open a story about escape with dust?
  • Eveline keeps slipping into the past — the field, the children, the time before. What is the past offering her that the present is not?
  • She has dusted these same objects "once a week for so many years." Is that a comfort or a sentence?

Part 2 — The reasons to go, and the reasons to stay

She had consented to go away, to leave her home. Was that wise? She tried to weigh each side of the question. In her home anyway she had shelter and food; she had those whom she had known all her life about her. Of course she had to work hard, both in the house and at business. What would they say of her in the Stores when they found out that she had run away with a fellow? Say she was a fool, perhaps; and her place would be filled up by advertisement.

She would not be treated as her mother had been. Even now, though she was over nineteen, she sometimes felt herself in danger of her father's temper. When they were growing up he had never gone for her, as he used to go for the others, because she was a girl; but latterly he had begun to threaten her and say what he would do to her only for her dead mother's sake. And now she had nobody to protect her.

But in her new home, in a distant unknown country, it would not be like that. Then she would be married — she, Eveline. People would treat her with respect then. She would not be treated as her mother had been.

Frank was very kind, manly, open-hearted. She was to go away with him by the night-boat to be his wife and to live with him in Buenos Aires, where he had a home waiting for her. How well she remembered the first time she had seen him; he was lodging in a house on the main road where she used to visit. He used to meet her outside the Stores every evening and see her home. He took her to see The Bohemian Girl, and she felt elated as she sat in an unaccustomed part of the theatre with him. He was awfully fond of music and sang a little. People knew that they were courting, and, when he sang about the lass that loves a sailor, she always felt pleasantly confused.

Her father had found out about the affair and had forbidden her to have anything to say to him. "I know these sailor chaps," he said. And one day he had quarrelled with Frank, and after that she had to meet her lover secretly.

Pause and discuss:
  • Make two columns in your head: reasons to go, reasons to stay. Which list is longer? Which is heavier?
  • Notice that Eveline never quite describes Frank himself — she describes the theatre, the song, the feeling. What does she seem to be in love with?
  • "People would treat her with respect then." How much of her decision is about Frank, and how much is about escaping who she is at home?

Part 3 — The quay

A bell clanged upon her heart. She felt him seize her hand:

"Come!"

All the seas of the world tumbled about her heart. He was drawing her into them: he would drown her. She gripped with both hands at the iron railing.

"Come!"

No! No! No! It was impossible. Her hands clutched the iron in frenzy. Amid the seas she sent a cry of anguish!

"Eveline! Evvy!"

He rushed beyond the barrier and called to her to follow. He was shouted at to go on, but he still called to her. She set her white face to him, passive, like a helpless animal. Her eyes gave him no sign of love or farewell or recognition.

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • Read the last sentence again. Joyce gives us no thoughts, no reasons — only a face. Why does he refuse to tell us what she is feeling?
  • Earlier, the future was a "home waiting." Here the sea will "drown her." What has happened to the language of escape?
  • Has Eveline made a choice, or failed to make one? Is there a difference?
  • Your turn: Tell me about a threshold you once stood at — a door you could have walked through but didn't, or one you walked through against every fear. What held you, or what carried you?

The grammar this story teaches itself

Almost every memory in this story is a habit: the children who used to play, the father who would hunt them in, the lover who used to meet her. That is exactly how English narrates the repeated, finished past — with used to and would.

1. used to + infinitive — past states and past habits

Pattern: used to + base verb. It tells us something was true repeatedly or for a long time in the past, but is not true now. It works for both habits (actions) and states (situations).

1. There (be) a field there where the children played. (state)

2. They (play) in that field every evening. (habit)

3. Frank (meet) her outside the Stores every evening. (habit)

4. Her father (not be) so bad when her mother was alive. (state)

1. used to be

2. used to play

3. used to meet

4. used not to be / didn't used to be

2. would + infinitive — past habits only (not states)

For repeated actions in the past, would + base verb is an elegant alternative to used to, common in storytelling. But there is a trap: would cannot describe a past state. You can say "He would come home late" (action), but not "There would be a field there" (state) — for states you must use used to or the past simple.

Rewrite with would where it is possible. If would is not possible, write "used to only".

1. Her father used to hunt them in out of the field. → Her father hunt them in.

2. Little Keogh used to keep watch and call out. → Little Keogh keep watch.

3. There used to be a field there. →

4. Her mother used to be alive. →

1. would hunt (repeated action — fine)

2. would keep (repeated action — fine)

3. used to only ("be" here is a state — "would be" is wrong)

4. used to only (being alive is a state, not a repeated action)

3. used to vs the past simple — what is the difference?

The past simple just says it happened. Used to adds the idea that it was a habit or a lasting state, and that it is over now. Choose the form that fits the meaning.

1. Last Tuesday she (clean) the whole house. (one finished action)

2. Every week she (dust) the same familiar objects. (repeated past habit)

3. The night she met Frank, she (feel) elated at the theatre. (one occasion)

4. When she was a child the family (be) rather happy. (past state, now over)

1. cleaned (a single completed action — not a habit)

2. used to dust / would dust (repeated habit)

3. felt (one specific occasion)

4. used to be (a past state — "would be" not possible)

What Joyce does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are why a story in which a woman simply does not get on a boat can stay with you for years.

1. Free indirect discourse — we are inside her head

Joyce drops the "she thought that…" and lets Eveline's own voice rise straight into the narration: "Was that wise?" "Home!" The technique collapses the distance between narrator and character, so we don't observe her hesitation — we inhabit it.

2. The epiphany — a small moment that suddenly opens

Nothing dramatic happens at the quay: a bell, a hand, a railing. Yet in that instant the whole weight of her life arrives at once. Joyce compresses a lifetime of duty and fear into a single silent refusal. This sudden ordinary revelation is the engine of every story in Dubliners.

3. Paralysis — the motif beneath the surface

Across the collection, Joyce returns to lives that cannot move — held in place by duty, religion, family, habit. Eveline literally cannot let go of the railing. The motif recasts her stillness as something larger than one girl's nerves: it becomes a portrait of a whole city's inability to leave.

4. The withheld interior — the face that gives "no sign"

At the climax Joyce suddenly steps out of her head, after a whole story spent inside it. We get only "a helpless animal," eyes with "no sign of love or farewell or recognition." By withholding her thoughts at the one moment we most want them, he leaves the ending open — and unbearable.

Spot-the-technique — quick MCQ

The narration slides from description into "Was that wise? … Home!" without any "she wondered." What technique is this?
Earlier, the future is "a home waiting"; at the quay it becomes a sea that "would drown her." What is Joyce doing with this shift?
At the climax Joyce gives us only Eveline's blank face — "no sign of love or farewell or recognition." Why end this way?

Three speaking tasks

Do all three. Each one builds on the last. The timer is for your benefit — it forces you to fill the time rather than escape it. Use the past-habit structures (used to / would) and the effect verbs from the previous tabs.

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · The place you came from (4 minutes)

Eveline's memories are built almost entirely from habits — what people used to do, what they would do every evening. Describe the place and the people of your own childhood the same way.

Talk for four minutes about where you grew up: what your family used to do, what a particular relative would always do, what used to be there that is gone now. Use at least five "used to / would" structures, and be careful with states (used to only).

Task B · The threshold (3 minutes)

The story turns on a single moment of decision at a barrier. Tell me about a real threshold in your own life — a job offer abroad, a relationship, a move, a resignation — where you had to choose between the safe known and the frightening unknown.

Set up the two sides as Eveline does (reasons to go, reasons to stay), then say what you actually did. Use at least three effect verbs (recast, compress, suspend, withhold…) to describe how the moment felt.

Task C · Stay or go? (4 minutes)

Eveline does not get on the boat. Was she right? Build a two-minute argument that her choice was wisdom — duty, loyalty, self-knowledge — and then a two-minute argument that it was a tragedy — fear winning over freedom.

Use conditionals to imagine the road not taken ("If she had stepped onto the boat, she would have…") and at least one structure that captures lasting past habit to show what her life will go back to.

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. Eveline says people "would treat her with respect then." How much of wanting a new life is really wanting to be a new self?
  2. Joyce called the condition of his characters "paralysis." Where do you see paralysis — staying put when you know you should move — in your own world?
  3. The story never lets us hear Frank's side. Write him three sentences: what does he think happened on that quay?
  4. Is staying always weakness and leaving always courage — or can it be the other way around?
