Are you your body, or is the real 'you' something more?

You wake up in a completely dark, silent room. You can't feel the bed, your hands or even your own breathing. You don't know where you are or what you look like. Would you still know that you exist? A thousand years ago, a Persian doctor imagined exactly this, and his answer still divides people today.

Meet Avicenna

Read one part at a time. After each part, close your eyes and answer the question before you check.

I

A teenage genius

Ibn Sina, known in Europe as Avicenna, was born near Bukhara, in today's Uzbekistan, in 980. He learned fast. As a boy he studied the Qur'an, mathematics, law and philosophy. Then he turned to medicine, which he said he found easy. When he was still a teenager, the ruler of Bukhara fell ill, and the young Avicenna helped to cure him. As a reward, he was allowed to use the ruler's royal library.

How did the teenage Avicenna get into the royal library?

He helped to cure the ruler of Bukhara, and as a reward he was allowed to use the library.
II

A doctor on the move

Avicenna's life was not peaceful. Rulers rose and fell, and he moved from court to court across Persia, working as a doctor, an adviser and even a minister. He is said to have written at night, often while travelling. His Canon of Medicine became so famous that European universities used it for around 600 years. His Book of Healing covered almost everything else: logic, nature, mathematics and philosophy. He died in Hamadan in 1037.

Why is The Canon of Medicine so famous?

Because universities in Europe used it to teach medicine for around 600 years.
III

The flying man

Avicenna's most famous thought experiment asks you to imagine a man who is created in a single moment, fully grown, floating in the air. His eyes are covered. His arms and legs don't touch each other. He can't see, hear or feel anything, not even his own body. Would he know that he exists? Avicenna said yes. So, he argued, the self that knows it exists cannot simply be the body.

What did Avicenna say the flying man would know?

That he exists, even though he can feel nothing at all, not even his own body.
Talk: Avicenna was a doctor who knew the human body extremely well, yet he argued that the self is more than the body. Does that surprise you? Why, or why not?

See it in action

Here is the thought experiment in a modern version. Suppose you are floating in a tank of warm salt water, in total darkness and silence. After an hour you can't feel where your body ends and the water begins. You have no idea what time it is. But one thing is still clear: "I am here. I am aware." Avicenna would say: that is the flying man's discovery. Before you know anything about your body, you already know that you exist.

Connect

Compare

Aristotle and Avicenna: Avicenna said he read Aristotle's Metaphysics forty times before he understood it. Aristotle saw the soul as the 'form' of a living body, a bit like the shape pressed into wax. How is the flying man different?
For Aristotle, soul and body belong together, like the wax and its shape. Avicenna admired Aristotle, but his flying man suggests that the self can know itself without the body at all, so it may be something separate from it.
Plato and Avicenna: Plato believed the soul is different from the body and lives on after it. Is Avicenna's flying man closer to Plato or to Aristotle?
Closer to Plato in one way: both suggest the self is something more than the body. But Avicenna made his case with one simple thought experiment that anyone can try in their own mind.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From Boethius

What did Lady Philosophy teach Boethius about Fortune?

That Fortune is a turning wheel: it gives and takes away money, power and fame, but it can never take what is inside you, like your mind and your goodness.
From Pyrrho

What did Pyrrho think we should do, since we can't be sure of anything?

Suspend judgement: don't insist that anything is true or false. He believed this brings peace of mind.
From Diogenes

What did Diogenes believe we need in order to be free?

Very little: only what nature really requires, like food, water and shelter. Most other 'needs' are just conventions, and they make us less free.
From Thales

What new question did Thales ask about the world?

Not who made the world, but what it is made of. He looked for a natural answer instead of a story about the gods, and his answer was water.
