The story, in three parts

This is a famous American story, told here in simple English. Read each part, then try the questions from memory.

Part 1 — The boy who quit

Dexter Green is fourteen. He lives in a small town in Minnesota, by a lake. In summer he works as a caddy at the golf club: he carries the rich men's golf clubs. He is the best caddy in the club. He never loses a ball.

In winter, the golf course is under snow. Dexter skis over it and dreams. He dreams that he is a golf champion. He dreams that he is rich, with a big car, and everybody looks at him. These are his winter dreams.

One morning a little girl comes to the club: Judy Jones, eleven years old. She is with her nurse, and she has five new golf clubs. She is not pretty yet — but she will be, and everybody can see it. "Boy!" she says to Dexter. "Do you know where the golf teacher is?" Then she has an argument with her nurse and hits her golf club on the ground, very hard.

Dexter watches her. Something happens inside him. When the caddy-master says, "Go and carry the young lady's clubs," Dexter says: "No. I'm quitting. I'm too old." He gives back his badge and walks home. He does not want to be a caddy for Judy Jones. His winter dreams have decided.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is Dexter's job, and what does he dream about in winter?

2. Why does he quit?

1. He is a caddy at the golf club. In winter he dreams that he is a golf champion and a rich man.

2. A rich little girl, Judy Jones, calls him 'Boy' and wants a caddy. Something happens inside him — he does not want to carry her clubs. He quits.

Discuss:
  • Dexter gives up the best job on the lake because of an eleven-year-old girl. Why? What does it tell you about him?

Part 2 — Judy Jones

Dexter goes to a famous university in the East. Then he starts a business: a laundry. He learns to wash rich men's golf socks perfectly. Soon he has five laundries. At twenty-three, he is making more money than any young man in the state.

One weekend, a rich man invites him to play golf at the same club where he was a caddy. Now he is a guest. On the course, a golf ball flies over a hill and hits one of the men in the stomach. A girl comes over the hill. "Sorry! I shouted 'Fore!'" It is Judy Jones. She is twenty now, and she is beautiful.

That night Dexter swims out to a raft in the lake. A motor-boat comes roaring across the water. Judy is driving it. "Can you drive a boat? Then drive this one, and I'll surf behind. My name is Judy Jones." He drives. She stands on the surf-board with her arms wide open. "Why don't you come to dinner tomorrow night?"

At dinner, her parents are not there. After dinner she says: "Do you mind if I cry a little? A man I liked told me today that he is poor. I didn't know. Let's start right — who are you?" "I'm nobody," says Dexter. "Are you poor?" "No. I'm probably making more money than any man my age." She smiles, and moves closer, and kisses him. Dexter knows now: he has wanted Judy Jones since he was a little boy.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Dexter become rich?

2. What does Judy ask Dexter after dinner?

1. He starts a laundry business and learns to wash rich men's golf socks perfectly. Soon he has five laundries.

2. 'Who are you?' and 'Are you poor?' He says he is nobody, but he is not poor — he makes more money than any man his age.

Discuss:
  • 'Are you poor?' — 'No.' — and then she kisses him. What is Judy really asking?

Part 3 — The dream is gone

For two years, Dexter loves Judy Jones. It is terrible. One week she loves him; the next week she goes away with another man. She has a dozen men around her. She says "kiss me", she says "I'd like to marry you" — and she means nothing. She is bored with everybody.

At last Dexter gives up. He gets engaged to a nice girl called Irene. Irene is kind and honest and a little fat. The wedding will be in September. Dexter is calm. He is not happy, but he is calm.

Then, one night in May, Judy comes back. She walks across the dance floor in a gold dress. "Hello, darling. Have you got a car?" In the car she says: "I'm tired of everything, Dexter. I wish you'd marry me." He should tell her about Irene. He cannot. Judy starts to cry. "I'm more beautiful than anybody. Why can't I be happy? Won't you come in?" "All right," he says. "I'll come in."

Judy loves him for one month. Then it is over. Dexter has lost Irene, and he has not got Judy. He goes to the war, and then to New York, and becomes very rich.

Seven years later, a man from Detroit comes to Dexter's office. "You're from the Middle West? A friend of mine married a girl from your town. Judy Jones. Her husband drinks. She stays home with the kids. She was a pretty girl once." "Once?" says Dexter. "She was the most beautiful girl in the world!" "Oh, she's all right," says the man. "She has nice eyes."

When the man goes, Dexter lies down and looks out at the New York sky. He tries to see Judy's face, the lake, the moon. He cannot. The dream is gone. For the first time in years, he cries — but only for himself. "Long ago," he says, "there was something in me. Now it is gone. I cannot cry. I cannot care. It will never come back."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Dexter do when Judy comes back in May?

2. What does the man from Detroit tell him seven years later?

1. He forgets Irene and goes with Judy. Judy loves him for one month, and then it is over. He loses both of them.

2. That Judy is married to a man who drinks, she stays home with the children, and she is not beautiful any more — 'she's all right'.

Discuss:
  • Dexter is rich. He has everything he dreamed of. Why does he cry?

The grammar this story runs on: wanting and hoping

This is a story about wanting. Dexter wants to be rich; Judy would like to marry him (this week); Irene hopes to be happy. Three verbs, one small rule: they are all followed by to + verb.

want to + verb — a strong wish.
• "I want to be rich." • "He doesn't want to be a caddy." • "What do you want to do?"
would like to + verb — a polite wish or offer.
• "I'd like to marry you." • "Would you like to come to dinner?" — "Yes, I'd love to."
• Short form: I'd, you'd, she'd = would.
hope to + verb — a wish for the future that may or may not happen.
• "She hopes to be happy." • "I hope to see you again."
Careful: after these verbs, always to + base verb: ✗ want be, ✗ would like marry. ✓ want to be, would like to marry.

Why people still read this story

F. Scott Fitzgerald (1896–1940) wrote this story in 1922, when he was twenty-six. Three years later he used the same idea for his famous novel The Great Gatsby: a poor boy, a rich girl, money, and a dream. The lake in the story is real — Fitzgerald spent his summers there.

Dexter gets everything he wants. He is rich. He is successful. Nobody can stop him. But the dream was never really about money — it was about wanting. When the man says Judy is 'all right', Dexter learns that the beautiful thing he wanted has gone — and, worse, that he cannot feel anything about it any more.

Look at the beginning again. Dexter quits the best job on the lake the first time he sees Judy. Fifteen years later he leaves Irene the first night Judy comes back. Is it the same thing?
