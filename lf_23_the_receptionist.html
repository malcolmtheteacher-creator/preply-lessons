The Receptionist

Eight in the morning, twenty minutes on hold, and a voice telling you there is nothing until the fourth of next month. She is not the obstacle — she is the only person who knows where the gaps are. Today you're going to get one.

The Scene 🎬

"I'm sorry, we've got nothing until the fourth."

You've been holding for twenty minutes. The thing on your back has gone from something you were ignoring to something that has changed noticeably since Friday, and the earliest appointment is three weeks away.

Here is the thing most people get wrong about this call. Denise is not the person refusing you. She is a receptionist on a low wage, taking her fortieth call of the morning, most of them from people who are frightened or angry or both, and she has a diary that genuinely is full. She also knows things you don't — which slots get released at eight, who cancels, what the nurse can do instead of the doctor, and which patients she can quietly fit in.

Nobody is ever nice to her. Being the person who is nice to her, while completely refusing to hang up without something, is the entire skill.

  1. When you've had to get something out of a system with no space in it — an appointment, a seat, a place on a course — what worked? Being firm, being charming, or being persistent?
  2. Is it fair to get an appointment by being good on the phone, when the person after you might need it more?
You Something has got worse since Friday. Not an emergency, but not something you want to sit on for three weeks. You need to come off this call with something.
Denise On the surgery front desk. Overwhelmed, spoken to badly all day, and holding all the useful information about how the diary actually works.

  2. Why does going to the nurse turn out to be better than waiting for the usual doctor — not just faster?
  3. You leaves the call with three separate things, not one. What are they?
1. Because it changes what she can do — she says so explicitly ("I'm not being nosy, it changes what I can do"). The nature of the problem unlocks different slots.

2. Because the nurse can photograph it and send it to the duty doctor the same day. So it isn't a lesser appointment — it gets a doctor's eyes on it faster than the doctor's own diary would.

3. A Wednesday appointment with the nurse; permission to ring at eight the next morning when cancellations are released; and Denise's name to ask for.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for getting something out of a system that has nothing left — by being the one caller who is decent to the person holding the diary. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The whole call rests on sounding like you're on her side of the desk rather than the other side of it. That means a warm, unhurried voice, no sighing when she says no, and a small pause before each new question so it doesn't sound like you're arguing. Persistence in English is not volume — it's staying pleasant for longer than the problem lasts.

🤝 Getting her on your side

Phrase 1

She's just said there's nothing. Your first move decides the whole call: acknowledge her situation before you ask for anything. Put the pieces in order:

but I could really do withI know you'rebeing seen.rushed off your feet,
"I know you're rushed off your feet, but I could really do with being seen."
To be rushed off your feet is to be extremely busy — so busy you're barely standing still. Saying it first tells her you can see her side of the desk, and after forty difficult calls that is genuinely disarming. "I could really do with" is softer than "I need" but no less clear; it's the standard British way of stating a need without making it a demand.
Phrase 2

She's said no again. Don't argue with the no — widen it. Which version opens a door rather than pushing on a closed one?

A. "There must be something. There always is."
B. "Is there anything at all, even later in the week?"
C. "So what am I supposed to do, just wait three weeks?"
B — "Is there anything at all, even later in the week?"
A calls her a liar, politely — she has just told you there's nothing and you've said there must be. C makes her responsible for a problem she didn't create, and it's the sentence she hears forty times a day. B accepts the no completely and then quietly enlarges the search area: "anything at all" and "even later in the week" give her permission to look at slots she'd already ruled out as unsuitable for you.

🩺 Giving her what she needs to help

Phrase 3

Here's the sentence that changes the call — it hands her the decision instead of asking her for a slot. Complete the missing word:

"What are my ___ here?"

"What are my options here?"
Deceptively powerful. "Have you got an appointment" is a yes/no question, and she's already said no; "what are my options" cannot be answered with no. It also flatters her expertise — she's the one who knows what exists — and it invites her to think laterally: telephone slots, the nurse, the duty doctor, the walk-in. Use it any time you've been refused the obvious thing.
Phrase 4

You need to convey urgency without becoming one of the shouty callers. The key is a specific change over a specific time. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "I don't want to make a fuss, but it's got noticeably worse since Friday."
Two halves doing different jobs. "I don't want to make a fuss" separates you from the callers who do, and it's true. "Noticeably worse since Friday" is the useful half — it's a change with a date, which is precisely what triage runs on. "It's been hurting for ages" gets you the fourth of next month; "worse since Friday" gets you looked at.

🔀 Taking whatever's going

Phrase 5

Half the reason diaries look full is that people want a specific doctor. Remove that constraint out loud. Complete the missing word:

"I'm happy to see anyone — it doesn't have to be my ___ doctor."

"I'm happy to see anyone — it doesn't have to be my usual doctor."
Say this early and you often unlock a diary that was never actually full. Most systems hold separate lists per clinician, and continuity is something they assume you want. Volunteering that you'll drop it is a small generosity that costs you almost nothing, and it makes you the easy patient — which is the patient who gets fitted in.
Phrase 6

Now go further and question whether you need a doctor at all — the nurse, the pharmacist, the physio. Put the pieces in order:

the nurse could doIs that somethinginstead?
"Is that something the nurse could do instead?"
In the conversation this turns out to be better than the doctor, not worse — the nurse photographs it and a doctor sees it the same day. Asking about a different kind of appointment rather than a different time is the move that most often works, because you're no longer competing for the scarce thing. Worth asking everywhere: is there another route to the same outcome?

⏳ Setting up the next chance

Phrase 7

You've got Wednesday, but you'd take sooner. Ask about a list — and if there isn't one, make yourself the easiest possible person to ring. Which version does that best?

A. "Can you ring me if anything comes up?"
B. "I'd take a cancellation if one came up — I'm five minutes away."
C. "Put me down as urgent, would you?"
B — "I'd take a cancellation if one came up — I'm five minutes away."
A asks her to do something extra, and she won't remember. C asks her to relabel you, which isn't hers to do and slightly insults her judgement. B is the only one that gives her a reason to pick you: when a slot falls free in twenty minutes, she needs someone who can physically be there. "Five minutes away" turns you from a name on a list into the solution to her problem.
Phrase 8

Find out how the system actually releases appointments, and volunteer to work with it. Complete the missing word:

"Would it ___ if I rang back at eight tomorrow?"

"Would it help if I rang back at eight tomorrow?"
"Would it help if…" is a small masterpiece of a phrase — you're offering to do work rather than asking her to. It also gets you the inside information almost as a side effect: her answer tells you when the slots are actually released, which is the thing you really needed to know. Ask it of anyone guarding a queue.
Phrase 9

She's mentioned a waiting list might not exist. Ask anyway — politely, and in a way that doesn't require her to invent a system. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Is there a waiting list I could put my name on?"
Note "I could put my name on" rather than "could you add me to" — you're volunteering to do the work again, and you're asking about a thing that exists rather than asking her for a favour. If the answer is no, you've still learned how it works, which is what leads straight into ringing at eight.

💐 The bit almost nobody does

Phrase 10

Close the call by thanking her for the effort, not the outcome — and name the thing that isn't her fault. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "You've been really good about this — thanks for trying. I know it's not you making the diary."
Thanking someone for trying is quite different from thanking them for succeeding, and much rarer. "I know it's not you making the diary" is the line that lands hardest, because it's the thing she says to herself all day and nobody says to her. This costs you eight seconds and it is the reason she'll recognise your name at eight o'clock tomorrow morning.

"I don't want to make a fuss, but it's got noticeably worse since ___." — a change, and a date. What's yours?

"Would it help if I…?" — what work could you take off the other person's hands?

⚡ The twist

You ring back at eight the next morning, as agreed, and ask for Denise. A different receptionist answers, has never heard of any arrangement, tells you there's nothing, and adds — not unkindly — that they don't hold slots for people who ring in.

Everything you built yesterday was with one person, and that person isn't there. You have thirty seconds before this becomes a wasted call. What do you say?

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you accept a "no" completely and still ask another question straight after it?
  • Can you ask a question that can't be answered with no?
  • Can you convey urgency with a change and a date, rather than with emotion?
  • Can you offer to do the work instead of asking them to?
  • Can you thank someone for trying, and mean it, when you didn't get what you wanted?

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Every stretched system ends up with a person on the front of it who has no power to fix anything and takes all the anger for it — the receptionist, the call handler, the person on the desk at the station. They are shouted at all day for decisions made somewhere above them by people nobody can reach.

And they almost always know more than the system admits. Which slots get released at eight. Who cancels. What the nurse can do that the doctor doesn't need to. None of that is written anywhere, and none of it comes out for the caller who is rude.

There's an uncomfortable edge to this, though: being good on the phone is a skill, and skills aren't evenly shared. The appointment you talk your way into came from the same finite pile as everybody else's.
