The Exit Nobody Priced

In 2022 Patagonia's founder gave the company away. It was either the purest act in modern business or the cleverest — and possibly both.

Today: you'll examine the strangest ownership decision in modern business — and you'll master the language of purpose and intention at C1: so as to, with a view to, for fear of, lest the mission drift.
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • What is a company actually for? Give the answer your organisation's actions imply, not the one its website states.
  • Founders talk constantly about legacy. What options does a founder actually have at the end — and what does each one do to the thing they built?
  • Can a business be genuinely values-driven while competing on price and growth? Where have you seen the tension resolved — in either direction?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. He refused to float the company, refused to sell it, and in the end refused even to leave it to his children. Every conventional exit was rejected.
an exit (n) — how an owner converts a business into money or passes it on: an IPO, a trade sale, a buyout, succession. An exit strategy. The case's title move: inventing a fifth option.
2. The fear was that new owners — or the founder's own heirs — would quietly soften the commitments once he was gone. The technical word for that slow softening is mission drift.
mission drift (n) — the gradual departure of an organisation from its founding purpose, usually without any single decision to depart. The whole ownership structure in this case is an anti-drift machine.
3. The ownership was split: a tiny class of shares carrying all the votes, and a huge class carrying all the economics. Control and money were deliberately decoupled.
to decouple (v) — to separate two things that normally move together. Decoupling ownership from control is the structural heart of this case — and of several less admirable ones, including WeWork's twenty-vote shares.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: a trust · a dividend · philanthropy. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means a legal arrangement in which assets are held and managed for a stated purpose rather than owned outright?
a trust (n) — assets held by trustees under binding terms. To hold shares in trust; a family trust; a purpose trust — the last being the exact instrument used here.
2. Which term means the portion of profit paid out to owners?
a dividend (n) — to pay / declare / reinvest a dividend. In this structure, every dividend flows to a climate organisation instead of a family — roughly a hundred million dollars a year, in the company's own estimate.
3. Which term means giving private wealth away for public good?
philanthropy (n) — a philanthropist; philanthropic. The case's live argument: whether this was philanthropy, tax planning, corporate design — or proof the categories overlap.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Critics noted the structure meant the family paid no capital-gains or estate tax on billions, while keeping control. Admirers called that beside the point. The disagreement is about motive. A motive is…
  (a) the stated goal of an action   (b) the real reason behind an action   (c) the legal consequence of an action
(b) — a motive (n) — to question / impugn someone's motives; an ulterior motive; mixed motives. This case is a masterclass in the last phrase.
2. The company had spent fifty years being awkward on purpose — telling customers not to buy its jacket, closing on election day. Behaviour that deliberately costs money to prove a point is a costly signal. It works because…
  (a) it is cheap to copy   (b) only the genuinely committed can afford to keep sending it   (c) regulators require it
(b) — a costly signal — credibility purchased by sacrifice; imitators reveal themselves because they flinch at the cost. The "Don't Buy This Jacket" advertisement is the canonical business example.

D · Collocation & word building

1. You know steward as a noun. Business English has made it a philosophy: complete the pair — the ___ of capital (looking after it for others) · a ___ owner (one who serves the asset rather than extracts from it).
stewardship of capital · a steward owner. The steward-ownership movement — companies owned by foundations or trusts so they cannot be sold — is well established in Germany and Denmark (several household names are structured this way); this case is its most famous American convert.
2. One verb completes all three: to ___ the mission in the legal structure (fix it permanently) · to ___ a principle in the constitution · values ___ in the bylaws.
to enshrine — to protect something inside a structure so it cannot easily be removed. "The purpose is enshrined in the trust deed." Near neighbours: to entrench, to hard-wire, to bake in.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — The reluctant businessman

Yvon Chouinard never wanted to be in business, and spent sixty years making the point expensively. A blacksmith and climber who started by forging pitons in a tin shed and selling them from his car, he built Patagonia into the outdoor industry's most admired brand while treating commerce with open suspicion. The company pioneered organic cotton at real cost, sent one per cent of sales to environmental groups from the 1980s, and in 2011 took out a full-page advertisement on the year's biggest shopping day with a photograph of its own bestselling fleece and the headline Don't Buy This Jacket — an anti-marketing masterstroke that, to Chouinard's genuine irritation, increased sales.

The awkwardness was the strategy, though nobody called it that for years. Every decision that cost money — repairing garments free, refusing fashion cycles, paying for childcare in the 1980s, telling customers to buy less — functioned as a costly signal: proof of commitment that competitors chasing quarterly numbers could not copy without bleeding. On that credibility Patagonia built margins and loyalty most of the industry would trade its logo for. By the 2020s the company was reckoned to be worth around three billion dollars, growing, profitable — and facing the one problem no amount of awkwardness could solve: its founder was in his eighties.

The conventional menu was short and, to Chouinard, uniformly repellent. Sell to a conglomerate, and watch the mission become a marketing asset; float on the stock market and hand the mission to quarterly earnings calls — he had watched what public ownership did to values-led firms and wanted none of it; leave it to the family, and bet a century of purpose on the character of unborn heirs. He would later write that there were no good options available — so, in his phrase, the company would create its own.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Patagonia's expensive awkwardness function commercially?

2. What were the three conventional exits, and what was wrong with each?

1. As costly signalling: decisions that visibly sacrificed profit (Don't Buy This Jacket, free repairs, 1% for the planet) bought credibility competitors couldn't imitate, which converted into loyalty and pricing power.

2. Trade sale — the mission becomes a buyer's marketing asset; IPO — the mission answers to quarterly earnings; inheritance — the mission depends on the character of future heirs. All three put purpose at the mercy of someone else's incentives.

Discuss:
  • "The awkwardness was the strategy." What is the most credible costly signal in your own industry — and what does it cost the sender annually?

Part 2 — "Earth is now our only shareholder"

In September 2022 the family announced the invented option. Ownership of Patagonia was split along the decoupling line: the voting stock — two per cent of the total, carrying all control — went into the Patagonia Purpose Trust, a small entity overseen by the family and closest advisers, legally bound to protect the company's mission and values. The other ninety-eight per cent, the entire economic interest, went to a new nonprofit, the Holdfast Collective, dedicated to fighting the environmental crisis. Every dollar of profit not reinvested in the business — projected at roughly a hundred million a year — would flow to climate work in perpetuity. The announcement was signed with a sentence built to be quoted: Earth is now our only shareholder.

The design's cunning was in what it prevented. The company was not donated to charity outright — a charity's trustees might one day be legally obliged to sell it to the highest bidder. It was not left to the family as wealth — though the family, controlling the Purpose Trust, retained something arguably more valuable than wealth: permanent command of what Patagonia means. It could never be floated, never acquired, never inherited into dilution. Mission drift was not discouraged; it was made structurally close to impossible. Chouinard called it going purpose — rather than public.

Then came the second reading. Tax analysts noted, accurately, that the structure was also brilliant estate planning: the family avoided capital-gains tax that a sale would have triggered and estate taxes that inheritance would have — figures in the hundreds of millions were cited — while the gift to a political-capable nonprofit (a 501(c)(4), permitted to lobby) earned no charitable deduction but paid only a modest gift tax on the voting shares. Critics called it the perfect scheme: control without ownership, sainthood without sacrifice, and a tax bill approaching zero on three billion dollars. Admirers replied that the family had, verifiably, given away every dollar the asset would ever produce. Both descriptions are factually correct, which is what makes the case worth teaching.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Describe the structure: what went where, and what does each part do?

2. State the two readings of the same facts.

1. Voting shares (2%, all control) → the Patagonia Purpose Trust, family-overseen, legally bound to the mission. Economic shares (98%) → the Holdfast Collective, a climate nonprofit receiving all distributed profits (~$100m/yr). The company can never be sold, floated or inherited.

2. Reading one: the purest founder exit ever — every future dollar given away, mission enshrined. Reading two: masterful tax and control planning — no capital-gains or estate tax, family keeps permanent command. Both are factually accurate.

Discuss:
  • Does motive change the value of the outcome? If the identical structure had been designed by tax lawyers and announced without poetry, would it deserve the same admiration?

Part 3 — The experiment runs

The case is now a running experiment, and the early data cuts both ways. The Holdfast Collective has been distributing tens of millions to climate causes; the company has continued behaving like itself — campaigning, suing, closing for elections. But purpose did not repeal retail gravity: in the softer outdoor market of the mid-2020s Patagonia has faced the same slowdown as its competitors, with reports of layoffs and restructuring — awkward headlines for a company whose ownership now exists to fund a mission from profits that must first exist. A structure that gives every spare dollar away has no patient shareholder to call on in a downturn; the mission's income is exactly as cyclical as fleece sales.

The model's older cousins suggest the experiment can run for generations. Steward-ownership is almost conventional in northern Europe: major German and Danish companies — from optics to pharmaceuticals to shipping — have been foundation-controlled for decades, and research on them shows survival rates and long-horizon investment that conventional ownership rarely matches, along with a familiar critique: entrenched control, muted accountability, nobody who can ever be fired by an owner. Patagonia imported the machinery and added an American export on top — the founder as moral celebrity, the announcement as manifesto.

Which is where the case leaves every founder, and every board, with the question it was built to raise. Chouinard solved the problem that defeats most values-led companies — that the values live in a person, and people die or sell. His answer was to move the values out of people and into structure: trusts, share classes, deeds. The unresolved part is whether structure can really hold what a person held. A trust cannot found a company twice; it can only refuse to betray the first founding. Fifty years from now, Patagonia will be run by people who never met Chouinard, constrained by paper he signed. Whether that produces a living mission or a beautifully preserved one — the case, genuinely, does not yet know.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What early tension has the structure already met?

2. What tradition does the structure belong to, and what does the research on it show?

1. The mission's funding is fully cyclical: in the mid-2020s retail slowdown the company faced layoffs and restructuring like its peers, and a structure that distributes every spare dollar has no patient owner to inject capital in bad years.

2. Steward-ownership, long established in Germany and Denmark: foundation-controlled firms show unusual longevity and long-horizon investment, at the cost of entrenchment and weak owner accountability. Patagonia added the founder-as-manifesto layer on top.

Discuss:
  • "A trust can only refuse to betray the first founding." Is enshrined purpose a living thing or a museum piece — and what would you build into the deed to keep it alive?

Grammar — purpose & intention at C1

This whole case is about why things were done — and English grades purpose language precisely, from the everyday to up to structures that carry legal and rhetorical weight. Mission statements, trust deeds and strategy papers live on these forms.

The ladder of purpose clauses:
• to + infinitive — neutral default: "He restructured the ownership to protect the mission."
• in order to / so as to — formal, deliberate; the negative is where they earn their keep: "…so as not to trigger a sale." · "…in order not to burden the heirs."
• so that + clause — purpose with its own subject: "…so that no future owner could sell."
• with a view to + -ing — strategic, longer horizon: "…with a view to funding climate work in perpetuity."
• for fear (that) / lest + should — purpose driven by avoidance; lest is formal-archaic and superb in written verdicts: "The votes were separated, lest the mission should drift."
Intention, declared and denied:
• be intended to / be designed to / be meant to: "The trust is designed to outlive its founders."
• aim at + -ing / be aimed at: "The structure is aimed at preventing a float."
• Denying an imputed motive: "The point was never to avoid tax; the point was to make betrayal impossible." — the cleft of motive, this case's signature sentence shape.

Register warning: stacking purpose clauses inflates fast — one so as to per paragraph; lest once per document, at the moment of maximum weight.

1. Choose the right rung

Complete each sentence with the form in brackets, adjusting the verb as needed.

1. The voting shares were placed in a trust (so that) no future generation could sell them.

2. The family took no charitable deduction, (so as / not) invite the charge of profiting from the gift.

3. The structure was built (with a view to) the mission for a century, not a news cycle.

1. so that   2. so as not to   3. with a view to funding / protecting

Note 2: the negative slot is exactly where "so as to" beats plain "to" — "not to invite" dangles ambiguously; "so as not to invite" cannot be misread.

3. The trust-deed preamble — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the purpose form the register requires, then read it aloud — slowly; this is written English performed.

The founders have transferred their entire economic interest (neutral) fund the defence of the natural world, and have separated the voting shares (so that) control may never pass to owners indifferent to that purpose. The company shall not be sold or listed, (lest) short-term interests displace the mission. One "lest", one "so as not to", one "with a view to" — each used once, each at its natural altitude. That restraint is what C1 formality actually is.

Going deeper

A note on the record: valuations, tax figures and profit projections are rounded estimates from public reporting; the family's actual motives are, definitionally, not in the public record — which is precisely what the second half of this case is about.

What the case actually tests. First, the succession problem for values. Most purpose-led companies are secretly person-led; the purpose is the founder's ongoing veto, and it dies or dilutes at the exit. Chouinard's wager is that values can be moved out of biography and into architecture — deeds, share classes, trustees. The northern-European evidence says such architecture preserves companies; whether it preserves purpose or merely embalms it is the open question, and the honest answer is that a deed can prevent betrayal but cannot supply conviction.

Second, mixed motives as the normal case. The structure is simultaneously the most generous founder exit on record and superb tax engineering, and the instinct to force a choice between those readings is the analytical error. Sophisticated actors build structures that satisfy several motives at once; the useful question is never "was it pure?" but "what does the structure make possible, and impossible, regardless of motive?" Judged that way: sale impossible, float impossible, inheritance impossible, roughly $100m a year to climate — outcomes that survive any reading of the heart.

Third, the series thread. WeWork decoupled votes from economics so a founder could extract without accountability; Patagonia used the identical instrument so a family could commit without extraction. The tool is neutral; the deed is not. Which sharpens this series' running lesson one more time: read the structure, not the story — but read the structure all the way to what it makes impossible.

Talk it through:
  • Steelman both critics: the tax critique and the entrenchment critique (nobody can ever fire the trustees). Which survives contact with the outcomes?
  • Germany and Denmark treat foundation ownership as normal; America treated it as a miracle. What does each reaction reveal about the surrounding business culture?
  • "What does the structure make impossible?" Apply that test to your own organisation's ownership. What can never happen — and is that list intentional?
  • Design the stress test: it is 2060, sales have halved, and the trustees must choose between the mission and survival. What does the deed you would write say about that day?
