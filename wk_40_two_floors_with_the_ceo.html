Two Floors with the CEO

You've seen her on the all-hands stream and the annual report. She's seen — statistically speaking — neither you nor your floor. Now the lift doors are closing on the two of you, she's put her phone away, and she's said 'morning'. You have two floors. Most people waste them on the weather.

The Scene 🎬

The accidental audience with power: too short to pitch, too long to stare at the numbers — and exactly long enough to be remembered.

Let's set the physics honestly. Ninety seconds with the CEO will not get you promoted, funded, or discovered — the lift is not a fairy tale. What it CAN do is precise and valuable: turn you from a lanyard into a name, plant one true sentence about your corner of the company where decisions get made, and — the rarest move — tell the top floor one thing it genuinely can't hear from up there. CEOs live behind filters; everything reaching them is pre-polished by three layers of nervous management. A floor-four person who speaks plainly for ninety seconds is, to a good CEO, drinking water in a desert.

The traps are the two default settings. Silence — respectful, wasted, indistinguishable from the four other silent lifts she'll ride today. And the pitch — the panicked festival of self-promotion, project acronyms and rehearsed enthusiasm that makes powerful people reach for their phones in self-defence.

The third way fits the time available: a name and a place, one concrete true thing, and — if she opens the door — one honest answer to the question executives always ask and rarely get answered: "how are things, really?" You're not performing. You're being the one employee today who talked to her like a person about real things. That's the whole game, and it's absurdly rare.

  1. What would you actually want a CEO to know about your daily work — one sentence, no jargon, no complaint-whine? Harder than it sounds?
  2. Why do we go strange around hierarchy — silence or performance, nothing between? What does the strangeness cost the people at the top?
YouFloor four, invoicing systems, three years in. Knows one thing about the customer-payments process the top floor would genuinely want to know.
Ms AdeyolaCEO, two years in post. Rides the lift like everyone else on principle — and is quietly starving for one unfiltered sentence about her own company.
First: read the conversation and clock what You does NOT do: no pitch, no CV, no "big fan of your strategy". Then find the sentence that makes the CEO stop the lift doors — it's the most specific one in the whole exchange.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for accidental audiences with power — lifts, coffee queues, conference corridors, the boss's boss at the summer party. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The register is relaxed usefulness — the opposite of both worship and performance. You're not seizing an opportunity; you're being a competent adult who happens to share a lift. Paradoxically, that's what gets remembered: everyone else was either wallpaper or an advert.

🙋 Becoming a person

Phrase 1

The job: the introduction — name, place, one line that sticks. Which version?

A. "Oh! Ms Adeyola! Huge fan of the five-year strategy. HUGE. The pillars especially."
B. "Morning — Ana, from invoicing systems, floor four. The quiet bit that works, so nobody's heard of us."
C. *stare at the floor numbers with total concentration*
B — "Ana, invoicing systems, floor four — the quiet bit that works."
C is the default and it's forgettable by design. A is flattery, and CEOs metabolise flattery instantly into nothing — they're marinated in it. B is the professional introduction with one earned flourish: name, function, location, and a single line of self-description that's modest AND memorable. Note it laughs at its own obscurity — which lands as confidence, because only people secure in their work can afford to joke about being unknown.

Take it anywhere: "___, from ___ — the ___ that ___." Build your one-liner before you need it; lifts don't give drafting time.
Phrase 2

The job: credit, shared at the first opportunity. Complete it:

"That was us — mostly my colleague ___, honestly, who rebuilt the reminder logic."

"That was us — mostly Denise, honestly, who rebuilt the reminder logic."
The moment praise arrives from on high, a fork appears: absorb it solo (tempting, invisible-feeling, and always eventually visible) or share it by name. Sharing costs you nothing measurable and buys three things: Denise's name now exists on floor six, your own credibility doubles (people who share credit are believed about everything else), and — watch the ending — the CEO still remembers YOU. The teller of the story is never lost by the story.

Take it anywhere: "That was us — mostly ___, honestly." Credit, upward, by name, at speed. It's the best-returning investment in office life.
Phrase 3

The job: the offer that no executive refuses. Complete it:

"Since I've got you for two floors — can I tell you one ___ thing from down in the payments engine? Thirty seconds, no ___ attached."

"Can I tell you one true thing? Thirty seconds, no complaint attached."
The sentence that stopped the lift doors. Deconstruct it: "one true thing" promises the rarest commodity on the sixth floor — unfiltered signal. "Thirty seconds" caps the cost. "No complaint attached" removes the flinch — executives brace for ambush-grievances in lifts, and you've just disarmed the brace. "Nobody offers me true things before nine" is not a joke; it's a job description of the filtered life. Be the exception and you're memorable for a year.

Take it anywhere: "One true thing, thirty seconds, no complaint attached." The offer that opens any senior ear — then honour all three promises.

📡 Delivering the signal

Phrase 4

The job: the true thing itself — concrete, invisible-from-above, dashboard-proof. Put the pieces in order:

so it never becomes a project —It works,and none of it shows up in any dashboard you see.but it's three people's Tuesdays, forever,
"It works, so it never becomes a project — but it's three people's Tuesdays, forever, and none of it shows up in any dashboard you see."
The anatomy of a perfect lift-signal: a specific process (Meyer invoices), a measured failure (a third fail format checks), a human cost (three people's Tuesdays), and — the part that made her look up — WHY the top floor can't see it: "the dashboards measure what fails loudly, and this fails politely." You're not complaining; you're explaining a blind spot in her instruments. That's not an employee grumbling. That's free consultancy.

Take it anywhere: "It works, so it never becomes a project — but it costs ___, invisibly." The template for every quiet broken thing you know about.
Phrase 5

The job: naming the invisible cost, plainly. Complete it:

"It works, so nobody ever ___ about it."

"It works, so nobody ever hears about it."
The plainest sentence in the whole exchange, and the one that landed hardest — because it names the blind spot in her instruments: dashboards catch what breaks loudly, never what three people quietly absorb. Executives can act on exactly this kind of sentence: concrete, unpolished, checkable by lunchtime. No jargon, no coinage — just the thing, said straight.

Take it anywhere: "It works, so nobody ever hears about it." For every quiet cost your workplace has stopped noticing.
Phrase 6

The job: the fix, pre-costed and unbundled. Complete it:

"Unbundled? Two ___ of work — and three people get their Tuesdays ___."

"Unbundled, it's two weeks of work — and three people get their Tuesdays back."
When power asks "what would you fix first?", the amateur answers with a wish and the professional answers with an estimate. Two weeks, a reason it's stuck (bundled into a big project — the corporate disease she recognised instantly: "of course it is"), and a human payoff. This is what turned the chat into a phone note with your name in it. Never carry a problem to a senior ear without its price tag; the price tag is what makes action possible.

Take it anywhere: "Unbundled, it's ___ of work." Know the real size of your corner's fixes before the lift arrives.
Phrase 7

The job: protecting your manager while going over his head. Complete it:

"Worth knowing: the fix has ___ on floor four. It just needed a lift ___."

"The fix has friends on floor four — it just needed a lift ride."
The danger of lift-signals: they can read, above and below, as end-runs around your boss. This sentence armours everyone — "he's the one who taught me to unbundle things; the bundling happened above him" converts the coming email from an ambush on your manager into vindication of him. Speak upward in a way your boss could overhear with pleasure, and lift rides stop being risky. (It also happens to be true, which is why it works.)

Take it anywhere: "The fix has friends at my level — it just needed the airtime." Signal upward; protect sideways and down.

🎁 The question you get — and the exit

Phrase 8

The job: your one question — the weather, not the forecast. Complete it:

"What should we on four actually be ___ about this year? We get the town-hall version, which is the weather ___, not the weather."

"What should we actually be worried about this year?"
Offered a free question, most people ask about strategy — "it's always disappointing for everyone", because the answer is the town-hall script. This question is different in kind: it asks power to point its telescope DOWN — what should my level brace for? — which executives can answer plainly and almost never get asked. The return was gold: the Keller renewal, March, the freeze condition. One question, one genuine piece of the real map.

Take it anywhere: "What should people at my level actually be worried about?" The question that gets the real answer instead of the town-hall version, at every altitude.
Phrase 9

The job: handling the confidence you were just handed. Complete it:

"Heard nothing, know nothing — and we'll have the Meyer fix done before ___ either way."

"Heard nothing, know nothing — and we'll have the fix done before March either way."
She marked the Keller intelligence "you didn't hear it in a lift" — a trust with a test inside it. The response does both required things at once: confirms the vault ("heard nothing") lightly enough to keep the register warm, and — the professional flourish — immediately USES the intelligence correctly: the fix moves before the freeze window. Discretion plus application: the combination that gets people told things twice.

Take it anywhere: "Heard nothing — and I'll act on it anyway." What you do with the first confidence decides whether there's a second.
Phrase 10

The job: the exit — clean, warm, and on time. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Thank you for the two floors, Ms Adeyola. This is me."
Exit ON your floor — never ride past it hoping for more; overstaying converts the whole exchange retroactively into neediness. No card thrust at her, no "we should get coffee sometime" (she decides that, and the phone note means she can). Just thanks, precisely sized, and out. Her closing words — your name, your line, "I'll remember" — happened BECAUSE the exit was clean. In lifts as in comedy: leave them wanting one more floor.

Take it anywhere: "Thanks for the two floors — this is me." End every accidental audience one beat early.

The Echo 🕰️

She was nobody's idea of a court power: a young woman from a mid-ranking family, in a palace stuffed with duchesses who outranked her. But Anne Boleyn understood the physics of the accidental audience better than anyone at Henry VIII's court — where the corridor, the masque, and the card table were the Tudor lift: brief, unscheduled proximities to the most powerful man in England, which most courtiers spent on flattery so uniform the king had stopped hearing it.

Anne's method, the ambassadors' letters agree, was precisely the opposite: she talked to Henry like a person — wit instead of worship, real opinions, actual disagreement about theology and policy delivered with charm and total nerve. To a king marinated in agreement, an unfiltered sentence was drinking water in a desert. He remembered her. Then he couldn't stop. The girl with no rank rewired a kingdom's history armed with nothing but memorable, true, well-timed sentences — the best lift game ever played.

And the coda is the caution the lesson needs: proximity to power is real power and real exposure, and Anne's story ends as a warning about how fast the second can outrun the first. Your stakes, mercifully, are smaller — the worst your lift ride summons is an email and a manager's brief good panic. So play the game she'd have recognised: be the person, not the flatterer; say the true thing; exit on your floor. And unlike Anne — always exit on your floor.
