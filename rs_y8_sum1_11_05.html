Ethical Dilemmas — Real-World Scenarios

How do we apply ethical frameworks to complex, real situations?

Dilemma 1: Medical Ethics and End-of-Life Care

The Situation: A 75-year-old woman has terminal cancer. She's in terrible pain despite medication. She asks her doctor to help her end her life. The doctor could provide a medication that would painlessly end her suffering, but it would also end her life.

Different Frameworks Respond:

Consequentialist: Focus on reducing suffering. If the pain is unbearable and there's no cure, perhaps helping her die reduces total suffering. The outcome matters.
Deontologist: Doctors have a duty not to kill. The rule "don't kill" is absolute. Even if the person asks, breaking this rule is wrong.
Virtue ethicist: A virtuous doctor would show compassion but also wisdom. Allowing a person to die with dignity is compassionate. But hastening death might violate professional integrity.
Religious perspectives vary: Some Christians say life is sacred and shouldn't be ended. Some Humanists say the person's autonomy matters—they should choose. Islamic scholars generally say only God decides when life ends.

Real-World Status:

Assisted dying is legal in some countries (Netherlands, Canada, some US states) and illegal in others. Medical professionals genuinely disagree about the ethics.

Dilemma 2: Environmental Ethics

The Situation: A factory produces medicines that save lives. But its operations pollute a river, poisoning fish and killing ecosystems. Closing it would harm the local economy. Continuing harms the environment. What's the ethical choice?

Different Frameworks Respond:

Utilitarian: Calculate the greatest good. Does saving human lives through medicine outweigh environmental harm? The numbers matter.
Environmental virtue ethics: Humans should develop virtues like stewardship and respect for nature. We shouldn't exploit the environment just for human benefit.
Religious stewardship: Many religions teach that humans are stewards of creation, responsible for protecting nature. This suggests the factory should find ways to reduce pollution, even if costly.

The Deeper Question:

Do humans have rights and interests that outweigh nature's? Or is nature inherently valuable, not just because it serves humans?

Dilemma 3: Animal Rights and Food

The Situation: Eating meat is part of human culture worldwide. But raising animals for food causes suffering. Should we eat meat? Is vegetarianism ethically required?

Different Frameworks Respond:

Peter Singer (Humanist): Animals can suffer. If we can avoid causing suffering, we should. Eating meat when plant-based alternatives exist is unethical. Vegetarianism is morally required.
Buddhist: The first precept is not to harm living beings. This suggests vegetarianism is best. But some Buddhists eat meat if it's already killed (not killing animals specifically for them).
Traditional view: Humans have dominion over animals. While we shouldn't cause unnecessary suffering, eating meat is permissible. The practice is culturally and nutritionally important.

The Disagreement:

These frameworks genuinely disagree about animals' moral status. This shows ethics isn't simple—reasonable people using different frameworks reach different conclusions.
