Question 1: What is Divine Command Theory, and how does it apply to Christian ethics?

🔒Think about this before revealing!

Answer: Divine Command Theory says an action is right if God commands it. Christians follow this through the Ten Commandments and the teachings of Jesus. Good actions obey God's laws; bad actions break them.

Question 2: What is Natural Law, and why do Christians use it to make moral decisions?

🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Natural Law is the belief that God created the world with built-in purposes. Humans have reason to discover these purposes. For example, sex has the purpose of creating children, so contraception goes against Natural Law. Christians use reason and nature to work out morality.

Question 3: What is Situation Ethics, and how does it challenge other Christian views?

🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Situation Ethics says the right action depends on the situation. The only absolute rule is love (agape). Christians should choose whatever action shows the most love, even if it breaks other rules. This challenges absolute rules.

Question 4: In Islamic ethics, what do halal, haram, and niyyah mean?

🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Halal means permitted by Islamic law. Haram means forbidden. Niyyah means intention — Muslims believe actions are judged by their intention, not just the outcome. These come from the Qur'an and Shariah law.

Encounter: Three Real-World Ethical Dilemmas

The Challenge

Real dilemmas don't come with clear labels. Different ethical frameworks give different answers. Here are three situations. As you read, notice how your instinct pulls you in different directions.

Dilemma 1: Animal Testing for Medicine

A company develops a life-saving drug for childhood cancer. Testing on animals causes suffering. Without testing, we can't know if it's safe. Should the company test it on animals?

Consequentialist: Yes. Saving thousands of children's lives outweighs animal suffering.

Deontologist: We have an absolute duty not to cause suffering. Test on volunteers instead, or don't develop the drug.

Virtue Ethicist: A wise, compassionate person would agonise over this. Compassion applies to animals too, but so does the duty to heal human suffering.

Dilemma 2: Wealth Inequality

A billionaire has $100 billion. Charities say that $10 billion could end world poverty for one year. Should wealthy people give most of their money away?

Utilitarian: Yes. One billion people's survival matters more than one person's luxury.

Rights-based: No. They earned their money. Forcing them to give it violates their rights.

Religious virtue: Generosity is a virtue in Christianity and Islam. Wealth is a trust from God, not a right to keep.

Dilemma 3: Lying to Protect Someone

Your friend is in danger from an abusive person. That person asks you: "Where is your friend?" Telling the truth will get your friend hurt. A lie will protect them. Should you lie?

Absolute Rule: Lying is always wrong. Never break the rule, no matter the consequence.

Situational: Love and protection matter more than a rule. The lie is right because it protects the innocent.

Buddhist: Right Speech means honest speech — but also speech that causes no harm. These conflict. Wisdom and compassion must guide you.

Evaluate: Is Morality Objective or Subjective?

The Big Question

If good people using good reasoning reach different moral conclusions, does that prove there are no objective moral facts? Or does it show that morality is always subjective — just opinion?

Morality is Objective (Fixed Rules)

Some things really are right or wrong, regardless of what anyone thinks. A medical researcher torturing children is wrong — full stop — even if they claim it will save millions. Without objective moral facts, we can't say slavery was wrong or that genocide is evil. Religions like Christianity and Islam claim to teach objective moral truth from God. If morality is just opinion, then on what grounds do we criticise other cultures?

Morality is Subjective (Depends on Perspective)

Look at the three dilemmas — people disagree using reason, religion, and experience. If morality were objective, everyone would agree. But they don't. Every culture has different moral rules. What the West calls "right" the East might call "wrong." Each person brings their own values, upbringing, and beliefs. Maybe "objective morality" is just what one group has power to enforce. True moral maturity means respecting that others see things differently.

There's a Middle Ground

Perhaps core human values are universal — reducing suffering, protecting the weak, telling the truth — but how we apply them varies. Two people might both value compassion but reach different answers in a dilemma. This doesn't mean morality is completely subjective; it means complex situations don't have easy answers. Moral disagreement is a sign that ethics requires wisdom, not just calculation.

Your evaluation:

Do you think morality has fixed rules that don't change, or does it depend on perspective? Can both be true?

Review: Check Your Understanding

Question 1: What is an ethical dilemma?

🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: A situation where you must choose between two or more options, and each one involves harm or moral cost. There's no clear right answer because good values conflict.

Question 2: Why do different ethical frameworks give different answers to the same dilemma?

🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: They focus on different things: consequentialists care about outcomes, deontologists care about rules, virtue ethicists care about character, and religions care about divine command and intention. What matters most determines the answer.

Question 3: In Situation Ethics, when is it right to break a rule?

🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: When breaking the rule shows more love (agape) than keeping it. Love is the only absolute. A Situation Ethicist might lie to protect someone because love and care matter more than the rule against lying.

Question 4: What is the difference between objective and subjective morality?

🔒Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Objective morality means right and wrong are fixed facts that don't change. Subjective morality means right and wrong depend on what someone believes or values. Disagreement between moral frameworks suggests morality might be partly subjective.

Key Terms

Ethical Dilemma
A situation where you must choose between options, and each choice involves moral cost or harm. Different frameworks suggest different answers.
Objective Morality
The belief that right and wrong are fixed facts that don't depend on what anyone thinks. Some actions are always wrong.
Subjective Morality
The belief that right and wrong depend on perspective, culture, or individual values. There are no absolute moral facts.
Moral Relativism
The position that morality is not objective — what is right in one culture may be wrong in another.
Absolute Morality
Moral rules that never change and apply to everyone, no matter the situation or culture.
Conscience
Inner sense of right and wrong that guides moral choices. Different people's consciences may give different answers.
Moral Agent
A being capable of making moral choices and being held responsible for them — usually humans.
Empathy
The ability to understand and share the feelings of another. A foundation for ethical reasoning across many frameworks.
