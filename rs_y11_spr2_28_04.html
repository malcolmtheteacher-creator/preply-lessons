Crime and Punishment

Year 11 — Unit 28: Themes D & E

What You'll Learn

Understand Christian and Muslim perspectives on crime, sin, and punishment. Explore purposes of punishment and compare justice systems across religions.

What are the four main purposes of punishment?

Answer: Retribution (punishment equals crime), deterrence (warn others), reform (rehabilitate offender), protection (remove danger from society).


What does Christian theology say about sin and crime?

Answer: Sin is moral wrongdoing against God. Crime is breaking civil law. Christians believe both require forgiveness and restoration.


What is Shari'ah law?

Answer: Islamic legal system derived from Qur'an and Hadith. Covers crime, punishment, family, commerce. Implementation varies by country.

Christian Views on Crime and Sin

The Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1–17)

Foundation of Christian ethics. Commands cover: - Worship (no other gods, no idolatry) - Respect (honour parents, no blasphemy) - Life (no murder, no adultery, no theft) - Truth (no false witness, no coveting)

Key: Sin vs. Crime
All crime is sin (breaking God's law), but not all sin is crime (judged privately by conscience or God). Crime is public transgression; sin is spiritual condition.

Theological Perspective

Sin: Rebellion against God. Humans are born sinful (original sin). Crime is public manifestation of inner sinfulness.

Redemption: Through Jesus Christ's sacrifice, sinners can be forgiven and transformed. This theological framework shapes Christian views on criminal justice—punishment should aim at rehabilitation and redemption, not merely retribution.

Example: Paul (apostle) was a persecutor of Christians, then became one himself. His transformation shows that even serious wrongdoers can be redeemed.

Islamic Views on Crime and Punishment

Shari'ah Law Framework

Islamic law is comprehensive, covering: - Hudud: Fixed crimes and punishments (theft, adultery, apostasy, murder, alcohol) - Qisas: Retaliatory justice (equivalence: "eye for eye"), but with exceptions - Ta'zir: Discretionary punishment by judge for lesser crimes - Diyah: Compensation to victim or family

Hudud Crimes (Fixed Punishment)

Examples: - Theft: loss of hand (Surah 5:38) - Adultery: stoning (hadith, if evidence meets strict standards) - Murder: death penalty (qisas) or compensation (diyah) Important caveat: - Evidence standards are extremely high (4 witnesses for adultery) - Judges must be absolutely certain - Many modern Islamic scholars argue these punishments are historically contextual, not binding today - Application varies: Saudi Arabia uses hudud; many Islamic countries use secular criminal law

Qisas and Diyah

Qisas (retaliatory justice): Victim or family has right to equivalent punishment. - "Life for life, eye for eye, nose for nose" (Surah 5:45) - BUT—forgiveness is explicitly preferred: "forgiveness is better" (Surah 42:40) - Many choose diyah (money compensation) instead of qisas Diyah (compensation): - Blood money paid to victim's family - Price set by Islamic law (varies by gender, status) - Often chosen over retaliatory punishment - Shows mercy and community restoration Key insight: Islamic law prefers forgiveness and compensation over endless revenge.

Four Purposes of Punishment

1. Retribution ("Eye for an Eye")

Definition: Punishment should match the crime in severity. The offender receives what they deserve.

Christian view: Romans 13:4 says government should "bring punishment on the wrongdoer." But Jesus teaches forgiveness, creating tension. Most modern Christians reject pure retribution.

Islamic view: Qisas provides retributive justice, but Qur'an explicitly prefers forgiveness. A victim can choose qisas or diyah (compensation), but choosing mercy is spiritually superior.

Criticism: Retribution doesn't reduce crime or rehabilitate offenders. It's motivated by anger.

2. Deterrence ("Fear of Punishment")

Definition: Harsh punishment discourages others from committing the same crime.

Example: Public executions were meant to deter crime. Modern research: uncertain effectiveness. People often don't think rationally before committing crimes.

Christian/Islamic view: Both traditions use deterrence (e.g., prison sentences) but prefer rehabilitation. Harsh corporal punishment is increasingly rejected.

3. Reform (Rehabilitation)

Definition: Punishment should transform the offender, teaching them to behave differently.

Methods: Education, therapy, job training, community service.

Christian motivation: Belief in human redemption. Paul was transformed; so can modern offenders. Prison chaplains work toward rehabilitation.

Islamic emphasis: Repentance (taubah) is central. If someone genuinely repents, they're forgiven by God. Justice systems should allow paths to redemption.

Modern success: Countries with rehabilitation-focused prisons (e.g., Norway, Netherlands) have lower re-offending rates than purely punitive systems.

4. Protection (Incapacitation)

Definition: Remove dangerous people from society to protect others.

Examples: Prison, life sentences, restraining orders.

Both traditions: Recognize need to protect innocent people. A murderer in prison can't murder again.

Balance: Protectionists ask: Can someone be eventually released? Or should they be imprisoned indefinitely?

Prison and Community Service

Christian Approaches

Prison chaplains: Christian volunteers offer spiritual support, helping inmates find redemption.

Community service: Offenders repay society directly (cleaning, building, helping). Aligns with Christian teaching on restitution and transformation.

Restorative justice: Offenders meet victims, understand harm caused, work toward making amends. Jesus modelled this (forgiveness before punishment).

Islamic Approaches

Tauzir punishment: Judges can impose community service, fines, or imprisonment for lesser crimes. Flexible, aiming at rehabilitation.

Taubah (repentance): If an offender sincerely repents, Islamic law recognizes transformation. Some hudud punishments can be waived if the offender shows genuine remorse.

Waqf (endowment): Wealthy offenders sometimes make large charitable donations as restitution, benefiting their community.

Summary: Crime and Punishment

Christian perspective: Sin is spiritual condition; crime is public transgression. Redemption is possible. Justice should aim at rehabilitation and restoration, not mere retribution. Prisons should offer chaplaincy and transformation programmes.

Islamic perspective: Shari'ah law includes fixed punishments (hudud) and discretionary justice. But Qur'an explicitly prefers forgiveness (taubah) over endless punishment. Qisas (retribution) is permitted but diyah (compensation) is preferred. Justice should be balanced and merciful.

Four purposes: Modern justice systems blend all four—retribution (fair punishment), deterrence (warning others), reform (rehabilitation), protection (incapacitation). Religious perspectives emphasize reform and forgiveness over pure retribution.
