1. Medicine, law and history are each an academic discipline, with its own methods, journals and ways of proving a point.

2. An interdisciplinary course lets you read a novel, a law and a map in the same week, because it refuses to keep the subjects apart.

3. Which books count as part of the canon — the ones everyone must read — is a question that scholars never stop arguing over.

4. A nation’s myth need not be false; it is simply a story that a people tells itself to explain who it is.
myth (n) — a widely held story or belief that expresses a group’s values, whether or not it is literally true.
5. The belief in exceptionalism — that one’s own country is different from, and perhaps better than, all others — is something critics love to examine.
exceptionalism (n) — the idea that a particular country is unique and not subject to the same rules as other countries.
6. For generations of settlers, the frontier was the line where familiar land ended and the unknown began.
frontier (n) — the edge of settled territory, beyond which lies wild or unexplored land.
7. Behind every confident claim sits an assumption — something taken to be true without ever being proved.
assumption (n) — a belief that is accepted as true without proof.
8. A transnational study follows ideas, money and people across borders instead of staying inside one country.
transnational (adj) — extending beyond or operating across national boundaries.

Lecture: why study a country as a whole?

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then reveal, then discuss.

Part 1 — Two departments, one idea

Before the 1930s, an American university usually taught the nation’s literature in one department and its history in another, and the two rarely spoke. Some scholars thought this was absurd. A novel about a whaling ship, they argued, cannot be understood without knowing about the economy, the religion and the politics of the world that produced it.

One writer who tried to cross the line was Vernon Louis Parrington, whose Main Currents in American Thought (1927–30) followed ideas through novels, sermons, speeches and economics all at once. It is often cited as a founding text. By the 1940s, universities such as Harvard were awarding doctorates in “American civilization”, and in 1949 and 1950 the field gained a journal, American Quarterly, and a national body, the American Studies Association.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did some scholars think teaching literature and history separately was a problem?

2. Which book is often cited as a founding text, and what did it do differently?

1. Because a work of literature cannot be fully understood without the history, religion, economy and politics around it.

2. Parrington’s Main Currents in American Thought (1927–30), which traced ideas through many kinds of writing and subjects at once.

Discuss:
  • Think of a famous book or film from any country. What would you need to know about its world to understand it properly?

Part 2 — Myth and symbol

The first great method of the new subject was known as the “myth and symbol” school. Henry Nash Smith’s Virgin Land (1950), R. W. B. Lewis’s The American Adam (1955) and Leo Marx’s The Machine in the Garden (1964) read recurring images — the frontier, the garden, the innocent American who starts the world afresh — as keys to a distinctive national culture.

The idea was seductive. If the same few images appear in novels, speeches, paintings and advertisements, then perhaps they reveal what a whole people believes about itself. A scholar could read a cowboy film and a president’s speech side by side, and find the same story underneath both.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name two books of the myth and symbol school.

2. What was the central idea behind the method?

1. Any two of: Virgin Land (Smith, 1950), The American Adam (Lewis, 1955), The Machine in the Garden (Marx, 1964).

2. That recurring images — the frontier, the garden, the American Adam — reveal the beliefs of a whole national culture.

Discuss:
  • Which image or story would a scholar use to “read” the country you know best? What would it reveal, and what would it hide?

Part 3 — The challenge, and the crossing of the Atlantic

From the 1960s and 1970s, critics argued that the myth and symbol scholars had assumed there was a single, exceptional America, and that they had left out everyone who did not fit the picture. The field turned towards race, ethnicity, gender and popular culture and, after about 1990, towards transnational approaches that follow people and ideas across borders.

Meanwhile the subject had crossed the Atlantic. The University of Manchester created Britain’s first American Studies programme and first professorial chair in the academic year 1947–48. The British Association for American Studies was founded in 1955, and the Journal of American Studies began in 1967. Recruitment to degree courses in Britain was particularly strong in the 1990s, when the subject, once the work of a few pioneers, was a familiar choice for undergraduates.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the main criticism of the myth and symbol approach?

2. Give two dates that mark the subject’s arrival and growth in Britain.

1. That it assumed a single, exceptional America and ignored those who did not fit the picture.

2. 1947–48 (first programme and chair at Manchester) and 1955 (British Association for American Studies); the Journal of American Studies followed in 1967.

Discuss:
  • Is it ever possible to study a whole country without leaving some people out? What would a fairer approach look like?

The grammar this lecture runs on: defining a field and hedging a claim

Scholars rarely say “this is how it is”. They say “this tends to be how it is” or “this is widely regarded as…”. That careful confidence is what makes writing and speaking sound academic — and it is the language you need to talk about a subject without overclaiming.

Hedging tools:
• Verbs: tend to, appear to, seem to, suggest that
• Adverbs: arguably, generally, largely, in many cases
• Phrases: is widely regarded as, is often cited as, to some extent, to a certain extent, it could be argued that
Definition versus hedge: a definition says what something is (A canon is a list of works accepted as the most important); a hedge softens how certain you are (It is arguably the most important). A hedge does not make you weak. It makes your claim defensible.

3. Define it

A good definition names the group the thing belongs to, then says what makes it different: A discipline is a branch of knowledge that has its own methods. Write one-sentence definitions in the same pattern.

1. canon

2. exceptionalism

3. transnational study

1. A canon is a list of works that is generally accepted as the most important in a field.

2. Exceptionalism is the belief that one country is unique and different from all others.

3. A transnational study is an investigation that follows people and ideas across national borders.

Hold on to this: claim less, defend more. A hedged sentence that you can support beats a bold one that you cannot.

1. Without looking: name three ways to talk about the past in English, and when you would use each one.

2. What is the difference between I used to and I would when talking about past habits?

1. Past simple for finished events; past continuous for background actions or something in progress; past perfect for something that happened earlier than another past event; also used to / would for repeated past habits.

2. Used to works for both habits and states (I used to be shy); would works only for repeated actions (I would visit every summer), not states.
