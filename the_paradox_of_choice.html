Would you be happier with 6 options or 30 options? The answer might surprise you.

The Famous Jam Experiment (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000)

Researchers set up a tasting booth in an upscale grocery store. On some days, they displayed 24 varieties of jam. On other days, only 6 varieties.

With 24 jams: 60% of shoppers stopped to look. Only 3% bought anything.

With 6 jams: 40% stopped to look. But 30% bought something.

More choice led to 10x fewer purchases.

Autonomy and freedom of choice are essential to well-being. But too much choice produces paralysis rather than liberation, and dissatisfaction rather than satisfaction.

Vocabulary Focus (1-3)

1. decision fatigue

Click to reveal

Meaning

The deteriorating quality of decisions made by an individual after a long session of decision-making. The mental exhaustion that comes from making too many choices, leading to poorer judgment or avoidance of decisions altogether.

Form

Noun (uncountable) | "decision fatigue" (no plural)

Collocates with: suffer from, experience, lead to, cause, combat, reduce

Pronunciation

/dɪˈsɪʒən fəˈtiːɡ/ - di-SIZH-un fuh-TEEG

Register: Semi-formal. Used in psychology, business, and everyday conversation.

"By Friday afternoon, I'm suffering from such severe decision fatigue that I can't even choose what to have for dinner."
"Mark Zuckerberg wears the same outfit every day specifically to combat decision fatigue - he wants to save his mental energy for important choices."

2. opportunity cost

Click to reveal

Meaning

The loss of potential gain from other alternatives when one option is chosen. What you give up by choosing one thing over another. Originally an economics term, now widely used in everyday decision-making.

Form

Noun (countable/uncountable) | "the opportunity cost" / "opportunity costs"

Collocates with: consider, weigh, calculate, high/low opportunity cost, the opportunity cost of [doing X]

Pronunciation

/ˌɒpəˈtjuːnɪti kɒst/ - op-uh-TYOO-nuh-tee cost

Register: Originally academic/economic, now mainstream.

"The opportunity cost of watching Netflix all weekend is the book I could have read or the friends I could have seen."
"When you have only two options, the opportunity cost is clear. When you have fifty options, every choice feels like you're losing forty-nine other lives you could have lived."

NOW YOU TRY:

Describe a decision you made recently. What was the opportunity cost? Did thinking about what you were giving up make the decision harder?

3. analysis paralysis

Click to reveal

Meaning

The state of over-analyzing a situation to the point where no decision or action is taken. Being so overwhelmed by options and information that you become unable to choose at all.

Form

Noun (uncountable) | "analysis paralysis" (no plural)

Collocates with: suffer from, fall into, avoid, overcome, lead to

Note the internal rhyme: analysis/paralysis - makes it memorable!

Pronunciation

/əˈnæləsɪs pəˈræləsɪs/ - uh-NAL-uh-sis puh-RAL-uh-sis

Register: Semi-formal. Common in business and psychology contexts.

"I've been in complete analysis paralysis about which apartment to rent - I've made spreadsheets comparing 30 properties and I'm no closer to deciding."
"The cure for analysis paralysis is often a deadline. When you must decide, you do."

NOW YOU TRY:

Describe a time when you fell into analysis paralysis. What finally broke the deadlock? Do you consider yourself someone who over-analyzes decisions?

Vocabulary Focus (4-5)

4. maximizer vs satisficer

Click to reveal

Meaning

Maximizer: Someone who always seeks the absolute best option, exhaustively researching all possibilities before deciding.

Satisficer: Someone who chooses the first option that meets their minimum criteria - "good enough" is good enough.

Research shows satisficers are generally happier, despite sometimes making objectively "worse" choices.

Form

Nouns (countable) | "a maximizer/maximizers" "a satisficer/satisficers"

Can also be used as adjectives: "maximizing tendencies" / "satisficing approach"

"Satisfice" = satisfy + suffice (coined by Herbert Simon, Nobel laureate)

Pronunciation

Maximizer: /ˈmæksɪmaɪzə/ - MAK-sih-my-zer

Satisficer: /ˈsætɪsfaɪsə/ - SAT-is-fy-ser

Register: Academic/psychological, but increasingly mainstream.

"My husband is a total maximizer - he spent six months choosing a TV. I'm a satisficer - I would have bought the first one with good reviews."
"The irony is that maximizers often end up less satisfied with their choices than satisficers, because they can always imagine something better."

NOW YOU TRY:

Are you more of a maximizer or a satisficer? Does it depend on the type of decision? Has being one or the other ever caused problems in relationships or at work?

5. the hedonic treadmill

Click to reveal

Meaning

The psychological tendency to return to a relatively stable level of happiness despite major positive or negative events. No matter what we acquire or achieve, we quickly adapt and return to our baseline happiness - like running on a treadmill and staying in the same place.

Form

Noun phrase | "the hedonic treadmill" (usually with "the")

Also called: hedonic adaptation

Hedonic = relating to pleasure (from Greek hedone)

Collocates with: stuck on, escape, trapped in, the concept of

Pronunciation

/hiːˈdɒnɪk ˈtredmɪl/ - hee-DON-ik TRED-mil

Register: Academic, but used in popular psychology and self-help contexts.

"I thought buying a bigger house would make me happier, but after a few months I felt exactly the same. That's the hedonic treadmill - we adapt to everything."
"More choices promise more happiness, but the hedonic treadmill ensures we'll never be satisfied for long. We always want the next upgrade."

NOW YOU TRY:

Can you think of something you really wanted, got, and then quickly took for granted? How does the hedonic treadmill relate to consumer culture and the pressure to always want more?

1. The Generational Divide

People who grew up with limited choice often seem more content than those who grew up with abundance. Is this just nostalgia, or is there something genuinely damaging about unlimited options? What would you tell a young person who feels overwhelmed?

2. The Freedom Illusion

"Choice is freedom" - but is it? When companies offer 50 options, is that serving customers or creating analysis paralysis to keep them engaged? Who really benefits from unlimited choice?

3. The Satisficer's Secret

Research shows satisficers are happier than maximizers, yet society rewards maximizing behaviour. Should we teach children to satisfice? Is "good enough" actually better than "the best"?

4. The Tyranny of Optimization

We can now optimize everything - the perfect workout, diet, morning routine, productivity system. But constant optimization is exhausting. When does self-improvement become self-torture? What's the opportunity cost of always trying to optimize?

5. Decision Outsourcing

Some people pay for "decision concierge" services, capsule wardrobes, or meal kits specifically to avoid choices. Is this a healthy response to decision fatigue or an abdication of adult responsibility?

6. The Teaching Angle

As a teacher, you make hundreds of micro-decisions daily. How does decision fatigue affect teaching? Should students have more or less choice in their learning? What's the right balance?
