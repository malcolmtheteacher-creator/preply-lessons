Corporate Social Responsibility

C1 Level · Business Ethics

Shareholder Value vs. Stakeholder Value

For decades, the dominant corporate philosophy was "maximize shareholder value." The company exists to generate profit for shareholders. Other stakeholders — employees, customers, communities, the environment — matter only insofar as they affect profit.

This worldview is increasingly challenged. Employees want to work for companies whose values align with theirs. Customers prefer brands that demonstrate social responsibility. Investors increasingly recognize that ignoring social and environmental risks creates long-term financial risk. Younger generations expect companies to take stands on social issues.

The shift toward "stakeholder capitalism" recognizes that companies operate within a system. They depend on stable communities, healthy environments, educated workers, and trust from customers. Destroying any of these for short-term profit is ultimately self-defeating.

The Moral Obligation Question

Do companies have moral obligations beyond profit? This is a genuinely contested question. Some argue that a company's only obligation is to obey the law and maximize returns to shareholders. Anything else is the government's job.

Others argue that companies are moral actors. They employ thousands, shape markets and culture, and accumulate power. With that power comes responsibility. A company that knowingly harms the environment or exploits workers, even if legal, is behaving unethically.

The pragmatic position: companies that ignore social and environmental concerns face regulatory risk, reputational risk, and talent risk. CSR is good business, not just good ethics. But the best CSR programs are driven by genuine values, not performative gestures.

💬 Reflection: If you worked for a company that was profitable but caused environmental damage, would you stay? Would it matter if the damage was legal? If the company promised to improve, would that convince you?

Corporate Social Responsibility Vocabulary

These terms define how companies approach social and environmental impact. Flip each card to reveal the definition.

CSR Corporate Social Responsibility. A company's commitment to operating ethically and contributing positively to society and the environment.
Triple Bottom Line Measuring company performance on three dimensions: profit, people, and planet. Not just financial returns but social and environmental impact.
Social Enterprise A business whose primary goal is social or environmental impact, not profit. Example: TOMS Shoes (one shoe sold = one donated to disadvantaged children).
Ethical Sourcing Ensuring suppliers operate ethically: fair wages, safe conditions, no child labor. Requires oversight and accountability throughout the supply chain.
Fair Trade A certification system ensuring producers (especially in developing countries) receive fair prices and work in safe conditions. Common in coffee, chocolate, textiles.
Philanthropy Giving money or resources to charitable causes. A company's charitable arm. Can be genuine impact or performative — giving to popular causes for PR.
Greenwashing Claiming environmental responsibility without meaningful action. Example: a company with terrible environmental practices launching a "green" product line.
Impact Investing Investing in companies or projects with measurable social or environmental impact alongside financial returns. Bridges profit and purpose.

CSR That Works vs. CSR Theater

CSR That Works: Patagonia, the outdoor apparel company, has built CSR into its core business. They use sustainable materials, pay workers fairly, and donate 1% of sales to environmental nonprofits. Their CSR is not separate from business — it is their business model. Customers know this and pay premium prices. Employees are attracted to the mission. Regulatory risk is minimized.

CSR Theater: A tobacco company donates to a cancer charity while spending millions lobbying against smoking regulations. A fast-fashion brand launches a "sustainable collection" while exploiting workers in the rest of their supply chain. A tech giant commits to diversity while laying off thousands without regard for impact. These are performative gestures that generate PR without changing behavior.

The Test: Would the company maintain this CSR commitment if no one was watching? Would it continue if it reduced profit? If the answer is no, it is theater.

Design a CSR Strategy

You are the CSR director of a mid-sized technology company (500 employees, $100M revenue). The board wants to build a CSR program. Your challenge: design a strategy that genuinely improves the world while also making financial sense.

1. Identify Your Material Issues

"Material issues" are the social and environmental challenges most relevant to your business and your stakeholders. For a tech company, this might be: data privacy, e-waste, employee diversity, carbon emissions from data centers. List three material issues for your company.

2. Set Specific, Measurable Goals

For one of the issues above, set a goal that is specific and measurable. Example: "Achieve 40% women in senior leadership by 2027" or "Reduce carbon emissions from data centers by 50% by 2030."

3. The Business Case

Explain to the board why this CSR strategy makes financial sense. How does it reduce risk, attract talent, or create opportunity? Do not just appeal to ethics — make the financial argument.

Issue: Employee diversity and inclusion.

Business Case: "Diverse teams make better decisions and innovation. Our current 20% female engineers is below industry average. This costs us talent: we lose candidates to companies with better diversity. It costs us business: major customers (especially in EU) are demanding diversity in vendor workforces. It costs us innovation: homogeneous teams are less creative. Goal: 40% women engineers by 2027. Investment: $500K annually in recruiting, mentoring, retention programs. Expected return: 5% reduction in turnover (saves $1.2M annually), 10% improvement in innovation metrics, improved customer retention ($2M+ in at-risk accounts)."

4. Implementation & Accountability

Who will own this strategy? How will you measure progress? What happens if you miss the goal?

The Evolution of Corporate Responsibility

CSR is evolving rapidly. Three trends are reshaping the landscape.

B Corps (Benefit Corporations): A legal structure that allows companies to prioritize social impact alongside profit. Becoming a B Corp requires audited accountability and transparency. It signals that purpose is not just marketing.

Regulation vs. Voluntary Action: Should companies be required to report carbon emissions, labor practices, and diversity? Or should they self-report? The trend is toward regulation. The EU, UK, and U.S. are all moving toward mandatory sustainability reporting. Companies that wait for regulation are already behind.

Consumer and Employee Activism: Employees increasingly expect their companies to take stands on social issues. Customers "vote with their wallets" by supporting companies aligned with their values. Companies that are neutral on major social issues risk being seen as complicit or indifferent.
