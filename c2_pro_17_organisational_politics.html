Navigating Organisational Politics

C2 Level · Strategic Communication

Is Workplace Politics Inevitable or Toxic?

Some organisations pride themselves on being "above politics." They say they're meritocratic. Decision-making is purely rational. The best ideas win. This is often a fantasy. Politics exists everywhere humans do. The question isn't whether politics exists—it's whether you acknowledge it and play it ethically.

What Is Organisational Politics?

Politics is the exercise of influence and power. It happens when resources are limited (they always are), when goals conflict (they often do), and when people have different interests (they do). A good idea doesn't automatically win because the person who champions it, the timing they choose, and the allies they have all matter.

This isn't necessarily corrupt. You can play politics ethically. You can build relationships without manipulation. You can advocate for your interests while helping others. You can seek influence while maintaining integrity.

Political Capital and Influence

Successful leaders build political capital—goodwill, credibility, and relationships. When you help a colleague solve a problem, you build political capital. When you deliver on your commitments, you build it. When you support someone else's initiative, you build it. Then when you need something, you can draw on that capital.

The difference between healthy and toxic politics: Healthy politics is reciprocal and ethical. You help me, I help you. Toxic politics is extractive. You accumulate capital through manipulation and spend it on self-interest.

The paradox: The most successful leaders don't see themselves as "playing politics." They see themselves as building relationships and influencing outcomes. But that's exactly what politics is. The difference is they do it openly, ethically, and with genuine relationship-building rather than calculation.

Three Political Styles

The Purist: Believes organisational politics is wrong. Avoids it entirely. Stays out of coalition-building, office alliances, and strategic relationship-building. Often sees themselves as superior—"I don't play games." The problem: Purists are ineffective. Their ideas don't win because they have no allies. They get passed over for promotions because they didn't build relationships with decision-makers. Their refusal to play politics becomes its own form of politics—one that leaves them powerless.

The Player: Fully embraces organisational politics. Excellent at building alliances, reading power dynamics, and leveraging relationships. Can be effective, but often crosses ethical lines. Players are calculated. They use people. They're not trusted with power because everyone knows they're self-interested. They also burn out relationships because they give nothing without expecting return.

The Pragmatist: Understands that politics is real and plays it ethically. Builds relationships genuinely. Helps others without keeping score. Advocates for their own interests while supporting others' interests. Pragmatists are effective and trusted. They accumulate real political capital because people believe they're working for mutual benefit, not just self-interest.

The Political Minefield

You're a director in a large company. Sarah (VP of Sales) and James (VP of Product) are in a competitive battle over resources for the next fiscal year. Both are politically powerful. Both want your support. Your project needs resources from both teams, so you need to stay allied with both. How do you navigate this without taking sides?

Sarah pulls you aside: "James is blocking my expansion. You know I'm right. Can you support me with the CFO?"

She's asking you to take sides. If you do, you lose James. If you don't, you lose Sarah. How do you respond?

Approach: "Sarah, I respect both you and James. I'm not going to take sides in this conflict. But I am interested in solving the underlying problem. From my perspective, our project needs both strong sales and strong product. What if we looked at how we can help both teams succeed? Let me set up a conversation with you and James to understand what you each really need."

Why this works: You don't abandon Sarah. You acknowledge her concern. But you refuse to be weaponised. You redirect toward mutual problem-solving. This is ethical politics—using your influence to solve problems, not to pick sides.

James texts you: "Sarah's lobbying the CEO against me. I need you on my side."

Now James is asking for loyalty. You need to respond quickly. What do you say?

Approach: "James, I'm not choosing sides in this conflict. What I am doing is trying to find a solution that works for everyone. I'd rather spend energy on that than on political positioning. Let's talk about what you both actually need. Maybe there's more overlap than this conflict suggests."

Why this works: You're clear that you won't be drawn in. But you're also offering something valuable—a third party who can help them see beyond the conflict. That's worth more than blind loyalty.

In a meeting with both of them, they're at an impasse. You need to move things forward without taking sides. How do you intervene?

Approach: "I hear two different perspectives. Sarah, you want resources for expansion because you see revenue opportunity. James, you want resources for product stability because you see risk if we don't invest. Both are legitimate concerns. The question isn't which one is right—it's how do we address both? What if we looked at sequencing? Or what if we found efficiencies that free up resources? Let's solve this together instead of competing."

Why this works: You validate both perspectives. You reframe the problem from "who wins?" to "how do we serve the company?" You position yourself as problem-solver, not judge. This builds political capital with both of them because you're helpful, not threatening.

Close with both of them separately. How do you maintain relationships with both?

Approach (with each): "I want you to know that I respect you and value the work you do. That's why I wasn't willing to just take your side in this conflict. If I had, I would have been disrespecting James. Instead, I tried to find a solution that works for both of you. That's more valuable long-term than temporary loyalty. I hope you see that."

Why this works: You're being transparent about your strategy. You're explaining that ethical politics serves everyone better. You're positioning yourself as someone they can trust with power because you won't abuse it, but you also won't abandon them.

Ethics of Organisational Politics

Where does smart political navigation cross into corruption? The line is different for different people, but there are some clear principles.

Red Lines in Office Politics

Don't lie. Misrepresenting facts to gain advantage is corruption, not politics. You can frame information strategically—"Here are the three reasons we should pursue this initiative"—but you can't present false information.

Don't spread rumours. Using unverified information to damage someone's reputation is crossing the line. Rumours are weapons. They're also untraceable, which is why they're so corrosive to organisational trust.

Don't extract at the cost of others. You can advocate for your interests, but not by sabotaging someone else's. You can say, "I want this resource," but not, "Block their project so I can have it."

Don't betray confidences. If someone tells you something in confidence, using it against them later is a profound betrayal. It poisons trust not just with that person, but with everyone who hears about it.

When to Walk Away from Toxic Politics

Some organisations are so politically toxic that no amount of ethics matters. You can be the most pragmatic, ethical player, and you'll still be burnt. Signs it's time to leave:

  • Lying is normalised and rewarded.
  • Relationships are purely transactional. No one helps anyone without expecting return.
  • Loyalty requires abandoning your values.
  • One person or group controls all power and refuses to share it.
  • Speaking up about problems is career suicide.
  • You find yourself doing things that compromise your integrity regularly.

Whistleblowing: Sometimes ethical politics requires speaking up about wrongdoing. Whistleblowers face tremendous political risk. Their careers are often destroyed. But they're also necessary for organisations to self-correct. The choice to blow the whistle is profound—it says your ethics matter more than your career. In toxic organisations, that might be the only ethical choice.

The Pragmatist's Advantage

Pragmatists who play politics ethically have an advantage long-term. Purists are ineffective. Players burn relationships. But pragmatists accumulate real political capital because people actually trust them. That trust is worth more than clever manipulation. It gives you the ability to move things, to influence outcomes, and to do good work. And you can look yourself in the mirror.
