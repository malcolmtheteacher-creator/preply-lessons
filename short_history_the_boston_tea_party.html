The Boston Tea Party

One December night in 1773, a crowd tipped a fortune in tea into the harbour — and lit the fuse of a revolution.

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Britain ran North America as a set of colonies — territories thousands of miles away, settled by its people but governed from London.
a colony (n) — a territory controlled and settled by a more powerful country, usually a long way away.
2. The government raised a new tax on tea, so that every pound bought in the colonies now cost a few pennies more, with the extra going straight to London.
a tax (n) — money that a government makes people pay, usually on goods, income or property.
3. The colonists had no representation in Parliament — not a single member spoke or voted for them — yet Parliament taxed them anyway.
representation (n) — having someone in a government or assembly who speaks and votes on your behalf.
4. Angry crowds staged a public protest, marching, shouting and refusing to buy British goods until the law was changed.
a protest (n) — a public act showing strong disagreement with something, often to force a change.
5. Rather than pay the duty, the merchants organised a boycott, agreeing among themselves to buy no British tea at all.
a boycott (n) — a deliberate refusal to buy or use something, in order to put pressure on the seller.
6. A tall merchant ship, its hold packed with crates of tea, dropped anchor in the harbour and waited for permission to unload.
a merchant ship (n) — a ship used to carry goods for trade, rather than for war.
7. The men worked in disguise, their faces darkened and blankets over their shoulders, so that no one could name them afterwards.
a disguise (n) — a change of appearance worn to hide who you really are.
8. They split the wooden crates open with axes and tipped the loose tea leaves over the side into the black water.
a crate (n) — a large wooden box built for storing or shipping goods.
9. London answered with harsh reprisals — punishing the whole city, closing its port, in order to make an example of Boston.
a reprisal (n) — an act of punishment or revenge taken in response to something.
10. What began as a tax dispute swelled into open rebellion, as colonists took up arms against the country that ruled them.
a rebellion (n) — an organised, often armed, resistance against a government or ruler.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A quarrel over a cup of tea

By the 1760s, Britain ruled thirteen colonies along the eastern coast of North America. The colonists were, for the most part, proud subjects of the King — but they governed much of their own daily life, and they had grown used to it. Then Britain, deep in debt after a long war with France, decided that the colonies should help pay the bill. Parliament in London began passing taxes on the goods the colonists bought.

The colonists were furious — less about the money than the principle. They had no members in the British Parliament to argue their case or vote on their behalf. To tax a people who had no voice in the decision, they said, was simply tyranny. Their rallying cry became four words that would echo for centuries: "No taxation without representation." Britain repealed most of the taxes to calm the anger — but kept one, a small duty on tea, in order to prove that it still had the right to tax the colonies at all.

Tea was the point precisely because it was so ordinary. Nearly everyone drank it. In 1773, Parliament passed the Tea Act, designed to help the struggling East India Company sell its tea cheaply in America. Cheaper tea sounds like a gift — but the colonists saw the trap. To buy the tea was to accept the tax hidden inside its price, and to accept the tax was to accept London's right to tax them at will. So they refused.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Britain start taxing the colonies, and why were the colonists so angry about it?

2. Why did the colonists refuse the cheaper tea offered by the Tea Act of 1773?

1. Britain was in debt after its war with France and wanted the colonies to help pay; the colonists were angry because they had no representation in Parliament — they were being taxed without a voice ("no taxation without representation").

2. Buying the tea meant paying the hidden tax, and paying the tax meant accepting that London had the right to tax them whenever it liked. The principle mattered more than the price.

Discuss:
  • The colonists rejected cheaper tea to defend a principle. Have you ever refused something that would have benefited you, purely on principle? Was it worth it?

Part 2 — The night of the raid

Through the autumn of 1773, three tea ships — the Dartmouth, the Eleanor and the Beaver — sailed into Boston Harbor and dropped anchor. The colonists demanded that the ships turn around and carry the tea back to Britain. The royal governor, Thomas Hutchinson, refused: under the law, once a ship had entered the port, its cargo had to be landed and the duty paid. A tense standoff followed, with thousands of townspeople crowding into meetings to decide what to do.

On the freezing night of 16 December 1773, the largest of these meetings broke up, and the crowd streamed down to Griffin's Wharf. Perhaps a hundred men — many of them thinly disguised as Mohawk warriors, their faces smeared with soot and coal dust so as to hide their identities — boarded the three ships. Working through the night by lantern light, in an oddly orderly and disciplined way, they hauled 342 chests of tea up from the holds, split the crates open, and emptied the contents into the harbour. The tea foamed on the water in the moonlight.

What is striking is how careful they were. This was not a riot. They broke nothing but the tea chests; one padlock accidentally damaged was quietly replaced the next day. No one was hurt, nothing else was stolen. When it was over, the men swept the decks clean and slipped away into the dark. By dawn, roughly £10,000 worth of tea — a fortune, worth well over a million pounds today — lay ruined in the mud of Boston Harbor.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why couldn't the standoff be resolved by simply sending the ships back to Britain?

2. What did the raiders do to disguise themselves, and how did they behave once aboard the ships?

1. The royal governor, Hutchinson, refused to let the ships leave; by law, once a ship had entered the port, its cargo had to be unloaded and the tax paid.

2. They disguised themselves — many as Mohawk warriors, faces darkened with soot — to hide who they were. Once aboard, they were disciplined and orderly: they destroyed only the tea, hurt no one, and stole nothing.

Discuss:
  • The raiders were careful to destroy only the tea and nothing else. Why might that discipline have mattered to how the event was remembered?

Part 3 — The long echo

News of the destruction crossed the Atlantic and enraged the British government. To punish Boston and to reassert its authority, Parliament passed a series of laws the colonists bitterly named the "Intolerable Acts." The port of Boston was closed until the ruined tea was paid for; the powers of the local assembly were stripped away. London's aim was to isolate Boston and frighten the other colonies into obedience.

It achieved the opposite. Instead of abandoning Boston, the other colonies rallied to its side. In 1774, delegates from across the colonies met in Philadelphia as the First Continental Congress, to coordinate their resistance. What had been thirteen separate quarrels was fusing into one. Within eighteen months, shots were fired at Lexington and Concord, and the dispute over tea had become the American War of Independence.

The event was not even called the "Boston Tea Party" until decades later; at the time, people simply spoke of "the destruction of the tea." But the image endured — ordinary people, in makeshift disguise, defying an empire over a boatload of leaves. To this day it is invoked in American politics as a symbol of resistance to distant, unaccountable power. A protest over a few pennies of tax on tea had, in the end, helped bring a nation into being.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What were the "Intolerable Acts," and what was Britain trying to achieve with them?

2. How did the other colonies respond, and where did the crisis ultimately lead?

1. Harsh punishment laws (closing Boston's port, stripping the local assembly's powers), meant to isolate Boston and frighten the other colonies into obedience.

2. The colonies rallied to Boston rather than abandoning it, met as the First Continental Congress in 1774, and within about eighteen months the dispute had become the American War of Independence.

Discuss:
  • Britain's punishment was meant to isolate Boston but did the reverse — it united the colonies. Why do harsh reprisals so often backfire?

Grammar — expressing purpose

This whole story is about why: why colonists refused the tea, why they wore disguises, why Britain punished Boston. English has a rich toolkit for answering "for what purpose?" — and choosing the right one makes your reasoning sound precise and mature.

The main ways to express purpose:
• to + base verb — the everyday choice: "They boarded the ships to destroy the tea."
• in order to / so as to + base verb — slightly more formal and emphatic; so as to is common before a negative: "They darkened their faces in order to hide their identities." "They worked quietly so as not to be recognised."
• so that + clause (subject + verb) — use when the purpose has its own subject, or involves can / could / would: "Britain kept the tea tax so that it could prove its right to tax the colonies."
• for + noun — purpose expressed as a thing, not an action: "They met in Philadelphia for a coordinated response." (But note: purpose before a verb is to, not for.)
Two traps to avoid:
• Before a verb, use to — never for. Say "He came to help," not "He came for help" (unless "help" is a noun).
• After in order to and so as to comes a base verb; after so that comes a whole clause (usually with can/could/would).

1. Choose the natural connector

Complete each sentence with to, in order to, so that, so as not to, or for. More than one may be possible — pick the most natural.

1. The colonists organised a boycott put pressure on Britain.

2. The raiders wore disguises no one could identify them afterwards.

3. They smeared their faces with soot be recognised.

4. Delegates travelled to Philadelphia a coordinated response.

1. to (purpose before a verb; "in order to" also works)

2. so that (the purpose has its own subject + could: "so that no one could…")

3. so as not to (negative purpose before a verb; "in order not to" also works)

4. for (purpose expressed as a noun: "a coordinated response")

2. Fix the form

Each sentence uses a purpose structure but the form after it is wrong. Write the correction.

1. Britain kept the tea tax in order (proving) its authority.

2. They came to the wharf for (destroy) the tea.

3. He whispered so that the guards (not hear) him.

1. to prove (in order + to + base verb)

2. to destroy (purpose before a verb is "to", not "for"; use "for" only before a noun)

3. would not hear / could not hear (so that + clause, typically with would/could)

3. Give the reason — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with a purpose connector (to, in order to, so that, so as not to, for). Read it aloud once you're done.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The colonists' real objection was to being taxed without a voice, not to the tax itself. Is "no taxation without representation" still a fair principle today — and do all taxpayers really have a voice?
  2. The raiders wore disguises and destroyed private property, yet they are remembered as heroes, not criminals. What decides whether history calls someone a rebel or a vandal?
  3. Britain's attempt to punish Boston united the colonies against it. Can you think of a modern example where a crackdown strengthened the very thing it was meant to crush?
