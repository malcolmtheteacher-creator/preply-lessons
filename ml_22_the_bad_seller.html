The Bad Seller

Ninety pounds, a "barely used" bike, and a rusted chain you only saw properly in daylight. He's standing by his car, keys in hand. There's no refund button out here — just you.

The Scene 🎬

The advert said barely used, hardly been out, immaculate condition. You went round on Tuesday evening, he wheeled it out of a dark hallway, you handed over ninety quid in twenties and rode it home feeling rather pleased with yourself.

Then Saturday came, and daylight with it. The chain is orange with rust — not a spot, the whole thing. The back wheel wobbles. This bike has spent a winter, possibly two, chained to a fence in the rain, and no amount of squinting makes it "barely used". So you messaged him, and to his credit he agreed to meet, and now here you both are: a supermarket car park, the bike between you, his engine still warm.

Here's the thing, though. There's no company behind this. No policy, no returns desk, no fourteen days to change your mind, nobody to escalate it to. There is a man with your ninety pounds in his account who could, if he decided to, simply get in his car and drive off. Whatever happens in the next ten minutes happens because of how you talk to him.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. What's the worst thing you've ever bought second-hand from a stranger? Did you say anything at the time — or did you just quietly put up with it and tell the story later?
  2. When someone has your money and no obligation to give it back, what actually makes them hand it over? Guilt, embarrassment, fear of a bad review — or something else entirely?

Who's who

You
The Buyer 🚲

Not angry, exactly — more embarrassed at having been so easily had. You want the ninety back and the bike gone. What you don't want is a row in a car park, and you're aware that shouting would lose you both the money and the morning.

The other voice
Deniz, the Seller 🔑

Not a villain. He's the sort who says "barely used" without ever quite deciding whether it's true. He'd like this to be small — a tenner off, a shrug, everyone home for lunch — and he can leave whenever he likes.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how the buyer keeps two things going at once that don't normally sit together — completely polite, and completely immovable. Notice how often they hand Deniz an easy way out, and how they never once call him a liar.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Deniz suggests the damage might have been done on the ride home. The buyer doesn't argue about the wheel at all — they point at something else instead. What, and why does it settle the argument on its own?

2. Twice, Deniz tries to make this smaller rather than undo it. What exactly did he offer — and what reason does the buyer give for turning it down?

3. The buyer mentions reviews and daily messages, but Deniz never accuses them of threatening him. How is it put, and what does that wording do for Deniz?

1. The rust on the chain. A pothole can buckle a wheel in an afternoon, but rust takes months of a bike sitting outside in the wet, so it quietly proves the bike was never "barely used" — and the buyer has had it three days. The wheel is arguable; the rust isn't.
2. He offers a tenner off, then twenty. The buyer refuses because they don't want a discount on a bad bike, they want the sale reversed — the ninety back and Deniz taking the bike away with him.
3. They say they'd rather sort it here than start leaving reviews and messaging every day. It's framed as the buyer's own preference rather than a warning, which leaves Deniz free to hand the money over as his own decision — he isn't being backed into a corner he has to fight his way out of.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the moment somebody has your money and no reason to give it back. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Standing your ground sounds nothing like winning an argument. Loud and fast reads as a row, and a row is something he can walk away from with his head held high. Go slower than feels comfortable, keep your voice flat and friendly, and leave the silences alone after you've said what you want — the pause does more work than another sentence would.

🅿️ Opening it, before it turns into a row

Phrase 1

He's parked badly, he's got somewhere to be, and he's braced for a shouting match. Which opener makes him relax enough to stay standing there?

A. "Right. I think you know why we're here."
B. "Thanks for meeting me — I'll not keep you long."
C. "Sorry to drag you out, this is probably nothing, sorry."
B — "Thanks for meeting me — I'll not keep you long."
A is a warning shot, so he'll come out fighting. C apologises so hard that he'll assume you can be talked out of it in a minute. B thanks him and promises this will be short, which costs you nothing and makes staying easy — and notice that promising to be quick is not the same as promising to be easy.
Phrase 2

He says the bike works fine, and he's right, it does. You need to move the argument off the bike and onto the gap between the bike and what he wrote. Which line is hardest for him to argue with?

A. "You lied in that advert."
B. "It's not what was in the advert, though, is it."
C. "I'm a bit disappointed with it, to be honest."
B — "It's not what was in the advert, though, is it."
A puts him on trial, so he defends himself instead of the advert. C is only about your feelings, and he can't be expected to refund a feeling. B compares two things that both exist — the advert and the object — and the tag question "is it" invites him to agree rather than telling him he's wrong. It's said with a falling tone, by the way; it isn't really a question.

🔗 Showing the problem without calling him a liar

Phrase 3

Don't describe the damage — make him look at it, using his own two words back. Put the pieces in order:

the chain.You saidthe state ofbarely used.Have a look at
"You said barely used. Have a look at the state of the chain."
Quoting his own words back, with no comment attached, does the accusing for you. "The state of" something is what we say when a thing is in an obviously bad condition — you'd say it about a kitchen after a party — and it carries a small amount of disbelief without any insult in it. And "have a look" beats "it's rusty" every time, because once he's looking at the chain, he's arguing with the chain and not with you.
Phrase 4

He's blaming the potholes. You need to say the timing is impossible without turning it into a character question. Complete the chunk — the missing word takes "of" after it: "I'm not ___ you of anything, but this didn't happen in three days."

"I'm not accusing you of anything, but this didn't happen in three days."
You accuse someone of something — the preposition is fixed, which is why no other word fits that gap. The first half hands him his innocence and the second half takes the excuse away, so there's nothing left for him to defend. He can't argue about what he meant to do; he'd have to argue about how long rust takes.

💷 Saying the number, and refusing the smaller version

Phrase 5

Time to say the actual thing you want, in one sentence, with no apology on the front of it. Put the pieces in order:

the bike.the ninety back,What I'd like isand you take
"What I'd like is the ninety back, and you take the bike."
"What I'd like is…" is a calm, grown-up way to open, because it's a preference rather than a demand and yet it still ends in a number. Both halves matter: the money coming to you and the bike going back to him. If you only ask for the money, you've asked for a favour; if you offer the bike back too, you've described a swap that undoes the whole thing.
Phrase 6

He's offering twenty quid off to make you go away. Complete the chunk with one word — normally it means "behind" or "later", but here it means "wanting": "I'm not ___ a bit off. I want to undo it."

"I'm not after a bit off. I want to undo it."
To be "after" something, in speech, is to be wanting it — "what are you after?" is what a market trader says to you. "A bit off" is a small discount, and refusing it out loud matters, because the moment you discuss the size of the discount you have agreed to keep the bike. "Undo it" is the whole point: not a better price, but the sale rubbed out and both of you back where you started on Tuesday.

🧍 Holding the line without threatening him

Phrase 7

He knows a bad review would hurt him. You know it too, and neither of you wants to say so. Which line makes him feel it without making him feel cornered?

A. "Give me the ninety or I'll leave you a one-star review tonight."
B. "I'd rather sort it here than start leaving reviews and messaging you every day."
C. "You realise I could really make life difficult for you here."
B — "I'd rather sort it here than start leaving reviews and messaging you every day."
A and C are threats, and a threat gives him something to be angry about, so now he's defending his pride instead of thinking about ninety pounds. B says the same thing as a preference — "I'd rather" — and the alternative sits there quietly, unpromised, doing its own work. He hears it. You never said it. To "sort it" is simply to settle it here and now, and it makes the easy option sound like the pleasant one.
Phrase 8

He says he hasn't got ninety on him — which is half an excuse and half a genuine problem. In your own words, take that excuse away by making paying you back as easy as you possibly can. What would you say?

e.g. "Cash is fine, or a transfer — whatever's easiest for you."
Notice what it does: it offers him two ways and lets him choose, so the last practical reason to say no disappears and he still gets to feel in charge of something. "Whatever's easiest for you" sounds like a kindness, and it is one, but it's a kindness about how, never about whether. That's the trick of the whole conversation — be as flexible as you like on the details, and immovable on the number.

🤝 The last push, and a clean ending

Phrase 9

He asks what happens if he simply says no. Complete the chunk with two words — it's what you'd say about an ache or a stain that won't disappear: "I'm not going to ___ ___ on this, I'm afraid."

"I'm not going to go away on this, I'm afraid."
Things that "go away" fade out on their own if you ignore them, like a cold, and ignoring you is exactly the plan he's currently testing. Saying it plainly tells him the waiting strategy has failed, and "I'm afraid" is the small apologetic touch that keeps it friendly — you're not enjoying this, it's simply how it is. Say it slowly. It's the least aggressive sentence in the lesson and the hardest one to get round.
Phrase 10

The money's landed. He's embarrassed, and an embarrassed man is not someone you want to leave standing there. Complete the chunk with two words — it's the fixed phrase for "I'm not going to hold this against you": "Right, that's it done — no ___ ___, honestly."

"Right, that's it done — no hard feelings, honestly."
"No hard feelings" is what you say when something has gone against someone and you want it clear that you're not carrying a grudge about it. It's generous here, because you won, and you're the one offering it — which lets him keep his dignity on the walk back to the car. "That's it done" closes the thing, so nobody has to work out whether the conversation is over.

The Twist

😬 "Forty now, fifty next week"

One more round, no script. This time he agrees you're right — and then tells you the money's gone. Spent. He can do forty today, right now, and fifty at the end of the month, promise, he's good for it. He's already got his phone out for the forty, and he's holding the bike's handlebars while he says it, waiting for you to let go. Start the timer. So: do you take the forty and hand him the bike on a promise? Do you keep the bike until it's all there — and if so, how do you say that to a man who's just admitted he's skint, without it turning into a row you'd lose? Whatever you choose, you have to say it out loud, to his face, and stay friendly. There's no right answer here. There's only the one you can defend afterwards.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Buying from a shop, you're protected by a system: a receipt, a policy, fourteen days, a manager somewhere who'd rather refund you than argue. Buying from a stranger on an app, you're protected by nothing except the awkwardness of standing in front of them.

And that awkwardness turns out to be worth about ninety pounds, if you can bear it. Most people can't. Most people go home with the rusty bike, tell the story at work on Monday, and let the whole thing go — which is roughly what "barely used" is banking on.

Standing your ground isn't really a matter of courage, in the end. It's a matter of being willing to stay in an uncomfortable conversation slightly longer than the other person expected — politely, and without ever raising your voice.

Two bigger questions

  1. The apps hand us all a star rating now, and a lot of small honesty seems to run on it. Is that a decent replacement for trust between strangers, or just a quieter kind of pressure? Would Deniz have written that advert at all if his name weren't attached to it?
  2. Some people find it almost impossible to ask for their own money back, and others do it without blinking. Where does that difference come from — how you were raised, what it costs you to be disliked, or something about the culture you're standing in? Which are you, and would you like to be the other one?
