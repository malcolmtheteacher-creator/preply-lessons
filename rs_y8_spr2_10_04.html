Buddhist Ahimsa and Sikh Warriors

YEAR 8 · UNIT 10: PEACE & CONFLICT

What You'll Learn

You will explore how Buddhism and Sikhism approach peace and violence in fundamentally different ways. Buddhism teaches ahimsa (non-harming) and metta (loving-kindness) as core to spiritual development, yet Buddhist communities are not immune to violence and nationalism. Sikhism, by contrast, embraces the concept of the sant-sipahi (saint-soldier)—a tradition of righteous warriors who defend the weak. You'll examine how both religions grapple with peace, violence, and protection, and consider whether non-violence and the willingness to fight can both be morally justified.

Question 2: What are the main conditions for Just War? Name at least three.

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Key conditions for Just War include: (1) Right cause—defending against aggression, protecting the innocent, restoring justice; (2) Last resort—all peaceful methods exhausted first; (3) Right intention—not hidden motives like territorial gain; (4) Legitimate authority—declared by proper state leadership; (5) Reasonable chance of success; (6) Proportionality—response matches the threat; (7) Discrimination—civilian lives protected where possible. Different thinkers emphasise different conditions.

Question 3: What does jihad mean, and what are the two main types?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Jihad literally means "struggle" or "striving." The greater jihad (al-jihad al-akbar) is the internal spiritual struggle to overcome one's desires and weaknesses and live righteously. The lesser jihad (al-jihad al-asghar) refers to physical struggle or fighting, permitted only under specific conditions: in self-defence, to protect the community, or against oppression. Most Islamic scholars emphasise the greater jihad as more important spiritually.

Encounter: Buddhism and Sikhism on Peace and Violence

Buddhism: The Way of Ahimsa

The Core Teaching: Ahimsa

Ahimsa (non-harming) is the first and foundational precept in Buddhism. It reflects the Buddha's central ethical teaching: all beings wish to avoid suffering, just as we do. Therefore, we should cultivate compassion for all living things and refrain from causing harm. The Buddha taught in the Dhammapada (1:5): "Hatred does not cease by hatred, but only by love; this is the eternal rule." This is not merely a rule of conduct—it is a spiritual truth. Violence originates from greed, hatred, and delusion; only compassion and understanding can break the cycle of suffering.

The Eightfold Path and Right Action

The Eightfold Path, central to Buddhist practice, includes Right Speech, Right Action, and Right Livelihood—all emphasising ethical conduct free from harm. Right Action specifically prohibits killing, stealing, sexual misconduct, and intoxication. For a Buddhist, deliberately harming another being—human or animal—creates negative karma that binds one to suffering. Non-violence is not simply a rule imposed from outside; it is a path to inner peace and spiritual liberation.

Metta: Loving-Kindness Meditation

Buddhists actively cultivate compassion through metta (loving-kindness) meditation. Practitioners mentally extend goodwill first to themselves, then to loved ones, then to neutral people, then to difficult people, and finally to all beings everywhere. This practice trains the mind to replace hatred, anger, and fear with warmth and acceptance. The goal is not sentimental—it is to fundamentally reshape one's emotional responses so that harming others becomes unthinkable.

Key Teaching: "All beings tremble before violence. All fear death. All love life. See yourself in others. Then whom can you harm?" (Dhammapada 129–130)

Buddhist Peace Activism: From Vietnam to Tibet

Many Buddhist leaders and communities have been at the forefront of peace movements. Thich Nhat Hanh, a Vietnamese Buddhist monk, pioneered "engaged Buddhism"—the application of Buddhist mindfulness and compassion to social and political suffering. During the Vietnam War, when pressed to choose sides between north and south, Thich Nhat Hanh insisted on aiding all suffering people equally, renaming his approach "interbeing." He refused both killing and silence; instead, he pursued peace through moral witness and non-violent resistance. The Dalai Lama, leader of Tibetan Buddhism, has been a global advocate for non-violence and religious freedom, consistently refusing to endorse violent resistance to Chinese rule of Tibet, despite enormous personal and political pressure. His commitment to ahimsa, even in the face of injustice, exemplifies Buddhism's principled stance on peace.

But Buddhism and Violence: The Complexity

It would be misleading to suggest that Buddhism is exempt from violence. In Myanmar, Buddhist nationalism has fuelled persecution of the Rohingya Muslim minority. Some Buddhist-majority societies have engaged in war. In Sri Lanka, Buddhist identity became entangled with Sinhalese nationalism, resulting in violence against Tamil and Muslim minorities. These contradictions reveal a crucial truth: Buddhism as a religion teaches ahimsa, but Buddhists as people, communities, and nations are subject to the same temptations toward hatred and violence as any group. When Buddhist identity becomes fused with national or ethnic identity, the religious commitment to non-violence can be overridden by political and communal allegiances. This does not invalidate Buddhism's core teaching, but it shows that religious ideals do not automatically shape behavior when other forces (nationalism, fear, economic competition) are at play.

Sikhism: The Sant-Sipahi (Saint-Soldier) Tradition

Guru Nanak: Peace and Justice

Sikhism began with Guru Nanak (1469–1539), who preached equality, justice, and peace. Like Islam and other traditions, Sikhism condemns unprovoked violence and teaches compassion. Sikhism rejects caste hierarchy, ritual superficiality, and oppression. Guru Nanak's message was one of spiritual equality before God. However, Sikhism departed radically from pure pacifism because of historical circumstances. When the Sikh community faced persecution from hostile rulers, Guru Gobind Singh (1666–1708), the tenth Guru, transformed Sikhism by introducing the Khalsa—a community of militant disciples willing to fight oppression.

The Khalsa and the Warrior Tradition

In 1699, Guru Gobind Singh instituted the Khalsa Panth (the Way of the Khalsa) to combat religious persecution. Khalsa members, both men and women, are initiated through a sacred ceremony (amrit) and adopt strict codes of conduct. They wear the Five Ks as visible signs of their commitment: kesh (uncut hair, often worn in a turban), kara (steel bracelet symbolising restraint and strength), kachera (shorts representing modesty), kanga (comb for discipline), and kirpan (ceremonial sword). The kirpan is not merely ceremonial; it represents the Sikh commitment to defend the vulnerable and fight oppression when necessary.

The Philosophy: The sant-sipahi is a "saint-soldier"—someone who combines spiritual devotion with martial courage. To stand by passively while innocents suffer is seen as a moral failure, not virtue. This sets Sikhism apart from strict pacifism.

Dharam Yudh: The Concept of Righteous War

Sikhism teaches the concept of dharam yudh (righteous or just war). However, Sikhism sets strict conditions for fighting, similar to Just War theory: (1) Fighting is a last resort—all peaceful methods must be exhausted first. (2) The cause must be defending the weak from oppression, not conquest, revenge, or territorial gain. (3) Force must be proportional and restrained—the minimum necessary to protect the innocent. (4) The war must be led by a Sikh authority (historically the Guru or Khalsa council) with legitimate spiritual standing, not by individual revenge seekers. (5) The fighter must maintain moral discipline—hatred and bloodlust are forbidden; fighting is done as a duty, not from anger. The Sikh view is that sometimes, protection of the innocent requires force. Standing silent while others are harmed violates dharam (righteousness).

This philosophy led to centuries of Sikh military resistance against oppression. Sikh warriors defended their communities against rulers who sought to suppress their faith, and they later played significant roles in defending India during colonial times and independence struggles.

The Kirpan: Symbolism and Reality

The kirpan, worn by Khalsa Sikhs, is both a symbol and a weapon. Symbolically, it represents the commitment to defend righteousness and protect the vulnerable. Practically, it indicates readiness to fight injustice. Sikhs do not glorify violence, but they refuse to accept oppression passively. This has led to historical tensions: Sikhs have been persecuted by Islamic rulers, Hindu rulers, and later by Indian governments. Yet Sikhs maintain their ethical commitment to fighting only when necessary and with moral restraint. The kirpan reminds Sikhs and others that some issues—religious freedom, protection of minorities, justice—are worth fighting for.

Sikh History: Defence Against Persecution

Throughout history, Sikhs have needed to live by their warrior code. Under Mughal rule, Gurus were executed for refusing to convert others to Islam. Sikh communities were massacred. Rather than abandon their faith, Sikhs fought back, developing into formidable warriors. Later, Sikhs served prominently in the Indian Army and continue to do so. For Sikhs, martial skill and spiritual devotion are not contradictory—they are complementary. A Sikh temple (Gurdwara) may house a langar (free kitchen) serving all people equally, alongside a history of military courage. This dual commitment—to service and to defence, to charity and to justice—defines Sikhism's distinctive approach to peace.

Summary: Two Approaches to Peace

Buddhism: Emphasises ahimsa, compassion, and the belief that violence perpetuates suffering. The ideal Buddhist is one who overcomes hatred through understanding and metta. Violence is spiritually counter-productive. Peace comes through inner transformation. Yet Buddhists have not always lived by this ideal, and Buddhist nationalism has sometimes fuelled conflict.

Sikhism: Teaches compassion and justice, but recognises that sometimes protection of the vulnerable requires force. The sant-sipahi stands ready to defend the defenceless. Peace is not achieved by tolerating oppression; it requires righteous action. The willingness to fight, when conditions are met, is a spiritual virtue.

The tension between these approaches reflects a deep question: Is non-violence always the right path, or are there circumstances where defending the innocent justifies force?

Comparison: Buddhist and Sikh Perspectives

How do these two traditions compare when facing similar issues?

When Facing Persecution

Buddhism: Seeks to respond with compassion even toward persecutors. Thich Nhat Hanh aided all suffering people regardless of which side they were on in war. The Dalai Lama refuses violence despite Chinese oppression.

Sikhism: Gurus and Khalsa fought back against persecution. Guru Arjun was executed; Sikhs responded with military resistance. Fighting oppressors is seen as defending dharma (righteousness).

View of Violence

Buddhism: Violence creates negative karma and perpetuates the cycle of suffering. Overcoming violence requires transforming the mind through meditation and compassion.

Sikhism: Unprovoked violence is wrong, but using force to protect the innocent is a moral duty. Violence becomes righteous when conditions are met: last resort, defence of the weak, proper authority.

On Standing Silent During Injustice

Buddhism: Engagement with social injustice is important (engaged Buddhism), but through non-violent means: witness, dialogue, moral persuasion, refusal to participate.

Sikhism: Standing silent while others suffer is a moral failure. Action is required. If oppressors will not listen to reason, force becomes justified as a last resort.

Spiritual Development

Buddhism: Spiritual development comes through inner transformation: discipline, meditation, compassion, wisdom. The goal is liberation from suffering.

Sikhism: Spiritual development includes both devotion and courage. A Sikh must be prepared to stand up for justice. Inner faith and outer action are inseparable.

Evaluate: When Is It Right to Use Violence?

The Question

Both Buddhism and Sikhism claim to promote peace and justice. Yet they reach opposite conclusions about violence. Is the difference in their theology (what they believe about karma, divine will, etc.) or in their history (the circumstances they faced)? And more importantly: is there ever a situation where using force to protect others is morally right?

Buddhist Perspective: Non-Violence as Spiritual Truth

The Buddhist Case Against Violence

From a Buddhist viewpoint, violence is fundamentally counterproductive. Violence arises from greed, hatred, and delusion—the three poisons that bind beings to suffering. When we harm others, we reinforce our own attachment to these destructive emotions. Violence begets violence in an endless cycle: the persecutor harms the oppressed; the oppressed (if they fight back) become like their oppressors, perpetuating hatred. The only way to truly end violence is to break the cycle through compassion and wisdom. The Dalai Lama's refusal to endorse violence against China is not weakness—it is a commitment to spiritual truth. If Tibetans responded with violence, they would lose their spiritual foundation and become oppressors themselves. Non-violence protects both the oppressed (spiritually) and potentially the oppressors (by refusing to escalate hatred).

Sikh Perspective: Justice Requires Protection

The Sikh Case for Righteous Defence

From a Sikh viewpoint, the Buddhist approach, while spiritually beautiful, can enable injustice. If a powerful oppressor knows that their victims will never fight back, they have no incentive to stop oppressing them. Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa not to glorify violence but to make clear that Sikhs would not tolerate tyranny. The sant-sipahi is someone who has tried all peaceful methods and then stands ready to defend the defenceless. This is not hatred-driven violence; it is love-driven action. A parent who forcibly stops an attacker from harming their child is not "perpetuating violence"—they are protecting the vulnerable. Some situations (genocide, slavery, invasion) cannot be resolved through dialogue. In such cases, refusing to fight is not virtue; it is complicity. The Sikhs who fought against persecution were not abandoning their faith; they were living it—protecting their community, practising justice, and refusing tyranny.

Key Terms Summary

Ahimsa
Non-harming; the first precept in Buddhism. Reflects the belief that violence arises from delusion and perpetuates suffering. All living things should be treated with compassion.
Metta
Loving-kindness; a Buddhist meditation practice in which one mentally extends goodwill to oneself, loved ones, neutral people, difficult people, and all beings. Transforms emotional responses toward compassion.
Dhammapada
A collection of Buddhist teachings in verse form, attributed to the Buddha. Contains fundamental Buddhist ethical and spiritual wisdom, including the teaching "Hatred ceases only by love."
Eightfold Path
The Buddha's path to the cessation of suffering: Right View, Right Intention, Right Speech, Right Action, Right Livelihood, Right Effort, Right Mindfulness, Right Concentration. Right Action forbids harm to living beings.
Khalsa
A community of initiated Sikhs established by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699, committed to defending righteousness and the vulnerable. Members follow strict codes and wear the Five Ks.
Kirpan
A ceremonial sword worn by Khalsa Sikhs as one of the Five Ks. Symbolises both the commitment to defend the weak and readiness to fight injustice. A practical sword and a spiritual symbol.
Dharam Yudh
Righteous or just war in Sikhism. Permitted only as a last resort, to defend the oppressed from tyranny, with proper authority, minimum force, and moral discipline. Not driven by hatred or revenge.
Sant-Sipahi
Saint-soldier; a Sikh ideal combining spiritual devotion with martial courage. Someone who pursues justice and defends the vulnerable, combining inner faith with outer action.
Five Ks
Five articles worn by Khalsa Sikhs: kesh (uncut hair), kara (steel bracelet), kachera (shorts), kanga (comb), and kirpan (sword). Each symbolises commitment to discipline, strength, modesty, and defence.
Guru Nanak
Founder of Sikhism (1469–1539). Taught equality, justice, peace, and rejection of caste hierarchy. Emphasised that all humans are equal before God regardless of birth, gender, or wealth.
Guru Gobind Singh
The tenth and last living Guru of Sikhism (1666–1708). Established the Khalsa Panth to combat religious persecution and empower Sikhs to defend righteousness through martial discipline and spiritual devotion.
Engaged Buddhism
A modern Buddhist approach that applies Buddhist mindfulness and compassion to social and political issues. Addresses suffering in the world through non-violent action, exemplified by Thich Nhat Hanh during Vietnam War.
Dalai Lama
The spiritual leader of Tibetan Buddhism. Consistently advocates for non-violence and religious freedom, refusing to endorse violence even against Chinese oppression of Tibet, exemplifying Buddhist commitment to ahimsa in politics.
Last Resort
A key condition in Just War theory and Sikh dharam yudh: fighting is permitted only after all peaceful methods have been exhausted. Violence is the final option, not the first.
Right Action
The fourth component of Buddhism's Eightfold Path. Involves ethical conduct free from harm: not killing, stealing, or engaging in sexual misconduct. Forms the foundation of Buddhist moral life.
Central Insight from This Lesson: Buddhism and Sikhism offer two profound but contrasting approaches to peace and justice. Buddhism teaches that lasting peace comes only through transforming the mind: overcoming hatred through compassion and rejecting violence as spiritually counterproductive. Sikhism teaches that peace requires justice, and sometimes justice demands defending the vulnerable through force—but only under strict conditions and with moral discipline. Neither tradition glorifies violence. Yet they differ fundamentally on whether standing silent during injustice is wisdom or complicity. This tension reflects a central moral question humans continue to face: Can violence ever be truly justified in defence of others? Different ethical traditions answer differently, and grappling with their different answers deepens our understanding of both peace and justice.
