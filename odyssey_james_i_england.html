The words for today's story

1. He united the crowns of England and Scotland.

2. Barrels of gunpowder were hidden.

4. The conspirators were Catholics.

5. Guy Fawkes was discovered at midnight.

6. Fawkes was tortured.

7. The King James Bible is a famous translation.
a translation (n) — a version in another language.

divine right (n) — the idea that kings get power from God.
9. People still light bonfires on 5 November.
a bonfire (n) — a large outdoor fire.
10. He was a scholar king who wrote books.
a scholar (n) — a learned person.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Two crowns

James VI of Scotland, son of Mary, Queen of Scots, became James I of England in 1603. He called himself "King of Great Britain", though the countries stayed legally separate until 1707.

He believed in the divine right of kings and wrote books on politics, witchcraft and tobacco — he disliked smoking.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What two titles did James hold?

2. What did he believe about kings?

1. James VI of Scotland and James I of England.

2. That they ruled by divine right.

Discuss:
  • What problems might a king of two countries face?

Part 2 — The Gunpowder Plot

A group of Catholic conspirators, including Robert Catesby and Guy Fawkes, planned to blow up Parliament on 5 November 1605 while the king was there. An anonymous letter warned a Catholic lord. Fawkes was discovered beneath the House of Lords with 36 barrels of gunpowder.

The plotters were executed. People celebrated the king's survival with bonfires, a tradition that continues.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the plotters want to do?

2. How was the plot discovered?

1. Blow up Parliament with the king inside.

2. An anonymous letter warned a lord, and Fawkes was found with the gunpowder.

Discuss:
  • Why did the plotters feel so desperate?

Part 3 — The Bible

In 1604 James approved a new English translation of the Bible. Around fifty scholars worked on it, and the King James Version was published in 1611. Its phrases — "the salt of the earth", "a law unto themselves" — entered everyday English.

James died in 1625.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. When was the King James Bible published?

2. Name a phrase from it.

1. 1611.

2. "The salt of the earth" or "a law unto themselves".

Discuss:
  • How can a translation shape a language?

Grammar — The Passive Voice

Historians often care more about what was done than who did it — or they simply do not know who did it. That is the job of the passive.

How it works:
• Form: be + past participle: "The king was crowned at Westminster."
• Add by only when the doer matters: "The castle was built by Norman masons."
• Reporting passives hedge a legend: "He is said to have…", "It was believed that…"

1. Make it passive

Complete each sentence about James I (and VI). Use the word in brackets.

1. Fawkes (discover) under the Lords.

2. The letter (send) anonymously.

3. The plotters (execute).

1. was discovered

2. was sent

3. were executed

2. Choose the right passive form

1. The Bible (publish) in 1611.

2. It (translate) by about fifty scholars.

3. Bonfires (still / light) every 5 November.

Going deeper

James's reign brought a union of crowns, a famous Bible, and the start of English colonies like Jamestown in Virginia (1607). His belief in divine right led to conflict with Parliament under his son.

Worth noticing: the rhyme "Remember, remember the fifth of November" keeps the story alive.
Learning from the past — for today: Collaboration creates lasting work. Fifty scholars produced a translation that has shaped English for 400 years. Big projects need teams.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Should we still celebrate 5 November?
  • Why does the King James Bible matter?
  • What team project are you proud of?
