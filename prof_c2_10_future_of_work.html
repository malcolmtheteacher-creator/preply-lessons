Setting the Scene

You are the Chief Human Resources Officer. Next week, you're presenting a 5-year workforce strategy to the board. The board is split. One faction—the aggressive tech investors—wants to maximize automation and reduce headcount by 35% over five years. Another faction—the old guard—worries that cutting people that aggressively will destroy culture and drive top talent out.

You're in the middle. You need to convince them that there's a third option: strategic automation, serious reskilling, and the maintenance of a culture that people actually want to be part of. But you also need to be honest about the cost.

Why this matters at C-suite level: The future of work is not a tactical issue. It's a strategic one. The organization that can automate efficiently while maintaining culture and developing people will outcompete those that don't. But getting there requires you to name the trade-offs clearly. Automation has winners and losers. The question is not "automation or not." It's "automation with what values, and for whose benefit?"

Speaking Prompt 2 Now you're the CHRO. You need a strategy that balances growth through automation with maintaining a workplace where people choose to stay and do their best work. What does that balance look like? What are you willing to compromise on? Think aloud for 2 minutes.

At C-suite level, workforce strategy is not about being nice. It's about competitive advantage. The companies winning are the ones that can invest in people while also deploying automation. That's expensive and it's complicated. But it's also the only way to build organizations that last beyond the next quarterly earnings call.

Language Moves for Future of Work Leadership

These moves allow you to talk honestly about automation and change while also talking about human value.

1 Automation Framing (Honest) +

The move is to be direct about what automation does: it eliminates jobs. You don't say "we're optimizing workflows." You say "machines will do work humans did, and some humans will need to do different work."

What sounds evasive

"We're investing in automation to enhance productivity. This will free people to do higher-value work."

Strong

"Automation will eliminate 40% of current roles over five years. We're not pretending those jobs will be "upgraded." We're committing to reskill people for new roles, and to do it in a way that's fair and dignified. Some people will choose to leave. Some will grow into different work. We're betting that if we invest seriously in reskilling, most will stay."

2 The Psychological Contract +

The move is to name the unspoken deal between employer and employee. In the past, it was "loyalty to the company means job security." In the future, it's "we invest in your growth, and you stay engaged." You need to be clear about what's changing.

What breaks trust

"Your job is secure." (It's not. You know it, they know it.)

Strong

"Job security is over. That's a hard truth. What we're offering instead: serious investment in your skills, transparent communication about where roles are going, and a guarantee that if your current role is automated, we'll help you transition into new work—either here or elsewhere. We're being honest about the change. We're asking you to be flexible. We're investing to make that flexibility sustainable."

3 Workforce Strategy Language +

The move is to frame strategy around two things: where the business is going and how people will get there. You're not just talking about technology or headcount. You're talking about careers and skills.

What's missing

"We're automating customer service. We're reducing headcount by 20%. We're investing in AI."

Strong

"We're automating routine customer service. That means 200 roles are changing. We're retraining 150 people into specialized support roles that AI can't handle—complex problem-solving, relationship management. We're being honest that some people will choose to leave. We're also being specific about what we're investing and how we're measuring success: not just how many we keep, but how many grow their skills and stay engaged."

4 Reskilling and Upskilling Positioning +

The move is to make reskilling real, not just rhetorical. You're not "offering courses." You're saying "you'll spend 10 hours a week in structured learning, you'll have a mentor, you'll have a clear pathway to a new role, and we'll measure whether it's working."

What sounds like platitude

"We're committed to reskilling our workforce."

Strong

"Reskilling is not optional. You'll spend 10 hours weekly in structured learning. You'll have a assigned mentor. You'll have a clear role-transition plan. We measure success by whether you move into a new role within 24 months. If you don't, that's a signal that our program isn't working, and we'll adjust. Here's the investment we're making: $5m over three years. Here's what we're measuring."

5 Hybrid Work at Scale Language +

The move is to have a clear policy that acknowledges that different roles need different approaches, and that the days of "everyone comes in on Tuesday" are over.

What creates chaos

"We're embracing hybrid flexibility." (Everyone interprets this differently.)

Strong

"Some roles are remote-first. Some require in-person collaboration. Some are flexible. We're defining this by role, not by individual preference. If your role is remote-first, you can be anywhere. If it's collaborative, we need you in-office three days a week. We're being clear, and we're hiring for the role as we've defined it."

6 The Board Division Navigation +

The move is to acknowledge both camps' concerns (automation AND culture matter) and then chart a third course that honors both—not as a compromise, but as a strategic insight.

Strong

"The tech faction is right: we need to automate or we'll be uncompetitive. The culture faction is right: if we cut aggressively, we'll lose the people who know how to work here. The answer isn't compromise. It's strategy. We automate the work. We reskill the people. We invest in the psychological contract that says: if you grow with us, we grow with you. That costs more than just cutting. It's also why we'll win."

Live Scenario: Board Presentation on Workforce Strategy

The Situation: You're the CHRO. You're presenting a 5-year workforce strategy to a divided board. One side wants aggressive automation. One side is worried about culture and talent. You have 7 minutes to present a strategy that acknowledges both concerns and charts a clear path.

Your position: You believe in automation. You also believe in people. Your strategy invests in both. It costs more than pure automation, but it positions you to compete on culture AND technology.

PART A: The Board Presentation (7 minutes)

You're standing in front of the board. You have 7 minutes to present your strategy. You need to: (1) acknowledge the tension, (2) present the strategy, (3) explain the financial case, (4) explain the competitive advantage.

Speak & Record Present your 5-year workforce strategy. Be honest about automation. Be clear about reskilling. Make the case that investing in people is not soft—it's strategic. Speak for 7 minutes.
7:00

Framing scaffold

TENSION "We need automation. We also need culture. Both are true."
STRATEGY Automate low-value work. Reskill people. Invest in new roles.
NUMBERS Cost of reskilling. ROI on retention. Competitive advantage.
SUCCESS METRICS How you'll measure whether this is working.

PART B: Handle Board Objections (5 minutes)

After your presentation, a tech-focused board member challenges you: "Your reskilling plan costs $5m. We could automate aggressively for $3m and reduce headcount by 35%. Why should we pay more to keep people?"

Speak & Record This is the crux. You need to answer this directly. Why is your approach better? What does the $2m buy you? Speak for 5 minutes.
5:00

Framing scaffold

ACKNOWLEDGE It's cheaper to automate and cut. That's true.
BUT Here's what you lose: institutional knowledge, culture, brand reputation.
AND Here's what you gain: a workforce that's invested, that grows with you.
OUTCOME "We can be the most efficient or the most desirable to work for. Or we can be both."

Synthesis & Reflection

Discussion 1: Progress and Displacement

Automation creates net positive value for society. But it creates displacement for individuals. How do you advocate for progress while also acknowledging and managing the human cost?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) Have you benefited from automation? Have you also been displaced by it? How do you hold both of those truths?
2:00

Discussion 2: The Psychological Contract

For decades, the deal was "loyalty = job security." That's over. What's the new deal? And is it strong enough to keep people engaged?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) What would make you stay in a job where job security is gone? Is it just money? Is it learning? Is it community? What does the new psychological contract look like?
2:00

Discussion 3: The Generational Divide

Older workers want security and stability. Younger workers want growth and optionality. How do you build a culture that works for both?

Reflect Aloud (2 minutes) Think about the generational differences in what you want from work. Are those differences bridgeable? Or are we moving toward a fundamentally different deal?
