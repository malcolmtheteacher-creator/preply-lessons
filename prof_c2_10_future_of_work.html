Future of Work Vocabulary

Automation The use of technology—robotics, software, AI—to perform tasks previously done by humans, increasing efficiency and reducing labour costs.
AI Displacement The loss of jobs due to artificial intelligence and machine learning, particularly in knowledge work previously thought secure from automation.
Gig Economy A labour market characterised by short-term contracts and freelance work rather than permanent employment; workers are project-based or on-demand.
Universal Basic Income A policy where all citizens receive unconditional regular payments from government, designed to provide economic security and counter job displacement.
Reskilling Training programmes designed to equip displaced workers with new skills for jobs in emerging sectors or roles.
Hybrid Work A work arrangement where employees split time between office and remote locations, offering flexibility and reducing office real estate needs.
Four-Day Week A work schedule where full-time employees work four days per week at full salary, aiming to improve well-being and reduce burnout.
Digital Nomad A remote worker who travels while working, typically in digital or knowledge roles, untethered to a physical office location.
Talent Marketplace A digital platform where organisations source skills on a project basis from a distributed pool of independent professionals.
Human Capital The skills, knowledge, and capabilities of people; increasingly recognised as the most valuable asset in a knowledge-based economy.

Four Forces Reshaping Work

The future of work is being driven by interconnected forces. Understanding their interactions is essential for strategic thinking.

1. Automation and AI

Automation is advancing rapidly. Early waves eliminated routine manual and clerical work. Current waves target professional services—legal research, financial analysis, coding, design. The question is not whether automation will displace workers but how societies will manage the transition.

Key consideration: Unlike previous technological shifts, AI may displace high-skill, high-wage workers faster than retraining can be deployed. This could exacerbate inequality unless proactive policies intervene.

2. Demographic Shift

In developed economies, populations are aging and birth rates are declining. This creates dual pressures: labour shortages in some sectors (requiring automation and immigration) and pension system strain. In developing economies, youth populations create both opportunity and unemployment risk.

Key consideration: Automation may reduce labour demand at the same time demographic change increases pension obligations. Countries must balance these forces strategically.

3. Climate and Sustainability Imperatives

The transition to net-zero will eliminate jobs in fossil fuel industries while creating jobs in renewables, retrofitting, and sustainable sectors. This requires significant reskilling and geographic reallocation of labour.

Key consideration: Climate transition could displace millions of workers in energy, transport, and heavy industry. Without managed reskilling, political backlash will be severe.

4. Globalisation Backlash and Reshoring

After decades of offshoring, some manufacturing and services are moving back to developed economies due to supply chain risk, rising wages in emerging markets, and political demand for local jobs. Automation enables this—factories near markets with fewer workers.

Key consideration: Reshoring may create jobs, but they will be lower-wage factory work or highly-skilled technical roles. Mid-skill jobs remain scarce.

Synthesis: These four forces interact. Automation reduces labour demand. Demographic change increases dependency ratios. Climate transition requires massive job shifts. Reshoring might offset some job losses but won't replace the scale of displacement. Policy responses—reskilling, social safety nets, UBI—will be defining political questions of the next decade.

Speaking About Systemic Change with Nuance and Hedging

At C2 level, discussing the future of work demands intellectual humility. Predictions are uncertain; unintended consequences are common. Your language must reflect this while still making clear arguments.

The Language of Uncertainty: Strategic Hedging

Weak (overconfident):

"Automation will eliminate 40% of jobs by 2040."

Strong (appropriately uncertain):

"Automation may eliminate 40% of current job categories by 2040, though new roles may emerge that we can't yet foresee. The key question isn't whether job losses will occur but whether policy and reskilling can manage the transition."

Acknowledging Trade-offs

On the four-day week:

"A four-day week could improve well-being and reduce burnout. However, it may reduce output, increase costs, or create scheduling challenges in service sectors. The trade-off depends on the business model and sector. What works in tech consulting may not work in healthcare or retail."

Discussing Universal Basic Income with Nuance

Balanced perspective:

"UBI offers a safety net if automation displaces millions. It could reduce poverty and eliminate stigma of means-tested welfare. However, funding mechanisms are complex, inflation risks are real, and it might reduce labour force participation. Evidence from pilot programmes is mixed and small-scale. Before large-scale implementation, we need clearer data on impact."

The Language of Hope and Realism

On reskilling and worker transition:

"History shows that workers displaced by technology can be reskilled for new roles. However, this requires investment, time, and willingness on the part of workers to move geographically or change careers. It's not automatic. Success depends on policy support, corporate investment in reskilling, and frankly, worker resilience."

Speaking About Business Responsibility

On what companies should do:

"Companies have an ethical obligation to manage the human impact of automation. This doesn't mean avoiding automation—it means investing in reskilling, supporting affected workers through transition, and engaging with communities and policymakers on how to manage broader impacts."

Key principle: When discussing systemic change, confidence in the direction of change (e.g., "Automation will advance") can be high. Confidence in specific predictions or outcomes should be lower. Distinguish between what's likely to happen and what you're uncertain about.

Debate: "The Four-Day Working Week Should Become the Global Standard"

At C2 level, rigorous debate is a tool for thinking. Your task is to develop both sides of this contentious proposition with evidence and nuance.

The Proposition

"The four-day working week, with full salary and benefits maintained, should become the global standard within ten years."

Consider: Well-being benefits, productivity studies, carbon reduction from reduced commuting, mental health impact, impact on service sectors and developing economies, cost to business, whether it's feasible universally.

Your Task: Rigorous Debate (10 minutes)

Build both sides with evidence, not just arguments. You are developing your strategic thinking, not advocating for your true position.

SIDE A: FOR the four-day week as global standard

Develop arguments including: Well-being and mental health benefits (cite evidence from trials in Iceland, UK), productivity gains (some studies show output maintained or increased), carbon reduction from reduced commuting, attractiveness for talent recruitment, alignment with changing work patterns (automation, remote work), and precedent from shorter working weeks historically.

SIDE B: AGAINST mandating the four-day week globally

Develop arguments including: Impact on service sectors (healthcare, retail, hospitality can't reduce hours), developing economy costs (lower-wage workers can't afford reduced income), competitive disadvantage if not universal (firms in countries without it would undercut), feasibility of maintaining output with 20% less time, impact on customer service and 24/7 operations, and whether it's appropriate to mandate globally given different economic contexts.

Synthesis question: After developing both sides, which constraints matter most? Is the proposition feasible universally, or should it be sector-specific or economy-specific? What conditions would need to be in place for this to work globally without harming developing economies or service sectors?
