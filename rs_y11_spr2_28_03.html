Religious Duty to Help Refugees and War Victims

Christian: The Good Samaritan

Luke 10:25–37 A lawyer asks Jesus: "Who is my neighbour?" Jesus responds with a parable: - A Jewish man is robbed and left dying on the road - A Jewish priest passes by (does nothing) - A Levite (temple assistant) passes by (does nothing) - A Samaritan (member of a group despised by Jews) stops, tends his wounds, takes him to an inn Lesson: Neighbourly love transcends ethnicity, religion, and cultural boundaries. Compassion for strangers in need is the core Christian virtue. In wartime, this means caring for refugees regardless of their background. Modern application: Churches run refugee support programmes, food banks, and trauma counselling for war victims. Christian Aid works globally.

Islamic: Surah 9:6 (Protection of Refugees)

"If any one of the polytheists asks you for protection, grant it to him so that he may hear the word of Allah." The Qur'an teaches: - Asylum and refuge are sacred duties - Hospitality to strangers is a core Islamic value - Protection of vulnerable people is mandatory Historical example: The Prophet Muhammad and early Muslims fled Mecca (hijra) to Medina in 622 CE. They were refugees themselves, aided by the people of Medina. Islamic law (Shari'ah) includes detailed protections for refugees and asylum seekers. Modern application: Islamic Relief operates in 20+ countries. Muslim communities provide shelter, food, and legal support to Syrian refugees, Afghan families, and Rohingya fleeing Myanmar.

International Peace Organisations and Religious Involvement

The United Nations (UN)

Founded: 1945 (post-WWII)

Mission: Maintain international peace and security, resolve conflicts peacefully.

Religious involvement: UN has observer status for religious NGOs. UN Interagency Task Force on Religion and Development promotes interfaith dialogue for peace.

Red Cross (Christian) and Red Crescent (Muslim)

Red Cross: Founded by Henry Dunant (Christian) in 1863 after witnessing carnage at the Battle of Solferino. Neutral humanitarian aid during conflicts.

Red Crescent: Islamic equivalent, founded in Ottoman Empire. Both operate under Geneva Conventions protecting war victims.

Principles: Neutrality, impartiality, independence—aid based on need, not religion or politics.

Christian Aid

- UK-based charity founded by Christian churches - Works in 40+ countries - Focuses on poverty, conflict, and disaster relief - Secular in operation (helps all people) - But motivated by Christian theology: "Love your neighbour" - Major campaigns: Syrian refugees, Yemen hunger crisis, humanitarian corridors

Islamic Relief Worldwide

- Founded 1984 by British Muslims - Works in 30+ countries - Same focus: poverty, conflict, disaster - Operates on Islamic principle of Zakat (mandatory charity) - Provides emergency aid, rebuilding, education - Active in Syria, Afghanistan, Yemen, Pakistan

Religious Leaders as Peace Brokers

Pope John Paul II (1978–2005): Peacemaker

As head of the Catholic Church, John Paul II: - Visited war zones (Croatia, Kosovo, Holy Land) - Met with world leaders to oppose wars - Supported the anti-apartheid movement in South Africa - Advocated nuclear disarmament - His moral authority derived from religious office; world leaders listened

Quote: "If you want peace, work for justice."

Rabbi David Kimche and Sheikh Mohammed Al-Asi: Israeli-Palestinian Dialogue

Despite political conflict, Jewish and Muslim religious leaders meet for dialogue: - Acknowledge mutual pain - Separate religion from politics - Model coexistence through interfaith meetings - Show populations that peace is possible

Key insight: Religious identity doesn't require hostility. Leaders can use their spiritual authority to bridge divides.

Summary: Victims, Aid, and Peacemaking

Religious duty to help: Both Christianity and Islam mandate care for refugees and war victims. This transcends sectarian boundaries—we help people in need regardless of their religion.

Organisations: Red Cross/Crescent, UN, Christian Aid, Islamic Relief provide neutral humanitarian aid during conflicts.

Religious leaders: Popes, bishops, imams, rabbis can use their moral authority to advocate for peace, broker negotiations, and model interfaith coexistence.

Long-term peace: Requires both immediate humanitarian aid AND reconciliation work (forgiveness, truth-telling, justice). Religious communities are uniquely positioned to offer both.
