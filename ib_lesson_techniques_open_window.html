Techniques in Action: The Open Window

Saki (H. H. Munro), 1914 — and what every choice does to you, the reader

A story that fools everyone — including you

In 1914, Saki (H. H. Munro) wrote a story barely 1,500 words long. By the final sentence, a grown man has fled across the countryside in terror — and the reader has been quietly tricked too. Nothing supernatural happens. The whole effect is built from technique.

Today you'll do two things: spot the techniques as they work, and then measure what each one does to you, the reader. Naming a technique is easy. The real skill is explaining its effect.

A few words to clear first

self-possessed

Meaning
Calm, confident, fully in control of oneself. Notice Saki gives this quality to a girl of fifteen — and to no one else.

infirmities / ailments

Meaning
Illnesses and physical weaknesses. Framton can talk about little else.

a treacherous piece of bog

Meaning
Soft, wet ground that looks solid but can swallow a person. "Treacherous" = dangerous in a way that hides itself — a clue to the whole story's method.

Romance (Saki's sense)

Meaning
Not love — but invention, a thrilling made-up tale. Hold on to this; it explains the last line.

The Caller: Framton Nuttel

Framton has come to the countryside for a "nerve cure." He knows almost no one, so his sister has given him letters of introduction to call on strangers. He is shown in to wait — and meets the niece.

"My aunt will be down presently, Mr. Nuttel," said a very self-possessed young lady of fifteen; "in the meantime you must try and put up with me."

Framton Nuttel endeavoured to say the correct something which should duly flatter the niece of the moment without unduly discounting the aunt that was to come. Privately he doubted more than ever whether these formal visits on a succession of total strangers would do much towards helping the nerve cure which he was supposed to be undergoing.

"I know how it will be," his sister had said when he was preparing to migrate to this rural retreat; "you will bury yourself down there and not speak to a living soul, and your nerves will be worse than ever from moping."

How does Saki present Framton in these opening lines?

Show Answer
Characterisation Anxious, over-formal and socially helpless. He calculates how to "flatter the niece... without unduly discounting the aunt" — he is so worried about doing the correct thing that he notices nothing real about the person in front of him. Effect The reader sees at once that Framton is the perfect victim: too self-absorbed to question what he's told.

Vera is introduced as "a very self-possessed young lady of fifteen." Why does that detail matter?

Show Answer
Characterisation Her composure makes us trust her — calm people seem truthful. Effect Saki lulls the reader: we, like Framton, take her at her word. The trap is set in seven words.

Talk it through

  1. What impression do you form of Framton's "nerve cure"? Does Saki want us to take his illness seriously?
  2. Whose voice do we trust more so far — Framton's or Vera's? What has Saki done to arrange that?

The Story Within the Story

Left alone with Framton, Vera asks whether he knows anything about her aunt. He doesn't. So she begins to explain why the tall French window is standing wide open on an October afternoon.

"Her great tragedy happened just three years ago," said the child; "that would be since your sister's time."

"Out through that window, three years ago to a day, her husband and her two young brothers went off for their day's shooting. They never came back. In crossing the moor to their favourite snipe-shooting ground they were all three engulfed in a treacherous piece of bog. It had been that dreadful wet summer, you know, and places that were safe in other years gave way suddenly without warning. Their bodies were never recovered. That was the dreadful part of it."

"Poor aunt always thinks that they will come back someday, they and the little brown spaniel that was lost with them, and walk in at that window just as they used to do. That is why the window is kept open every evening till it is quite dusk."

At the Window

Mrs. Sappleton comes down at last and chatters cheerfully — about her husband and brothers, who will be back from shooting any moment. Framton is sure she is deluded by grief. And then the light begins to fade.

"Here they are at last!" she cried. "Just in time for tea, and don't they look as if they were muddy up to the eyes!"

Framton shivered slightly and turned towards the niece with a look intended to convey sympathetic comprehension. The child was staring out through the open window with dazed horror in her eyes. In a chill shock of nameless fear Framton swung round in his seat and looked in the same direction.

In the deepening twilight three figures were walking across the lawn towards the window; they all carried guns under their arms, and one of them was additionally burdened with a white coat hung over his shoulders. A tired brown spaniel kept close at their heels. Noiselessly they neared the house, and then a hoarse young voice chanted out of the dusk: "I said, Bertie, why do you bound?"

Framton grabbed wildly at his stick and hat; the hall door, the gravel drive, and the front gate were dimly noted stages in his headlong retreat.

Why does Vera stare "with dazed horror in her eyes"?

Show Answer
Characterisation It is pure performance. The real horror would be Framton's if he believed her — so she mirrors it back to him, completing the illusion. Effect The reader, still trusting Vera, takes her fear as proof the figures are ghosts.

The figures are perfectly ordinary — guns, a coat, a tired dog, a cheerful song. So why is the moment terrifying?

Show Answer
Dramatic Irony Atmosphere The "deepening twilight" and the figures arriving "noiselessly" feel ghostly only because of the story we were told. Effect Saki shows that terror is manufactured by narrative, not by events. The same three men would be harmless without Vera's tale.

"Here we are, my dear," said the bearer of the white mackintosh, coming in through the window; "fairly muddy, but most of it's dry. Who was that who bolted out as we came up?"

"A most extraordinary man, a Mr. Nuttel," said Mrs. Sappleton; "could only talk about his illnesses, and dashed off without a word of goodbye or apology when you arrived. One would think he had seen a ghost."

"I expect it was the spaniel," said the niece calmly; "he told me he had a horror of dogs. He was once hunted into a cemetery somewhere on the banks of the Ganges by a pack of pariah dogs, and had to spend the night in a newly dug grave with the creatures snarling and grinning and foaming just above him. Enough to make anyone lose their nerve."

Romance at short notice was her speciality.

The twist works on two victims at once. Who are they?

Show Answer
Situational Irony Framton and the reader. We expected a ghost story; we got a portrait of a teenage liar. Effect The reveal makes us re-read everything — and laugh at our own gullibility.

"Romance at short notice was her speciality." Why end on this line?

Show Answer
Comic Irony Characterisation One cool sentence reveals Vera invents tales on demand — and has just done it again, instantly, about the dogs. Effect It turns dread into delight, rewards the alert reader, and leaves us admiring the very skill that fooled us.

From "spotting" to analysis

The examiner has read the story too. Naming a technique earns almost nothing. The marks live in the next step: what the technique does to the reader.

Weak: "Saki uses a framed narrative."
Strong: "Saki's framed narrative misleads the reader by trapping us inside Framton's false belief, so the twist ambushes us as much as it does him."

Saki's use of the framed narrative in "That is why the window is kept open every evening till it is quite dusk" misleads the reader by making us share Framton's belief that the men are dead. As a result, the situational irony of their return ambushes us as much as it does him, and the final line — "Romance at short notice was her speciality" — amuses the reader while exposing how easily a calm voice and a few precise details can manufacture fear.
