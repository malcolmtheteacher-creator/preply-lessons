Where Did the Free Time Go?

In 1930 the world's most famous economist promised that by now we would work fifteen hours a week. This is the true story of the promise, the theft, and the experiment that might finally keep it.

Today: you'll follow the hundred-year battle over the working week — and you'll master the grammar of effort and exhaustion: I've worked here for ten years versus I've been working every evening this month.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — Eight hours: the time that was won

Free time had to be invented, and then it had to be fought for. In the mill towns of the early industrial revolution, fourteen- and sixteen-hour days were ordinary, six days a week, for adults and children alike; the factory bell, not the sun, owned the day. Against this, in 1817, the reformer Robert Owen coined a slogan of beautiful arithmetic: eight hours labour, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest. It took a century of strikes, laws and lost livelihoods to make those numbers real — the eight-hour day and the weekend are not features of nature; they are trophies, won an hour at a time.

Then something unexpected happened: big business joined in, for business reasons. In 1914 Henry Ford stunned America by doubling his workers' pay, and in 1926 he moved his factories to a five-day, forty-hour week — arguing, in public, that exhausted workers built worse cars, and that people with no free time and no money would never buy the cars they built. Leisure, in Ford's cold arithmetic, was good business. Others went further: in 1930 the Kellogg's cereal company in Michigan replaced its three eight-hour shifts with four six-hour shifts, partly to share jobs in the Depression — and something remarkable took root. Workers gardened, coached teams, saw their children; families reorganised around the extra hours; and the six-hour day at Kellogg's survived, for parts of the workforce, all the way to 1985. For half a century, one ordinary town lived the future everyone else had been promised.

Because promised it was — and by the grandest voice available. In 1930 the economist John Maynard Keynes published a small essay called Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren. Looking a hundred years ahead — to about now — he predicted that machines and compound growth would solve "the economic problem," and that our real difficulty would be what to do with our freedom: perhaps fifteen hours of work a week, he suggested, mainly to give the day some shape. He worried, sincerely, that ordinary people would struggle to fill their leisure. Hold that worry in your mind, at the school gate, at 18:47, with the shopping still in the car.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did the eight-hour day come from — nature, generosity, or something else?

2. What did Ford argue, what did Kellogg's do, and what did Keynes predict?

1. It was won — a century of strikes and laws turned Owen's 1817 slogan (8-8-8) into reality. Free time is a trophy, not a default.

2. Ford: rested workers build better cars and workers with time and money become customers — so he doubled pay and cut to five days. Part 2 — The promise that went missing

Here is the strange part: Keynes got the machines right. Productivity since 1930 has multiplied roughly five times over — one hour of work today produces what took most of a day then. By his arithmetic, the fifteen-hour week was affordable decades ago. And for a while the world moved his way: working hours fell steadily for a hundred years. Then, around the 1970s and 80s — in some countries more sharply than others — the great shrinking simply stopped. The productivity kept climbing; the free time stopped arriving. Somewhere between the machine and the calendar, the dividend went missing.

The investigators of that missing dividend point to several thieves working together. The gains flowed more into consumption and into fewer pockets than into time — we bought bigger lives instead of longer evenings, and for many families rising costs made two full salaries a requirement, not a choice. Status flipped upside down: a century ago the rich advertised idleness, while today the successful compete about exhaustion — "crazy busy" has become a boast. And then technology dismantled the wall the reformers had built: Ford's workers left work at the gate because work was a physical place; the smartphone put the factory in the kitchen, the bedroom, the beach. Studies of "always-on" workers keep finding the same result — the expectation of availability, even without actual emails, is enough to keep the body's stress system idling all evening.

And one more finding, which every working parent should hear twice, because it is the opposite of the guilt: time-use researchers comparing diaries across decades find that today's parents — despite both working — spend more hours actively caring for their children than parents did in the supposedly golden 1960s. Both shifts grew: the paid one refused to shrink, and the parenting one expanded into everything modern childhood now demands. The tiredness, in other words, is not a personal failure of organisation. It is arithmetic — two expanding jobs in the same twenty-four hours — and it has a history, which means it can have a future.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Keynes get right, and where did the prediction break?

2. Name three "thieves" of the missing free time — and the finding about modern parents.

1. The productivity: it multiplied about fivefold, making short weeks affordable long ago. But after a century of falling hours, the decline stopped around the 1970s–80s — output kept rising, free time didn't.

2. Gains went to consumption and unequal pockets; busyness became a status symbol; the smartphone dissolved the wall between work and home (with "always-on" availability keeping stress idling). And parents today spend more active hours on childcare than 1960s parents — both shifts grew at once, so the exhaustion is arithmetic, not personal failure.

Part 3 — The experiment returns

The counter-attack began, fittingly, with a man reading on a plane. In 2018 Andrew Barnes, founder of a New Zealand trust company, read an article claiming office workers manage barely three productive hours a day — and made his staff an offer that sounded like a trick: work four days, be paid for five, if the work still gets done. The trial made global news; productivity held, stress fell, and the four-day week became permanent. The idea he insisted on is the part worth quoting: the deal was not "less work" but a trade — your focus for your Friday — meetings cut, distractions redesigned, the day earned rather than gifted.

Then the idea scaled from a company to a country. Iceland, between 2015 and 2019, ran public-sector trials covering thousands of workers — hours cut with no pay cut — and the researchers' verdict entered the movement's folklore: an overwhelming success. Services held up, wellbeing and burnout measures improved, and in the negotiations that followed, the great majority of Iceland's entire workforce won rights to shorter hours. In 2022 came the biggest company experiment yet: sixty-one British firms, thousands of employees, six months of four-day weeks under university observation. Revenue held roughly steady, sick days fell, resignations dropped — and when the trial ended, more than nine in ten of the companies simply kept going; most later made it permanent. Similar pilots have followed across Europe and beyond, including in Germany, and governments from Spain to Japan have flirted with supporting them.

Honesty requires the other column. The trial companies were volunteers — believers, mostly offices; nobody has yet shown how a hospital ward, a school timetable or a delivery depot compresses five days of physical presence into four, and sceptics note that "productivity held" is easier to declare than to measure. The debate is genuinely open. But something has shifted that no critic disputes: for the first time in half a century, the length of the working week is a live question again — argued about in parliaments, tested in trials, negotiated in job interviews. The hundred-year pause may or may not be ending. What this story proves is only, and exactly, this: every hour of free time your family has ever had was invented, argued for, and won by people who were told it was impossible — and the winning, on the evidence of the last few years, has started again.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the New Zealand deal, and what made it a trade rather than a gift?

2. What did the Iceland and UK trials find — and what does the honest other column say?

1. Four days' work for five days' pay, if output held: focus traded for Friday — meetings cut and distractions redesigned, so the day was earned, not gifted. It went permanent and went global.

2. Iceland's public trials: an "overwhelming success" — wellbeing up, services stable, leading to shorter-hours rights for most of the workforce. UK 2022: 61 firms, revenue roughly steady, sick days and resignations down, 90%+ continued. The other column: volunteer office-heavy samples, hard-to-measure productivity, and no answer yet for wards, schools and depots.

Discuss:
  • "Your focus for your Friday." Would that trade work in your job — genuinely? What would have to change on the other four days?

Grammar — present perfect simple vs continuous

The grammar of tiredness. English separates what you have achieved from what you have been doing — and for anyone describing a busy life, the difference is everything.

Present perfect simple — the result, the total, the achievement:
• "I've finished the report." (it's done)
• "We've lived here for ten years." (states and totals: live, know, have, be)
• With counts: "She's had three meetings today."
Present perfect continuous — the activity, the duration, the visible effort:
• "I've been finishing the report all evening." (still at it — or just stopped, and it shows)
• "We've been juggling two jobs and the school run for years."
• The classic exchange: "You look exhausted." — "I've been driving since six." — the continuous explains the state you can see.

The parent's contrast in one pair: "I've cooked dinner" = it's on the table. "I've been cooking dinner" = that's why I haven't answered your emails. Result verbs (finish, decide, break, win) prefer simple; duration verbs (work, wait, try, juggle) prefer continuous; and How long…? almost always takes the continuous for activities.

Going deeper

A note on the record: figures are rounded; Keynes's essay says "three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week" with his own hedges attached; the trial results summarised here are the researchers' published headline findings, and their critics' points are given in the story because they are fair.

What the story actually teaches. First, that the week is a technology, not a law of nature. Eight hours, five days, the weekend itself — each was designed, argued over and installed, which means each can be redesigned. People trapped inside a schedule tend to experience it as physics; the history exists precisely to break that illusion. The practical question it hands any household is disarmingly small: which parts of our week did we choose, and which did we merely inherit?

Second, the boundary lesson. Ford's workers had worse jobs and better borders: work ended at the gate. Modern work traded the gate for the phone, and the research is blunt — availability itself, before a single message arrives, keeps the stress system running. That reframes the evening phone not as a habit question but as an infrastructure question: families that succeed tend to rebuild a gate somewhere — a drawer, an hour, a room — because willpower against infrastructure loses.

Third, the guilt correction, which may be the most useful sentence in the lesson: parents today work more paid hours than the fifteen-hour prophecy promised and more parenting hours than their own parents gave — simultaneously. The exhaustion of a two-job household is not disorganisation; it is two centuries of history landing on one kitchen calendar. Naming that accurately changes the conversation from "what's wrong with us?" to "what do we want to win back first?" — which is, as the whole story shows, the question that has always started the winning.

Talk it through:
  • Inventory your week honestly: which three parts were chosen, and which three were inherited? Would you re-choose them?
  • Design your gate: what would a realistic "end of work" look like in your household — where, when, enforced how?
  • Keynes asked what wealth is for; Ford answered "customers"; Kellogg's workers answered "gardens and children". What is your answer — the real one your calendar shows, and the one you'd prefer it to show?
  • If the four-day week reached your industry tomorrow, who in your workplace would resist it — and what would their honest reason be?
