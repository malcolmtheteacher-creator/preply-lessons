Listen in on this conversation

Ana: How's the new flat working out?

Sam: Actually really well — it's got a little balcony whose view stretches right across the valley, which I definitely wasn't expecting for the price.

Ana: No way. How did you even find it?

Look again at what Sam says. Three grammar choices stand out: "whose view" (for a balcony, not a person), "which I definitely wasn't expecting" (commenting on the whole idea, not one word), and "through whom" (the preposition moved in front of the pronoun). Try to say why each one works before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
All three are typical of fluent, natural English at this level. "Whose" isn't just for people — it works for things too. "Which" can sum up a whole situation, not just refer to one noun. And moving a preposition in front of "whom" or "which" is the mark of a more careful, considered sentence. Today you'll get comfortable using all three — plus two more related tricks.
Today: five ways to add precise, natural detail with relative clauses — prepositions with which/whom, of which/of whom, whose for things, commenting with which, and clauses trimmed right down — so you can sound sharper and more natural, in speech and in writing.

Prepositions with Which and Whom

Adding an aside about someone or something, formally — and the everyday alternative.

Spot the pattern

Three asides, added mid-sentence:

  • "The lawyer, for whom this case is deeply personal, has worked on it for free."
  • "This is a debate about which reasonable people disagree."
  • "Their office, in which no natural light ever reaches the back desks, is due for a refurbishment."

Before you check: where does the preposition go, and which pronoun follows it for people, which for things?

Check the pattern CLICK
The preposition sits directly before the pronoun: whom for people, which for things. This is the formal, careful version — the kind you'd write, or say when choosing your words with care.
..., preposition + whom + clause... (people)
..., preposition + which + clause... (things)

Formal vs everyday

Formal:
"My tutor, with whom I still keep in touch, taught me everything about writing."
→
Everyday:
"My tutor, who I still keep in touch with, taught me everything about writing."

Same meaning. In everyday speech, the preposition drops to the end and "whom" relaxes to "who". The fronted version is the one people reach for in writing, interviews, or anywhere they want to sound precise.

Part of a Group: "Of Which" and "Of Whom"

Referring back to some, not all, of a group you've just mentioned — and "whose" for things.

Spot the pattern

  • "The charity has around three hundred volunteers, a handful of whom have been involved since it started."
  • "She showed me her postcard collection, most of which came from places she's never actually visited."
  • "They bought a warehouse whose windows had been bricked up for decades."

What comes before "of whom" and "of which" in the first two? And what does "whose" do in the third, given a warehouse isn't a person?

Check the pattern CLICK
A quantity word (a handful, most, three, several, none...) + of whom (people) or of which (things) refers back to part of a group already mentioned. And whose shows possession for things exactly as it does for people — "a warehouse whose windows" works just like "a woman whose car".
quantity + of whom (people) / of which (things)
noun + whose + noun (people AND things)

Two ways to say the same thing

Everyday:
"A cottage whose roof leaks every winter."
→
Formal:
"A cottage, the roof of which leaks every winter,"

"Whose" is by far the more natural choice for everyday use. "The roof of which" exists and is grammatically correct, but it sounds noticeably formal — you'll meet it more in writing than in conversation.

Comments and Cut-Down Clauses

Commenting on a whole situation with "which" — and trimming a clause down to its essentials.

Spot the pattern: commenting with "which"

  • "The flight got delayed by six hours, which meant we missed the connection completely."
  • "He never once said thank you, which I found pretty rude, to be honest."
  • "Half the staff called in sick on the same day, which nobody could quite explain."

In each sentence, what single noun does "which" refer to?

Check the pattern CLICK
None. "Which" here doesn't point back to one noun at all — it points back to the whole idea that came before the comma (the delay, the missing thank-you, the staff all calling in sick). It's a comment on the entire situation.
[whole idea], which + comment

Spot the pattern: cutting the clause down

  • "The man standing by the door is my brother-in-law."
  • "Documents submitted after the deadline won't be accepted."
  • "Anyone caught smoking inside will be asked to leave."

What's missing from these compared with "the man who is standing" or "documents which were submitted"?

Check the pattern CLICK
The relative pronoun and "be" are simply dropped. Use the -ing form for an active meaning ("who is standing"), and the past participle for a passive one ("which were submitted", "who was caught"). The meaning stays exactly the same — the sentence just gets shorter.
noun + -ing... (active: "who is/was doing")
noun + past participle... (passive: "which is/was done")

What Do You Remember?

First, from earlier lessons

2. Say it out loud: what's the difference between a defining and a non-defining relative clause? Give one example of each.

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: a defining clause identifies which person or thing you mean, and has no commas — "The man who called yesterday is my landlord" (without it, you wouldn't know which man). A non-defining clause adds extra, non-essential information, set off with commas — "My landlord, who called yesterday, is very easy-going" (we already know who he is).
