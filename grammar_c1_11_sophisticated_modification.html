Matching the Intensity: "fairly cold" or "absolutely freezing"?

Some adjectives come in degrees. Others are already at the top or bottom of the scale. Each type needs its own booster.

Spot the pattern

Three real sentences:

  • "The office was fairly cold, but by the time we got outside it was absolutely freezing."
  • "I'm not saying the film was amazing, but it was pretty good, and the ending was utterly perfect."
  • "She seemed rather nervous before the interview, but afterwards she looked completely relaxed."

Look at the two boosters in each sentence. What's different about the adjective they go with?

Check the pattern CLICK
cold, good, nervous are gradable — you can be a little cold or very cold, so soft boosters like fairly / pretty / rather / quite / a bit / very work.
freezing, perfect, relaxed (in this extreme sense) are already at the top of the scale, so they need strong boosters: absolutely / utterly / completely / totally / really. "Very freezing" or "very perfect" sound odd to a native speaker — the booster has to match the type of adjective.
gradable (cold, tired, big, busy) + very / fairly / pretty / rather / quite / a bit / somewhat
extreme (freezing, exhausted, huge, perfect) + absolutely / utterly / completely / totally / really
"The water was very freezing." (sounds foreign)
→
"The water was absolutely freezing."
A third group doesn't grade at all — classifying adjectives like digital, wooden, medical, monthly. You can't be "very wooden" or "fairly monthly"; the table either is wooden or it isn't.

Longer Descriptions: order, and -ing or -ed?

When you stack more than one adjective, order matters — and so does choosing between the -ing and -ed form.

Spot the pattern

Three real descriptions:

  • "We stayed in a beautiful, newly renovated apartment right in the old town."
  • "He gave a genuinely fascinating, slightly rambling talk about local history."
  • "It's a small, incredibly relaxing beach bar, completely empty on weekdays."

Notice two things: the order the adjectives come in, and words like "fascinating" versus "fascinated." Why "a fascinating talk" but "I was fascinated"? Say your guess before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Order: opinion words (beautiful, fascinating, relaxing) come before more fact-based ones (age, size, origin, material). "Newly renovated, beautiful apartment" feels back-to-front, even though it isn't strictly ungrammatical — native speakers strongly prefer opinion first.
-ing vs -ed: the -ing form describes the thing that CAUSES the feeling (a fascinating talk = the talk causes fascination); the -ed form describes the person who EXPERIENCES it (I was fascinated). "A fascinated talk" would mean the talk itself felt fascination — impossible, so it's wrong.
opinion adjective + fact adjective (age / size / origin / material) + noun
-ing = the thing that causes the feeling (a boring meeting)
-ed = the person who experiences it (I was bored)
"a wooden, very old chair" (order reversed)
→
"a very old, wooden chair"

Adding Your Angle: "frankly," "admittedly," "surprisingly"

These words don't describe an action — they show your attitude to the whole sentence.

Spot the pattern

  • "Frankly, I don't think the plan will work."
  • "The restaurant was, admittedly, a bit overpriced, but the food was excellent."
  • "Surprisingly, nobody complained about the delay."

Where do these words sit, and what do they do — describe HOW something happened, or show your attitude to the whole statement? Say your guess before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
These are comment adverbs — they show the speaker's attitude to the whole sentence, not how an action was done. Frankly = I'll be honest, admittedly = I accept this even though it works against my point, surprisingly = this wasn't expected, understandably = there's a good reason for this. They open the sentence ("Frankly, ..."), or sit mid-sentence set off by commas ("was, admittedly, a bit...").
comment adverb + sentence.
sentence, comment adverb, rest of sentence.
(frankly, admittedly, understandably, surprisingly, honestly, obviously, arguably)

Your turn

2. Insert "admittedly" right after "was": "The hotel was a little noisy, but the view made up for it."

3. Choose the comment adverb that fits: "The trains were cancelled all morning — ___, quite a few people arrived late."

Compare with the rule CLICK
Short adjectives (one syllable, most two) take -est: tall → tallest. Longer adjectives take "the most" + adjective: beautiful → the most beautiful. It's the same short/long split you already use for comparatives (nicer, but more interesting).
