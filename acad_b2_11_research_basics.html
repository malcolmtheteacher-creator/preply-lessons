What Is Research?

Research is systematic inquiry—a methodical process of collecting and analyzing information to answer a specific question or test a hypothesis. It's not opinion or casual observation; it follows structured procedures.

Research in University

As a B2 student, you encounter research in several contexts:

  • Reading research papers to understand what scholars have discovered
  • Critiquing research design (does the methodology actually answer the question?)
  • Conducting small-scale research projects for coursework
  • Planning research for dissertations (especially postgraduate)

Two Main Research Approaches

Quantitative Research: Collects numerical data. Answers questions like "How much?" "How many?" "What's the relationship?" Uses statistics and numbers.

Example: A study surveying 500 students about study habits, measuring hours studied vs. exam scores.

Qualitative Research: Collects descriptive data. Answers "Why?" "How?" "What's the experience?" Uses words, interviews, observations.

Example: Interviews with 20 students exploring why they struggle with academic writing.

Many studies combine both approaches (mixed methods).

The Research Process

  1. Identify a question — What do you want to know?
  2. Review existing literature — What's already been discovered?
  3. Develop a hypothesis — What do you predict?
  4. Design methodology — How will you collect data?
  5. Collect data — Conduct your study
  6. Analyze results — What does the data show?
  7. Draw conclusions — What does it mean? What are limitations?
  8. Disseminate findings — Share results through papers or presentations

Research Language and Concepts

Common Research Terms

Population: The entire group you want to study

Example: All university students studying English in the UK

Sample: The subset of the population you actually study

Example: 200 English students from three universities

Independent variable: What the researcher changes or manipulates

Example: Type of study method (active vs. passive)

Dependent variable: What the researcher measures; the outcome

Example: Exam scores resulting from study method

Control group: Participants not receiving the intervention; used for comparison

Example: Students using traditional study methods (vs. new method group)

Experimental group: Participants receiving the intervention

Example: Students using new study methods

Bias: Systematic error that distorts results

Example: Researcher only recruiting motivated students (skewing results)

Validity: Whether the research actually measures what it claims to measure

Example: A study measuring "intelligence" through vocabulary tests has questionable validity

Research Language in Writing

Describing methods: "We distributed questionnaires to 150 participants." / "The study employed a mixed-methods design." / "Data were collected through semi-structured interviews."

Reporting findings: "The results indicate..." / "The data suggest..." / "A positive correlation was found between..."

Discussing limitations: "This study was limited by a small sample size." / "A caveat is that participants were self-selected." / "The findings may not generalize to other populations."

Important distinction: Research findings don't prove; they suggest, indicate, or provide evidence. Researchers speak carefully about their conclusions because research has limitations.

Planning a Research Study

Step 1: Develop a Research Question

Your research question drives everything. It should be:

  • Specific (not vague: "Why do students struggle?" is too broad)
  • Answerable (you can actually investigate it)
  • Significant (matters to your field or society)
  • Interesting (to you and others)

Example: "Does using active recall while studying improve long-term retention in university students compared to passive rereading?"

Step 2: Literature Review

Search existing research. What's already known? What gaps exist? Your study should address a gap.

Step 3: Develop a Hypothesis

Based on literature, predict what you expect to find.

Example: "Students using active recall will score significantly higher on delayed tests than students using passive rereading."

Step 4: Plan Your Methodology

Decide:

  • Approach: Quantitative, qualitative, or mixed?
  • Design: Experiment, survey, interviews, observations?
  • Participants: Who will you study? How many?
  • Data collection: How will you gather information?
  • Analysis: How will you make sense of the data?

Step 5: Ethical Considerations

All university research involving humans must be ethical:

  • Informed consent: Participants know what they're involved in
  • Confidentiality: Personal information stays private
  • No harm: Research doesn't hurt participants
  • Right to withdraw: Participants can quit anytime

Your university has an ethics committee reviewing research. Don't proceed without approval.

Becoming a Critical Research Consumer

When Reading Research, Ask:

Is the research question clear?

Can you state what the researcher investigated? If not, the paper may be unclear or the question unfocused.

Is the methodology appropriate?

Does the study design actually answer the question? Is the sample size adequate? Could design flaws explain results?

Are the findings credible?

Are statistics used correctly? Do the conclusions match the data? Are limitations acknowledged?

Could bias affect the results?

Who funded the research? Did researchers have conflicts of interest? Could this bias findings?

Do the conclusions go too far?

Are they claiming too much from limited evidence? Are they acknowledging study limitations?

The reality of research: All research has limitations. Perfect studies don't exist. Good researchers acknowledge what their study can and cannot conclude. Be suspicious of research claiming absolute certainty.
