After Twenty Years

O. Henry's story of two friends, one promise, and the twenty years in between.

Today: you'll tell the story of a promise kept for twenty years — and you'll finally sort out the two tenses learners mix up most: I met him in 2005 versus I haven't seen him since.
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • Do you think you'll still know your current best friend in twenty years? What would have to happen for that to be true?
  • Can people change completely, or does something always stay the same?
  • If a friend of yours did something seriously wrong, where would your loyalty stop?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four short activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. The officer walked the same streets every night at the same times, trying doors and watching the corners. It was a quiet beat, and he knew every shop on it.
a beat (n) — the regular route a police officer walks. "An officer on the beat."
2. Twenty years earlier the corner had been full of light and noise and people eating late. Now the restaurant was gone and the building was dark and boarded up.
boarded up (adj/phrase) — closed with wooden boards over the doors and windows, because the building is empty.
3. He said the words carefully, without any doubt at all, because he had made the arrangement himself and had never once considered breaking it. His tone was firm.
firm (adj) — steady, certain, not to be changed. "A firm promise." Adverb: firmly.

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: appointment · fortune · plain clothes. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means an arrangement to meet someone at a particular time and place?
an appointment (n) — "He kept the appointment exactly twenty years later." (Also: a doctor's appointment.)
2. Which word means a very large amount of money?
a fortune (n) — "He went west to make his fortune." Note the collocations: make / inherit / spend a fortune.
3. Which phrase means ordinary clothes worn by a police officer instead of a uniform?
plain clothes — "A plain clothes officer." The point is that nobody can tell what he is.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. The two men had been inseparable as boys — same school, same streets, same evenings, every single day. Inseparable means…
  (a) always together and impossible to divide   (b) very competitive   (c) completely silent
(a) — inseparable (adj) = never apart. From separate + the negative prefix in-.
2. He could not do it himself, so he asked a colleague to go instead. He couldn't bring himself to face his old friend. This means he…
  (a) forgot to do it   (b) was physically unable to do it   (c) found it emotionally impossible to make himself do it
(c) — can't bring yourself to do something = you are able to, but you cannot make yourself. "I couldn't bring myself to tell her."

D · Word building & word partners

1. You know loyal. Make the noun ("she showed great ___ to her friends") and the opposite adjective ("a ___ friend who talks about you behind your back").
loyalty (n) · disloyal (adj). Useful collocations: loyal to someone, a question of loyalty, divided loyalties — which is exactly this story.
2. One verb fits all three: to ___ a promise · to ___ an appointment · to ___ a secret. And its opposite in the first two cases?
keep — keep a promise / an appointment / a secret. The opposite of the first two: break a promise, miss an appointment.

Now use them — out loud

Talk it through before we read:
  • Using appointment, fortune, loyalty and plain clothes, predict what happens when two friends meet after twenty years apart.
  • Which of today's words are about friendship, and which are about the law? Which one belongs to both? Sort them out loud.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A man in a dark doorway

It was barely ten o'clock at night, but the wind had emptied the streets. The officer moved along his beat impressively, trying doors as he went, turning now and then to cast his watchful eye down the avenue. The shops had closed hours ago.

In the doorway of a darkened hardware store a man was leaning, with an unlit cigar in his mouth. As the officer walked up, the man spoke quickly. "It's all right, officer. I'm just waiting for a friend. It's an appointment made twenty years ago. Sounds funny to you, doesn't it? Well, I'll explain if you'd like to make certain it's all straight."

Twenty years ago, he said, there had been a restaurant on this corner. He and his closest friend, Jimmy Wells, had eaten there the night before he left for the West. They had grown up together in this city, inseparable, and they knew that life would pull them in different directions. So they agreed to meet again on this exact spot, at this exact hour, twenty years later — whatever had happened to either of them in the meantime. He struck a match to light his cigar, and for a moment the little flame showed a pale square-jawed face with sharp eyes and a small white scar near the right eyebrow. Then it went dark again.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the man doing in the doorway, and what was on this corner twenty years ago?

2. What agreement did the two friends make, and why?

1. Waiting to keep an appointment made twenty years earlier. There was a restaurant on the corner, where the two friends ate their last meal together.

2. To meet on the same spot at the same hour exactly twenty years later, no matter what had become of them, because they knew life would separate them.

Discuss:
  • The story shows us his face for one second, in a match flame, and then hides it again. Why show it at all at that moment?

Part 2 — "The best chap in the world"

The officer asked whether he had heard from his friend since he left. A little, the man said — they had written for a while, and then lost touch, the way people do. But he had no doubt at all that Jimmy would come. Jimmy was the truest, most reliable person he had ever known; he would never forget. A thousand miles could not change that.

The West had been good to him, he explained. He had gone out there with nothing and had worked hard, and now he had a good deal of money. He pulled out a handsome watch, its lid set with small diamonds, to show the time: three minutes to ten. He had needed to compete out there, he said, to keep sharp. Jimmy had stayed here in the old city, plodding along in the same steady way — a fine fellow, but slow. Sometimes you have to leave, he said, if you want to make a fortune.

The officer said he hoped his friend would come, wished him good night, and continued along his beat, trying doors as he went. A fine cold rain had begun to fall, and the wind was rising. The man in the doorway settled back to wait, with his collar turned up, twenty minutes past the hour, then thirty.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What has happened to the man in the twenty years since he left?

2. How does he describe his old friend Jimmy?

1. He went West with nothing, worked hard, competed, and made a great deal of money — he shows a valuable diamond-set watch.

2. As the truest and most reliable friend he ever had, who would certainly come — though also as a slow, plodding sort of man who stayed in the same place.

Discuss:
  • He calls Jimmy the best person he ever knew, and in almost the same breath calls him slow. What does that tell you about how he has come to see success?

Part 3 — Under the streetlight

At last a tall man in a long overcoat hurried across from the opposite side of the street, straight to the doorway. "Is that you, Bob?" he asked. "Is that you, Jimmy Wells?" cried the waiting man. The two shook hands and gripped each other's arms, both talking at once about the twenty years, and set off together up the street towards a place where they could sit and talk properly.

At the corner they passed under the bright light of a chemist's window, and Bob turned to look at his friend's face. He stopped. He let go of the arm. "You're not Jimmy Wells," he said. "Twenty years is a long time, but not long enough to change a man's nose from a Roman one to a pug."

"It sometimes changes a good man into a bad one," said the tall man. "You've been under arrest for ten minutes, Bob. Chicago wired us that you might be coming this way; they'd like a word with you. Now, before we go along, here's a note somebody asked me to hand you." Bob unfolded the little piece of paper. His hand was steady when he began to read and shaking a little by the time he finished. It was short. "Bob: I was at the appointed place on time. When you struck the match to light your cigar, I saw it was the face of a man wanted in Chicago. Somehow I couldn't bring myself to do it myself, so I went round and got a plain clothes man to do the job. JIMMY."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Bob know that the tall man is not Jimmy?

2. What does the note reveal — and what had Jimmy done when he recognised his friend's face?

1. Under the bright light he sees the man's nose is the wrong shape. Twenty years cannot change a Roman nose into a pug nose.

2. That Jimmy was the officer on the beat, and kept the appointment exactly. He recognised Bob's face in the match flame as a wanted man, could not arrest his old friend himself, and sent a colleague in plain clothes instead.

Discuss:
  • Jimmy walked up, talked to him, wished him good night and walked on — knowing everything. What was going through his mind during that conversation?

Grammar — present perfect vs past simple

This whole story is twenty years long, and every sentence about those years has to choose between two tenses. Get the choice right and your English immediately sounds a level higher.

Past simple — finished time. The action is over, and so is the time period it happened in. Usually with a time expression: yesterday, in 2019, last summer, when I was ten, twenty years ago.
• "We moved house in 2019." · "I saw her last Tuesday."
Present perfect — the time is still open, or the result matters now. have/has + past participle.
• Unfinished time: "I've read three books this month." (the month isn't over)
• Experience, no date given: "Have you ever been to Iceland?"
• Result now: "She's broken her arm." (it's in plaster today)

The words that decide it:
for + a length of time, since + a starting point, ever, never, yet, already, just, so far → present perfect.
ago, in 2005, last night, when → past simple.
The trap: ✗ I have seen him last year. A finished time expression forces the past simple: ✓ I saw him last year.

1. Which tense?

Put the verb in brackets into the past simple or the present perfect.

1. The two friends (eat) their last meal together twenty years ago.

2. They (not / see) each other since that night.

3. "I (never / forget) that appointment," said Bob.

1. ate ("twenty years ago" — finished time)

2. haven't seen ("since" — the time is still running)

3. have never forgotten (experience across his whole life up to now)

2. for or since — and the right form

Write two or three words in each gap.

1. Bob has been waiting in that doorway half an hour.

2. Jimmy has walked this same beat he joined the force.

3. How long (you / know) your oldest friend?

1. for   2. since   3. have you known

for + a length of time (for three years) · since + a point when it started (since 2019, since I was ten).

3. Retell the twenty years — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the correct form, then read the whole paragraph aloud.

Bob (leave) New York twenty years ago and (go) west to make his fortune. Since then the two friends (lose) touch completely. Bob (make) a great deal of money, and he (become) a man the police in Chicago want to talk to. Jimmy (stay) in the same city all his life, and he (walk) the same streets ever since. Tonight, for the first time in twenty years, they (meet) again — and only one of them knows it.

left · went · have lost (or: lost) · has made · has become · stayed · has walked · have met

Watch the signals: "twenty years ago" pulls the past simple; "since then" and "ever since" pull the present perfect.

Hold on to this contrast — in the Speak tab you'll talk about your own long friendships, where both tenses are needed in almost every sentence.

Going deeper

O. Henry published this in 1906. He wrote hundreds of very short New York stories, almost all built the same way: an ordinary situation, a few pages of easy talk, and one final paragraph that turns the whole thing over. He knew the material from the inside — he had served time in prison himself before he started writing.

The heart of it. Read it a second time and it becomes a different story. Jimmy recognises his friend within seconds, in the light of a match — and then goes on chatting, asks kind questions, hopes his friend's friend will turn up, says good night, and walks away to arrange the arrest. Everything he says is true and nothing he says is honest. Notice what the story refuses to do: it never tells us what Bob did in Chicago, and it never lets us watch Jimmy decide. The decision happens off the page, in the dark, between two lines of dialogue. And notice the last detail. Jimmy could have made the arrest himself and kept it quiet; instead he sends a stranger and writes a note explaining why. He needed his friend to know that the appointment was kept.

Talk it through:
  • Did Jimmy do the right thing — and would your answer change if he had simply walked away and told nobody?
  • Why does the story give us Bob's version of Jimmy ("slow", "plodding") before we know who is speaking to him?
  • Both men kept the appointment exactly. Does that make this a story about loyalty or about the end of it?
  • The note is four sentences long and signed with a first name. What would be lost if Jimmy had explained himself properly?

Speak

This is the heart of the lesson — do all of it out loud. The timer is there to keep you going: fill the time with detail rather than racing through it.

Useful language: lean on today's tenses — we met in…, I've known him since…, we haven't spoken for years, he's changed a lot. For judging: "In his position, I'd…", "What he should have done is…", "You can see why, but…".

Task A · Tell the whole story (4 minutes)

Tell After Twenty Years in your own words. Move through the arc: the empty street and the officer on his beat → the man in the doorway → the promise made twenty years ago → the match, the face, the scar → the talk about the West and about Jimmy → the tall man in the overcoat → the light at the corner → the note.

Use at least three present perfect verbs and three past simple verbs, and at least five key words (beat, boarded up, firm, appointment, fortune, plain clothes, inseparable, loyalty, keep a promise, couldn't bring himself to).

Task B · Was Jimmy right? (4 minutes)

Take a side and argue it properly. He kept the appointment and then had his oldest friend arrested — but he could not do it with his own hands, and he made sure Bob knew he had come. Give three reasons, answer the strongest objection to your view, and say what you would have done walking up to that doorway.

Then argue the opposite side for one minute.

Task C · Friends, time and change (4 minutes)

Talk about long friendships and what time does to them.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Describe your oldest friendship: when it started, what you've done together, and what's changed.
  • Write your own twenty-year appointment out loud: where would you tell someone to meet you in 2046, and why there?
  • Do people drift apart because of distance, or because they become different people? Argue for one.
  • Should loyalty to a friend ever outweigh the law? Give a case where you'd say yes and a case where you'd say no.
  • Imagine meeting yourself in twenty years. What would surprise you most?
  • Describe someone you've known for years who has changed a great deal. Use both tenses carefully.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Jimmy could have quietly walked away and never told anyone. Why does he write the note?
  2. Bob travelled a thousand miles to keep this appointment, knowing he was wanted. What does that say about him?
  3. O. Henry never tells us what Bob did. Does the story work better without that information — and would you judge either man differently if you knew?
