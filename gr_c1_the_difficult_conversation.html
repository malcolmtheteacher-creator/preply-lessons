The Connection

Politicians and CEOs use specific techniques to handle tough questions. But those same techniques work in everyday life too. Today, you will use them in real situations — asking for a raise, making a complaint, delivering bad news, and saying no.

Same moves. Personal stakes.

The 6 Moves

You learned these in The Press Conference. Here is how they work in everyday conversations.

1. The Redirect

Steer the conversation to what you actually want to talk about.

Press conference: "The real issue is..." → Real life: "What I really want to discuss is..."

↓ Tap for real-life example

Boss: "We don't have budget for raises right now."

You: "I understand, and what I really want to discuss is my role going forward. I have been leading the client accounts for six months now, and I think it is worth talking about what that role looks like long term."

You did not argue about the budget. You redirected to your value and future.

2. The Empathy Shield

Show you understand their position before making your point.

Press conference: "I completely understand the concern..." → Real life: "I know this is not easy to hear..."

↓ Tap for real-life example

Situation: Telling a friend you cannot lend them money.

You: "I know you are in a tough spot right now, and I really wish I could help. Unfortunately, I am not in a position to lend money at the moment. What I can do is help you look at other options."

Empathy first. Then the "no." Then an alternative. The friendship survives.

3. The Vague Commitment

Sound positive and willing without locking yourself in.

Press conference: "We are actively exploring..." → Real life: "I am definitely open to..."

↓ Tap for real-life example

Colleague: "Can you take over the Friday meetings permanently?"

You: "I am definitely open to helping out. Let me look at my schedule and see what is realistic. Can we revisit this next week?"

You did not say yes. You did not say no. You bought time and stayed positive.

4. The Context Bomb

Add information that changes how your position looks.

Press conference: "What has not been reported..." → Real life: "What you might not know is..."

↓ Tap for real-life example

Landlord: "The rent is going up 15% next month."

You: "I understand costs rise, and what you might not know is that I have been a reliable tenant for four years with zero missed payments. I have also handled several repairs myself at my own expense. I think that is worth considering."

The context bomb. Suddenly the landlord sees you differently.

5. The Bridge

Agree with something small, then cross to your real point.

Press conference: "That is a fair point, and..." → Real life: "You are right about that, and what I would add is..."

↓ Tap for real-life example

Partner: "You never help with the housework."

You: "You are right that I have not been doing enough recently, and I think the reason is that my hours have been insane this month. What I would like to do is sit down and divide things more fairly now that things are calming down."

You agreed (bridge). You gave context (why). You proposed a solution (pivot). No defensiveness.

6. The Non-Answer

When you genuinely cannot or should not answer directly.

Press conference: "I am not in a position to comment..." → Real life: "I would rather not get into that right now..."

↓ Tap for real-life example

Nosy relative: "So, how much do you earn now?"

You: "Ha! I would rather not get into specifics, but I can tell you things are going well and I am happy with where I am."

Polite deflection. Positive tone. No information given. No offence caused.

Four Conversations

Each one is uncomfortable. Each one requires strategy. Read the situation, think about which moves to use, then respond under time pressure.

Conversation 1: The Raise

You have been in your job for two years. You have taken on extra responsibilities, stayed late many times, and delivered strong results. Your salary has not changed. You have a meeting with your manager in 5 minutes.

You have 60 seconds to make your case. Be strategic, not emotional.

Conversation 2: The Complaint

You bought a product online. It arrived damaged. You called customer service and they said the return window has passed. You know this is unfair because the item was damaged on arrival. You are now speaking to a supervisor.

You have 60 seconds. Be firm but controlled. Getting angry will not help.

Empathy Shield Context Bomb Vague Commitment

Start with empathy ("I understand you have policies..."). Then drop the context bomb ("The item arrived damaged — this is not a change of mind, this is a faulty product"). Push for a vague commitment from them ("What options can you offer me?").

Conversation 3: The Bad News

A close friend has asked you to be the best man / maid of honour at their wedding. You genuinely cannot do it — the date clashes with something you cannot move. You need to tell them without damaging the friendship.

You have 60 seconds. The friendship matters more than being right.

Empathy Shield Bridge Redirect

Lead with how honoured you feel (empathy). Bridge to the reality ("and that is exactly why this is so hard to say..."). Redirect to how you will still be involved ("What I would love to do instead is...").

Conversation 4: The Refusal

Your manager asks you to work this weekend. Again. You have already worked three weekends in a row. You need to say no, but you do not want to seem uncommitted or lazy.

You have 60 seconds. Say no without burning bridges.
