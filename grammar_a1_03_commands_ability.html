Listen in on this conversation

Tourist: Excuse me — how do I get to the pharmacy from here?

Local: Easy. Walk straight down this street, then turn left at the traffic lights. Don't cross at the market — use the crossing by the bank, it's safer.

Look again at what the local says. There's no "you" anywhere — not "you walk", not "you turn". Why do you think instructions in English drop the subject? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
Because the listener obviously knows who's being told — it's always "you". So English drops it and starts straight with the verb: Walk, turn, don't cross. That's the imperative — the form we use for directions, instructions, warnings and everyday encouragement.

Check the pattern CLICK
Every sentence starts with the base verb (turn, take, mix) — no subject like "you", and no "to". That's it. One word, straight into the instruction.
Verb (base form) + rest of the sentence — no subject, no "to"

From explaining to instructing

"You need to cross the road at the lights."
→
"Cross the road at the lights."

The extra words ("you need to") disappear. Just the verb, then the instruction.

Don't Do That! Warnings

For telling someone NOT to do something — warnings, rules, safety.

Spot the pattern

Three warnings you might hear:

  • "Don't touch the paint — it's wet."
  • "Don't walk on the grass."
  • "Don't forget your umbrella — it looks like rain."

What's added to the front of the verb this time? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Don't (short for do not) + the base verb. The verb itself never changes — no "-ing", no "-s", no "to".
Don't (Do not) + verb (base form) + rest of the sentence

From positive to negative

"Cross here."
→
"Don't cross here — it's dangerous."

Just add "don't" in front. The verb after it stays exactly the same — base form.

Being Polite: "Please Wait Here" / "Don't Worry!"

For softening an instruction, or reassuring someone.

Spot the pattern

  • "Please take a seat — the doctor won't be long."
  • "Come in, please, and make yourself comfortable."
  • "Don't worry — everything will be fine."

Where can "please" go? And what does the last sentence do that's a bit different from a warning?

Check the pattern CLICK
Please can go at the front ("Please take a seat") or the end, with a comma ("Come in, please"). It doesn't change the verb at all. And "Don't worry" uses the same negative imperative pattern, but instead of a warning it's used to reassure someone — one of the most common phrases in everyday English.
Please + verb ... / ... verb, please.
Don't worry — (for reassuring, not warning)

Adding "please" to soften an instruction

"Wait here."
→
"Please wait here." / "Wait here, please."
