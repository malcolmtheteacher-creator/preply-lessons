One hour, a thousand digits

In a large hall, a competitor sits in front of a sheet of paper covered in rows of random digits. There are no words, no names, no dates: just 7, 3, 9, 1, 0, 4, 2, and thousands more. The rule is simple. You have one hour to learn as many as you can, and then you must write them back in order.

This is one of the long events at a memory championship. The best competitors in the world learn thousands of digits in an hour, and then recall them with only a few mistakes. For this event, one picture for every two digits is too slow. They need a picture for every three.

What is the task in the hour event?
Learn as many random digits as possible in an hour, then write them back in order.
Talk about it. How many digits do you think a person could learn in an hour? What would the limit be?

A British memory athlete, Ben Pridmore, who won the world title several times in the 2000s, is the person after whom a three-digit system is named. The idea is natural: if two digits can be one picture, three can be one picture too. The cost is size: instead of 100 pictures, you need a thousand, one for every number from 000 to 999.

To build a thousand pictures you need a rule that makes them quickly. The rule is the syllable. A syllable has a start, a middle and an end. Three digits: three sounds. That is the key to the whole system.

How many pictures does a three-digit system need?
A thousand: one for each of 000 to 999.
Talk about it. Would you be willing to learn a thousand pictures to be able to memorise faster? What would you use it for?

The cost is not as high as it sounds. You will not build the thousand in a week. Most people start with a hundred and add a hundred at a time. And the pictures are not random: they come from a rule, and the rule gives you a word or a name or a sound-alike for every number.

In this room you learn the rule. The first digit gives a consonant, the second a vowel, the third another consonant, and the three together give a syllable. A syllable is a word, or close enough to one to make a picture.

What does the rule give you for each three-digit number?
A syllable: a consonant, a vowel and a consonant, which gives a word or something close to one.
Talk about it. How would you make a picture for a syllable that is not a real word?

When the syllable is not a word
