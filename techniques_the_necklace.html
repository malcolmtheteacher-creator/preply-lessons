SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B2/C1 · 50 min

The Necklace

Guy de Maupassant (1884) — a borrowed jewel, a single careless night, and ten years to pay for it

Read in three parts

Maupassant tells the whole of a wasted decade in a few pages. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The last line is one of the most famous endings in European fiction — do not skip ahead to it.

Part 1 — A woman born for a life she does not have

She was one of those pretty, charming young women who are born, as if by an error of fate, into a family of clerks. She had no dowry, no expectations, no means of being known, understood, loved or married by any rich and distinguished man; and so she let herself be married to a minor clerk at the Ministry of Education.

She dressed plainly because she could not afford fine clothes, but she was as unhappy as if she had truly fallen from a higher station; for women have neither caste nor rank — their beauty, their grace and their charm serve them in place of birth and family. She suffered endlessly, feeling herself born for every delicacy and every luxury. She suffered from the poverty of her rooms, from the worn chairs, from the ugliness of the curtains. All these things, which another woman of her station would not even have noticed, tortured her and made her angry.

One evening her husband came home with an air of triumph, holding a large envelope.

"Look," he said, "here is something for you."

It was an invitation to a reception at the Ministry — a grand evening that every clerk longed to attend. But instead of being delighted, she threw the invitation onto the table and murmured impatiently:

"What do you want me to do with that? I have nothing to wear, and so I cannot go to this party. Give the card to some colleague whose wife has a better wardrobe than I have."

He had not thought of that. He stammered, and at last, to buy a suitable dress, he gave up the four hundred francs he had been saving to buy himself a gun. Yet as the day of the ball drew near, Madame Loisel seemed anxious and troubled, though the dress was ready.

"It annoys me," she said, "that I have not a single jewel, not a single stone, nothing to put on. I shall look like a pauper. I would almost rather not go at all."

Then her husband had an idea: she should go to her old school friend, Madame Forestier, who was rich, and ask to borrow some jewels. The next day Madame Loisel went, and Madame Forestier brought out her jewel-box and told her to choose. Among the treasures she found a superb necklace of diamonds, and her heart began to beat with desire. She asked, in a hesitating, anxious voice:

"Could you lend me this — only this?"

"Why, yes, certainly."

Pause and discuss:
  • Maupassant says Mathilde suffers from things "another woman would not even have noticed." Is her unhappiness caused by poverty, or by something inside her?
  • Her husband sacrifices his gun savings without a word. What kind of marriage does that small detail reveal?
  • She borrows only the necklace, the most dazzling thing in the box. What does that single choice tell us about her?

Part 2 — The triumph, and the loss

The night of the ball arrived. Madame Loisel was a triumph. She was prettier than any other woman present, elegant, graceful, smiling and wild with joy. All the men looked at her, asked her name, sought to be introduced. She danced with delight, intoxicated by pleasure, thinking of nothing but the admiration she was arousing, the completeness of a victory so dear to a woman's heart.

She left at about four in the morning. Her husband threw over her shoulders the modest wraps of common life, whose poverty clashed with the elegance of the ball dress. She felt it, and longed to escape, so as not to be noticed by the other women who were wrapping themselves in costly furs. They hurried out into the cold of the early morning and at last found a shabby old cab.

It took them to their door, and sadly they climbed the stairs. For her, it was finished. And for him, he was thinking of the office at ten o'clock. She removed the wraps before the glass, so as to see herself once more in all her glory. But suddenly she gave a cry. She no longer had the necklace around her neck.

"What is the matter?" her husband asked.

She turned towards him, panic-stricken.

"I have — I have — I no longer have Madame Forestier's necklace."

He searched the folds of her dress, the folds of her cloak, her pockets, everywhere. He went out again to retrace their steps on foot, while she sat waiting on a chair, without strength to go to bed, in a chill room, without thought. Towards seven o'clock he came back. He had found nothing. He went to the police, to the cab companies, offered a reward; he did everything. She waited all day in the same state of stupefied terror before this frightful disaster.

At the end of a week they had lost all hope. And Loisel, who had aged five years, declared:

"We must consider how to replace the jewel."

They went from jeweller to jeweller, searching for a necklace like the other, consulting their memory, both sick with grief and anguish. At last, in a shop in the Palais-Royal, they found a string of diamonds that seemed exactly like the one they had lost. It was worth forty thousand francs. They could have it for thirty-six.

Loisel possessed eighteen thousand francs which his father had left him. He would borrow the rest. He borrowed, asking a thousand francs from one, five hundred from another, signing notes, entering into ruinous agreements, dealing with usurers, with the whole race of money-lenders. He compromised the rest of his life, risked his signature without even knowing whether he could honour it; and, frightened by the anguish yet to come, by the black misery about to settle upon him, he went to buy the new necklace, laying down upon the jeweller's counter thirty-six thousand francs.

When Madame Loisel took the necklace back, Madame Forestier said, in a frosty tone:

"You should have returned it sooner; I might have needed it."

She did not open the case, as her friend had so much feared she would. If she had noticed the substitution, what would she have thought? What would she have said? Might she not have taken Madame Loisel for a thief?

Pause and discuss:
  • At the ball Mathilde is wild with joy; hours later, the disaster begins. Why does Maupassant place her highest moment right next to her ruin?
  • The couple decide to replace the necklace in secret rather than confess to Madame Forestier. Was that pride, fear, or honour? What would you have done?
  • Loisel signs ruinous notes "without even knowing whether he could honour it." How does that one decision rewrite the rest of his life?

Part 3 — Ten years, and one sentence

Madame Loisel now came to know the horrible life of the poor. She took her part of it, moreover, all at once, heroically. That dreadful debt must be paid. She would pay it. They dismissed their servant; they changed their rooms; they took a garret under the roof.

She came to know the heavy work of the household, the odious labours of the kitchen. She washed the dishes, wearing down her pink nails on the greasy pots and the bottoms of the pans. She washed the dirty linen, the shirts and the dish-cloths, which she hung to dry on a line; she carried the slops down to the street every morning, and carried up the water, stopping at each landing to breathe. And, dressed like a woman of the people, she went to the grocer's, the butcher's, the fruiterer's, her basket on her arm, bargaining, insulted, defending her miserable money sou by sou.

Each month they had to pay off some notes, renew others, gain time. Her husband worked in the evenings, keeping a tradesman's books, and at night he often did copying at five sous a page. And this life lasted ten years. At the end of ten years they had paid off everything, everything, with the rates of usury and the accumulations of compound interest.

Madame Loisel looked old now. She had become the woman of impoverished households — strong and hard and rough. With her hair badly dressed, her skirts awry, her hands red, she spoke in a loud tone, and washed the floors with great swishes of water. But sometimes, when her husband was at the office, she sat down near the window and thought of that evening long ago, of that ball, where she had been so beautiful and so admired.

What would have happened if she had not lost that necklace? Who knows? Who knows? How strange and changeful is life! How small a thing is needed to make or ruin us!

Then one Sunday, having gone for a walk to refresh herself after the labours of the week, she suddenly saw a woman leading a child by the hand. It was Madame Forestier, still young, still beautiful, still charming. Madame Loisel felt moved. Should she speak to her? Yes, certainly. And now that she had paid, she would tell her all. Why not?

She went up.

"Good morning, Jeanne."

The other, astonished to be addressed so familiarly by this common woman, did not recognise her at all, and stammered:

"But — madame! — I do not know — you must be mistaken."

"No. I am Mathilde Loisel."

Her friend uttered a cry.

"Oh, my poor Mathilde! How you have changed!"

"Yes, I have had hard days since I last saw you, and many sorrows — and all on your account."

"On my account! How is that?"

"You remember that diamond necklace you lent me to wear at the Ministry ball? Well, I lost it. And now for ten years we have been paying for it. You can understand that it was not easy for us, who had nothing. At last it is ended, and I am very glad."

Madame Forestier had stopped.

"You say that you bought a necklace of diamonds to replace mine?"

"Yes. You never noticed it, then? They were very similar."

And she smiled with a joy that was at once proud and innocent. Madame Forestier, deeply moved, took both her hands.

"Oh, my poor Mathilde! But mine was paste. It was worth at most five hundred francs!"

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • Read the last line again. What does it do to the ten years we have just lived through with the Loisels?
  • If the couple had simply confessed the loss that first week, what would have changed? Why couldn't they?
  • Maupassant stops the story dead on the reveal — we never hear Mathilde's reply. Why does he refuse to give it to us?
  • Your turn: Tell me about a time you paid a heavy price for something — only to discover later that the thing was never worth what you thought. What did you learn from it?

The narrator's irony — how Maupassant lets us judge without telling us to

Maupassant rarely condemns Mathilde directly. Instead he reports her feelings in a flat, even sympathetic tone — and trusts the facts to expose the irony. Watch how the same calm voice can pity her and quietly indict her at once.

WhereWhat the narrator doesThe irony it produces
The opening portraitStates her suffering as plain fact: "She suffered endlessly."The grand language of tragedy is spent on worn curtains and ugly chairs — we feel the gap between her pain and its cause.
The borrowed necklaceReports her delight neutrally as she chooses the diamonds.We already sense the danger she cannot; her triumph is quietly framed as a trap.
The closing lineGives the reveal to Madame Forestier and then stops, saying nothing himself.The silence is the verdict — the narrator never moralises, yet the whole decade is judged in a sentence.

The grammar this story teaches itself

This is a story made of regret. So it hands us the perfect grammar for regret: wishes about the past (I wish / if only + past perfect) and the third conditional (if + past perfect, would have + past participle) — the language we use to talk about what did not happen, but might have.

1. The third conditional — the whole story in one pattern

Maupassant himself almost writes one: "What would have happened if she had not lost that necklace?"

Pattern: If + past perfect, would have + past participle. (Counterfactual past — talking about something that did not happen.)

1. If Mathilde (not lose) the necklace, she (not spend) ten years in poverty.

2. If she (confess) the loss at once, Madame Forestier (tell) her the truth about the stones.

3. If the diamonds (be) real, the couple's sacrifice (make) sense.

4. If Loisel (not borrow) from the usurers, they (never replace) the jewel.

5. If I (be) in Mathilde's place, I (admit) the loss immediately.

1. had not lost / would not have spent

2. had confessed / would have told

3. had been / would have made

4. had not borrowed / would never have replaced

5. had been / would have admitted

2. Wishes & regrets — I wish / if only + past perfect

To express regret about a finished past action, we use wish or if only + the past perfect. It means: I am sorry this happened (or did not happen) — but it is too late to change it.

1. Mathilde wishes she (not borrow) the necklace at all.

2. If only they (tell) Madame Forestier the truth that first week!

3. Loisel wishes he (not sign) those ruinous notes.

4. Looking back, Mathilde wishes she (be) content with her quiet life.

5. If only she (check) the case before buying the replacement!

1. had not borrowed

2. had told

3. had not signed

4. had been

5. had checked

Complete each sentence so it makes sense. More than one structure can be correct — say your version aloud and check it against the key.

What Maupassant does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are the reason this story has survived since 1884.

1. Situational irony — the engine of the whole tale

Everything the Loisels do is reasonable, even honourable — and every step takes them further from the truth. They sacrifice a decade to repay a debt that did not exist. The gap between their belief and the reality ironises the entire story: their virtue is real, but it is spent on an illusion.

2. Foreshadowing — the clues hidden in plain sight

The signs are all there. Madame Forestier hands over the necklace with careless ease, "Why, yes, certainly." She never opens the case when it is returned. The jewels are kept loose in an ordinary box, not a safe. Maupassant plants the truth early and foreshadows the reveal — on a second reading, the ending was always visible.

3. The reversal — one line that rewrites everything

The closing sentence — "But mine was paste. It was worth at most five hundred francs!" — reframes and recontextualises the whole text. A tale of noble sacrifice becomes a tale of catastrophic waste. The story pivots on a single revealed fact, and every scene before it changes meaning.

4. Compression of time — ten years in a few lines

Maupassant lingers on a single evening at the ball, then compresses and distils ten years of grinding poverty into a handful of sentences. The speed is the point: a decade of a life vanishes as quickly as the night that caused it, and we feel how cheaply time can be thrown away.

Three speaking tasks

Task A · Vanity and the pressure to keep up (4 minutes)

Mathilde is ruined less by poverty than by the need to appear richer than she is. Think of a moment — your own or one you have watched — when someone spent money, energy or pride they did not really have, simply to keep up appearances: a wedding, a car, a holiday, a job title, a school.

Describe what they wanted others to see, what it actually cost, and whether it was worth it. Use at least one third conditional ("If they had not…") and at least three effect verbs from the Technique tab (ironises, reframes, recasts, distils…).

Task B · A small mistake, an enormous price (3 minutes)

Maupassant's whole story turns on one careless night and a single lost object. Tell me about a small slip — a forgotten email, a missed signature, a wrong turn, a careless word — that grew into consequences far larger than it deserved.

Use wishes and regrets ("I wish I had…", "If only we had…") to describe what you would change, then one third conditional to imagine how differently it might have gone.

Task C · Honesty versus pride (4 minutes)

The Loisels could have confessed the loss in the first week. Instead, pride and fear sent them into ten years of debt. Argue this out: when is it right to admit a mistake at once, whatever the embarrassment — and when, if ever, is it wiser to quietly fix it yourself and say nothing?

Take two minutes for each side. Use cleft sentences ("What they should have done is…", "It was their pride that…") and at least one modal of obligation ("they ought to have…", "she should have…").

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. Maupassant asks: "How small a thing is needed to make or ruin us!" Is that true of your own life — has a tiny event ever decided something large?
  2. Who do you blame for the ten lost years: Mathilde, her husband, Madame Forestier, or the society that made a borrowed necklace feel necessary?
  3. The reveal comes only after the debt is fully paid. Would the ending be crueller if Madame Forestier had spoken ten years earlier — or kinder?
  4. If you could write Mathilde a single sentence to read on the morning after the ball, what would it say?
