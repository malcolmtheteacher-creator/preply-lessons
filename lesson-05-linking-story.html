The singers who never learned their songs

In the 1930s a young American scholar of Greek, Milman Parry, travelled through the villages and coffee-houses of what was then Yugoslavia, with his assistant Albert Lord and a recording machine. He was looking for something he could not find in a library. He wanted to hear how an epic poem is actually performed by someone who cannot read.

Wherever they went, they found singers, usually men, who sat with a one-stringed instrument called a gusle and sang stories of battles and heroes. Some of these songs were thousands of lines long, and they went on for hours.

What did Parry want to find out?
How a long epic poem is performed and kept by singers who cannot read.
Talk about it. Could you sing or tell a story that lasts for hours? What would you need to remember it?

Parry and Lord asked the same question again and again: how do you remember so much? The answers surprised them. The singers did not learn the songs word for word. They did not even think they sang the same song twice. Each time, they built the song again as they sang, using a store of stock phrases, familiar scenes (the hero arming himself, the messenger riding out, the feast) and, above all, a strong sense of the story's shape.

The story was the memory. If you know what happens next, you can find the words. If a singer lost his place, he did not stop: he knew the next event and moved on to it.

How did the singers keep such long songs in mind?
They did not memorise the words. They remembered the story and its familiar scenes, and rebuilt the song as they sang.
Talk about it. Why do you think a story is easier to remember than a list? Give an example from your own life.

Parry and Lord's work changed how scholars think about the poems of Homer, which may have been made in a similar way. For us the lesson is practical. You can use the same power for any list. When you have no palace, or no time, or only a few things to remember, make the items into a story in which each one leads to the next.

This is the link method. It is not as strong as a palace, because if one link breaks the chain can break, but it is quick, it needs no building, and it works anywhere. It is the second great tool in your kit, and in the next lessons you will start to combine it with the others.

Why is the link method useful?
It is quick, needs no place, and works for any list, because each item leads to the next in a story.
Talk about it. Tell a short story that connects what you did this morning, step by step. Is it easier than just listing the steps?

Chains and stories

A link is a picture in which two items act on each other. A chain is a row of links. A story is a chain that makes sense of itself.

Item 1 acts on item 2, item 2 on item 3, and so on.
  1. Make a picture for each item. Use what you learned in lesson 2. Substitute if it is not a thing.
  2. Link item 1 to item 2. Let them act on each other: the first one hits, rides, eats, drowns or melts the second.
  3. Link item 2 to item 3. Now item 2 is the active one. Keep the action visible.
  4. Go on to the end. Each link only has to hold two pictures. Never try to link item 1 to item 5.
  5. Retell it. Start with the first picture. It will bring the second, and so on.
A linked story of five items
Items: apple → bicycle → elephant → telephone → volcano.
1 → 2A giant red apple rolls downhill, as big as a bicycle wheel, and you chase it on your bicycle.
2 → 3An elephant jumps in front of you, grabs your bicycle and pedals away.
3 → 4The elephant picks up an enormous telephone and trumpets into it.
4 → 5The telephone melts and drips into a volcano, which erupts.
The picture: An apple rolls; you chase it on a bicycle; an elephant steals the bicycle; it trumpets into a telephone; the telephone melts into a volcano.

Two links

Link orange → bicycle → cloud. Write what happens between the orange and the bicycle, and between the bicycle and the cloud.
An orange the size of a beach ball is rolling along the road. It jumps up and lands in the basket of your bicycle, making the front wheel lift. You pedal so hard that the bicycle takes off and rides into a huge pink cloud, leaving a trail of sparkling juice.

A story of four

Make one linked story with these four items, in order: hammer → penguin → pyramid → star.
You swing a golden hammer and a penguin pops out of the floor, waddling and flapping. The penguin dashes to a pyramid and hits it with its beak. The whole pyramid bursts into dust and a star rises out of the dust, singing.
