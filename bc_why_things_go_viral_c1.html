Why Things Go Viral

Every day, roughly 95 million photos and videos are posted to Instagram, another 34 million TikToks hit the platform, and over half a billion tweets arrive on X. Almost all of them vanish without a trace. A tiny fraction — perhaps one in a hundred thousand — go viral, racking up millions of views in a matter of hours. What separates the clips that disappear from the ones that ricochet around the planet? It turns out the answer is far less about luck than most people assume.

The first thing to understand is that "going viral" is not really about the video itself. It is about the invisible machinery working underneath it. Every major platform — TikTok, Instagram, YouTube, X — is run by algorithms that decide, second by second, who sees what. These algorithms are optimised for one thing above all: keeping you scrolling. If a post holds your attention, the algorithm amplifies it to more people. If those people also linger, it amplifies it further. Within hours, a clip that started in a teenager's bedroom can be in front of ten million strangers. The video didn't "catch fire" — the algorithm caught it and threw fuel on it.

But algorithms do not work in a vacuum. They respond to what humans respond to, and humans are not the rational decision-makers we like to imagine. Researchers at Yale studied half a million tweets and found that posts containing words such as "attack", "blame" or "bad" spread roughly 20% faster than neutral ones. Anger is unusually contagious online — one of the purest examples of what psychologists call emotional contagion, the tendency for feelings to leap between people without anyone noticing. A post that sparks outrage is not just read; it is reacted to, quoted, argued about. Every argument is more engagement, and more engagement is more amplification.

Not all viral content is angry, though. The second great driver of virality is surprise. The neuroscientist Wolfram Schultz showed decades ago that the human brain releases dopamine not when a reward arrives, but when a reward arrives unexpectedly. Unexpected content — a cat doing something impossible, a stranger performing an act of unexpected kindness, a twist ending — hits the brain's prediction system in a way that ordinary content cannot. We feel compelled to share it, as if passing along a small gift.

A third ingredient is identity. People share what signals something about who they are. A well-timed meme functions as a cultural touchstone — a shorthand that tells your followers which group you belong to, what you find funny, what you care about. In a sense, sharing is not really about the content at all; it is about the sharer. That is why the same joke can feel mandatory to share on a Tuesday evening and completely stale by Thursday morning. The signal is only valuable while it is fresh.

All of this plays out inside what economists now call the attention economy. The phrase, coined in 1997 by the scholar Michael Goldhaber, rests on a simple argument: as information becomes effectively infinite, the one thing that remains scarce is human attention. Every platform is therefore locked in a battle for seconds of your focus. This is why your feed feels engineered to be impossible to put down — because it is. The designers are not accidentally addictive; they have teams of engineers and psychologists whose literal job is to maximise time on site.

The implications are uncomfortable. Cognitive biases that once operated at a village scale now operate at the scale of billions. A rumour that would have died at a dinner table can circle the earth before breakfast. Content that triggers outrage, surprise or belonging is systematically amplified over content that does not — even when the outraging content is false and the quiet content is true. Studies from MIT have shown that false news stories on X spread, on average, six times faster than accurate ones.

Understanding virality is not the same as being able to produce it on demand — the people claiming to have "cracked the code" are usually selling a course. But it does change how you read your own feed. The video you saw today wasn't neutral. Something put it in front of you. That something was probably designed by people who know exactly which of your psychological buttons they were pressing. Scrolling is never just scrolling.
