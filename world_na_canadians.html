The stereotype: "Canadians are extremely polite and say sorry for everything." Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Sorry, eh?

Jokes about Canadians saying "sorry" are everywhere — Canadians tell them about themselves too. Linguists point out that "sorry" in Canadian English often means "excuse me" or "that's unfortunate", not "it was my fault". It works as a small social lubricant: a way of keeping things smooth in a crowded shop or on a bus.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What can "sorry" mean in Canada?

2. What does it help with?

1. "Excuse me" or "that's unfortunate".

2. Keeping social situations smooth.

Discuss:
  • Does your language have a word like this?

Part 2 — A law about sorry

In 2009 the province of Ontario passed an Apology Act, and several other provinces have similar laws. They say that an apology — for example, after a car accident — cannot automatically be used in court as an admission of liability. The idea is that people should be able to say sorry, which is more human, without being afraid of being sued. It's a good example of a stereotype with a real, if small, kernel of truth.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Ontario pass in 2009?

2. What does the law say?

1. An Apology Act.

2. An apology isn't automatically an admission of liability.

Discuss:
  • Should saying sorry have legal effects?

Part 3 — Polite — but not always

Canadians are not more polite than everyone else in every situation. They are among the most passionate fans of ice hockey, a fast and very physical sport, and Canadian political debates can be fierce. Canadian politeness is perhaps strongest in public, where many people try harder not to stand out than they do in private. Canadians also often describe themselves as more modest and self-deprecating than Americans.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What sport shows the less polite side?

2. How do Canadians often see themselves compared with Americans?

1. Ice hockey.

2. More modest and self-deprecating.

Discuss:
  • Are people more polite in public or private?

Grammar — Comparatives & Superlatives

Cultural differences are comparisons. To compare fairly and precisely, English uses comparatives and superlatives — with modifiers.

How it works:
• -er / more … than: "Greetings are warmer in the south."
• (not) as … as: "Finns are not as touchy as Italians."
• modifiers: much, far, a bit, slightly + comparative.

Going deeper

The stereotype grows from real habits (frequent "sorry", queueing, quiet public manners) and from Canada's wish to be seen as different from the USA. A friendly, modest image is part of Canada's national branding — and Canadians themselves enjoy the joke, which keeps it alive.

Worth noticing: Use "not as… as" to deny equality and "much/far/a bit + comparative" to show degree.
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Don't take "sorry" literally. In Canada, a "sorry" in a crowded place is a friendly signal, not an admission. Be modest about your own achievements, queue patiently, and never call a Canadian "American" — politeness has limits!
