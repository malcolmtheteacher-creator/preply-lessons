The Delivery Gone Wrong

Your parcel says "delivered". It says "left in a safe place". There is no safe place, and there is no parcel — and the birthday is Saturday. Time to make a phone call.

The Scene 🎬

The email arrived at 2:14 on Tuesday, cheerful as anything: Great news — your parcel has been delivered! Underneath it, a blurry photograph of a doorway that might be yours, might be anyone's, and a line of text that has quietly ruined a lot of afternoons: Left in a safe place.

There is no safe place. You live on the third floor of a block of flats. There is no porch, no shed, no bin store, no helpful neighbour who takes things in. There is a communal front door and a corridor. And there is no parcel — not on Tuesday, not on Wednesday, not now.

Inside it is a birthday present. It needs to exist, wrapped, in your hands, by Saturday. So you find the number that the website has hidden three menus deep, you listen to four minutes of a piano loop, and eventually a human answers.

Her name is Ellis. She is polite, patient, and reading from a screen. She will offer you a ten-pound voucher. She will offer you an investigation lasting up to ten working days. She will apologise, sincerely, roughly every ninety seconds. She will not, at any point, offer you the one thing you rang for. And here is the thing worth remembering as you begin: none of this is Ellis.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When something goes wrong with a company, are you a ring-them-immediately person or a put-it-off-for-a-week person? What does the delay actually cost you — and is that, perhaps, the point?
  2. Have you ever been rude to someone on a phone line who had no power to help you? Or been on the receiving end of it? What did it change?

Who's who

You
The One Who Needs It By Saturday 📦

Not angry — clear. You know exactly what you want and you're not leaving the call without it. You also know the woman on the phone didn't lose your parcel, and you're not about to make her pay for a system she didn't build.

The other voice
Ellis, the Agent 🎧

Eight pounds an hour, a headset, and a screen that tells her what she's allowed to say. She's kind, she's genuinely sorry, and she has almost no power — which is precisely why the script keeps offering you vouchers instead of parcels.

First: read the conversation in the next tab — and watch how it's done. Notice something strange: the temperature never rises, but the position never moves. Nobody shouts, nobody threatens, nobody is unkind. And the caller ends up with exactly what they asked for. That's the whole game.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Ellis says: "the driver has marked it as delivered — so from our side, it has been delivered." What is the caller actually being told by that sentence? And why do they not argue with it?

2. One question in this call changed everything — right after it, Ellis went and found her team leader. Which question was it, and why was it so hard for her to answer?

3. The caller refuses every single thing Ellis offers, and Ellis still ends up on their side. Name two things the caller said that made that possible.

1. They're being told that the record has replaced the reality: the parcel exists in the system, so as far as the company is concerned it exists in the world. The caller doesn't argue with it because arguing about the record is a fight you cannot win — Ellis can't delete what the driver typed. So instead of attacking the sentence, they simply repeat what they need, and leave Ellis holding the gap between the record and the truth.
2. "Can you give me a date rather than 'up to ten working days'?" It was hard because there is no date. The phrase only sounds like an answer; asked for a real day of the week, it collapses, and Ellis had to admit out loud that the thing she was offering was nothing. That admission is what sent her to her team leader.
3. Two of these: "None of this is your fault, I know" — which tells Ellis the caller isn't hunting her. "I appreciate that, but that doesn't really work for me" — which turns down the voucher without turning on her. "Thanks for actually helping" — which thanks her for the one real thing she did, not for the script. The position never moved; the aim was never on Ellis.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for holding your ground on a phone line without becoming somebody's worst call of the day. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Firmness is slow, quiet and repetitive. It has no adjectives in it and no exclamation marks. It sounds almost boring — the same sentence, the same tone, the fourth time as calmly as the first. Shouting is not the strong version of this; shouting is what people do when they've run out of position and only have volume left. If you have a position, you don't need volume. Drop your voice, slow right down, and be willing to say the identical thing again.

🎯 Starting straight

Phrase 1

You've waited four minutes for a human. You have about ten seconds before the script starts. Which opening gets a script-reader working for you fastest?

A. "Right, I've got a complaint."
B. "Let me give you the order number and exactly what's happened."
C. "You've lost my parcel."
B — "Let me give you the order number and exactly what's happened."
A announces a category and makes them reach for the complaints script. C is an accusation, so their first move is defence, not typing. B hands them the two things they physically need — the number to find you, the facts to act on — and it says "exactly", which promises this will be quick and organised. You sound like someone who will be easy to help and hard to lose.
Phrase 2

They've apologised. It's the third apology and the second minute. You want to lift the blame off the person while putting the problem firmly back on the table. Put the pieces in order:

but I do needNone of thisit sorted.is your fault,I know —
"None of this is your fault, I know — but I do need it sorted."
Two halves, and both matter. The first half disarms them completely: they spend all day braced for the blame, and you've just taken it away, so they can stop defending and start thinking. The second half arrives on "but", and "I do need" — that stressed do is the whole sentence's spine. "Sorted" is everyday British English for dealt with, finished, gone. Kind to the person, immovable on the problem: that's the entire lesson in one line.

📌 Naming what you want

Phrase 3

Here's the moment most people lose the call. They say "I want this sorted out" — and a vague ask gets a vague answer, every time. In your own words: name exactly what you want. Not a feeling. A thing, a place, a deadline. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "What I'd like is a replacement, here, by Saturday."
Count the specifics: a replacement (not a refund, not an investigation, not a voucher), here (my address, not a depot), by Saturday (a day with a name). Three commas, three facts, nine words. "What I'd like is…" is softer than "I want" but just as definite — it's a preference, calmly stated, and it's much harder to argue with a preference than a demand. And a specific ask has a lovely side-effect: when they say no, they have to say no to something real.
Phrase 4

They've offered you a ten-pound voucher as "a gesture of goodwill". It isn't what you rang for. Which reply keeps them helping you rather than closing the call?

A. "No, that's not good enough."
B. "I appreciate that, but that doesn't really work for me."
C. "Honestly, what am I supposed to do with a voucher?"
B — "I appreciate that, but that doesn't really work for me."
A is a bare "no", and a bare "no" gives them nothing to do except repeat themselves or dig in. C is sarcasm — it may feel good for one second and it costs you the person. B outperforms both because "I appreciate that" receives their offer properly before returning it, so they don't feel rejected as a human; and "doesn't really work for me" declines the offer without judging them. "Really" isn't hedging here — it makes it sound reasonable rather than dramatic. The kindest possible no is still, entirely, a no.

🧱 Not being fobbed off

Phrase 5

Now the hard one. They have repeated the ten-days line for the third time. You are going to say the exact same thing you said before — no louder, no new argument, no fresh complaint. The gap takes one word, and it has a precise job: it must tell them that you heard them, that nothing has changed, and that you are both back exactly where you were two minutes ago. Which word does all three?

"I understand. What I need is ___ a replacement here by Saturday."

"I understand. What I need is still a replacement here by Saturday."
That little word still is doing enormous work: it means "I heard you, nothing has changed, we are back where we were." This move has a name — the broken record. An old vinyl record with a scratch plays the same half-second forever, and that's the technique: you calmly repeat the same sentence every time they offer you something else. It sounds too simple to work. It works because a script has a limited number of moves and you have one, and yours doesn't run out.

And to be fobbed off is to be sent away with something small and useless instead of the thing you asked for — the voucher instead of the parcel. The broken record is how you refuse to be fobbed off without ever raising your voice.
Phrase 6

"Up to ten working days" sounds like an answer. It isn't — it's a fog. One word turns it back into a question they have to answer honestly. Complete it: "Can you give me a ___ rather than 'up to ten working days'?"

"Can you give me a date rather than 'up to ten working days'?"
Beautiful, quiet demolition. "Up to ten working days" is a range with no floor, so it commits to nothing at all — it could mean tomorrow, it could mean a fortnight, and that vagueness is the point of it. Asking for a date — a real day with a real name — forces them to either give you one, which you can hold them to, or admit there isn't one, which tells you the offer was empty. Either way you learn the truth, and you've asked politely enough that nobody can accuse you of anything.
Phrase 7

Their screen says your parcel is somewhere safe. Your building has nowhere safe. Name the gap between the record and the world — and hand the question back. Put the pieces in order:

There isn't one.It says it wasSo where is it?left in a safe place.
"It says it was left in a safe place. There isn't one. So where is it?"
Three short sentences, no anger, no adjectives. Notice the first word: "It says" — not "you said", not "your driver lied". You're quoting their record, not accusing their colleague, so there's nothing to defend. Then four flat words of fact. Then the question, which is unanswerable — and that's exactly why you ask it. You're not expecting an answer; you're making them hear, out loud, that the record and the world disagree. The silence after "So where is it?" is worth more than any speech.

🪜 Going up a level

Phrase 8

You've realised something: they aren't refusing you, they're unable. The screen won't let them. You need the next person up — without punishing this one. Which line does that?

A. "Let me speak to your manager."
B. "Are you actually able to do anything?"
C. "Is there someone who can authorise that?"
C — "Is there someone who can authorise that?"
A is the line every agent dreads, and it lands as a threat — you're going over their head to complain about them. B is simply cruel; you're mocking someone for the cage they work in. C asks about the system, not the person: it quietly says "I know this isn't within your power, so let's find whose power it's within." To authorise something is to have the permission to say yes to it. Half the people on those lines are desperate for you to ask this — it's the only door out of their script, and you've opened it for them.
Phrase 9

Something's finally been agreed. Don't say thank you and hang up — nail it down first, so the agreement survives the end of the call. Put the pieces in order:

and when?So just sowhat happens next,I'm clear —
"So just so I'm clear — what happens next, and when?"
"Just so I'm clear" is the politest possible way of saying "say it back to me" — you're framing it as your own need for clarity, not as distrust of them, so nobody bristles. Then the two questions that matter: what, and when. Making them say the plan out loud does two things — it catches any gap between what you agreed and what they typed, and it turns a vague good feeling into a commitment with a time on it.

🤝 Closing decently

Phrase 10

They went off script, found their team leader, and got you the thing. Now close: thank them for the real thing they did — not for the apologies, which cost nothing — and get yourself proof that the call happened. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Thanks for actually helping — can I take a reference for this call?"
"Thanks for actually helping" is specific praise, and specific praise is the only kind anyone believes. Everyone on that line apologises all day; almost nobody gets thanked for the one moment they went and fought for a stranger. It costs you nothing and it's the best thing that will happen to them this shift.

A reference is the code they read out that lets any future agent find this exact call and see what was promised. Asking for it isn't distrust of the person — it's how you make a promise survive the moment somebody else picks up the phone. Ask for it warmly, right at the end, and it never sounds like suspicion.

🔑 Make them yours

These phrases aren't really about parcels. They're for every counter, helpline and waiting room where someone kind explains, very patiently, why you can't have the thing you're owed. Pin three of them to your own life so you'll reach for them when it happens. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"What I'd like is a replacement, here, by Saturday." — think of something unresolved in your own life right now. Name the thing, the place, and the deadline.

"I understand. What I need is still…" — where have you accepted a small useless thing because saying it again felt rude?

"None of this is your fault, I know — but I do need it sorted." — who was the last person you took it out on who hadn't caused it?

The Twist

🚫 The thing you asked for doesn't exist

One more round — no script, no safety net. This time Ellis isn't stalling; she's telling you the truth. The system physically will not let her send a replacement on a parcel marked delivered. Not "won't" — can't. There is no button. The only thing that exists is a refund, and the refund takes three to five working days to reach your bank, which means it cannot possibly arrive before Saturday. So the exact thing you spent this whole lesson demanding is genuinely, provably impossible. You can repeat it as calmly as you like — it isn't coming. Start the timer and find a new specific ask: something real, something she or her team leader can actually do, that still gets a wrapped present into your hands by Saturday. Same rules as before — you may not shout, you may not sulk, and you may not take it out on Ellis. One rule: the moment you accept "we'll write to you with the outcome" and put the phone down, you've lost it.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I named exactly what I wanted — a thing, a place and a day — not just "sort it out".
  • I said the same thing again, calmly, at least twice, without a new argument each time.
  • I turned down what I didn't want without turning on the person offering it.
  • My voice never got louder, and my position never moved.
  • I left the call with a plan, a time, and a reference — and the human on the other end wasn't worse off for having taken my call.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Somewhere in a building you'll never see, a driver photographed a doorway and tapped a button, and your parcel became a fact. Not a real one — a recorded one. And once the record exists, the company you're arguing with isn't a company at all; it's a screen with a limited number of buttons, sitting in front of someone who isn't allowed to press the ones you need.

None of this is an accident. The four minutes of piano, the menu three levels deep, the "up to ten working days", the voucher offered instead of the thing — these aren't failures of the system. They are the system. Exhaustion is the policy. Most people give up somewhere around the second apology, and the numbers work out beautifully for everyone except them.

And the person holding all of it — the one with your parcel's history on her screen and eight pounds an hour in her pocket — has less power over your Saturday than you do. She takes the anger meant for a boardroom. Every day, all day, from people who have run out of position and only have volume left.

So: be immovable about the outcome, and be kind to the human. Not because kindness is a technique that works — though it does — but because the alternative is letting a company that designed your exhaustion also decide who you are on the phone. Hold your ground. Just don't do it standing on someone.

Two bigger questions

  1. If a company makes complaining slow and unpleasant on purpose, knowing most people will give up — is that clever business, or is it simply taking money from the people with the least time and patience to fight back? And who ends up paying for it?
  2. The person who answers the phone has no power, and the people with power never answer the phone. Does that arrangement suit companies a little too well — and what does it do to us, over a lifetime of being angry at people who couldn't have helped us anyway?
