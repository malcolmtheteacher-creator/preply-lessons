The Honest Review

She's asked how it was, and she means it — this place is her savings and her name. Something genuinely wasn't right. A one-star review at midnight is the coward's version; this is the other one.

The Scene 🎬

"Was everything alright for you?" — and she's actually waiting for the answer.

It's her place. Her name is above the door, the money is hers, and she has come over at the end of the meal because she wants to know, not because it's a script.

Most of it was very good. One thing wasn't, and it wasn't trivial. You have three options: lie and say it was lovely, say nothing and leave a review tonight where she can't reply, or tell her now — which is the only one that's any use to her and the only one that's uncomfortable.

The skill is doing it so she can hear it: proportion first, one specific thing, no diagnosis of why, and an ending that makes clear you're on her side.

  1. Have you ever left a bad review online? Would you have said the same thing to the owner's face?
  2. When something's wrong in a restaurant or shop, do you say so at the time? What decides it?
YouA regular who wants this place to survive. Prepared to say the awkward thing once, properly, and then leave it.
AggieThe owner. Genuinely asking. Will take it well if it's proportionate — and will remember it either way.
First: read the conversation and notice the order — she's offered a choice before anything is said, then the good comes before the bad, and the bad is one thing, said once. Nobody explains why it happened.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for telling someone their work wasn't right — to their face, kindly, once. Each works on a restaurant, a builder, a hairdresser, a colleague's draft. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Warm and matter-of-fact, at normal speed. The trap is dropping your voice into something grave, which turns one cold plate into a serious complaint and makes her defensive. You're doing her a favour, not delivering a verdict — sound like it. And say the critical sentence once. Repeating it is what turns feedback into a telling-off.

🚪 Getting permission first

Phrase 1

The job: letting them choose whether they want the truth. Which one?

A. "Yeah, lovely thanks — well, mostly."
B. "Do you want the polite version or the honest one?"
C. "Actually, since you ask, there was a problem."
B — "Do you want the polite version or the honest one?"
A is the passive-aggressive worst of both — a complaint she can hear but can't address. C launches straight in without checking she's in a state to receive it. B hands her the choice, which does two things: it warns her something's coming, and it means whatever follows was invited. Almost everybody picks honest, and having picked it they listen differently.

Take it anywhere: "Do you want the polite version or the honest one?" Works on a colleague's draft, a friend's business idea, a haircut.
Phrase 2

The job: explaining why you're saying it to her rather than online. Put the pieces in order:

than leave you guessing.I'd rather tell youto your face
"I'd rather tell you to your face than leave you guessing."
Names the alternative you've rejected, which reframes the whole thing: you're not complaining, you're choosing the harder and more respectful route. "Leave you guessing" is the phrase that lands with anyone who runs a business — the silent unhappy customer is the one they can do nothing about.

Take it anywhere: "I'd rather tell you to your face than ___."

⚖️ Getting the proportion right

Phrase 3

The job: establishing the ratio before the criticism, so she can size it correctly. Complete it:

"Most of it was lovely — one thing ___ ___ ___."

"Most of it was lovely — one thing let it down."
To let something down is to spoil an otherwise good whole. This sentence is the entire proportion in eight words: most good, one bad. Say it before the specific, because otherwise she hears the criticism first and everything after it sounds like consolation. And "one thing" quietly promises there's only one — so she can stop bracing for a list.

Take it anywhere: "Most of it was ___ — one thing let it down."
Phrase 4

The job: the specific itself — one fact, no adjectives, no story. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "The main course came out cold. Not lukewarm — properly cold in the middle."
The second sentence is the useful one. "Cold" is arguable and she may reasonably think you mean slightly cool; "properly cold in the middle" is precise and unarguable, and it's the level of detail she needs to work out what happened. Be exact once, then stop — precision is kind, elaboration is cruel.

Take it anywhere: name it, then sharpen it once. "Late — not by an hour, by three days."

🙅 What not to do

Phrase 5

The job: refusing to diagnose the cause — because you don't know and she does. Which version?

A. "I'd guess it had been sitting under the pass too long, was it?"
B. "I'm not telling you what went wrong in there — I just thought you'd want to know it happened."
C. "You probably need more staff on a Friday, if I'm honest."
B — "I'm not telling you what went wrong in there — I just thought you'd want to know it happened."
A and C are the commonest way good feedback goes wrong: the customer moves from reporting to advising, and the owner — who knows her own operation — stops listening and starts defending. B stays strictly in your own lane. You have information she doesn't: what it was like to receive. She has information you don't: why. Trade only in yours.

Take it anywhere: "I'm not telling you ___ — I just thought you'd want to know."
Phrase 6

The job: answering "why didn't you say at the time?" honestly, and taking a share of it. Complete it:

"Because you were slammed and I didn't want to be ___ ___. Which is probably my fault more than yours."

"Because you were slammed and I didn't want to be that person. Which is probably my fault more than yours."
Slammed is informal English for extremely busy — a kitchen, a shop, a shift. That person means the difficult customer nobody wants to be, and everyone recognises it instantly. The second sentence is what stops this being a criticism of her service: you're admitting the silence was yours, which is true and disarms the whole exchange.

Take it anywhere: "I didn't want to be that person." / "That's probably my fault more than yours."

💛 Landing it on the right side

Phrase 7

The job: confirming the scale when she asks — reassuring without retracting. Complete it:

"Just the one. Everything else was ___ ___ we come here."

"Just the one. Everything else was exactly why we come here."
She'll ask whether it was more than one thing — she's mentally checking whether tonight was a disaster. "Just the one" is a straight answer. The second half returns her to solid ground without softening what you said: you haven't withdrawn the criticism, you've put it back in proportion.

Take it anywhere: "Just the one. Everything else was exactly why ___."
Phrase 8

The job: stating your motive, so the whole thing is read correctly. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "I want this place to do brilliantly — that's the whole point."
Feedback without a stated motive gets assigned one, usually the worst available. Saying plainly that you want them to succeed reframes everything that came before it as investment rather than complaint. Say it at the end, not the start — at the start it sounds like a softener, at the end it sounds like the reason.

Take it anywhere: "I only say it because I want ___ to do well."

🤝 The two that keep you welcome

Phrase 9

The job: making clear this doesn't change your custom. Put the pieces in order:

and we'll keep coming.We've been comingfor two years
"We've been coming for two years and we'll keep coming."
Loyalty stated as a fact, with a number. This is what separates useful feedback from a complaint: a complainer is leaving, and their opinion is therefore worth less. Saying you'll be back tells her the information came from someone with a stake in the answer — which makes her far more likely to act on it.

Take it anywhere: "We've been coming for ___ and we'll keep coming."
Phrase 10

The job: accepting her instruction to speak up next time — briefly, and meaning it. Complete it:

"___. Next time I will."

"Noted. Next time I will."
Two short sentences. She has just told you to say something at the time, and the temptation is to explain again why you didn't — which reopens the awkward bit and keeps you both in it. "Noted" accepts the point without a paragraph. It also quietly agrees a future arrangement between you, which is a nice thing to leave a place with.

Take it anywhere: "Noted. Next time I will." Any time you're given fair advice you intend to take.

🔑 Make them yours

These work on anyone whose work you're commenting on — a tradesperson, a colleague, a friend who's cooked for you. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"Do you want the polite version or the honest one?" — where have you given the polite version and regretted it?

"Most of it was ___ — one thing let it down." — try it on something recent.

"I'm not telling you what went wrong — I just thought you'd want to know." — where have you given advice you weren't qualified to give?

⚡ The twist

Aggie takes it well, thanks you — and then says, quietly: "Can I ask you something? We've lost about a third of our regulars since March and nobody's told me why. Is there something else? Be honest, you're the only one who's said anything."

There is something else. It's bigger than a cold plate and you'd decided not to mention it. She's now asked directly, and she's clearly frightened. What do you say?

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you offer someone the choice between the polite and the honest version?
  • Can you give the proportion before the criticism?
  • Can you state one specific fact and then stop elaborating?
  • Can you resist explaining why you think it happened?
  • Can you finish so they know you're on their side?

The Echo 🕰️

Two swindlers tell an emperor they can weave a cloth invisible to anyone unfit for their post. There is no cloth. The emperor cannot see it and says nothing. His ministers cannot see it and praise it extravagantly. The whole court agrees, loudly, about a fabric that does not exist.

The procession goes ahead. Everyone lining the street sees a naked man and everyone cheers — until a child, who has not yet learned what it costs to be the one who says it, points out the obvious.

Aggie has been walking down that street for weeks. Everyone who had a bad meal smiled and said lovely, thank you, and never came back. Somebody has to be the child.
