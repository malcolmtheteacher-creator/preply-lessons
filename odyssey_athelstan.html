The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. In 927 Æthelstan was able to unite the English under one king for the first time.
to unite (v) — to join together into one.
2. He moved to annex the Viking kingdom of York after its ruler died.
to annex (v) — to take control of a territory and add it to your own.
3. The kings of Scotland and Wales acknowledged him as their overlord at a meeting near Penrith.
an overlord (n) — a ruler who has power over other rulers.
4. In 937 a coalition of Scots, Strathclyde Britons and Dublin Vikings invaded England.
a coalition (n) — an alliance of groups who join forces for a shared aim.
5. The battle of Brunanburh was a decisive English victory.
decisive (adj) — settling a matter clearly; very important to the result.
6. Each royal charter — a written grant of land — was signed with grand new titles.
a charter (n) — a formal written document granting rights or land.
7. His prestige in Europe was so great that foreign kings married his half-sisters.
prestige (n) — respect and admiration that comes from success or power.
8. Æthelstan was a keen collector of holy relics, such as the bones of saints.
a relic (n) — a part of a holy person's body or belongings, kept as an object of worship.
9. He was also keen to legislate, issuing law codes against theft.
to legislate (v) — to make laws.
10. Æthelstan died unmarried and childless, so his half-brother Edmund became king.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The crown of a new kind

Æthelstan was Alfred the Great's grandson and Edward the Elder's eldest son. He was raised partly in Mercia, at the court of his aunt Æthelflæd, and when Edward died in 924 the Mercians accepted him as king first; Wessex accepted him after a short delay. He was crowned at Kingston upon Thames in 925.

In 927 the Viking king of York died, and Æthelstan moved quickly to annex the north. For the first time, all the English were ruled by one king. At Eamont, near Penrith, the kings of the Scots and of Strathclyde accepted him as their overlord, and Welsh kings later agreed to pay him tribute.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Where was Æthelstan raised, and why does it matter?

2. What happened in 927?

1. Partly in Mercia at Æthelflæd's court — so the Mercians knew him and accepted him first.

2. He took control of the Viking kingdom of York, becoming king of all the English; northern kings accepted him as overlord.

Discuss:
  • Why might a king raised in one region find it easier to win that region's loyalty?

Part 2 — Brunanburh

Not everyone accepted this new power. In 937 Olaf Guthfrithson, the Viking king of Dublin, joined Constantine II of Scotland and the Britons of Strathclyde in a great invasion. Their aim was to destroy the new English kingdom.

At Brunanburh — a site still argued over, though many historians now favour Bromborough on the Wirral — Æthelstan and his half-brother Edmund won a decisive victory. A famous Old English poem in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle celebrates the slaughter, and later writers simply called it "the great battle".

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who formed the coalition against Æthelstan in 937?

2. Do we know exactly where Brunanburh was fought?

1. Olaf Guthfrithson of Dublin, Constantine II of Scotland, and the Britons of Strathclyde.

2. No — the site is debated, though Bromborough on the Wirral is a strong candidate.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think the location of such an important battle could be forgotten?

Part 3 — King of all Britain

Æthelstan's charters called him "king of the whole of Britain". His court welcomed foreign princes, and his half-sisters married into the greatest families of Europe, including the future Holy Roman Emperor Otto I. He issued law codes, and coins were struck with his name across the kingdom.

He was also deeply religious and collected relics. He never married, and when he died in 939 he was buried at Malmesbury Abbey. Today he is far less famous than Alfred, but many historians call him the first true king of England.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Æthelstan build links with Europe?

2. Why did Edmund succeed him?

1. Through his court and by marrying his half-sisters to European rulers, including Otto I.

2. Æthelstan died unmarried and childless; Edmund was his half-brother.

Discuss:
  • Æthelstan is called the first king of England, yet few people know his name. Why?

Grammar — The Passive Voice

Historians often care more about what was done than who did it — or they simply do not know who did it. That is the job of the passive.

How it works:
• Form: be + past participle: "The king was crowned at Westminster."
• Add by only when the doer matters: "The castle was built by Norman masons."
• Reporting passives hedge a legend: "He is said to have…", "It was believed that…"

1. Make it passive

Complete each sentence about Æthelstan. Use the word in brackets.

1. Æthelstan (crown) at Kingston upon Thames in 925.

2. The kingdom of York (annex) in 927.

3. The invaders (defeat) at Brunanburh in 937.

1. was crowned

2. was annexed

3. were defeated

2. Choose the right passive form

1. Brunanburh (believe / be) on the Wirral by many historians.

2. Coins (strike) with his name across England.

3. He (bury) at Malmesbury Abbey.

1. is believed to have been

2. were struck

3. was buried

Going deeper

Æthelstan turned a family project — Alfred's defence, Edward's conquest — into a single kingdom with a single law and coinage. His court was cosmopolitan: scholars, exiled princes and relics arrived from across Europe. But unity by conquest is fragile: within a few years of his death, York had slipped back into Viking hands.

Worth noticing: the passive is everywhere in history writing because we often care more about the action — a town annexed, a battle won — than the person who did it.
Learning from the past — for today: Unity needs systems, not just victories. Æthelstan's coins, laws and charters made "England" real in daily life. In any team or organisation, shared rules and shared tools hold people together long after the big moment has passed.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Is a country created by conquest truly united?
  • Why would sharing coins and laws make people feel like one nation?
  • Why do some great figures fall out of popular memory?
