A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson VII of L

The Last Day

In which the wisest man in Athens drinks poison rather than stop asking questions, and forces the world to decide what a human life is worth

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We arrive at one of the most pivotal moments in intellectual history. In 399 BC, the greatest philosopher Athens has ever known was tried, convicted, and executed. But Socrates did not apologize, did not beg for mercy, and did not run. Instead, he drank the hemlock poison with calm courage, leaving behind a question that philosophy has never stopped asking: what is a human life worth? This lesson explores not just his death, but why he chose it — and what his choice means for how we should live.

The Hook

A courtroom in Athens, 399 BC. Five hundred and one citizens sit on wooden benches. The greatest philosopher in history stands on trial for his life. He is accused of corrupting the youth and introducing new gods. And as the prosecution makes its case, Socrates does not apologize, does not beg for mercy, does not try to save himself. Instead, he tells the court that he is the greatest gift Athens has ever received. What kind of man walks into a courtroom and says that? What kind of man chooses death rather than silence?

II. The Story — A Courtroom in Athens

Athens in 399 BC was a city in turmoil. Seventy years earlier, the city had been the jewel of the Greek world. But then came the Peloponnesian War — a thirty-year conflict with Sparta that drained the city’s treasury, killed thousands of its young men, and ended with Athens defeated. The democracy that had produced Pericles and built the Parthenon now felt like it was tearing itself apart. In the chaos, people looked for someone to blame.

The Charges

In 399 BC, Socrates was brought to trial on two charges:

1. Corrupting the youth of Athens — teaching them to question authority, to reject the values of their elders

2. Impiety — not recognizing the gods of the state, and introducing new gods instead

The real charge, though, was not written in the indictment. The real charge was: you have made young men think for themselves, and thinking for themselves, they have questioned us. Some of Socrates’ most famous students — including Alcibiades, a brilliant but reckless general, and Critias, a ruthless oligarch — had scandalized Athens. The prosecution used them as evidence that Socrates had poisoned the minds of the young.

The Trial from Plato’s Apology

We know what happened in that courtroom because Plato was there. He recorded Socrates’ defense in a text called the Apology (from the Greek word apologia, meaning “defense”). What Plato preserved is extraordinary.

Socrates began his defense not by apologizing, but by attacking. He told the court:

How you have been affected by my accusers, men of Athens, I do not know; but I myself was almost made to forget who I was by them, so persuasively did they speak. And yet, to tell the truth, they have said nothing. But you will hear from me the truth. — Plato, Apology, 17a–b

Then he made a stunning claim. He said he was not the corrupt one. He was the gift Athens needed. He was, he said, the gadfly of Athens — the insect sent by the gods to sting the lazy horse and keep it awake.

If you kill me, you will not easily find another like me, who, if I may use a ludicrous comparison, am a sort of gadfly, sent by the gods to the state; and the state is a large thoroughbred horse which is rather slow in its motions owing to its size, and requires to be stirred into activity. — Plato, Apology, 30e–31a

Think about what he was doing. Rather than defending himself, he was redefining his role. He was not a corrupter. He was a doctor. Athens was sick — sick from power, from arrogance, from the loss of the war — and Socrates was the medicine it needed, even if the medicine stung.

The Verdict: Guilty

The jury voted. Out of 501 citizens, 280 voted guilty and 221 voted not guilty. The margin was narrow but decisive. Socrates was convicted.

The Second Vote: Under Athenian law, after conviction, the prosecution and the defense each proposed a penalty. The jury voted again on which penalty to accept. The prosecution proposed death. Socrates, facing execution, proposed that he be given free meals at the Prytaneum — the public hall where Olympic victors ate. It was a sarcastic gesture, but it was also true. In his mind, he had earned the greatest honor Athens could give.

The second vote was even closer: 360 for death, 141 against. Socrates was sentenced to execution.

The Final Days: From Crito and Phaedo

After the trial, Socrates was imprisoned. His friends came to visit him and begged him to escape. Escape would have been easy — his friends had arranged it, bribing the guards, planning a route out of the city. Socrates refused.

Plato recorded this conversation in a dialogue called the Crito, named after one of Socrates’ closest friends. When Crito begged him to run, Socrates gave an answer that would echo through twenty-five centuries of political philosophy:

I have made an agreement with the laws of Athens, and I cannot now flee and break that agreement. The law must be obeyed, even if it is unjust, because the alternative is chaos. If good people break the law whenever they think it is wrong, then the very concept of law dissolves. And without law, there is no justice at all. — Plato, Crito, 50b–51c (paraphrased)

This was not resignation. It was a philosophical principle: civil obedience. You have a duty to obey the laws of the state, even when the state has treated you unjustly. The alternative — everyone breaking the law when they disagree — is worse than any individual injustice.

The Death Scene: From Phaedo

On his final day, Socrates met with his closest friends and students in prison. Plato recorded these conversations in a dialogue called the Phaedo, one of the most moving texts in all of philosophy.

As the sun set, the executioner arrived with the poison — hemlock, a bitter drink that causes paralysis and suffocation. Socrates drank it with extraordinary calm. His students wept. Socrates smiled and told them not to cry. He was not afraid of death, he said, because death was nothing to fear:

Either death is like a dreamless sleep, in which case it is nothing to fear — for a dreamless sleep is no harm at all. Or the soul lives on in another place, and I will meet with the good people of antiquity. Both alternatives are good. — Plato, Apology, 40c–41a

As the hemlock spread through his body, Socrates remained conscious and rational. He felt the numbness creeping up from his feet. He tested it, pressing his foot, feeling nothing. The poison rose slowly — feet, legs, torso, chest. At one point, as his breathing began to fail, his final words were:

Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius. Pay it and do not neglect it. — Plato, Phaedo, 118a

Asclepius was the god of healing. Why, at the moment of death, does Socrates remember a vow to the god of healing? Some scholars think it was ironic — death is the final cure. Others think Socrates was saying he owed a debt, and was settling his accounts before the journey to the afterlife. We will never know what he meant. Those were his last words.

Then his eyes fixed. His jaw clenched. His breathing stopped. The man who had spent a lifetime asking questions had asked his final one, and now he would never ask another.

Modern Perspective: Why Did He Die?

Was Socrates’ death a philosophical choice or a political trap? Historians still debate this. Did Socrates genuinely choose to die as a matter of principle? Or did he stumble into a trap set by a city still reeling from war, looking for someone to blame? The most honest answer is: probably both. Socrates’ commitment to truth and questioning made enemies in a city that wanted stability and obedience. His refusal to compromise — even to save his life — was real. But it emerged from a political crisis that no philosophy could resolve. The gap between what Socrates believed he was doing (serving Athens as a gadfly) and what Athens believed (that he was corrupting the young) was unbridgeable. Death became inevitable.

III. The Ideas — Three Lessons from a Death

Socrates’ trial and death left behind three philosophical ideas that would shape everything that came after.

Idea 1: Civil Obedience — The Social Contract

We have a duty to obey the laws of the state, even when the state is unjust, because the alternative is worse.

Socrates argued that by living in Athens, he had made an implicit contract. He had benefited from Athenian law and Athenian protection. Now that the city had condemned him, he could not turn his back on that contract, even to save his life. To flee would be to say that he agreed to the laws only when they benefited him — a kind of selfishness dressed up as principle.

This idea — that we have duties to the state even when it wrongs us, because order is more important than individual justice — became the foundation of political philosophy. Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau would all build on Socrates’ insight. It would justify revolutions (when the social contract is truly broken) and also justify obedience to unjust laws (when the system itself is worth preserving).

The tension is real. Socrates himself shows us the problem: the verdict was unjust, Athens was corrupt, yet he obeyed anyway. Can obedience to an unjust law ever be the right thing to do?

Idea 2: Death is Not Evil

Death is not something to fear, because either it is nothing (like sleep) or it is the liberation of the soul.

Socrates approached death with an equanimity that shocked his students. He did not bargain, did not beg, did not rage. Why? Because he had reasoned his way to peace.

If death is simply the end of consciousness, then it is like dreamless sleep — and we do not fear sleep. If death is the separation of the soul from the body, then the soul goes on to a better place, and the good person has nothing to fear there either. Either way, the good person — the person who has lived a life of virtue and truth — will be safe.

This is not Socrates saying death is good. It is him saying: given that I must die, and given that I have lived a good life, death cannot harm me. What can harm me? Only dishonor, cowardice, betrayal of truth. Death cannot do those things.

This idea echoes through all later philosophy — Stoicism, Christianity, existentialism. It says that the worst things in life are not the painful things, but the shameful things. And no external power can force you into shame if you have chosen your principles.

Idea 3: The Examined Life is Worth More Than Life Itself

Some things — the pursuit of truth, the integrity of the soul, the life of the mind — are worth more than physical survival.

Socrates could have escaped. He could have lived. But living in exile, in silence, unable to ask questions, would have been a kind of death anyway. What is the point of living if you cannot pursue the truth? What is the point of drawing breath if you must betray your principles to do it?

This idea changed the Western world. It said that the human being is not just a body that eats and breeds and avoids pain. The human being is a thinker, a questioner, an examiner of life. And the examined life — the life of inquiry, of dialogue, of honest pursuit of truth — is the only life worth living.

It is a radical claim. It means that a person who is physically enslaved but free to think is better off than a person who is physically free but enslaved in mind. It means that poverty, illness, even death, are not the worst things. The worst thing is to stop thinking, to stop questioning, to accept received answers without examination.

The Unresolved Question

Socrates dies with extraordinary peace. He has reasoned his way to tranquility. But something is left unresolved, and it will haunt the next phase of philosophy:

Think about it before clicking: Socrates was an innocent man condemned unjustly. His closest friends are weeping as he dies. Their grief is real. Can philosophy — reason alone — heal that wound? Can it bring back the dead? Can it comfort the people who loved him?

Socrates meets death with courage, with reason, with peace. But he leaves behind grief, injustice, and questions that reason cannot answer. His student Plato will spend his entire life trying to solve the problem Socrates left behind: if the soul is immortal, if there is a realm of perfect, eternal truths, how can injustice exist in this world? How can a good person suffer? This search will lead Plato to his greatest idea: the Theory of Forms. But even Plato will never fully resolve what Socrates left open. The good person can be wronged. Philosophy can comfort us, but it cannot undo the harm. We will live with this tension for the rest of this course.

A Thesis Seed for You

Socrates dies with peace because he has lived well and examined himself rigorously. His peace comes from having lived the examined life. But can examination alone heal all wounds? Can reason answer every question? As we continue through this course, we will watch this tension unfold: the power of philosophy to give us peace, and its limits when it comes to suffering, loss, injustice, and death. Pay attention to what each philosopher leaves unresolved. Those gaps will lead us to the next philosopher, and the next, and the next.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on. There are no right answers here — only your answers.

Should Socrates have escaped?

His friends arranged it. It would have been easy. If you were Crito, how would you convince him to run? And if you were Socrates, how would you answer?

Is it ever right to die for an idea?

Socrates chose death rather than silence. Was that brave or foolish? Can an idea be worth more than a life?

The cock owed to Asclepius

Socrates’ last words: “Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius. Pay it and do not neglect it.” Asclepius is the god of healing. What did Socrates mean? Was death the cure? Was he settling debts? Was he joking?

Justice and the social contract

The jury was wrong. The verdict was unjust. Yet Socrates obeyed it. When, if ever, is it right to disobey an unjust law? And when is obedience more important than justice?

Is Socrates’ death more powerful than his life?

Before his death, Socrates was a local teacher. After his death, he became immortal. Did he become more powerful by dying? Is martyrdom a form of victory?

Can reason heal grief?

Socrates reasoned his way to peace. But his friends wept, and that grief was real. Can philosophy comfort them? Can it answer their loss?

The examined life

Socrates said the unexamined life is not worth living. Do you agree? Is there such a thing as a good life without questioning? What does examination require?

Socrates is dead. But his death raises a question that his greatest student, Plato, will spend his entire life answering: if the soul is immortal, if there is a realm of eternal, perfect truths beyond this world of shadows and suffering, then how can there be injustice in this world? How can a good person suffer? Plato will propose that everything we see — every rock, every person, every virtue — is just a shadow of something more real, more eternal, more perfect. And that perfect realm is where the true reality lives. Welcome to Plato’s Theory of Forms. Welcome to the world beyond the cave.

What Socrates Left Unresolved

Socrates died at peace, but he left the world in grief. His death was an injustice, and no amount of philosophy could undo it. His friends would carry that wound forever. Philosophy can comfort us, can give us courage, can teach us to live well and die without fear. But it cannot prevent suffering. It cannot restore the dead. It cannot heal all wounds. This gap — between what philosophy can do and what we wish it could do — is the space where the next philosopher will build. Plato will try to reach beyond this world. And we will follow him.
