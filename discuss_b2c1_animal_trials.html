Animal Trials of the Middle Ages

Challenging assumptions and digging beneath the surface

💬 Opinion & Argument

The Accused

Lavegny, France. 1457.
The accused: a sow and her six piglets.
The charge: murder of a five-year-old boy.
The verdict: The sow was found guilty and hanged. The piglets were acquitted — on account of their youth and the "bad influence" of their mother.

This wasn't unusual. For over 500 years, courts across Europe put animals on trial — complete with judges, lawyers, witnesses, and formal verdicts.

Before we go further: Why would a medieval court put an animal on trial? What reasons can you think of?

Speak for 1 minute. Don't worry about being "right" — just explore the idea.

Your first reaction is probably: "That's absurd." But is it? Today we'll explore what these trials really tell us — and practise the language for digging beneath the surface of an idea: questioning assumptions, challenging first impressions, and getting to what's really at stake.

❌ Surface-level

"I think it's stupid. It doesn't make sense to put animals on trial."

Dismisses without analysing

✓ Digging deeper

"On the face of it, this seems absurd. But what's really at stake here is how a society defines accountability — and that presupposes a specific worldview about what 'guilt' means."

Analyses, challenges, finds the deeper question

Digging Beneath the Surface

These four phrases help you move from a surface reaction to a deeper analysis. Click each one to explore it:

"On the face of it... but when you look more closely..." Click to expand
MEANING

Acknowledge the obvious interpretation, then reveal a deeper one. Shows intellectual sophistication.

FORM

On the face of it, [surface view]. But when you look more closely, [deeper insight].

PRONUNCIATION

Stress shifts: "On the FACE of it... but when you look more CLOSELY..." Pause between the two halves.

Example in context

"On the face of it, putting a pig on trial seems like medieval madness. But when you look more closely, you see a society trying to impose moral order on a chaotic world."

Now you try:

Use this phrase to talk about something that seems simple but is actually more complex: social media, working from home, or anything else.

"That presupposes that..." Click to expand
MEANING

Identifies a hidden assumption inside someone's argument. Powerful for challenging ideas without being aggressive.

FORM

[Statement/argument], but that presupposes that [hidden assumption].

PRONUNCIATION

/ˌpriː.sʌˈpəʊ.zɪz/ — 4 syllables, stress on third. Often preceded by a thoughtful "well..."

Example in context

"The court found the sow guilty — but that presupposes that a pig can understand right from wrong. Can it?"

Now you try:

Challenge one of these hidden assumptions: "University education is essential for success." What does that presuppose?

"There's a case to be made that..." Click to expand
MEANING

Introduces an alternative perspective — even one you don't fully agree with. Shows you can think from multiple angles.

FORM

There's a (strong/reasonable/compelling) case to be made that [alternative view].

PRONUNCIATION

Stress on "case": "There's a CASE to be made..." Sounds measured and authoritative.

Example in context

"Everyone laughs at medieval animal trials. But there's a case to be made that they took animals more seriously than we do — at least they gave the pig a lawyer."

Now you try:

Use this phrase to defend an unpopular position: "There's a case to be made that..." (e.g. homework is a waste of time / social media does more good than harm).

"What's really at stake here is..." Click to expand
MEANING

Cuts through the surface debate to identify the core issue. Shows you see the bigger picture.

FORM

What's really at stake here is [the fundamental question/issue].

PRONUNCIATION

Emphasis on "really" and "stake": "What's REALLY at STAKE here is..." Sounds decisive.

Example in context

"We can laugh at a pig in court. But what's really at stake here is a question we still haven't answered: who — or what — deserves legal rights?"

Now you try:

Pick a current debate (AI regulation, climate policy, education reform) and identify what's really at stake beneath the surface arguments.

The Evidence

Read each case. Then use the target phrases to discuss what you think — don't just react, dig deeper.

🐷 The Pig Trials

Between the 13th and 16th centuries, at least 60 recorded pig trials took place in France alone. Pigs roamed freely and occasionally killed or injured children. They were tried individually, given court-appointed lawyers, and could be acquitted. Convicted pigs were dressed in human clothes before execution.

Use "On the face of it..." to discuss: What seems absurd here? What might be more complex than it looks?

🐀 The Rat Summons (1508)

The French jurist Bartholomew Chassenée was appointed to defend a group of rats accused of destroying a barley crop. He argued his clients couldn't attend court because the journey was too dangerous — there were cats along the route. The court accepted this and granted a delay. Chassenée later became one of France's most respected legal minds.

Use "That presupposes that..." to unpack this: What assumptions did the court make about the rats? What does Chassenée's defence presuppose?

⛪ Excommunicating Insects

The Catholic Church held trials against locusts, weevils, and beetles that destroyed crops. Insects were formally excommunicated — banned from the Church and from the affected land. In 1478, a court in Berne excommunicated a plague of beetles. The beetles did not comply.

Use "There's a case to be made that..." to argue: Were these communities being ridiculous, or logical within their own worldview?

🐴 The Donkey Acquittal (1750)

A man and a donkey were both charged with a crime in France. The man was convicted. The donkey was acquitted after the parish priest and several citizens signed a certificate vouching for its "good moral character."

Use "What's really at stake here is..." to find the deeper question: What does this trial tell us about how a society constructs the idea of "character"?

AI & Personhood

Self-Driving Cars

A self-driving car kills a pedestrian. Who goes on trial — the car, the programmer, the company, or nobody?

Try: "What's really at stake here is..." / "There's a case to be made that..."

Legal Personhood for Nature

New Zealand's Whanganui River has the same legal rights as a human being. Progress — or back to the Middle Ages?

Try: "On the face of it, this seems like progress. But that presupposes that..."

Dangerous Dogs

A dog bites a child and is put down. How is this different from a medieval pig trial? Is it different?

Try: "There's a case to be made that we're still doing the same thing, we just..."

Industrial Farming

Medieval courts gave animals lawyers. Today we slaughter billions without any legal process. Which approach takes animals more seriously?

Try: "What's really at stake here is how we define..." / "That presupposes that legal rights equal..."
