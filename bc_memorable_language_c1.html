Rhetorical Questions in Presentations

What is a rhetorical question?

A question asked to create an effect or make a point, not because you need an answer. The audience thinks about the answer themselves, which makes them engaged and active participants in your presentation.

Examples:
  • "What could be better than a quick dip in the pool before breakfast?"
  • "How will you know if you never try it?"
  • "What do we really want from our careers?"

Three Structures for Rhetorical Questions

Structure A: Statement + Rhetorical Question

Make a statement first, then follow it with a question that reinforces your point.

Example: "Building a global brand is the dream of every entrepreneur. But are we prepared for the challenges?"

Structure B: Rhetorical Question + Emphatic Statement

Ask the rhetorical question first to engage, then make your emphatic statement answer it.

Example: "How do we transform a local business into a global powerhouse? We invest in quality, consistency, and understanding our audience."

Structure C: Statement + Question + Answer

Make a statement, ask a rhetorical question, and then provide the answer yourself.

Example: "Technology is reshaping how we work. But what skills will remain irreplaceable? The human ability to connect, create, and think critically."

Why are rhetorical questions powerful? Because they force your audience to think, and when people think, they engage. And when they engage, they remember.

Metaphors: Making Ideas Stick

What is a metaphor?

A figure of speech that describes an object or action in a way that isn't literally true, but helps explain an idea or make a comparison.

Examples:
  • "There is an elephant in the room" — acknowledging a problem no one wants to address
  • "Data is the new oil of the digital economy" — showing how valuable and transformative data is
  • "Chaos is a friend of mine" (Bob Dylan) — comfort with unpredictability

Three Key Functions of Metaphors

1. Breakdown Complex Concepts

Turn abstract ideas into concrete images your audience can understand.

Example: "Building a company is like gardening—you plant seeds, nurture them carefully, and wait for growth."

2. Encourage Thinking in Images

Help your audience visualize and remember through pictures in their minds.

Example: "If my business was a garden, my social media posts would be a colourful display of flowers."

3. Make Data Meaningful

Transform numbers and statistics into human-scale comparisons.

Example: "5GB equals 1000 songs in your pocket."

Metaphors translate intangible concepts into digestible and memorable ideas. They're the bridge between your expertise and your audience's understanding.
