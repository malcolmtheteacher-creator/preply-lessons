# Functional English — the build rule

This replaces every earlier role-play spec. It exists because the first attempt
built situations and then harvested phrases out of the invented dialogue. That
produced set dressing: *"Mum mentioned the wing mirror."* Nothing transferable,
nothing worth carrying out of the room.

## The order of work — non-negotiable

1. **Name the situation.** A real one, in a real area of life or work.
2. **List the functions that situation genuinely demands.** The jobs the
   language has to do. Not a generic list — the specific ones *this* situation
   needs. A doctor breaking bad news and a salesperson handling a brush-off
   share almost nothing.
3. **Write the exponents** — the actual phrases people really say to perform
   each function. This is the product. Everything else is packaging.
4. **Malcolm approves the phrase list.** Before any HTML exists.
5. **Only then write the dialogue**, built to showcase the phrases naturally.

The dialogue serves the phrases. Never the other way round.

## What counts as a phrase

Every candidate must pass all four:

**1. Name the function without mentioning the scene.**
If you can't say what job it does in three words — *prefacing bad news*,
*asking for a discount*, *checking understanding* — it isn't a phrase.

**2. Swap the nouns and it still works.**
"Is there any movement on the price?" works at a market, a garage, a supplier.
"Mum mentioned the wing mirror" works nowhere else. That's the test.

**3. Prefer a frame with a slot to a closed sentence.**
- "It's more of a ___ than a ___."
- "I'd hate to have to ___, but…"
- "What I'd really like is ___ — or ___, if that's simpler."
A student fills the slot with their own life. A closed sentence can only be
repeated.

**4. It has to be something people actually say.**
Not plausible-sounding. Not written-to-land. If you can't hear a real person
saying it on a real Tuesday, cut it.

## Functions expand — they are not a fixed list

Do **not** build a canon of thirty generic functions and recycle them. Each
area has its own functional language, and the inventory grows with every area:

- *Doctor with a patient* — taking a history, pressing for specifics, checking
  the patient has actually understood, breaking bad news, reassuring without
  promising, getting consent.
- *First sales call in English* — earning the first twenty seconds,
  establishing relevance before pitching, handling the brush-off, the price
  objection, proposing a next step rather than a sale, leaving on a no.

Overlap where it's genuine (proposing a compromise really does appear in
haggling and complaining) — recycling helps retention. But never force a
situation into functions it doesn't have.

## Always include the second-language functions

The student is usually the professional in the room doing their own job in
English. Generic native-speaker material for that job leaves these out, and
they are often the most valuable thing in the lesson:

- not understanding, without losing authority — *"Sorry, could you run that
  past me again?"*
- checking you've got it right — *"Just so I've got that straight, you're
  saying…?"*
- buying a second to think — *"Let me just get this clear in my head."*
- asking for a word you don't have — *"What's the word for when…?"*

## Structure of a lesson

Unchanged and still right: 🎬 The Scene · 💬 The Conversation · 🧰 Useful
Phrases · 🎭 Your Turn · 🔭 The Bigger Picture. Two rounds with the roles
swapped. Guess-first teaching. All the hard rules from before still bind —
the 4th wall, empty inputs, never showing an answer above its own exercise,
exercises that actually test, nothing untaught in an answer key.

What changes is that the Useful Phrases tab is now the thing the lesson is
built *from*, not the thing scraped out of it afterwards.
