A Noisy Neighbour

The bass is coming through the bedroom wall, it's a work night, and you still have to live next door tomorrow. Time to knock — and keep the peace.

The Scene 🎬

It's a Tuesday, gone eleven, and the bass from next door is doing a slow drum solo on your bedroom wall. You've got an early start, you've been staring at the ceiling for the better part of an hour, and the annoyance is starting to feel personal — which is exactly the moment a good neighbour makes a bad decision.

Here's the catch: you don't get to move. Whatever you say in the next five minutes, you'll still be nodding at this person by the bins every morning for the next two years. So the goal was never to win. It's to get some quiet and keep the peace — knock, stay friendly, be firm, and come away with an agreement you can both live with.

You pull on a jumper, take a breath to get the edge out of your voice, and go and knock.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last have a run-in with a neighbour — noise, parking, a bin, a boundary? Did you actually say something, or grit your teeth and hope it faded away? How did that work out?
  2. Some people would sooner move house than have one awkward conversation. Are you a "knock and sort it out" type or a "leave a passive-aggressive note" type — and where do you reckon that comes from?

Who's who

You
The Neighbour Next Door 🚪

Reasonable, tired, and after sleep rather than a feud. Ready to knock, stay warm, and hold your line without letting it become a war.

The other voice
The One With the Speakers 🎧

Not a monster — just oblivious. A little defensive at first, decent enough underneath, as long as you don't back them into a corner.

🔍 Say what is happening

🤝 Give them the benefit of the doubt
"I'm sure you've no idea how much it travels."
"I'm sure you didn't realise."
📅 Say why it matters
"The trouble is, we're both up at six, so it really knocks us out the next day."
"It makes it hard to sleep on work nights."
🎯 Ask for a change
"I'm not asking you to sit in silence, but could we…?"
"Could we maybe find some middle ground?"
🌟 Keep it friendly
"If we're ever the noisy ones, just bang on the wall."

"I didn't realise it carried that far."

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Listen for the softeners, the moments blame gets aimed at the wall instead of the person, and how "turn it down" becomes something you'd happily agree to.

NeighbourOh — hey. Everything alright?
YouHi — sorry to land on your doorstep out of the blue. I'm from number 12, just the other side of that wall. I don't think we've ever properly met, actually.
NeighbourAh — no, I don't think we have. Have we… been too loud? I did half wonder.
YouHonestly, I don't want to make a big thing of it. It's just that the music carries straight through the wall — more than you'd ever think. And it's not really the daytime, it's more the late nights that get us.
NeighbourRight. I mean… it's only gone eleven, though, isn't it?
YouNo, totally — and I'm sure you've no idea how much it travels; the walls in these places are like paper. The trouble is, we're both up at six, and once it's coming through the bedroom wall we just lie there wide awake.
NeighbourAh. Yeah. I genuinely didn't realise it came through like that. Fair enough.
YouLook, I'm not asking you to sit in silence in your own place — could we maybe find some middle ground? Say, keep the big nights to weekends, and just ease it down a bit after eleven when it's a work night?
NeighbourYeah, that's more than reasonable. Tell you what — I can shift the speakers off that wall as well; they're sat right against it, that's probably half the problem.
YouHonestly, that alone would make a world of difference. And look — if we're ever the noisy ones, just bang on the wall, I won't take it the wrong way.
NeighbourDeal. Sorry again — you should've said something weeks ago, saved yourself the grief.
YouNo harm done. I'd far rather have a quick word than let it fester into something silly. Thanks for being so decent about it.
NeighbourCourse. I'll knock it down right now. Nice to finally put a face to the wall, anyway.
YouLikewise. Right — I'll let you get back to your night. Cheers.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The whole reason for knocking was the noise — yet that isn't the first thing out of the visitor's mouth. What do they spend the opening on instead, and what does that buy them before the complaint even lands?

2. At one point the neighbour gets a little defensive ("it's only gone eleven"). The visitor doesn't argue back. So what do they do to get the neighbour back onside?

3. By the end, two concrete changes are on the table — one the visitor asked for, one the neighbour offered. What are they, and why does the neighbour's offer count for more than a vague promise to "be quieter"?

1. They introduce themselves and make plain they're not there for a fight ("I don't want to make a big thing of it"). That turns a stranger banging on the door into a neighbour having a friendly word — so the other person never gets defensive enough to dig their heels in.
2. They agree with them ("No, totally") and hand them an easy way out — blaming the wall, not the person ("I'm sure you've no idea how much it travels"). Then they give a warm, concrete reason (a six o'clock start). Empathy plus a reason, never a counter-attack.
3. The visitor asked to keep big nights to weekends and ease off after eleven; the neighbour offered to move the speakers off the shared wall. Moving the speakers fixes the actual cause — the vibration through the party wall — rather than relying on someone remembering to behave. A physical change beats a good intention.

Your Turn 🎭

No script this time. You'll have a real conversation with a real person. They have their own mind, and you have a goal.

1 · Warm up your voice 🎤

Say each step once, out loud, in your own words. No writing, no peeking at the phrases.

  1. Knock politely. Say who you are and why you came, gently.
  2. Say what is happening. Be specific and keep it about the noise, not them.
  3. Give them the benefit of the doubt. Make it easy for them.
  4. Say why it matters. Explain the real impact.
  5. Ask for a change. Suggest something reasonable.
  6. Keep it friendly. Leave the relationship better than you found it.

The Echo 🕰️

When the great fire of AD 64 gutted the heart of Rome, the emperor Nero looked at the smoking ruins of his neighbours' homes and saw not a tragedy but a building plot. He annexed a huge slice of the burnt city and threw up the Domus Aurea — the "Golden House" — with a private lake where an entire crowded district used to live.

He is, in a way, the noisy neighbour taken to its logical extreme: a man so untroubled by anyone else's comfort that he'd redecorate the whole neighbourhood in his own honour — and expect the survivors to applaud.

You, knocking politely next door about the bass, are doing the exact opposite. Two thousand years of aggrieved neighbours are quietly on your side.

Two bigger questions

  1. Nero never once had to hear the word "no" — no neighbour dared knock on his door. Does power make people worse neighbours, or just make it easier to get away with? Can you think of a modern "Nero" who never gets told to turn it down?
  2. A good neighbour and a good citizen are nearly the same thing: both mean putting up with a little so that everyone gets more. Where's the line between reasonable give-and-take and simply letting yourself be walked over — next door, or across a whole society?
