A Noisy Neighbour

The bass is coming through the bedroom wall, it's a work night, and you still have to live next door tomorrow. Time to knock — and keep the peace.

The Scene 🎬

It's a Tuesday, gone eleven, and the bass from next door is doing a slow drum solo on your bedroom wall. You've got an early start, you've been staring at the ceiling for the better part of an hour, and the annoyance is starting to feel personal — which is exactly the moment a good neighbour makes a bad decision.

Here's the catch: you don't get to move. Whatever you say in the next five minutes, you'll still be nodding at this person by the bins every morning for the next two years. So the goal was never to win. It's to get some quiet and keep the peace — knock, stay friendly, be firm, and come away with an agreement you can both live with.

You pull on a jumper, take a breath to get the edge out of your voice, and go and knock.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last have a run-in with a neighbour — noise, parking, a bin, a boundary? Did you actually say something, or grit your teeth and hope it faded away? How did that work out?
  2. Some people would sooner move house than have one awkward conversation. Are you a "knock and sort it out" type or a "leave a passive-aggressive note" type — and where do you reckon that comes from?

Who's who

You
The Neighbour Next Door 🚪

Reasonable, tired, and after sleep rather than a feud. Ready to knock, stay warm, and hold your line without letting it become a war.

The other voice
The One With the Speakers 🎧

Not a monster — just oblivious. A little defensive at first, decent enough underneath, as long as you don't back them into a corner.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't just clock what gets asked for — watch how a real complaint gets raised without a single accusation. Notice that nobody ever says "you're too loud". That's the whole trick.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the softeners, the moments blame gets aimed at the wall instead of the person, and how "turn it down" becomes something you'd happily agree to.

NeighbourOh — hey. Everything alright?
YouHi — sorry to land on your doorstep out of the blue. I'm from number 12, just the other side of that wall. I don't think we've ever properly met, actually.
NeighbourAh — no, I don't think we have. Have we… been too loud? I did half wonder.
YouHonestly, I don't want to make a big thing of it. It's just that the music carries straight through the wall — more than you'd ever think. And it's not really the daytime, it's more the late nights that get us.
NeighbourRight. I mean… it's only gone eleven, though, isn't it?
YouNo, totally — and I'm sure you've no idea how much it travels; the walls in these places are like paper. The trouble is, we're both up at six, and once it's coming through the bedroom wall we just lie there wide awake.
NeighbourAh. Yeah. I genuinely didn't realise it came through like that. Fair enough.
YouLook, I'm not asking you to sit in silence in your own place — could we maybe find some middle ground? Say, keep the big nights to weekends, and just ease it down a bit after eleven when it's a work night?
NeighbourYeah, that's more than reasonable. Tell you what — I can shift the speakers off that wall as well; they're sat right against it, that's probably half the problem.
YouHonestly, that alone would make a world of difference. And look — if we're ever the noisy ones, just bang on the wall, I won't take it the wrong way.
NeighbourDeal. Sorry again — you should've said something weeks ago, saved yourself the grief.
YouNo harm done. I'd far rather have a quick word than let it fester into something silly. Thanks for being so decent about it.
NeighbourCourse. I'll knock it down right now. Nice to finally put a face to the wall, anyway.
YouLikewise. Right — I'll let you get back to your night. Cheers.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The whole reason for knocking was the noise — yet that isn't the first thing out of the visitor's mouth. What do they spend the opening on instead, and what does that buy them before the complaint even lands?

2. At one point the neighbour gets a little defensive ("it's only gone eleven"). The visitor doesn't argue back. So what do they do to get the neighbour back onside?

3. By the end, two concrete changes are on the table — one the visitor asked for, one the neighbour offered. What are they, and why does the neighbour's offer count for more than a vague promise to "be quieter"?

1. They introduce themselves and make plain they're not there for a fight ("I don't want to make a big thing of it"). That turns a stranger banging on the door into a neighbour having a friendly word — so the other person never gets defensive enough to dig their heels in.
2. They agree with them ("No, totally") and hand them an easy way out — blaming the wall, not the person ("I'm sure you've no idea how much it travels"). Then they give a warm, concrete reason (a six o'clock start). Empathy plus a reason, never a counter-attack.
3. The visitor asked to keep big nights to weekends and ease off after eleven; the neighbour offered to move the speakers off the shared wall. Moving the speakers fixes the actual cause — the vibration through the party wall — rather than relying on someone remembering to behave. A physical change beats a good intention.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that raise a real problem without lighting a fuse — at this door and every other one. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚪 Knocking softly

Phrase 1

They've opened the door and have no idea who you are or why you're there. You want to disarm them in one line — before a word about noise. Which opener sets the friendliest tone?

A. "We need to talk about your music."
B. "Do you know how late it is?"
C. "Sorry to land on your doorstep out of the blue — I'm from number 12, just next door."
C — "Sorry to land on your doorstep out of the blue — I'm from number 12, just next door."
You apologise for the intrusion and put a face to the wall before you've asked for anything. A and B start a fight; C starts a relationship. "Land on your doorstep" is a warm, self-deprecating way to say "turn up unannounced".
Phrase 2

You want to signal, up front, that you're not here to make a scene — that this is a small favour, not a formal complaint. There's a set phrase for playing something down: "I don't want to ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ it, but…"

"I don't want to make a big thing of it, but…"
It lowers the temperature before the complaint arrives, so the other person hears a request, not an attack. Cousins: "I don't want to make a fuss, but…" and "It's not a huge deal, but…".

🗣️ Naming the problem — without blame

Phrase 3

Now describe the problem. The secret is to blame the wall, not the person — talk about the effect, not their behaviour. Which version does that?

A. "You play your music way too loud."
B. "The music carries straight through the wall, more than you'd think."
B — "The music carries straight through the wall, more than you'd think."
A points a finger ("you… too loud") and invites a row. B makes the thin wall the villain, so there's nothing to defend against. The verb to learn: sound carries = it travels further than you'd expect.
Phrase 4

You don't mind noise in the day — it's specifically the late nights that ruin you, and saying so makes you sound fair, not fussy. Put the pieces in order:

it's morethe daytime,it's not reallythe late nights.
"It's not really the daytime, it's more the late nights."
The "it's not so much X, it's more Y" frame narrows a complaint to the one thing that actually matters — which makes you sound reasonable and makes the fix easy to picture. You're not banning music; you're protecting your sleep.
Phrase 5

Before you ask for anything, you want to take the sting out — to signal you assume they had no idea and aren't doing it on purpose. What short, generous line hands them that benefit of the doubt?

"I'm sure you've no idea how much it travels."
You write their innocence into the conversation for them, so they can fix the problem without ever admitting fault. Relatives: "You probably can't even hear it from your side" and "I'm sure it sounds fine where you are".

🧱 Firm but friendly

Phrase 6

Now the quiet firm bit — a clear reason why this genuinely matters to you, delivered without a hint of drama. Which line would you rather receive at your own door?

A. "Some of us have to work, you know."
B. "The trouble is, we're both up at six, so it really knocks us out the next day."
B — "The trouble is, we're both up at six, so it really knocks us out the next day."
A is a dig; B is a fact about you. "The trouble is…" flags a real obstacle honestly, and a concrete detail (up at six) earns far more sympathy than a sarcastic jab ever could.
Phrase 7

You have to head off the worry that you're asking them to live in total silence — reassure them you're being fair before you make the request. Complete it: "I'm not asking you to ___ ___ ___, but could we…?"

"I'm not asking you to sit in silence, but could we…?"
Naming the extreme you don't want makes your actual request sound modest and easy to grant. Same move: "I'm not saying never, I'm just saying…" and "I don't expect you to tiptoe about, but…".

🤝 Finding a workable agreement

Phrase 8

Time to propose a compromise you can both live with. There's a warm idiom for meeting halfway. Put it in order:

findcould wesomemaybemiddle ground?
"Could we maybe find some middle ground?"
"Middle ground" frames the outcome as a shared win, not your demand — you're solving it together. The soft "maybe" invites them to shape the deal too. Cousins: "meet in the middle" and "come to some sort of arrangement".
Phrase 9

Seal the goodwill by opening the door both ways — invite them to complain right back if you're ever the loud ones. What generous, disarming offer does exactly that?

"If we're ever the noisy ones, just bang on the wall."
Offering them the same right you're claiming makes the whole thing feel fair rather than one-sided — and quietly says "we're in this together". Variants: "give us a knock" and "don't hesitate to say something".

🕊️ Keeping the peace

Phrase 10

They've apologised and agreed. You want to leave the whole thing feeling small and settled — no lingering awkwardness by the bins tomorrow. Which closing line does that best?

A. "Right, well, make sure it doesn't happen again."
B. "See that it's sorted, then. Goodnight."
C. "No harm done — I'd far rather have a quick word than let it fester. Thanks for being so decent about it."
C — "No harm done — I'd far rather have a quick word than let it fester. Thanks for being so decent about it."
A and B end on a warning and leave a bad taste; C forgives, explains why you came, and thanks them for it. "Let it fester" = let a small problem quietly grow into a big one — exactly what a good word prevents.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole visit out loud with all ten, in order — soft knock → name it without blame → firm reason → offer a deal → keep the peace. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your wall, your Tuesday night. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer to keep it short and civil — long complaints turn into arguments.

Round 1 — you are the one knocking 🚪

Their door opens. You answer. No looking back at the phrases — trust your ear. Remember: introduce, disarm, blame the wall, then ask.

NeighbourOh — hi. Can I help you with something?
NeighbourToo loud? Really? It's not even that late, is it?
NeighbourHuh. I honestly hadn't thought about it coming through like that.
NeighbourSo what, you want me to sit here in total silence?
NeighbourYeah… I suppose that's fair enough, actually.
NeighbourAlright. Sorry — you should've said something sooner, really.

Round 2 — now YOU are the one with the speakers 🎧

Swap sides. Someone's just knocked about YOUR music. The easy move is to get defensive — don't. Own it lightly, and be the neighbour you'd want next door. Invent your own fix.

NeighbourHi — sorry to turn up like this. I'm from number 12, just the other side of the wall. I don't think we've properly met.
NeighbourI don't want to make a big thing of it — but the music does carry through the wall, especially late on.
NeighbourIt's mainly the work nights, to be honest — we're both up at six.
NeighbourI'm not asking for silence — could we maybe find some middle ground?
NeighbourHonestly, that would make all the difference. Thanks.
NeighbourCheers for being so decent about it. Right — I'll leave you to it.
The Twist

🌩️ The broken promise

One more round — no script, no safety net. This isn't the first time. The music's been keeping you up for a month; you knocked once before and they swore they'd sort it, then nothing changed. Now it's midnight on a Sunday, you've got the biggest day of your year tomorrow, and the bass is still going. Start the timer and talk it through — and still, no war. How do you raise a broken promise without accusing them of lying? When, if at all, do you mention the tenancy, the landlord or the council — and how do you keep that as a genuine last resort rather than a threat? Can you walk away with a real agreement AND the relationship intact? One rule: the second you threaten or raise your voice, you've lost the room — and you still have to live next door.

The Echo 🕰️

When the great fire of AD 64 gutted the heart of Rome, the emperor Nero looked at the smoking ruins of his neighbours' homes and saw not a tragedy but a building plot. He annexed a huge slice of the burnt city and threw up the Domus Aurea — the "Golden House" — with a private lake where an entire crowded district used to live.

He is, in a way, the noisy neighbour taken to its logical extreme: a man so untroubled by anyone else's comfort that he'd redecorate the whole neighbourhood in his own honour — and expect the survivors to applaud.

You, knocking politely next door about the bass, are doing the exact opposite. Two thousand years of aggrieved neighbours are quietly on your side.

Two bigger questions

  1. Nero never once had to hear the word "no" — no neighbour dared knock on his door. Does power make people worse neighbours, or just make it easier to get away with? Can you think of a modern "Nero" who never gets told to turn it down?
  2. A good neighbour and a good citizen are nearly the same thing: both mean putting up with a little so that everyone gets more. Where's the line between reasonable give-and-take and simply letting yourself be walked over — next door, or across a whole society?
