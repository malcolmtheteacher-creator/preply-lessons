Kintsugi: The Beauty of Broken Things

In Japan, a broken bowl is not thrown away. It is repaired with gold — and treasured even more than before.

Broken — or just begun?

When something we love breaks, most of us throw it away or hide the damage. But what if the damage could become the most beautiful part? Let us start with you.

Let's talk:
  • When something you own breaks, do you usually repair it or replace it? Why?
  • Is there an old or damaged object you have kept, even though it is not perfect? What is the story behind it?
  • Do you think new and perfect things are always better than old, used ones?
  • In your country, do people tend to fix things or throw them away?

Hide it, or show it?

Imagine a beautiful cup falls and breaks into pieces. One person glues it back together and hides the cracks, hoping no one will notice. Another person fills the cracks with gold, so that everyone can see exactly where it broke. Same cup, two completely different ideas about what to do with damage.

Over to you:
  • Which person do you understand more — the one who hides the cracks, or the one who shows them?
  • Can the history of an object — where it has been, how it has been damaged — make it more interesting, not less?

The art of golden repair

Here is the idea behind a famous Japanese art. Read it, then we will talk about it.

In Japan, there is an old art called kintsugi. The word means something like “golden joinery” or “golden repair”. It is used to fix broken pottery — bowls, cups and plates that have cracked or fallen to pieces.

In much of the world, when a favourite cup is broken, it is thrown away. Or it is quietly glued back together and pushed to the back of a cupboard. We try to make the damage invisible. Kintsugi does the opposite.

In kintsugi, the broken pieces are collected carefully. They are joined together again with a special lacquer. Then powdered gold is mixed in or sprinkled along the lines of the break. The cracks are not hidden — they are filled with gold and turned into something beautiful. The repair becomes the most striking part of the whole object.

The idea behind it is simple but powerful. A bowl that has been broken and mended is not seen as ruined. It is seen as more interesting, more valuable and more beautiful than before — because now it has a history. Its cracks are part of its story, and not something to be ashamed of.

Kintsugi began hundreds of years ago, but the idea has travelled far beyond pottery. Today it is often used to talk about people: that the difficult things we go through, and the way we put ourselves back together, can make us stronger and more beautiful — not less.

The broken places are not hidden away. They are filled with gold — and the bowl is loved more, not less, because of them.

Grammar focus — the passive voice

Notice how the reading describes the process: “The pieces are collected. They are joined with lacquer. Gold is sprinkled along the cracks.” This is the passive voice, and it is one of the most useful structures in English — especially for describing how something is done.

1. What it is, and when we use it

In an active sentence, we say who does the action: “A craftsman repairs the bowl.” In a passive sentence, we focus on the action or the thing it happens to: “The bowl is repaired.”

We use the passive when:

  • we don't know who does the action, or
  • it doesn't matter who does it, or
  • the action itself is the important thing (for example, when we describe a process).
Form: be + past participle
am / is / are / was / were + done, made, broken, repaired…

Present: The cracks are filled with gold.   (it happens, generally / now)

Past: The bowl was broken many years ago.   (finished past)

2. Active and passive side by side

TenseActivePassive
Present simpleAn artist repairs the bowl.The bowl is repaired.
Past simpleSomeone broke the cup.The cup was broken.
Present (plural)They collect the pieces.The pieces are collected.

If we do want to say who does it, we add by: “The bowl was repaired by a master craftsman.” But often we leave that out, because it is not important.

Practice B — change active into passive

Rewrite each sentence in the passive. Only add “by…” if it feels important.

2. A skilled artist repairs the pottery. → The pottery ____.

3. They sprinkle gold along the cracks. → Gold ____ along the cracks.

4. People all over the world admire this art. → This art ____ all over the world.

2. The pottery is repaired (by a skilled artist).

3. Gold is sprinkled along the cracks.

4. This art is admired all over the world.

B · Hide it, or show it? (4 minutes)

Kintsugi shows the cracks instead of hiding them. In life, people do both — some hide their mistakes and weaknesses, others are open about them.

Which do you think is better, and why? Give your opinion clearly, then give the strongest argument against your own view.

C · Repair or replace? (4 minutes)

Today, many things are thrown away and replaced instead of being repaired.

Is that a problem, or just normal modern life? Talk about it — you can use the passive a lot here: “things are made cheaply…”, “they are designed to be replaced…”, “old things are thrown away too easily…”
