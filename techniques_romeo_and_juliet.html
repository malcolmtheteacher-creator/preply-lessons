Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Nobody could remember how the feud between the two families had started, but for three generations they had refused to speak, trade or marry with one another.
feud (n) — a long, bitter quarrel between two families or groups, often lasting for years. Example: "The feud between the neighbours began over a fence."
2. What began as an insult outside the market turned into a brawl — fists, swords, overturned stalls and twenty men rolling in the dust.
brawl (n) — a rough, noisy fight in a public place, usually involving several people. Example: "Police were called to a brawl outside the stadium."
3. He had seen her only twice, yet he was infatuated: he wrote her name on every page and could think of nothing else — though he hardly knew her at all.
infatuated (adj) — filled with a strong but often short-lived passion for someone, usually without really knowing them. Example: "At sixteen she was infatuated with a singer she had never met."
4. Standing before the altar, they made a vow to stay together whatever happened — a promise neither of them ever broke.
vow (n) — a serious, solemn promise, often made in public or before God. Example: "They wrote their own wedding vows."
5. The prince did not sentence the young man to death; instead he chose to banish him — he must leave the city before morning and never return, or die.
banish (v) — to send someone away from a country or place as a punishment and forbid them to come back. Example: "The king banished his brother for plotting against him."
6. The old monk mixed herbs into a small glass bottle. "Drink this potion," he said, "and for two days you will seem as cold and still as the dead."
potion (n) — a liquid with special medical, magical or poisonous effects. Example: "In the fairy tale, the witch gives the princess a sleeping potion."
7. The family's dead were laid in a stone tomb beneath the churchyard, each on a shelf, with a heavy door that only the gravediggers ever opened.
tomb (n) — a large grave, often built of stone, especially one above or below ground for a whole family. Example: "Tourists queue for hours to see the pharaoh's tomb."
8. Everyone agreed it was a rash decision: he resigned, sold his flat and bought a boat, all in one afternoon, without asking a single person's advice.
rash (adj) — done too quickly, without thinking about the results. Example: "Don't make any rash promises when you're angry."
9. The two lovers seemed star-crossed from the start: every lucky chance turned against them, as if the sky itself had decided they would never be happy.
star-crossed (adj) — (of lovers) destined by bad luck or fate to be unhappy; Shakespeare's own phrase. Example: "The film is about two star-crossed lovers on opposite sides of a war."
10. After twenty years of silence, the brothers finally met at their mother's funeral and were reconciled — they shook hands, then held each other and cried.
reconcile (v) — to make two people or groups friendly again after a quarrel. Example: "The peace talks tried to reconcile the two sides."

The story, in three parts

Shakespeare wrote Romeo and Juliet as a play in the 1590s. Here it is retold as a story, with some of his most famous lines left exactly as he wrote them. Read each part. After it, try the quick check from memory, then discuss.

Part 1 — "What's in a name?"

In the Italian city of Verona lived two great families, the Montagues and the Capulets, and between them lay a feud so old that nobody remembered its cause. One Sunday morning their servants met in the street, an insult was thrown, swords were drawn, and a brawl spread across the square until the Prince of Verona himself rode in to stop it. "If ever you disturb our streets again," he warned the two old fathers, "your lives shall pay the forfeit of the peace."

Romeo, the only son of Lord Montague, had missed the fight. He was too busy being miserable: he was infatuated with a girl called Rosaline, who wanted nothing to do with him. So when his friends heard that the Capulets were holding a masked party that night, they dragged him along — in disguise, into the house of his enemies — to show him that Verona had prettier girls than Rosaline.

They were right. Across the crowded hall Romeo saw a girl dancing and forgot Rosaline in a single breath: "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright!" He took her hand, they spoke, they kissed — and only when she was called away did he learn who she was. Juliet. Thirteen years old. The only daughter of the Capulets.

That night Romeo climbed the wall into the Capulets' orchard. Above him, on her balcony, Juliet was talking to the stars, not knowing anyone could hear her: "O Romeo, Romeo, wherefore art thou Romeo?" — why must you be Romeo, a Montague? "What's in a name? That which we call a rose by any other word would smell as sweet." Romeo stepped out of the shadows. Before the sun rose they had exchanged vows, and the next afternoon Friar Laurence married them in secret. The old monk agreed for one reason: he hoped the marriage might reconcile the two families at last.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does Romeo go to the Capulets' party, and what does he discover there?

2. Why does Friar Laurence agree to marry them?

1. His friends take him, in disguise, to cure his sadness over Rosaline. He falls in love with a girl at first sight, and only afterwards learns she is Juliet, the daughter of his family's enemies.

2. He hopes the secret marriage will end the feud and reconcile the Montagues and the Capulets.

Discuss:
  • Romeo was in love with Rosaline that very morning. Is what he feels for Juliet real love, or just a new infatuation? What in the story makes you think so?

Part 2 — "A plague o' both your houses!"

An hour after the wedding, Romeo walked into the square and found Tybalt, Juliet's hot-tempered cousin, waiting for him. Tybalt had recognised him at the party and wanted revenge. "Thou art a villain," he said, and drew his sword. But Romeo would not fight. Tybalt was family now — though nobody else knew it. "I love thee better than thou canst devise," Romeo told him, and turned away.

His best friend Mercutio could not bear to watch. He drew his own sword and fought Tybalt himself. Romeo stepped between them to stop it — and Tybalt's blade went under Romeo's arm and into Mercutio. Dying, Mercutio cursed both families: "A plague o' both your houses! They have made worms' meat of me."

In a moment of rage, Romeo forgot every promise. He chased Tybalt, fought him and killed him. Then he stood over the body and understood what he had done: "O, I am fortune's fool!" The Prince did not sentence him to death; he banished him. Romeo must leave Verona for the city of Mantua by morning — and never come back.

Romeo spent one secret night with his wife and left at dawn. That same morning, Juliet's father announced wonderful news: in three days' time she would marry Count Paris, a rich young nobleman. When Juliet refused, Lord Capulet exploded. Marry Paris on Thursday, he told her, or be thrown out to "beg, starve, die in the streets."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does Romeo refuse to fight Tybalt at first — and what makes him change his mind?

2. What is the Prince's punishment, and what new problem does Juliet face?

1. Tybalt is now his wife's cousin, so Romeo sees him as family. But when Tybalt kills Mercutio, under Romeo's own arm, Romeo loses control and kills Tybalt.

2. Romeo is banished to Mantua. Juliet's father orders her to marry Count Paris in three days — and she is already secretly married.

Discuss:
  • "O, I am fortune's fool!" Romeo blames luck. Mercutio blames both families. Who or what do you blame for Mercutio's death?

Part 3 — "Thus with a kiss I die"

Desperate, Juliet ran to Friar Laurence. He gave her a plan and a small bottle. On the night before the wedding she must drink the potion. For forty-two hours she would lie cold and breathless, as if dead. Her family would carry her to the Capulet tomb; meanwhile he would send a letter to Mantua, and Romeo would be waiting in the tomb when she woke, to take her away. Juliet took the bottle, and that night, alone and full of dread, she drank.

The plan worked — until the letter. The friar who carried it was shut inside a house by officials afraid of the plague, and it never left Verona. But Romeo's servant had seen Juliet laid in the tomb, and rode to Mantua with the news: she was dead. Romeo did not hesitate. He bought a deadly poison from a poor chemist, rode through the night, and broke open the tomb. Paris, who had come to mourn, tried to stop him; they fought, and Paris died.

Inside, Romeo found Juliet lying as if asleep, still beautiful, her lips still red. He kissed her one last time and drank the poison: "Thus with a kiss I die." Minutes later, Juliet woke. Friar Laurence rushed in — too late — and begged her to come away, but at the sound of the night watch he fled. Juliet found the empty cup in Romeo's hand. She kissed his lips, hoping some poison was left on them. Then she took his dagger — "O happy dagger!" — and stabbed herself.

At dawn the two families stood together among the dead. Lord Capulet and Lord Montague took each other's hands; they promised to raise golden statues of each other's child. The Prince had the final word: "For never was a story of more woe than this of Juliet and her Romeo."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Friar Laurence's plan, and exactly where did it go wrong?

2. How do the two families react at the end?

1. Juliet would drink a potion, seem dead for 42 hours and be placed in the family tomb; a letter would tell Romeo to come and take her away. The letter never arrived — its messenger was locked in because of the plague — so Romeo only heard that she was dead.

2. Over their children's bodies, the fathers are finally reconciled: they shake hands and promise to build golden statues of each other's child.

Discuss:
  • The feud ends — but only after five young people have died: Mercutio, Tybalt, Paris, Romeo and Juliet. Is this a happy ending, a sad ending, or both?

Grammar — Third & Mixed Conditionals

Every tragedy is a chain of "if only"s. The story could have ended differently at a dozen moments — and the grammar for imagining those other endings is the third conditional and the mixed conditional.

Third conditional — an imaginary past, and its imaginary past result:
If + past perfect, would / could / might have + past participle.
• "If the guard had checked the door, the thief wouldn't have got in." (He didn't check; the thief got in.)
• The order can flip, with no comma: "The thief wouldn't have got in if the guard had checked the door."
Mixed conditionals — when past and present cross:
• Past cause → present result: If + past perfect, would + infinitive. "If I had taken the job, I would live in Paris now."
• Present (permanent) cause → past result: If + past simple, would have + past participle. "If she weren't so stubborn, she would have apologised yesterday."
• Formal inversion (very common in written English): drop if and invert — "Had the guard checked the door, …"

1. Write a third conditional for each turning point

1. Romeo's friends took him to the Capulet party. He met Juliet. → If Romeo's friends him to the party, he Juliet.

2. The letter didn't reach Mantua. Romeo didn't know the plan. → If the letter Mantua, Romeo the plan.

3. Juliet woke a few minutes too late. She didn't save Romeo. → If Juliet a few minutes earlier, she Romeo. (use might)

1. If Romeo's friends hadn't taken him to the party, he wouldn't have met Juliet.

2. If the letter had reached Mantua, Romeo would have known the plan.

3. If Juliet had woken a few minutes earlier, she might have saved Romeo.

2. Past cause or present cause? Write the mixed conditional

Decide first which kind it is — then write it. They are deliberately mixed up.

1. Romeo killed Tybalt. Because of that, he is not in Verona now. → If Romeo Tybalt, he in Verona now.

2. Romeo is a rash young man. That's why he bought the poison without waiting. → If Romeo so rash, he before buying poison.

3. The two fathers hate each other. So they didn't notice their children's secret. → If the fathers each other, they their children's secret. (use might)

1. (past cause → present result) If Romeo hadn't killed Tybalt, he would be / would still be in Verona now.

2. (present cause → past result) If Romeo weren't / wasn't so rash, he would have waited before buying poison.

3. (present cause → past result) If the fathers didn't hate each other, they might have noticed their children's secret.

3. Make it formal — rewrite with inversion

Begin each sentence with Had. Then read all three aloud, like a lawyer summing up.

1. If the Prince had executed Romeo, Juliet would never have taken the potion. →

2. If Mercutio had stayed out of the fight, he would have lived. →

3. If Friar Laurence hadn't run away, Juliet might still be alive. →

1. Had the Prince executed Romeo, Juliet would never have taken the potion.

2. Had Mercutio stayed out of the fight, he would have lived.

3. Had Friar Laurence not run away, Juliet might still be alive. (note: the negative is Had … not, never "Hadn't Friar Laurence…")

Explain it in your own words:
  • In one sentence each: when do we use a third conditional, and when do we need a mixed one? Then give one example about your own life — a past choice that still affects you now.

Going deeper — take the idea further

You have the story and the words. Now push the ideas outward — argue, disagree, change your mind. Do all of this out loud. There are no right answers here, only good reasons.

Useful language for building an argument: "What's striking is…", "You could argue that…", "Had they… they would have…", "On one level… but on another…", "That may be true, but…".
1 · Love at first sight

Romeo and Juliet meet, marry and die within about four days. Is love at first sight real, or is it always infatuation that might — or might not — grow into love? What would a modern psychologist say about their decisions?

2 · Fate or choice?

Shakespeare calls them "star-crossed" before the play even begins, so the audience knows they will die. Yet at every step, someone makes a choice. Is the story about bad luck or about bad decisions? Find the single most important choice in the story and defend your answer.

3 · The adults

The parents, the Friar, the Prince — the adults in this story are supposed to protect the young. Which adult fails them most? Is this really a story about teenagers, or a warning to their parents?

4 · Feuds today

Two families hate each other and nobody remembers why. Where do you see the same pattern today — between countries, football clubs, political groups, neighbours? Why are old hatreds so hard to end, and why does it so often take a disaster to end them?
