Leading Without the Org Chart

Behavioural questions are really asking one thing: how do you get things done when nobody has to listen to you?

Today: you’ll work through the language of leading across functions — and sharpen the grammar that makes results and decisions sound deliberate: so that… · which meant… · having aligned… · rather than…
Before we start. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • Think of a project that needed people who did not report to you. What did you need from them, and what did they need from you?
  • When did you last change your plan because someone else’s objection was right? How did it feel?
  • Is it harder to lead a team of specialists who know more than you, or a team that knows less? Why?

The vocabulary of this lesson

Ten items. Work through the activities and say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. Nothing moved until the finance director gave her buy-in: once she believed in the plan, the other departments followed.
buy-in (n, uncountable) — genuine support and commitment from the people who must make a plan work. To get / secure / win buy-in; lack of buy-in. “We needed buy-in from the regional heads.”
2. The launch depended on a single dependency: engineering could not start until the data team had finished its migration.
a dependency (n) — something that must happen first, or something your work relies on. To map / manage dependencies; a critical dependency. Verb: to depend on.
3. She was working at full stretch: the team simply had no bandwidth for a second project this quarter.
bandwidth (n, uncountable) — informal for the time, attention and capacity available to take on work. “I don’t have the bandwidth for that right now.” Borrowed from engineering; very common in offices.
4. Every project needs an executive sponsor: someone senior who protects the budget and removes obstacles when things stall.
a sponsor (n) — a senior person who backs a project, funds it and unblocks it. An executive sponsor; verb to sponsor. A sponsor is not the project manager.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: escalate · push back · align. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which verb means to bring a problem to a more senior person because you cannot resolve it at your level?
to escalate (v) — escalate an issue to the director. Noun: escalation. Not neutral: escalating too early can look like weakness; too late, like concealment.
2. Which phrasal verb means to disagree or resist, politely but firmly?
to push back (on something) — “Engineering pushed back on the deadline.” Noun: pushback. A sign of a healthy team when done constructively.
3. Which verb means to bring people’s goals and plans into agreement?
to align (v) — align stakeholders / priorities / incentives (around a goal). Noun: alignment. “I aligned the three teams around a single target.”

C · Choose the meaning: a / b / c

1. The chief executive said that although engineering was responsible for the build, the programme director was accountable for the outcome. What is the difference?
  (a) they mean the same   (b) accountable means you answer for the result, whoever does the work   (c) accountable means you do the work yourself
(b) — responsible = does the work; accountable = answers for the outcome. In senior interviews, “I was accountable for…” signals ownership of the result.
2. After the merger there was constant friction between the two sales teams. Friction here means…
  (a) a friendly rivalry   (b) tension and resistance that slows people down   (c) a formal complaint procedure
(b) — friction (n) — in the plural or uncountable: friction between teams, to reduce friction. Borrowed from physics.

D · Collocation & word building

1. A person or thing that stops progress is a ___. Complete the noun (it comes from the same word as “block”), then make a sentence about a project you know.
a blocker (n) — “The main blocker was the legal review.” Also a bottleneck (a point where work queues up). Verb: to block.
2. Complete the collocation: to ___ a project’s dependencies means to draw them out so everyone can see what waits for what. Which verb fits, and which two other verbs combine with “dependencies”?
to map dependencies; also manage and untangle. “In week one I mapped the dependencies and found three that nobody owned.”

Now use them: out loud

Discuss:
  • Tell me about a project where you needed buy-in and had a blocker. What did you do before you escalated?
  • Sort the terms by register: which are board-paper language (accountable, dependency, alignment) and which are spoken (bandwidth, push back, friction)? Which could you not say to a client?

The launch three departments could stop

A composite scene, built for teaching: the people and figures are invented, but the pattern is one that senior people describe again and again.

Part 1 — A plan nobody asked for

A programme director is given six months to launch a customer reporting platform. The work depends on three functions — engineering, finance and operations — and none of them reports to her. She has a mandate from the chief executive, a sponsor, and a budget of about two million euros.

She spends the first two weeks writing an excellent plan: milestones, owners, dependencies. At the kick-off she presents it and asks for questions. Engineering says it cannot commit to the dates. Finance says the budget was approved for a different scope. Operations says nothing at all, which is worse.

Three weeks in, the project is behind a plan that nobody believes in. Her sponsor asks her, gently, whether she needs “more authority”. She thinks for a moment and says no.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the programme director have, and what did she lack?

2. Why was operations’ silence a worse sign than the objections of the other two?

1. She had a mandate, a sponsor and a budget of about two million euros, but none of the three functions reported to her.

2. Open objections can be negotiated; silence means the function has not accepted the plan and will quietly not deliver it.

Discuss:
  • Why does a perfect plan often fail to produce commitment?
  • When have you presented a finished plan and asked for “questions”? What would you do differently?

Part 2 — Three meetings and a dashboard

Over the next ten days she sits down with each function separately and asks the same three questions: What are you measured on? What would make this project a problem for you? What would make it a win? She learns that finance is measured on cost control and does not care about the launch date; engineering is measured on stability and dreads a rushed release; operations is measured on customer complaints and is afraid of being blamed for every defect.

She rewrites the plan with the three functions, not for them. Each lead proposes their own milestones. They agree a single target: no increase in customer complaints, delivery within the budget, and a stable release. She builds one weekly dashboard that shows all three measures, and she insists that every meeting begins with it.

Within a month, the dates that engineering “could not commit to” have become engineering’s own dates. Nothing about her formal authority has changed.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What three questions did she ask each function, and what did she learn?

2. What single change made the dates become engineering’s own?

1. What are you measured on, what would make this a problem, and what would make it a win. Finance is measured on cost control, engineering on stability, operations on complaints.

2. She rewrote the plan with the functions, so each lead proposed their own milestones and owned them.

Discuss:
  • What is each function you work with measured on, and how does your plan help or hurt it?
  • Is it manipulative to design a plan around what other people are measured on? Where is the line?

Part 3 — The conflict, the result and the cost

In month three, finance and engineering clash. Engineering wants two more features before the release; finance says they will cost 8% more than the approved budget. Both leads escalate to her within the same hour, each assuming she will take their side.

She calls each lead separately, then both together. She does not decide. She asks the sponsor’s question instead: which of the two features would a customer actually miss? They agree to cut one feature and defer the other. She raises the decision with the sponsor only to record it.

The platform launches two weeks early and about 8% under budget. The client extends the contract by a year. But the head of operations tells a colleague that the project felt “run by a spreadsheet”, and she is not invited to the next programme. Was the cost worth it?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How did she resolve the clash between finance and engineering?

2. What was the cost she paid, despite the successful launch?

1. She asked which of the two features a customer would actually miss; they agreed to cut one and defer the other, and she told the sponsor only to record the decision.

2. The head of operations felt the project was “run by a spreadsheet”, and she was not invited to the next programme.

Discuss:
  • Does influence without authority take longer than authority? Is the result better, or only quieter?
  • When is it right to decide yourself instead of letting two leads resolve it?

Grammar: results, purposes and participle clauses

A strong leadership story is a chain of cause and effect, and the grammar of that chain is what separates a senior answer from a list of tasks.

Purpose: so that, in order to, with a view to. I met each lead separately so that nobody felt ambushed. · I built one dashboard in order to keep the three measures visible. Use so that + a clause (often with could / would in the past); in order to + infinitive.
Result: which meant, resulting in, with the effect that. We cut two features, which meant we stayed within budget. · The change was agreed in a week, resulting in an earlier launch. Which refers to the whole previous clause, so it needs a comma before it.
Participle clauses: Having + past participle, and Rather than + -ing. Having mapped the dependencies, I sequenced the work. · Rather than present a finished plan, I rebuilt it with the three functions. The subject of the participle clause must be the same as the subject of the main clause.

1 · Purpose and result

Complete each sentence with one or two words.

“I met each function separately nobody felt ambushed in the room.”

“We cut one feature, meant we stayed under budget.”

“I introduced a single dashboard to keep all three measures visible.”

“We agreed the dates in a week, in an earlier launch.”

so that · which · in order · resulting

Remember the comma before “which” and “resulting in” when they refer back to the whole idea.

2 · Participle clauses

Put the verb in brackets into the correct form to complete the participle clause.

“ the dependencies, I could sequence the work so that nothing waited for a decision that nobody owned.”

“ a finished plan, I rebuilt it with the three functions.”

“ to each lead, I realised that finance cared about cost, not dates.”

Having mapped · Rather than presenting · Having listened

“Having + past participle” shows that the first action was completed before the second. “Rather than + -ing” contrasts what you did with what you might have done.

3 · A complete leadership answer

A panel asks: “Tell me about a time you led people over whom you had no authority.” Complete the answer.

“I was for a platform launch that depended on three functions, none of which reported to me. presenting a plan, I asked each lead what they were measured on, the plan would support their targets. agreed one target, we built a weekly dashboard. We launched two weeks early, meant the client extended its contract.”

accountable · Rather than · so that · Having · which

Notice the story’s chain: accountability → method → purpose → result. Each connector earns its place.

Going deeper

This is a composite scene, so there is no real balance sheet to check. What follows is the argument about what a leadership question actually tests.

How panels score an answer

Most senior panels score on the same five things, whatever the question. You will use this scorecard to mark your own mock interview in the Speak tab.

CriterionWhat the panel is listening for
StructureThe listener can follow the shape of the answer (headline, steps, result).
EvidenceA specific example, with a number, instead of a general claim.
OwnershipIt is clear what I personally decided or did, and what the team did.
RelevanceIt connects to the role and the organisation I am talking to.
DeliveryCalm pace, no long fillers, finished within the time.

What “tell me about a time you led” is really measuring

Panels use leadership questions to look for three things: whether you can diagnose what people need, whether you can choose an intervention rather than follow a formula, and whether you can own a result you did not control. Most weak answers describe effort; strong ones describe decisions.

Authority is a shortcut, not the point

In a hierarchy, authority lets you skip the question “why would they do this?”. Without it you must answer that question for each person. Seen this way, influence without authority is not a soft skill: it is the discipline of understanding what each person is measured on, rewarded for and afraid of.

Two readings of the same project

The generous reading: she built commitment, so the result was robust and the team would work again. The sceptical reading: the head of operations felt managed; a project can succeed and still leave people resentful. A senior answer admits the cost without apology.

Discuss:
  • What do you do when you realise a colleague is “managing” someone and they can tell?
  • Should leaders ever use authority, even when they could persuade? When?
  • Which of your own project stories does the least credit to you, and why is it also the most useful to tell?

All of it out loud, against the clock. These are the rooms in which a senior leader has to explain a decision: upwards, sideways, down and out.

Useful language: I was accountable for… · My first step was to… · I sought buy-in early… · rather than… I… · so that… · which meant… · as a result… · in hindsight, I would…

  5. Say it a last time in forty-five seconds, without notes.

Which answer would you trust?

Decide before you click, and say why.

“Tell me about a time you led a difficult project.”
A“We had six weeks to launch a service that normally takes four months. I split the work into three tracks, put a lead on each, and held a daily fifteen-minute review. We launched on time, with a 12% saving against budget.”
B“It was difficult and stressful but the team worked very hard and in the end it was fine and everyone was happy.”
A has the situation, the specific actions and a measurable result. B contains no decision, no detail and no evidence.
“How do you get people to follow you when you have no authority?”
A“I explain very clearly what has to be done and I insist until they do it.”
B“I start by finding out what each person is measured on, then show how the project helps them hit those targets. I involve them in shaping the plan, so by the time we start, it’s partly theirs.”
B shows influence through their interests and involvement. A relies on insisting, which only works when you have authority.
“Tell me about a project that did not go well.”
A“We missed the deadline by three weeks. I had underestimated the dependency on another team. I now map dependencies in week one, and my last three projects have all been on time.”
B“The client changed the requirements, the other department was slow, and the budget was too small, so it failed because of them.”
B blames others. A owns the mistake, names the cause and shows what changed, which is the learning an interviewer wants.

Task C · Four rooms, one escalation (4 minutes)

A key dependency has failed and the launch will slip by three weeks. Say it four ways, one minute each. Notice what you add and what you leave out.

  • To your sponsor: the facts, the options and your recommendation.
  • To the other function lead: candid, without blame.
  • To your own team: honest, and clear about what changes for them this week.
  • To the client: accurate, with a recovery plan.

Prefer a different situation? Take one of these instead:

  • A software release delayed by a supplier’s security audit.
  • A regional rollout that cannot start because local legal has not approved the contract.
  • A merger integration where two teams have not agreed who owns the customer data.
