Evidence beats adjectives

Senior interviewers rarely believe “I’m a strong leader.” They believe a specific story with a decision, a result and a lesson.

Language you can reuse

Each card describes a moment in the interview. Decide what you would say, then check the phrases.

Situation: You set the scene and your responsibility in one or two sentences.

Say how you would handle it first.

  • “At the time, we were facing [challenge].”
  • “I was accountable for [scope].”
  • “The brief I had been given was to [goal].”
  • “The difficulty was that [constraint].”
Situation: You explain the actions you personally took.

Say how you would handle it first.

  • “My first step was to [action].”
  • “I brought together [groups] to [purpose].”
  • “I made a deliberate decision to [choice].”
  • “I delegated [task] so that I could focus on [priority].”
  • “I aligned everyone around a single target.”
Situation: You report the result with a measurable number.

Say how you would handle it first.

  • “As a result, we cut [cost / time] by 30%.”
  • “We delivered two weeks ahead of schedule and 8% under budget.”
  • “This translated into [business impact].”
  • “The outcome was [result], and the client [reaction].”

If you do not have exact figures, use honest estimates: “roughly”, “in the region of”, “over a third”.

Situation: You need to persuade people who do not report to you.

Say how you would handle it first.

  • “I made the case by linking it to their own priorities.”
  • “I sought buy-in early rather than presenting a finished plan.”
  • “I took time to understand what each function needed.”
  • “I framed it as a shared problem, not my agenda.”
Situation: You describe a disagreement and how you handled it.

Say how you would handle it first.

  • “There was a genuine difference of view between [A] and [B].”
  • “I raised it directly but privately.”
  • “I brought the conversation back to the shared goal.”
  • “We agreed to disagree on [point] but commit to [decision].”
  • “I escalated only once we had exhausted the options.”
Situation: You reflect on what you would do differently.

Say how you would handle it first.

  • “In hindsight, I would have involved [group] earlier.”
  • “What I took from it was [lesson].”
  • “I now make a point of [habit].”

A short, honest reflection makes a story more credible, not weaker.

Upgrade the sentence

Rewrite each sentence so it sounds like a confident senior candidate. Then compare.

Make it sound senior: “We had a problem in the team and I solved it.”
Two functions were blocking each other on priorities, so I brought both leads together, agreed a single shared target, and the delays disappeared within a month.
Make it sound senior: “I told everyone what to do.”
I set the direction and the outcomes, then let people decide how to deliver them.
Make it sound senior: “The result was good.”
We reduced delivery time by 30 per cent, and the client extended the contract by two years.
Make it sound senior: “I had a conflict with a colleague but it was fine in the end.”
A colleague and I disagreed about priorities. I asked to talk privately, listened to their constraints, and we agreed a compromise that protected both our deadlines.

Same question, two answers

Read both answers. Decide which one an interviewer would rate higher, and say why before you click.

“Tell me about a time you led a difficult project.”
A“We had six weeks to launch a service that normally takes four months. I split the work into three tracks, put a lead on each, and held a daily fifteen-minute review. We launched on time, with a 12% saving against budget.”
B“It was difficult and stressful but the team worked very hard and in the end it was fine and everyone was happy.”
A has the situation, the specific actions and a measurable result. B contains no decision, no detail and no evidence.
“How do you get people to follow you when you have no authority?”
A“I explain very clearly what has to be done and I insist until they do it.”
B“I start by finding out what each person is measured on, then show how the project helps them hit those targets. I involve them in shaping the plan, so by the time we start, it’s partly theirs.”
B shows influence through their interests and involvement. A relies on insisting, which only works when you have authority.
“Tell me about a project that did not go well.”
A“We missed the deadline by three weeks. I had underestimated the dependency on another team. I now map dependencies in week one, and my last three projects have all been on time.”
B“The client changed the requirements, the other department was slow, and the budget was too small, so it failed because of them.”
B blames others. A owns the mistake, names the cause and shows what changed, which is the learning an interviewer wants.

Interview question

“Tell me about a time you led a cross-functional team through a difficult project.”

Build your answer in three parts

Situation and task: “A client needed a new reporting platform in five months, and the work depended on engineering, finance and operations, none of whom reported to me. I was accountable for delivery and for keeping the three groups aligned.”

Actions: “First, I met each function separately to understand its constraints. Then I agreed one shared goal and a single weekly dashboard. When engineering and finance disagreed on scope, I raised it privately with both leads and we agreed to cut two features and protect the deadline.”

Result and reflection: “We delivered two weeks early and 8% under budget, and the client extended the contract by a year. In hindsight, I would have brought operations in a month earlier.”

Notice: roughly 20% situation, 50% actions, 30% result. The actions use “I” and the result uses numbers.

Speak

Close the notes and give your answer aloud in about two minutes. Then give it a second time with one improvement.

Answer in two or three sentences: “How do you handle a team member who consistently underperforms?”

“I start with a private conversation to understand the cause, because it is often unclear expectations or an obstacle I can remove. Then we agree specific goals and a review date. If there is still no improvement, I involve HR early and act decisively, because the rest of the team is watching how I respond.”

Question bank

Choose three questions you find hardest. Prepare a structure for each, then answer them aloud.

Leading teams

  1. Tell me about a time you led a cross-functional team through a difficult project.
  2. How do you build a high-performing team from people who have never worked together?
  3. Describe your leadership style, and give an example of when you had to change it.
  4. How do you develop the people who report to you?

Influence and conflict

  1. Tell me about a time you persuaded people who did not report to you.
  2. Describe a disagreement with a senior colleague. How did you handle it?
  3. How do you manage stakeholders who want different things?
  4. Tell me about a time you had to deliver bad news to a senior audience.

Results and learning

  1. What is the most significant result you have delivered, and how did you measure it?
  2. Tell me about a project that did not go as planned.
  3. How do you review the quality of work from your team before it reaches a client?
  4. How do you decide what to delegate and what to keep?
