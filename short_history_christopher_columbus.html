Christopher Columbus

The navigator who was wrong about almost everything, reached a continent he never accepted he had found, and is still being argued about five centuries later.

Today: you'll tell the story of a man whose plan rested on a badly wrong map, whose critics were right all along, and whose landfall changed the world anyway — and you'll master corrective clefts and myth-busting language, the structures English uses to say it wasn't him who did that and what actually happened was this.

Now — the words for today's story

Today's ten words come in pairs, each beside a plainer word you already know. Say the pair aloud and answer the question: how does the new word differ — stronger, more formal, more precise, more critical? Grade it, then reveal.

1. to get a number wrong → to underestimate
Only one tells you which way the error went. Which — and why does that matter here?
to underestimate (v) — to judge something to be smaller, weaker or easier than it really is. Not just wrong: the error is always too low. Opposite: to overestimate.
2. a supporter → a patron
A supporter only has to agree with you. What does a patron have as well?
a patron (n) — a wealthy or powerful person who funds and protects an artist, explorer or project. More formal than supporter: a patron pays.
3. to ask for something → to petition
Which would you use in a shop, and which at a royal court? What does that tell you about the asker?
to petition (v) — to make a formal, often repeated request to an authority with the power to refuse. Far more formal than ask, and the asker is the weaker party.
4. arrival → landfall
Arrival works for a bus. What must be true before you can call something a landfall?
landfall (n) — the first sighting of, or arrival at, land after a sea voyage. A technical, seafaring word — narrower and more vivid than arrival.
5. local → indigenous
Everyone living in a place is local. Which word do historians choose for the peoples who were there first, and why is that choice careful?
indigenous (adj) — native to a particular place, especially of peoples living there before colonists arrived. Formal and precise; it carries a claim about who came first.
6. to control → to subjugate
A parent controls a child. Grade the second word: how much force does it add?
to subjugate (v) — to defeat a people and force them into obedience and servitude. Far stronger than control, and openly critical. Noun: subjugation.
7. to prove someone right → to vindicate
What has to have happened before you can be vindicated?
to vindicate (v) — to show in the end that someone was right or blameless after being doubted or blamed. Formal: it needs an earlier accusation to make sense.
8. bad → catastrophic
Put these on a scale: bad — serious — severe — catastrophic. What must be true for the last to be honest rather than dramatic?
catastrophic (adj) — causing sudden, enormous and often irreversible damage or loss of life. The top of the scale.
9. what someone left behind → a legacy
Is a legacy automatically a good thing? Try mixed, poisonous and lasting in front of it.
a legacy (n) — what a person, event or period leaves behind for those who come after. Neutral in itself — the adjective does the judging.
10. famous → notorious
Both mean widely known. Which could never be a compliment?
notorious (adj) — famous for something bad. The same reach as famous, the opposite flavour: a notorious prison.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, a quick check (try it from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The wrong map

He was born in the Republic of Genoa in or around 1451, a wool weaver's son, in a city where the sea was the obvious way out of a small workshop. Cristoforo Colombo — Cristóbal Colón to the Spanish, Christopher Columbus to the English-speaking world — went to sea young, and by his twenties had settled in Portugal, then the sharpest navigational culture in Europe. By the traditional account he arrived there after a sea fight off the coast, swimming ashore from a burning ship. In Lisbon and on Madeira he sailed with Portuguese crews, traded down the African coast, and taught himself Latin so that he could read the geographers. His surviving books are heavily marked in the margins. It is a myth that he was an uneducated dreamer with nothing but a hunch: he was a capable, self-taught seaman who read everything he could find. The truth is stranger. The more he read, the more confidently he built an argument that was, in almost every measurable respect, wrong — and the more certain he became that God had chosen him to carry it out.

Here is the myth that has to go first. It is not true that Columbus set out to prove the earth was round, and it is not true that learned Europeans thought it was flat. Educated opinion had accepted a spherical earth since Greek antiquity; Eratosthenes had measured it with impressive accuracy, and every navigator who had watched a ship's hull vanish before its mast knew what shape the sea lay on. What was genuinely disputed was not the shape but the size. Columbus took the smallest estimate of the earth's circumference he could find, combined it with the most generous estimate of how far Asia stretched eastwards, and squeezed the numbers further still. On his map, Japan sat a few thousand miles west of the Canaries — a hard voyage, but a possible one. In reality that ocean was vastly wider, and no ship of the period could have carried enough water and food to cross it to Asia. He did not slightly underestimate the distance. He underestimated it by roughly a factor of three.

This is why he was refused, again and again, and why the refusals were the sensible decisions. In the mid-1480s he put his scheme to King John II of Portugal, whose expert committee examined the arithmetic and rejected it. He crossed into Spain and began to petition Ferdinand and Isabella, who passed the plan to a commission of scholars at Salamanca; they too concluded that his figures were unsound, and they too were right. For years he waited, argued, and sent his brother to sound out England and France. The old picture of a lone visionary shouted down by superstitious churchmen belongs to romantic fiction, not to the record. What actually happened was a competent technical review reaching a correct technical conclusion. The experts told him the world was too big. It was, and it took an unsuspected landmass in the way to save him. What he needed was not a better argument but a patron with a reason to gamble — and in 1492 the Spanish crown suddenly had one.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the real disagreement between Columbus and the experts?

2. Why is it fair to say the Portuguese and Spanish commissions were right to reject him?

1. Not the shape of the earth — educated opinion accepted it was a sphere — but its size. He took the smallest figure for the circumference and the largest for the eastward extent of Asia, so his ocean was far too narrow.

2. His arithmetic really was wrong: the distance to Asia was roughly three times what he claimed, and no ship of the day could have crossed it. Only the unexpected Americas saved the voyage.

  • His critics' correct calculation was cancelled out by his own huge mistake. Does that make him lucky, brave or reckless — and does the result change your answer?

Part 2 — Landfall, and what followed

1492 was the year Spain finished one long project and began another. In January, Granada fell and the centuries-long Reconquista was complete; in March the same monarchs signed the decree expelling Spain's Jews, and tens of thousands left that spring. A crown flushed with victory, in debt and hungry for a route to the spice trade was suddenly willing to fund a cheap gamble, and the terms Columbus extracted were extraordinary: the title of Admiral of the Ocean Sea, the governorship of whatever he found, and a tenth of the wealth. Three ships left Palos in August — the Niña, the Pinta and the flagship Santa María — refitted in the Canaries, then sailed west into an empty Atlantic for five weeks. The crews grew frightened as the days ran past every distance he had promised. Land was sighted in the small hours of 12 October 1492. Landfall was made on an island in the Bahamas whose indigenous Taíno inhabitants called it Guanahani; Columbus renamed it San Salvador. Which island it actually was, historians still cannot agree.

He believed he was in the outer islands of Asia, and he never stopped believing it. Sailing on to Cuba and then to the island he named Hispaniola, he lost the Santa María on a reef on Christmas Day, used her timbers to build a small fort called La Navidad, and left around forty men there for want of room. He returned to Spain a celebrity. The second voyage, in 1493, was a different thing altogether: seventeen ships and well over a thousand settlers, sent to occupy rather than to explore. At La Navidad he found the fort burnt and every man he had left dead. What followed on Hispaniola was subjugation in the plainest sense. Gold had been promised to the crown, so a tribute system was imposed on the Taíno: adults had to deliver a fixed quantity of gold at fixed intervals, and those who failed were mutilated or killed. It became notorious even in its own time. Captives were shipped to Spain to be sold, and the island's indigenous population began to collapse.

It was not Spanish outrage over the Taíno that brought him down, and that is worth saying plainly — it was the complaints of the Spanish colonists themselves. Columbus proved a disastrous governor: autocratic, secretive, unable to control his men, and free with the gallows when they rebelled. Settlers promised gold who had found fever and hunger sent furious reports home. In 1500 the crown despatched an investigator, Francisco de Bobadilla, who arrested Columbus and his brothers and sent them home in chains — a humiliation the Admiral pointedly refused to have removed until he stood before the king. He was released and his wealth largely restored, but the governorship was gone for good. A fourth voyage in 1502 took him along the coast of Central America and left him marooned on Jamaica for a year. He died in Valladolid in 1506, wealthy, aggrieved, and still insisting to anyone who would listen that he had reached the outskirts of Asia. On that last point, the geographers who had turned him down were entirely vindicated.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What two other things happened in Spain in 1492, and how did they help Columbus?

2. Why was Columbus arrested and sent home in chains?

1. Granada fell, completing the Reconquista, and the decree expelling Spain's Jews was signed. A victorious, indebted crown was in the mood to gamble on a cheap route to the spice trade.

2. Because of the Spanish colonists' own complaints about his rule on Hispaniola: he was an autocratic, incompetent governor. The crown sent Bobadilla, who arrested him in 1500.

Discuss:
  • He was punished for governing Spaniards badly, not for what was done to the Taíno. What does that tell you about whose suffering counted, and about how history gets recorded?

Part 3 — The exchange, and the argument

The voyages mattered far beyond anything Columbus intended or understood, and historians group the consequences under a single phrase: the Columbian Exchange. After 1492 two biological worlds that had been separate for thousands of years were joined. Eastwards travelled maize, potatoes, tomatoes, cassava, beans, chillies, cacao and tobacco — crops that would feed and reshape Europe, Africa and Asia, and without which Italian, Irish, Indian and West African cooking would be unrecognisable. Westwards came wheat, sugar cane, coffee, cattle, pigs and horses, which transformed the landscapes and the economies of two continents. And westwards, invisibly, came smallpox, measles, typhus and influenza, against which indigenous American populations had no acquired immunity. The result was catastrophic on a scale that is genuinely difficult to hold in the mind. Epidemics ran ahead of the Europeans themselves, emptying villages before any soldier arrived. Scholars dispute the pre-contact population figures fiercely and estimates vary enormously, but none seriously doubt that the loss ran into many millions, approaching total collapse in some regions within a century.

Two more myths are worth clearing away. First, he was not the first European to reach the Americas. Around the year 1000, Norse sailors from Greenland and Iceland built a small settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland — excavated in the 1960s, and now firmly established archaeology. That is roughly five centuries before Columbus. What was different about 1492 was not priority but permanence: the Norse contact faded, while the Spanish crossing was followed by ships, settlers, colonies and an unbroken link that has never closed. Second, the flat-earth story. It owes an enormous amount to Washington Irving, whose romantic and partly invented biography of 1828 gave the world the unforgettable scene of a lone genius facing down flat-earth clerics — a scene in schoolbooks ever since. Even the naming went elsewhere: the two continents took their name not from Columbus but from Amerigo Vespucci, a Florentine navigator who grasped, as Columbus never did, that this was a new landmass altogether, and whose name a German mapmaker printed across it in 1507.

Which leaves the argument, and it is a real one. For centuries Columbus was a hero of nerve and navigation; in the United States he became more, a patron figure for Italian and Catholic immigrants who were themselves despised, and Columbus Day was made a federal holiday in the 1930s partly for that reason. Against this stands the record of what began in 1492: enslavement, subjugation and demographic catastrophe, and the fact that the man himself was arrested for misrule by his own side. From the late 1980s, American states and cities began replacing or twinning the holiday with Indigenous Peoples' Day, and in 2020 statues of Columbus were pulled down, beheaded or quietly removed in a dozen cities. Defenders answer that judging a fifteenth-century sailor by twenty-first-century standards is a category error, and that erasing him flattens history rather than teaching it. Critics reply that no one is obliged to keep celebrating, and that a statue is an honour, not a lesson. Both sides are arguing about the same legacy: the moment the world's halves were locked together, for everyone who came after.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the Columbian Exchange, and what was its most destructive element?

2. Where does the "flat earth" story really come from, and who gave America its name?

1. The vast two-way transfer of crops, animals, people and diseases between the Americas and the rest of the world after 1492. The most destructive element was disease — smallpox, measles, typhus, influenza — against which indigenous populations had no immunity, causing losses in the millions.

2. Largely from Washington Irving's romantic 1828 biography, not from the historical record. The continents were named after Amerigo Vespucci, who realised it was a new landmass, and whose name appeared on a mapmaker's chart in 1507.

Discuss:
  • Is it fair to judge a fifteenth-century man by today's moral standards — and if not, does that mean we must keep his statues up?

Grammar — corrective cleft sentences and myth-busting language

Almost everything most people "know" about Columbus needs correcting, and English has neat machinery for that job. To knock down a wrong idea and put the right one in its place, you split the sentence and push the corrected part into the spotlight: It wasn't Columbus who discovered the earth was round…, What they actually argued about was the size of the ocean…, It's a myth that the experts were superstitious fools. This is the language of the well-informed correction, and it is everywhere in podcasts and good history writing.

How the structures are built:
It-cleft: It + was / wasn't + the focus + who / that + the rest. → "It wasn't Columbus who reached the Americas first — it was the Norse."
Wh-cleft: What + subject + verb + was / is + the focus. → "What actually happened was that he underestimated the ocean."
Myth-busting frames: "It's a myth that …", "Contrary to popular belief, …", "Far from being a visionary, he was …", "Not X but Y: the dispute wasn't about the shape but the size."
When we choose them:
• to correct one element while accepting the rest: "It wasn't the Church that rejected his plan — it was a committee of navigators."
• to delay and highlight the key information: "What saved him was a continent he didn't know existed."
• to flag a claim as false before replacing it: "It's a myth that he died in poverty."
• Agreement: who or that for people, that for things; after What actually happened the verb is singular — was, never were.

1. Turn the correction into a cleft

3. People say Columbus gave his name to America — but Vespucci did.

Notice the rhythm: name the false idea, knock it down, put the corrected fact in the spotlight. That three-step move makes a correction sound informed rather than rude.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Columbus was wrong about the size of the earth, wrong about where he had landed, and wrong to the end about what he had found. Does being wrong for the right reasons deserve any credit?
  2. The Columbian Exchange fed billions and killed millions. Can a single historical event honestly be weighed as good or bad, or is that the wrong question to ask of history?
  3. Washington Irving's invented scene has outlived the truth by two hundred years. Why do good stories beat accurate ones — and what should teachers and writers do about it?
