The Big Favour

You need a room for six weeks. Your friend has one. They'll say yes — that's the problem. Now you have to ask in a way that lets them say no.

The Scene 🎬

The flat fell through on Tuesday. Not "delayed" — fell through, the landlord's daughter is moving in instead, sorry, here's your deposit back. You start the new job on the third and the job is here, in this city, and the earliest anything decent comes up is September. Which leaves a six-week hole where your home should be.

Kit has a spare room. Kit is one of your oldest friends, and that is precisely the problem. Because Kit will say yes. Kit will say yes in about a second and a half, before they've thought about the noise, or the kitchen, or the fact that their washing machine is in there — and then they'll live with the consequences for six weeks and never once mention it. Good friends are terrible at refusing. That's what makes them good friends, and it's exactly why asking one for something big is dangerous.

So tonight, in the pub, you're going to ask. And most of what you say won't be the ask at all. It'll be the scaffolding around it: the warning, the exact dates, the exit. Because a yes is only worth having if a no was genuinely available — and Kit can't build that no on their own. You have to build it for them, before they need it.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. What's the biggest favour anyone has ever asked you? Did you want to do it — and did they ever find out either way?
  2. Is there someone in your life you'd never ask for anything, precisely because you know they'd say yes? What does that protect, and what does it cost?

Who's who

You
The One Doing the Asking 🎒

Six weeks homeless from the third, and one friend with a spare room. You've rehearsed this on the walk over, twice. You want the room — but not at the price of Kit quietly hating you in it.

The other voice
Kit, the Good Friend 🔑

Generous by reflex, which means the word "yes" arrives long before any actual thinking does. If they ever have a reservation, they tend to keep it, politely, for years.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch the order of it. Long before Kit has to be brave, the asker has already handed them the dates, the end point, the permission, and the way out — so that when the answer comes, whatever it is, it costs Kit nothing to give. Count how much work happens before the actual question.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Kit agrees almost instantly, before hearing what the favour even is. The asker doesn't take that yes. Why not — and what do they make Kit do instead?

2. Once Kit actually thinks, they give a real reason — and then a story about someone called Sam. What was the reason, and what did the Sam story reveal about Kit?

3. Before Kit answered at all, the asker had already given three hard pieces of information about the favour. What were they?

1. Because it wasn't an answer — Kit said yes on reflex, out of friendship, without knowing the size of the thing. A yes like that can't be trusted, since it would have been a yes to anything. So the asker refuses it and tells Kit to sleep on it, which forces a real decision rather than a kind one.
2. The spare room is where Kit works — the desk is in there and it's the only room with a door. The Sam story shows Kit is someone who says nothing for months and then loses it over something small, which is exactly why a comfortable yes from Kit would have been dangerous for both of them.
3. Six weeks; starting on the third; and out by the middle of August whatever happens. Kit therefore knew the whole shape of it — the size, the start and the end — before having to decide anything.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for asking someone for something big without trapping them. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Get the weight right. A big ask is unusually easy to ruin with tone. Throw it away too lightly — a laugh, a shrug, "no worries either way!" — and it sounds as though you don't really mean the exit you're offering, so your friend hears "please" underneath anyway. Load it too heavily — long pauses, wounded eyes, the whole story of your terrible week — and the exit disappears completely, because now refusing you would be cruel. The tone you want is somewhere in between and rather flatter than feels natural: steady, unhurried, almost administrative about the facts, warm only at the edges. You are handing someone a decision, not a performance.

⚠️ Warning them it's coming

Phrase 1

You're about to ask for something big. Before a word of the actual favour, you want to hand your friend permission to refuse — while they're still calm, and before they can feel cornered. Which opener does that?

A. "So I need a massive favour and you're literally my only hope."
B. "I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to know the answer can be no."
C. "Can I ask you something? You can always say no obviously, ha, don't worry about it."
B — "I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to know the answer can be no."
A closes the exit before it opens, because "my only hope" makes refusal a kind of abandonment. C offers the exit but drops it in as a joke, so it doesn't count. B is slow and deliberate, and it arrives first — the permission lands while your friend is still relaxed, rather than being handed to them once they're already trapped.
Phrase 2

Your friend has opened their mouth to agree and you can see they haven't thought about it. You want to take the decision off the table for tonight and send it home with them. There's an English idiom that uses a night's rest to mean "take your time and decide later". Complete it: "Don't answer now. Genuinely — ___ on it."

"Don't answer now. Genuinely — sleep on it."
To sleep on something is to delay the decision until the next day, on the assumption that you'll see it more clearly once the moment and the mood have passed. Here it does a particular job: it removes the pressure of your face being right there. The word "genuinely" is doing real work too, because "sleep on it" is often said as a politeness, and you need them to hear that you actually mean it.

📐 Being specific, because vague asks are a trap

Phrase 3

An ask with no edges is impossible to refuse, because your friend can't see what they'd be refusing. So you give the size and the start date, and then you close the ask off so they know nothing else is coming. Put the pieces in order:

the third.That's theSix weeks.whole ask.From
"Six weeks. From the third. That's the whole ask."
Three short pieces of information, and then a full stop. "That's the whole ask" means there is nothing further — this is the complete request, and it's there because your friend is otherwise sitting waiting for the rest of it. People who are braced for more can't decide about what they've already heard. Take the bracing away and they can actually look at the thing.
Phrase 4

The real fear with a houseguest isn't the six weeks, it's week nine. You want to promise an end date that doesn't depend on your plans working out — even if the next flat collapses too, you're gone. Complete it: "I'd be out by the middle of August ___ ___."

"I'd be out by the middle of August whatever happens."
"Whatever happens" is what turns a hope into a promise, because it hands over the one thing your friend is quietly worried about — that your six weeks becomes their problem indefinitely. Without those two words the sentence is only a plan, and plans, as they know perfectly well, fall through. That's rather the reason you're in their pub asking.

🚪 Making the no real

Phrase 5

Your friend's real worry isn't tonight — it's what a refusal would cost them afterwards: the coolness, the slight distance, the thing that never quite gets mentioned again. Which line kills that worry?

A. "Honestly, it's completely up to you, no pressure at all."
B. "Say no and I'll never mention it again — I mean that."
C. "I'd understand if you said no. I'd be gutted, but I'd understand."
B — "Say no and I'll never mention it again — I mean that."
A is the standard formula and your friend has heard it a hundred times, so it carries no information. C attaches a price to the no — "gutted" — which is quiet pressure wearing a kind face. B makes a specific promise about the future, which is where the real cost of refusing lives, and it's a promise your friend can check you on later.
Phrase 6

You'd rather have a clean refusal tonight than a soft "let's see how it goes" that leaves you both managing it for a month. You want to say that anything short of a real yes should simply count as a refusal, and that you won't mind. Complete it: "If it's a ___, that's a ___, and that's completely fine."

"If it's a maybe, that's a no, and that's completely fine."
This one gives away a hiding place, on purpose. "Maybe" is where reluctant people go when they can't face refusing, and it costs everyone weeks. By declaring in advance that you'll read a maybe as a no, you make the soft option pointless — which sounds hard, but is a kindness, because it means your friend never has to work up to a conversation they've been dreading.

🫣 Naming the awkward part yourself

Phrase 7

Your friend is thinking something they'd never say: that this is their place, and you'd be in it. You want to say it for them, so they don't have to feel selfish for thinking it. Complete it: "I know what I'm asking. It's ___ ___."

"I know what I'm asking. It's your house."
Three words, and they hand your friend their own home back. The unsaid thought in the room is that this is their space and their rules, and while they'd feel mean saying so, they'd feel worse if you seemed not to have noticed. Saying it yourself proves you understand the size of the thing, which is oddly the only way to be trusted with it.
Phrase 8

You want to say plainly that a reluctant yes is worse for you than a refusal — because you'd rather not have the thing than have it and be quietly resented for it. Put the pieces in order:

"I'd rather lose the room than have you resent me in it."
To resent someone is to hold a slow grudge you never quite say out loud, which is exactly what a reluctant yes produces. The sentence ranks the two outcomes openly and puts the friendship above the favour — and because it costs you something real to say, it's believable in a way that "no pressure!" never is.

🍺 Handling the answer

Phrase 9

They've said no, and they feel awful about it. Everything you promised now has to be true within about two seconds. Which reply actually delivers on the exit you built?

A. "No, no, don't be silly, it's fine, honestly, it's fine, it's fine."
B. "That's honestly fine. I'd have said no too."
C. "Ah. Okay. No, that's fair enough, I suppose."
B — "That's honestly fine. I'd have said no too."
A is technically forgiving but the panic in it makes the moment bigger, and your friend ends up comforting you. C has gone cold — "I suppose" is where the grudge starts. B does something the others don't: the second sentence puts you in their chair and agrees with their decision, which tells them they haven't failed a test, because there was no test.
Phrase 10

The refusal has landed and your friend is still sitting in it, waiting to see whether things have changed between you. You want to shut the subject down and hand them something completely ordinary to answer — a short line to end it, and then a small everyday question. You're in a pub. In your own words, what do you say?

e.g. "Right — that's that. What are you having?"
"That's that" means the matter is closed and won't be reopened, and "Right —" is the sound of someone changing the subject on purpose. What makes it work is the second half: an easy, dull question that gives your friend something normal to say. You can promise you'll never mention it again, but nobody believes a promise about the future until they watch you keep it, and this is you keeping it while they watch.

🔑 Make them yours

An ask that leaves a real exit is a rare thing to receive, and rarer still to give. Pin three of these to something in your own life, so they're there when you need them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to know the answer can be no." — something you've been putting off asking someone. What is it, and who?

"Six weeks. From the third. That's the whole ask." — now make that same ask specific. Size, timing, and where it ends.

"I'd rather lose the room than have you resent me in it." — what would you genuinely rather go without, than get it and be quietly resented for it?

The Twist

🙂 The yes with a hook in it

One more round — no script. This time Kit says yes. And then, halfway through the second drink, adds the condition: yes, but the room's the office, so you'd be on the sofa. Or yes, but only if you're out every weekend. Or yes, but Kit's brother is there in July too. It's still a yes. It's just not the favour you asked for, and you don't like it — and now you're the one who has to consider refusing a friend who has just done you an enormous kindness. Start the timer. Can you turn down a favour without turning down the person? One rule: no sulking and no swallowing it. You either say the true thing or you take the sofa gladly.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I told them the answer could be no before I told them what the favour was.
  • My ask had real edges — how long, from when, and when it ends whatever happens.
  • I refused their reflex yes instead of pocketing it.
  • I said the awkward part myself, so they didn't have to.
  • When the answer came, my voice didn't change — no panic, no chill.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

"Of course!" arrives faster than thought, which is rather the trouble with it. It's generous in the moment and expensive later, because nothing was actually decided — and the person who said it will be paying for it in small instalments for weeks, quietly, without ever mentioning the bill.

A favour you couldn't have refused isn't really a gift. It's a debt with a nice ribbon on it, and both of you can feel the weight of it long after the six weeks are up. The kindness, oddly, is in the exit: build someone a proper way out and whatever they choose is theirs, which is the only thing that makes a yes worth having.

And then there are the people we've quietly stopped asking. Not because they'd refuse — because they wouldn't, and we couldn't bear what it would cost them. It feels like consideration. It looks, from where they're standing, a lot like being left out.

Two bigger questions

  1. Is there anyone who has never asked you for anything? What do you think that means — and would you rather they did?
  2. Some cultures treat the big ask as ordinary and the refusal as ordinary too; others treat asking itself as an imposition, so nobody asks and nobody has to refuse. Which do you live in — and which would you choose?
