The Keyword Method

Speak from ideas, not from scripts

🎯 Fluency

The Preparation Trap

You can express complex ideas. You have the grammar and vocabulary. But there's a trap that many learners fall into: the more capable you become, the more you prepare — and the more you prepare, the more you read.

Here's what that looks like. Imagine the question is: "Do you think social media has changed the way people communicate?"

📝 Way 1: Full Notes

"In my opinion, social media has fundamentally changed communication. On the one hand, it allows people to stay connected across distances. On the other hand, many argue that the quality of interaction has decreased because people tend to have superficial conversations online rather than meaningful face-to-face dialogue."

This sounds impressive — but it's reading, not speaking.

🔑 Way 2: Just Keywords

connected superficial face-to-face

Sees a concept → builds the argument live.

The full-notes version sounds polished — but in a real conversation, nobody gives you time to read. Your brain needs to construct arguments in real time. That's the skill that matters.

🤔 Why is this especially important now? (click to discuss)

At lower levels, the goal was just getting words out. Now, you're expected to structure complex arguments, use hedging language, and respond to counterarguments — all in real time.

If you read a scripted answer, your brain never practises doing this. You sound fluent in the lesson, but in a real conversation — at work, in an interview, with friends — you freeze because you don't have a script.

The keyword method forces your brain to do the hard work: choosing discourse markers, structuring ideas, and self-correcting — all from just a few anchor words.

Today's Goal: Take the keyword method further — using it with abstract topics, longer turns, and more sophisticated language.

Keywords: The Next Level

The rule hasn't changed: maximum 3 keywords per question. But now your keywords should be concept anchors — not just nouns. They can be abstract ideas, contrasts, or even single phrases that trigger a whole line of reasoning.

How it works at this level

Question: "Should governments invest more in renewable energy?"

❌ Don't write this:

"I strongly believe governments should invest more in renewable energy. Firstly, climate change is becoming worse every year. Secondly, renewable energy creates jobs. Furthermore, it reduces dependence on other countries for fossil fuels."

✅ Write this:

climate urgency jobs independence

Then when you speak, your brain selects the right discourse markers, builds the argument structure, and adds hedging — all live: "climate urgency" → "Well, I'd say the most compelling reason is the urgency of the climate crisis — we simply can't afford to wait..."

Your brain is choosing "compelling," "urgency," and "we can't afford to" in real time. That's the practice that builds real fluency.

More examples

"What are the pros and cons of working from home?"

Keywords:

flexibility isolation blur boundaries

From these 3 concepts: "I think the biggest advantage is the flexibility it gives you — you can organise your day around your own needs. Having said that, there's a real risk of isolation, especially if you live alone. And perhaps the most underestimated issue is how the boundaries between work and personal life tend to blur."

Notice how your brain added "having said that," "the most underestimated issue," and "tend to" — none of that was in the keywords. That's the skill.

"How has technology changed education?"

Keywords:

access distraction teacher role

From these: "The most obvious change is access — students anywhere in the world can now take courses from top universities. On the flip side, technology brings constant distraction, which arguably undermines deep learning. But what I find most interesting is how the teacher's role has shifted from being a source of information to more of a guide or facilitator."

Three keywords produced a structured, nuanced argument. Your brain chose "arguably," "on the flip side," and "what I find most interesting" — all in real time.

💡 Tip:

Your keywords don't have to be single words. A concept like "blur boundaries" or "teacher role" is one keyword — it's a thought trigger, not a vocabulary item. The goal is: see the concept, build the argument.

The Keyword Ladder

Take one complex topic — "How has globalisation affected your country?" — and speak about it five times, removing keywords each round. Watch your brain take over.

🤔 What happened at 0 keywords? (click after trying)

By the fifth round, you had already explored the topic from multiple angles. Your brain had activated vocabulary, built argument structures, and practised transitions — all without knowing it.

The ladder didn't just reduce keywords — it built a mental map of the topic. By the time you reached zero, you weren't starting from nothing. You were drawing on everything the previous rounds had activated.

This is how expert speakers work. They don't memorise — they activate ideas and let their language system do the construction.

The New Standard

From now on, when you prepare for a lesson:

1. Read the prep pack — absorb the vocabulary and ideas.

2. For each discussion question, write maximum 3 concept keywords.

3. Before the lesson, practise speaking from the keywords out loud — at least 45 seconds per question. Not in your head. Out loud.

You will hesitate more in lessons. You will self-correct more. That is exactly what we want — because that is real speaking.

Today's Key Idea

🎙 Explain the keyword method to someone who's never heard of it. Why does it matter at this level?

What: You prepare for speaking by writing maximum 3 concept keywords — not full sentences or notes.

Why it matters: You have the grammar and vocabulary, but if you always read from notes, you never practise the skill that actually matters: constructing sophisticated arguments in real time — choosing discourse markers, hedging, structuring ideas — all live.

The rule: A hesitant, self-correcting response from keywords > a polished response read from a script.
