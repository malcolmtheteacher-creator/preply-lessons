Today we’re building a method for analysing any poem, so you can explain not just what a poet does but why it works.

Two versions of the same idea

Version A

The soldiers were exhausted. They walked slowly, some without boots, and they could hardly see or hear.

Version B

Men marched asleep. Many had lost their boots
But limped on, blood-shod. All went lame; all blind;
Drunk with fatigue; deaf even to the hoots

SPEAK Both say the same thing. Read them aloud. Why does B feel so different? Try to give three reasons, and use the word because each time.
  • Image: blood-shod puts blood where the boots should be, so the missing boots become something you can see and feel.
  • Diction: marched asleep sets purposeful movement against exhaustion, in two words.
  • Shape: short, clipped phrases (“All went lame; all blind;”) stack up like a list of injuries, so the rhythm itself limps.
  • Voice: A is a plain report. B sounds like someone who was there and cannot look away.

Notice that each reason can be sorted into a type. That sorting is the heart of today’s method.

Four Lenses

Instead of hunting for devices at random, look through four lenses, one at a time. For each lens, say the questions you would ask before you reveal them.

First Poem

Wilfred Owen, written in 1917 and published in 1920. Read it aloud once, slowly, before doing anything else.

Dulce et Decorum Est
1Bent double, like old beggars under sacks, 2Knock-kneed, coughing like hags, we cursed through sludge, 3Till on the haunting flares we turned our backs 4And towards our distant rest began to trudge. 5Men marched asleep. Many had lost their boots 6But limped on, blood-shod. All went lame; all blind; 7Drunk with fatigue; deaf even to the hoots 8Of tired, outstripped Five-Nines that dropped behind. 9Gas! Gas! Quick, boys!—An ecstasy of fumbling, 10Fitting the clumsy helmets just in time; 11But someone still was yelling out and stumbling 12And flound’ring like a man in fire or lime.— 13Dim, through the misty panes and thick green light, 14As under a green sea, I saw him drowning. 15In all my dreams, before my helpless sight, 16He plunges at me, guttering, choking, drowning. 17If in some smothering dreams you too could pace 18Behind the wagon that we flung him in, 19And watch the white eyes writhing in his face, 20His hanging face, like a devil’s sick of sin; 21If you could hear, at every jolt, the blood 22Come gargling from the froth-corrupted lungs, 23Obscene as cancer, bitter as the cud 24Of vile, incurable sores on innocent tongues,— 25My friend, you would not tell with such high zest 26To children ardent for some desperate glory, 27The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est 28Pro patria mori.
SPEAK First reaction, before any technique: in one minute, tell me what the poem is about and what it makes you feel. Then say which lens you think this poem leans on most.

Name the feature

For each quotation, say the effect aloud first, then choose the feature and check.

Say Why

A device is only worth mentioning if you can say what it does. Compare two answers about the same line.

Answer 1

Owen uses a simile in “like old beggars under sacks”. This makes the description more interesting and adds to the poem. The poem is about soldiers in the First World War.

Answer 2

In the opening line, Owen compares the soldiers to “old beggars under sacks”, so young men look ancient, poor and helpless. The simile strips away the heroic picture of the soldier before the poem has properly begun, which prepares the ground for the final attack on “the old Lie”. Owen’s point is that war does not ennoble men; it breaks them.

SPEAK Which answer would earn more marks, and why? Find where Answer 2 does each of the four steps (name, quote, effect, idea), then say exactly what Answer 1 is missing.

Name: a simile. Quote: “old beggars under sacks”. Effect: makes young men look old and helpless, stripping away the heroic image. Idea: war breaks men; it does not ennoble them.

Answer 1 names the device and quotes it, but its effect is vague (“more interesting”) and it never reaches an idea.

Build your own

Take the short stanza in lines 15–16 (“In all my dreams…”). First speak your analysis, then write one line for each step.

Lines 15–16 form a stanza on their own, the shortest in the poem. “In all my dreams, before my helpless sight, / He plunges at me, guttering, choking, drowning.” The word “drowning” returns from line 14, just as the memory returns to the speaker, and the isolation makes it feel like a flashback he cannot control. The effect is that the reader is held on the image before the argument begins. It also gives the speaker his authority: he has paid for his right to say what follows.

Challenge: your version does not have to match. Where is yours stronger or weaker than this one?

Who is speaking?

SPEAK Follow the pronouns through the poem: “we” in the first stanzas, “I” in lines 14–16, then “you” at the end. Trace the shift. Why might Owen move through all three? Give two reasons.

Second Poem

William Blake, 1794. This time, you lead. Read it aloud first.

London
1I wander thro’ each charter’d street, 2Near where the charter’d Thames does flow. 3A mark in every face I meet, 4Marks of weakness, marks of woe. 5In every cry of every Man, 6In every Infant’s cry of fear, 7In every voice, in every ban, 8The mind-forg’d manacles I hear. 9How the Chimney-sweeper’s cry 10Every black’ning Church appalls; 11And the hapless Soldier’s sigh 12Runs in blood down Palace walls. 13But most thro’ midnight streets I hear 14How the youthful Harlot’s curse 15Blasts the new-born Infant’s tear, 16And blights with plagues the Marriage hearse.

SHAPE notes

  • Voice: a walking, first-person witness (“I wander”, “I hear”) who listens more than he looks. Where does the tone harden?
  • Image: “charter’d” means licensed or owned, and it is applied to the street and the river. What does it say about freedom in the city? And why forg’d manacles, if they are made in the mind?
  • Sound: “every” appears five times in the second stanza. What does that relentless repetition do to the reader’s sense of escape?
  • Shape: four tight stanzas, each rhyming ABAB with a steady beat. Could the regularity itself be a kind of manacle?
  • Oxymoron: “Marriage hearse” joins a wedding to a funeral in two words. What is Blake saying about what institutions have turned into?
