The First Date

Three weeks of messages, and now she's actually walking through the door. Two hours to find out whether you like each other — and the nerve to say so either way.

The Scene 🎬

A pub on a Tuesday, half seven, the good table by the window because you got here fifteen minutes early and sat in it like a man guarding something. You matched three weeks ago. Since then it's been messages: some of them funny, a couple of them at two in the morning, all of them written with the luxury of a delete key.

None of that helps you now. In about ninety seconds Priya is going to walk in and be a whole person — a face, a voice, a way of putting her coat over the back of the chair — and everything you think you know about her was assembled from roughly forty text messages and four photographs.

You've got two hours, more or less, to work out something you can't really work out on paper: whether you actually like her, and whether she actually likes you. And then, at the end, whichever way it's gone, you have to say something true out loud. Which is the hard bit, because "we should do this again sometime" is right there, and it costs nothing, and it means nothing.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. How long does it honestly take you to decide whether you like a new person — a minute, an evening, a month? And how often does that first read turn out to be wrong?
  2. When you don't want to see someone again, do you say so, go quiet, or make a vague promise you won't keep? What have people done to you, and which did you mind least?

Who's who

You
The One Who Got There First 🍺

Out of practice, slightly over-prepared, and quietly aware you've got six anecdotes and a habit of doing all of them. You'd like to be honest tonight. You've not always managed it.

The other voice
Priya 🍷

You matched three weeks ago and this is the first time you've been in a room together. Warm, a bit late, funnier than her messages suggested — and, it turns out, braver than you about saying what she wants.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how the questions work — small, easy ones that hand the other person an open door, rather than big ones that put them on the spot. And watch what happens at the end: notice that the honest thing gets said plainly, once, and nobody dies.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how much of it is nothing much: weather, journeys, a pub in a part of town neither of you knows. The real things arrive on the back of the small ones.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Both of them admit an unimpressive habit in the first few minutes, and it's the same habit seen from opposite ends. What are the two habits, and why does swapping them so early change the mood of the evening?

2. Priya asks one big, serious question all night. What happens to it? And what does the answer he gives instead tell you about him?

3. Priya's last line does something surprising after being turned down. What does she ask for, and why is that a kinder ending than storming off or than pretending she doesn't mind?

1. He's always early and would rather wait than be waited for; she's always late and always saying "two minutes". Neither habit is impressive, which is the point — they've each handed over a small true thing about themselves before the drinks arrive, so the evening starts from honesty rather than from a performance.
2. He doesn't answer it. He asks to come back to it, because he can tell that anything he said in that moment would be one of his six rehearsed answers, and he'd rather give her a real one late than a polished one now. It tells you he's noticed his own script and doesn't want to run it at her.
3. She asks him to stay and finish his drink and tell her the story about the boat. She doesn't get her second date, and she doesn't pretend the answer didn't sting — she just takes the evening for what it actually was, which is one good evening, and refuses to throw it away because it isn't going to be two.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for meeting a stranger and finding out, out loud, whether you like each other. Say each one before you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Almost all of these want to sound thrown away rather than prepared — a shrug in the voice, not a speech. The trick is speed and volume: say them lightly and slightly too fast, the way you'd say something that only just occurred to you. The one exception is the last one of the evening, which wants the opposite — slow, quiet, and looked at, because that's the one you actually mean.

🚪 The first thirty seconds

Phrase 1

You've been sitting at the table for a quarter of an hour and they've just walked in apologising. You'd like to take the awkwardness out of it and give them something about yourself at the same time. Which line does both?

A. "Don't worry, you're not that late."
B. "Sorry, I'm a bit early — I always do this."
C. "I've been here a while, actually."
B — "Sorry, I'm a bit early — I always do this."
A quietly confirms they were late, and C makes them feel it. B takes the whole thing off them and puts it on you, and the "I always do this" is what makes it work, because it turns a one-off fact into a confession about your character — you're the sort of person who sits in pubs waiting. "A bit" keeps it small. You've apologised for nothing and handed them a piece of yourself in the same breath.
Phrase 2

Neither of you has a drink yet and you can both feel the questions coming. You want to name that and delay it, cheerfully, for ninety seconds. Put the pieces in order:

a drink inshall we getthe whole interview bit?Right,before we do
"Right, shall we get a drink in before we do the whole interview bit?"
To "get a drink in" is to go and buy the round — the "in" makes it sound like something you're getting out of the way, which is exactly the tone you want. "The whole interview bit" is the clever half: you both know the next ten minutes will be job, flat, family, and calling it an interview says you know how daft it is, which somehow makes it much less like one. "Right," at the front is what British people say when they've decided to start.

🚌 Small questions, open doors

Phrase 3

They've sat down. You want a first question that's easy to answer without thinking, and that quietly gives you something to ask about next. Which one?

A. "So, what do you do?"
B. "Tell me about yourself."
C. "How was the journey over — did you have far to come?"
C — "How was the journey over — did you have far to come?"
The journey is the one thing they definitely know about and don't have to perform, so they can answer it while they're still getting their coat off. And it pays you back, because the answer nearly always contains where they live, which is your next question sitting there waiting for you. A makes them do their job in one sentence, and B is a request for a monologue from someone who has been in the room for eleven seconds.
Phrase 4

They've just named a part of town you've never heard of. You want to ask roughly which bit it's in — and the word isn't "where". One word: "Oh, ___ is that?"

"Oh, whereabouts is that?"
"Whereabouts" asks for the rough area rather than the exact spot, so it's the chattier, lower-stakes cousin of "where" — you're not asking for their address, you're asking them to place it for you. The "Oh" at the front matters more than it looks: it makes the question sound like a genuine reaction to what they just said, rather than the next item on a list you brought with you.

🫱 Admitting the obvious

Phrase 5

They've just said something honest about how strange all this is, and you'd like to meet them there. You want to admit — lightly, once, and then move on — that you're out of practice at this and it doesn't come easily. Not an apology, not your whole history. Say it out loud, in your own words. What do you say?

e.g. "I'll be honest, I've not done a lot of this."
Look at how little it claims. It doesn't say you're bad at it, or nervous, or explain why — it just puts a plain fact on the table and stops, so there's nothing for them to have to reassure you about. "I'll be honest" is the flag: it tells them the next thing is true and slightly costly to say, which is exactly what makes them trust the things you say afterwards. Note "I've not done" rather than "I haven't done" — it's the more spoken, more offhand of the two.

🎯 Showing you're actually listening

Phrase 6

They've just landed on the same opinion you were about to give. You want to say so — and give them the credit rather than just nod. Complete it: "See, that's exactly ___ ___ ___ said."

"See, that's exactly what I'd have said."
"I'd have" is "I would have" — the answer you'd have given, if you'd got there first, which you didn't. That's the whole charm of it: you're not agreeing with them, you're admitting they said your thought before you did. "See," at the front is the sound of two people arriving at the same place and noticing, and "exactly" is what stops it being ordinary politeness.
Phrase 7

You're four minutes into a story and you can hear yourself doing it. Rather than trailing off and hoping nobody noticed, you catch yourself out loud and hand the floor over. Put the pieces in order:

far too much —Sorry,your turn.I'm talking
"Sorry, I'm talking far too much — your turn."
Saying it beats trailing off, because trailing off leaves them working out whether you've finished. "Far too much" is a deliberate exaggeration — you probably weren't, and they'll say so, which is half the point. And "your turn" is the bit that does the work: it hands the floor over flatly, without a new question attached, so they can talk about whatever they like rather than answering you again.

🤔 Answering something properly

Phrase 8

They've asked you something real and you haven't got an answer ready. You don't want to reach for the first impressive thing in the drawer. Which line buys you honest time?

A. "I don't know."
B. "I'd have to think about that one, actually."
C. "Ooh, good question."
B — "I'd have to think about that one, actually."
A shuts the door, and C is what people say while they think of something to say — everyone knows it means nothing. B treats their question as worth more than a quick answer, which is a compliment you can't fake. "That one" quietly says the others were easier, so you're not stalling generally, just here. And "actually" on the end is the small British marker for having surprised yourself, which is why it sounds honest rather than evasive.

💛 The end of the evening

Phrase 9

The chairs are going up on the tables. You'd like to see them again, and you'd rather say so than leave it in the air. Put the pieces in order:

if you would.Look, I've reallyI'd like to do it again,enjoyed this —
"Look, I've really enjoyed this — I'd like to do it again, if you would."
Notice the order: the true thing first, the ask second. It says how the evening was before it asks for anything, so the compliment doesn't read as a tactic. "Look," is the small warning that you're about to be direct. And "if you would" is the mercy in it — three words that leave them a clean, undramatic way to say no, which is exactly what makes it a question rather than a squeeze.
Phrase 10

Now the other one. You liked them, genuinely, and you don't want a second date — and they've just asked. You have to say the true thing plainly: the evening was good, and it isn't going anywhere romantic for you. No excuse, no "it's not you", no vague promise. Say it out loud, in your own words. What do you say?

e.g. "I've had a lovely evening, honestly — I'm just not sure it's a romantic thing for me."
Both halves are true and both have to be said, or it doesn't work: without the first half it's a rejection, and without the second half it's a lie. "Honestly" is doing something specific — it's there because "I've had a lovely evening" is exactly what people say when they haven't, so you have to mark this one as real. "A romantic thing" is deliberately vague and gentle, and "for me" puts it where it belongs, on your feeling rather than on anything they did wrong. "I'm just not sure" is softer than "it isn't", and it's honest, because you aren't.

🔑 Make them yours

These are not really dating phrases. They're for any evening where you're in a room with someone you don't know yet and one of you has to go first. Pin three of them to your own life, so you'll actually reach for them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I'll be honest, I've not done a lot of this." — something you're genuinely out of practice at, that you'd normally hide. What is it?

"I'd have to think about that one, actually." — a question people ask you where you always give the same tidy, rehearsed answer.

"I've had a lovely evening, honestly — I'm just not sure it's a romantic thing for me." — a kind no you've been avoiding. Not necessarily romantic: a friendship, a job, an invitation.

Your Turn 🎭

Now she's walking in. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer — nobody sits opposite you in a pub waiting thirty seconds while you craft a sentence.

The Twist

😬 She asks first — and you haven't decided

Run it again, no script. You've been here an hour. It's going… fine? Good, possibly. You genuinely don't know yet, and you were counting on the walk home to work it out — you'd planned to be certain by the time anyone had to say anything. Then she puts her glass down and says: "Right. Honestly. Is this going anywhere for you? Because I'd much rather know now than get a nice text tomorrow." She's looking at you and she isn't filling the silence. Start the timer and answer her in the room. Two rules: you can't say "let's see how it goes" or anything else that means it, and you can't say a thing you don't believe just because it's the smoother sentence. If the honest answer is "I don't know yet", you have to say that out loud and then say what you'd need in order to know — which is a much harder sentence than either yes or no.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • My first questions were small and easy — I didn't put her on the spot in the first two minutes.
  • I admitted at least one true, unimpressive thing about myself, and didn't turn it into a speech.
  • I noticed when I was running my own script — and stopped, out loud.
  • I gave a real answer to her real question, even when the polished one was right there.
  • I said what I actually wanted at the end, warmly and plainly — not "we should do this again sometime".

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Three weeks of messages will tell you what someone is like when they've had four minutes to think of the reply. They will not tell you how that person orders a drink, or what they do with their face when they're bored, which are the things you'd actually need to know.

So the evening isn't a test you pass. It's mostly a lot of nothing — journeys, buses, a pub behind a Tesco — and somewhere in the nothing, if you're lucky, one true thing gets said and the other person doesn't flinch.

And then it ends one of two ways, and both of them require the same thing from you: saying, out loud, to someone's face, what you'd much rather put in a text tomorrow. Priya's ending isn't the good one. But she got the truth, and she got the boat story, and she got to go home knowing where she stood — which, on the whole, is more than most Tuesdays offer.

Two bigger questions

  1. Is it kinder to say no to someone's face, or to let them work it out from your silence? Be honest about which one you actually do — and whether you'd want the same done to you.
  2. We now spend weeks reading a stranger before we ever meet them. Does all that reading make the first evening easier, or has it just given us more to be wrong about?
