A Unique Scripture

What is the Guru Granth Sahib?

The Guru Granth Sahib is Sikhism's holy scripture. But it's not like the Bible, Qur'an, or Torah. It's treated as a LIVING GURU (teacher). Not just a book—the eternal spiritual teacher of the Sikh community.

The History: How It Became a Guru

1469: Guru Nanak's Birth

The founder of Sikhism. He taught that there is one God and that the path to God is through devotion and service.

1604: Guru Arjan Compiles the Adi Granth

The first complete version of the scripture. It includes hymns from the Sikh Gurus and also from Hindu and Muslim saints—showing Sikhism's openness to other traditions.

1708: Guru Gobind Singh's Decision

The 10th and final human Guru declared that there would be no more human Gurus after him. Instead, the scripture itself—updated by Guru Gobind Singh—would be the eternal Guru. The book became a PERSON.

How the Guru Granth Sahib Is Treated as a Living Person

If it's a guru, it must be treated like a teacher. Here are six practices that show this:

Daily Awakening

Each morning, the Guru Granth Sahib is taken from its resting place (prakash) and opened. It's being "woken up" for the day.

A Throne to Sit On

It sits on an ornate throne called a manji sahib. The throne is often decorated with precious cloth.

A Ceremonial Fan

A chaur sahib (a ceremonial whisk) is waved over the book as a sign of respect. This is the same gesture used for royalty.

Never on the Floor

No Sikh would ever place the Guru Granth Sahib on the floor or below eye level. It must be treated with physical respect—lifted high, carried on the head.

Daily Rest

At night, it's given sukhasan (rest). The book is gently put away in special coverings, like putting a teacher to bed.

A Private Room

The book has its own room in the gurdwara (Sikh place of worship) called the Darbar Sahib (the Court of the Guru).

What's Inside: Content and Structure

A Surprising Feature: Multiple Voices

The Guru Granth Sahib isn't just the words of Sikh Gurus. It also includes hymns (shabads) from Hindu and Muslim saints. This shows Sikhism's belief that truth can come from many sources. It's written in Gurmukhi script, the script of Punjabi.

Important: Every copy has exactly 1,430 pages. No variations. No different editions. Every Sikh reads the identical text.

The Opening Words: The Mool Mantar

Mool Mantar (The Root Mantra): The opening lines of the Guru Granth Sahib express the fundamental belief of Sikhism:

"There is one God
Truth by name
Creator
Without fear
Without hatred
Timeless
Unborn
Self-existent
Known by the Guru's grace"

This opens every section of the Guru Granth Sahib. Every Sikh knows these words by heart.

How It's Used in Worship and Daily Life

Daily Reading in the Gurdwara

The text is read aloud in the gurdwara. The granthi (the reader) reads with reverence.

Akhand Path

A continuous 48-hour reading of the entire text. Readers take turns so the reading never stops. It's an act of devotion.

Hukamnama: Daily Guidance

Sikhs open the book at random and read a passage to get guidance for the day. The passage they land on is seen as the Guru's advice.

Kirtan: Singing the Words

The hymns are sung aloud. Music is central to how Sikhs engage with their scripture.

Key Fact

Every copy of the Guru Granth Sahib has exactly 1,430 pages. No Sikh would ever place it on the floor, sit with their feet pointing toward it, or turn their back on it. It's treated with the same respect a person would receive.

The Big Question

Agree or Disagree? "The Sikh tradition of treating the Guru Granth Sahib as a living Guru shows more respect for their scripture than any other religion."

Arguments in Favour (You Could Agree)

  • The practices (daily awakening, waving the chaur sahib, the 48-hour Akhand Path) show extraordinary reverence
  • Treating a book as a guru is more respectful than just reading it
  • The restriction that every copy must have 1,430 identical pages shows perfect reverence
  • Other religions don't treat their texts with these specific ceremonial practices

Arguments Against (You Could Disagree)

  • Muslims treat the Qur'an with immense respect (never touching it without ritual purity, reciting it daily)
  • Jews hand-write Torah scrolls and keep them in the Ark—that's also extraordinary reverence
  • Respect doesn't have to look the same to be real. A Christian reading the Bible prayerfully shows deep respect
  • Treating a book as a person might be more unique than more respectful

Your Thinking

Write a short paragraph (4-6 sentences) with your own view. You don't have to agree with one side completely—you could say multiple traditions show equal respect in different ways. Use at least one example from the traditions you've studied.

Quick-Fire Recall from Units 1-3

Q1: What does the Qur'an claim about itself?

It is the literal, perfect word of God, revealed to Muhammad by the angel Gabriel.

Q2: Name the three Abrahamic religions.

Judaism, Christianity, and Islam. They all trace their roots back to Abraham.

Q3: What is the Jewish Sabbath called?

Shabbat. It runs from Friday evening to Saturday evening and is a day of rest.

Q4: Name one of the Five Pillars of Islam.

Shahada (faith), Salah (prayer), Zakat (charity), Hajj (pilgrimage), or Sawm (fasting during Ramadan).
