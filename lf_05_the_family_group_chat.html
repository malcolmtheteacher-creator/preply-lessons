The Family Group Chat

Forty messages a week, most of them frightening, all of them forwarded by a man who loves you. And the person they're landing on is your mum.

The Scene 🎬

The family chat has nine people in it and one main contributor. Uncle Ray — your mum's older brother, retired, on his own since Auntie Jean died — forwards things. Videos about the water supply. A man in a car park explaining what the doctors won't tell you. Something about injections that a friend of a friend sent him. Forty a week, roughly, at all hours.

Most of the family scroll past it. Your mum doesn't. She reads every one, believes about half, and rang you last week at eleven at night, genuinely frightened, wanting to know if she should stop taking her tablets. That is the actual problem — not the chat, not Ray, but a woman lying awake because her brother is worried about her and has nowhere to put it.

You could mute him. You nearly did — thumb hovering over the button a fortnight ago. Instead you're going to ring him, which is harder, and which he will not initially enjoy. He is proud, he means every message kindly, and the chat is more or less his social life.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Is there someone in your family who forwards things — news, warnings, videos, that kind of thing? What does everyone else do about it: read, scroll, mute, or complain to each other instead of to them?
  2. When an older relative is doing something that bothers you, who in your family gets to say so? Is there an unwritten rule about who's allowed to raise it — and does that rule ever leave the wrong person doing nothing?

Who's who

You
The One Who Rang 📞

Fond of Ray, tired of eleven o'clock phone calls from your mum. You want one thing to change and nothing else — and you'd like him to still be speaking to you afterwards.

The other voice
Uncle Ray 📲

Your mum's brother. Retired, on his own, and quietly certain that somebody has to look out for her. Defensive at first, because being told off by your niece or nephew stings — but he isn't a fool, and he loves his sister.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how the caller never once argues about whether the videos are true — that row can't be won. They move it onto the one thing Ray can't defend: what it's doing to his sister at eleven at night. Watch where the ask gets smaller, not bigger.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the moment Ray stops defending himself, and for what caused it.

Uncle RayHello? Hang on, let me just — right. Sorry, love, I was down the shed. Everything alright? You don't normally ring.
YouNo, everyone's fine, nothing's — nothing's happened, honestly. Nobody's ill. I just wanted a quick word about something a bit daft, really. I thought I'd ring rather than put this in the chat.
Uncle RayOh God. What have I done now.
YouNothing, you haven't — look, it's about the family chat. It's mainly the videos, to be honest.
Uncle RayThe videos. Right. Well, they're not mine, are they, I'm only passing them on. And some of them are true, some of them. That water one was on the news, that was actually on the news, I didn't make it up.
YouMaybe, yeah. That's sort of the problem, though. Mum takes that stuff seriously — she doesn't know it's nonsense. She rang me at eleven at night in a right state about it. She'd been lying there for hours with it going round.
Uncle RayShe — eleven at night? She never said a word to me. Well. No. She wouldn't, would she. Not to me. But hang on, so I'm meant to just say nothing, am I? If there's something out there and she doesn't know about it, and I've seen it, and I —
YouNo. No, I'm not saying that. I know you only send it because you're worried about her. I do know that, honestly. It's just she can't tell the real ones from the rubbish, and I can't sit up till midnight sorting them out for her. So — would you send them to me instead, and I'll have a look? If it's real, I'll ring her myself and go through it properly. If it's not, nobody's awake at eleven.
Uncle RaySend them to you. Just you. Not the chat.
YouJust me. And I'm not asking you to leave the group, God, no. Could we keep it to the birthdays and the photos?
Uncle RayIt's a group chat, though, isn't it. Everyone sticks everything in it. Kelly's got that dog in there forty times a day and nobody's ringing her about — well. Anyway. That's different, I know.
YouThe dog's not keeping anyone awake, is it. Look — Ray. You'd hate it if someone frightened your mum like that. Nan. If somebody had been sending Nan that at eleven at night you'd have gone round there.
Uncle RayMm. Yeah. Yeah, I would've. I'd have had a word, alright. You could have just muted me, you know. I expect half of them already have.
YouI'd rather say it to you than quietly mute you, which is what I nearly did. Thumb over the button, about a fortnight back.
Uncle RayHa. Well. There's honesty. Right — send them to you, then, and the birthdays. It's only because — you know. There's nobody checking on her but us, is there.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Ray defends himself twice near the start. What changed his mind in the end — and it wasn't a better argument about whether the videos were true?

2. The caller never says the videos are lies, never asks him to check his sources, never mentions the other seven people in the chat. Why would each of those have made things worse?

3. Right at the end, the caller admits they nearly did something much easier than ringing. Why does admitting that land as a compliment rather than a threat?

1. Two things, and neither is a fact: hearing that his sister had rung at eleven at night, frightened, which he hadn't known — and then being asked to imagine his own mother on the receiving end of it, which he answered before he'd finished thinking about it. The harm stopped being an argument and became a person.
2. Calling them lies starts a row he'd defend for an hour, because he's already told you the water one was on the news. Asking him to check his sources treats him like a child. And bringing in the other seven turns one quiet phone call into a family verdict, with him outnumbered — at which point he's not listening, he's defending himself.
3. Muting him would have been easy, silent, and permanent, and he'd never have known. Saying it out loud means the caller chose the awkward version instead — which tells Ray he's worth the awkwardness. A threat says "do this or else"; this says "I didn't want to lose you quietly."

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for raising something with a relative you can't unfriend. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. The tune here is not the tune of a complaint. Keep it light and slightly apologetic — the voice of someone raising something small, almost embarrassed to be bringing it up at all — because the moment you sound like a man reading out charges, he stops listening and starts defending himself. Slow, warm, and a bit reluctant. Let the content be firm and the voice be soft.

📞 Choosing where to say it

Phrase 1

You've decided this has to be a phone call, not a message everyone else can read. Your first job is to say why you've rung — so it doesn't feel like an ambush. Which opener does that, and does it gently?

A. "Have you got a minute? A few of us have been talking."
B. "I thought I'd ring rather than put this in the chat."
C. "I need to talk to you about your behaviour in the family chat."
B — "I thought I'd ring rather than put this in the chat."
It does two things in one breath: it explains the phone call before he starts wondering who's died, and it quietly tells him you're keeping this between the two of you rather than saying it in front of the family. A hints that there's a committee, which is the fastest way to make anyone defensive, and C is a headmaster's sentence — the word "behaviour" alone will finish the conversation before it starts.

🎯 Naming it, small

Phrase 2

He's braced for a long list. Give him the opposite: one thing, named lightly, with a hedge on the end so it sounds like an admission rather than an accusation. Put the pieces in order:

to be honest.It's mainlythe videos,
"It's mainly the videos, to be honest."
"Mainly" is the kind word here, because it says the rest of what he does is fine and only one part isn't, which shrinks the whole thing to something fixable. And "to be honest" on the end makes it sound like you're confessing rather than charging him with anything — it's a softener, not information.
Phrase 3

Now the actual harm. Notice you're not arguing about whether the things he sends are true — you're saying who they land on, and why she can't defend herself against them. Put the pieces in order:

she doesn't knowMum takesit's nonsense.that stuff seriously —
"Mum takes that stuff seriously — she doesn't know it's nonsense."
"That stuff" is deliberately vague, which keeps it from sounding like a list of his crimes, and "nonsense" is said as an agreed fact between the two of you rather than an insult aimed at him — you're standing next to him looking at it, not opposite him. The heart of it is "she doesn't know", because that moves the problem off him and onto someone he'd protect.

🌙 One fact, not a case

Phrase 4

One concrete thing that actually happened beats ten opinions, because he can't argue with it. Complete the chunk — the missing word is what British people put after "in a right" when someone is upset and worked up and not thinking straight: "She rang me at eleven at night in a right ___ about it."

"She rang me at eleven at night in a right state about it."
To be "in a state" is to be upset and agitated, and the "right" in front of it is a very British intensifier meaning thoroughly — a right state, a right mess. The power of the line isn't the idiom though, it's "eleven at night": a time, a real event, a thing he didn't know about, and nothing he can dispute.

💛 Credit the motive, then give him a job

Phrase 5

He's about to dig in, because he thinks you're accusing him of frightening his sister on purpose. Head that off by saying out loud what you both know about why he does it. Which line does that without sounding sarcastic?

A. "I'm sure you don't mean any harm by it."
B. "I know you only send it because you're worried about her."
C. "You've obviously got a lot of time on your hands."
B — "I know you only send it because you're worried about her."
It names his good reason before he has to defend it, which takes the fight out of him, because there's nothing to argue about once you've already agreed he's the one looking out for her. A sounds like the thing people say just before they tell you off, and C is a slap dressed up as an observation. The word doing the work in B is "only" — it says this and nothing worse.
Phrase 6

Here's the move that makes the whole call work: don't ask him to stop caring, give the caring somewhere else to go. In your own words, offer to take the videos yourself — he sends them to you, you're the one who decides whether they're worth passing on. What would you say?

e.g. "Would you send them to me instead, and I'll have a look?"
Notice what it does. It's a question, not an instruction, so he can say yes rather than obey. "Instead" is the whole deal in one word — he isn't losing the job of protecting his sister, he's being given a better one. And "I'll have a look" is casual and small; offering to fact-check him would be an insult, while offering to have a look is a favour.

🚪 Making the ask smaller

Phrase 7

The fear behind his defensiveness is that this call ends with him being pushed out of his own family's chat, which is most of his social life. Say the fear out loud and kill it. Complete it — the missing word is the one-word exclamation people put in front of "no" when the idea horrifies them: "I'm not asking you to leave the group, ___, no."

"I'm not asking you to leave the group, God, no."
Saying the worst version out loud and rejecting it is far more reassuring than never mentioning it, because it was already in his head. "God, no" isn't really swearing here — it's the sound of the idea being unthinkable, and that's why it convinces: you're not reassuring him, you're recoiling at the suggestion, which is much harder to fake.
Phrase 8

Now say what you do want, and make it sound tiny and rather nice — a chat for the good things, which he's still in. Note the "we", which puts you both under the same rule. Put the pieces in order:

and the photos?Could we keep itto the birthdays
"Could we keep it to the birthdays and the photos?"
"Keep it to" means limit it to, and everything about the phrasing shrinks the ask: "could we" is a request rather than a rule, "we" means you're agreeing to it too, and naming the birthdays and the photos describes a chat he'd want to be in rather than a list of things he mustn't do.

🪞 Landing it

Phrase 9

Facts have run out; he half agrees and hasn't moved. The last thing left is to put him on the other side of it — not as an argument, as a picture. Which line makes him feel it rather than concede it?

A. "How would you feel if the situation were reversed?"
B. "You'd hate it if someone frightened your mum like that."
C. "Imagine if I did that to you every day."
B — "You'd hate it if someone frightened your mum like that."
It's specific and it's his: not a situation, not a principle, but a particular woman he'd have gone round to protect. He answers it before he's decided to answer it. A is the language of a form, and C is about you, which invites him to argue about whether you're being fair — B gives him nothing to argue with.
Phrase 10

He points out that you could have taken the silent option and spared you both the call. Tell him the truth: you nearly did, and you chose this instead. Put the pieces in order:

which is what I nearly did.than quietly mute you,I'd rather say it to you
"I'd rather say it to you than quietly mute you, which is what I nearly did."
"I'd rather X than Y" lays the two options side by side and lets him see which one you picked. The "which is what I nearly did" is the part that costs you something to admit, and that's exactly why he believes it — you've handed him a small piece of evidence against yourself, and people trust the person who does that.

🔑 Make them yours

Raising something with family is a skill you'll need long after this lesson, and these lines travel — a brother-in-law, a neighbour, a colleague who copies everyone in. Pin three of them to something real in your own life, so you'll reach for them when it happens. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I thought I'd ring rather than put this in the chat." — something you've been meaning to raise with someone, that you've been leaving in writing because it's easier. What is it?

"I know you only send it because you're worried about her." — someone whose annoying habit has a good reason underneath it. What's the good reason?

"Could we keep it to the birthdays and the photos?" — the smallest possible version of something you want changed. How small can you make the ask?

The Twist

😬 The one that was true

Three weeks on. It worked — Ray sends them to you now, and the chat is birthdays and photos, and your mum sleeps. Then, this morning, Ray rings you. Nine days ago he sent you something about a recall on the fish your mum buys every Friday. It was real. It was on the council's own website. You glanced at it, meant to check it, and forgot — and she's eaten it twice since, and she's fine, but she's fine by luck. He isn't shouting. He's saying, quite quietly, "I'd have put it in the chat, and she'd have known that same night." Start the timer. He's right, and you cannot hand the chat back, and you have to say both of those things to him in the same minute. One rule: you don't get to explain why you were busy.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I said why I was ringing before he had to ask, and nobody else was in the room.
  • I never argued about whether the videos were true — I said who they were landing on.
  • I gave one real fact with a time on it, instead of a general complaint.
  • I said his good reason out loud before he had to defend it, and I gave him something to do instead of nothing.
  • My ask got smaller as the call went on, and he ended it still in the chat and still speaking to me.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Every family chat has a Ray, and most families deal with him the way they deal with the weather: by putting up with it and complaining to each other. Muting is free, silent, and permanent, and it costs you nothing except the person.

What makes it hard is that he isn't wrong to worry. He's a man on his own with a phone and a sister he can't get to, and forwarding things is the only door he's got. Take that away and you'd better have something to put in its place, or you've just made him quieter, not happier.

The call works because it asks for less than you wanted, and because it lets him keep the job. That's usually the trade with family: you don't get the person fixed, you get one thing changed, and you get to keep them.

Two bigger questions

  1. Muting someone is silent, and they never know. Is that a kindness — nobody's feelings hurt, nobody told off — or is it the coldest thing we do to each other now? Would you rather be told, or muted?
  2. Ray forwards things because it's the only way he can look after his sister from twenty miles away. Which of the habits that irritate you in older relatives are actually love with nowhere sensible to go — and whose job is it to give it somewhere to go?
