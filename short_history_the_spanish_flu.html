Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. It began in one village and, within months, had spread to every continent at once; doctors stopped calling it an outbreak and began, gravely, to call it a pandemic.
a pandemic (n) — an outbreak of disease that spreads across many countries or the whole world at the same time.
2. In the laboratory they compared the new strain with last year's: same family of virus, but a slightly different version, against which old vaccines no longer worked.
a strain (n) — a particular variety or version of a virus or bacterium within the same wider type.
3. The disease proved so contagious that a single sick passenger in the corner was enough: by the end of the journey, a dozen people who had boarded in good health were already coughing.
contagious (adj) — (of a disease) easily passed from one person to another by close contact.
4. Those who had survived the first illness rarely caught it again — their bodies had built up an immunity that the newcomers simply did not have.
immunity (n) — the body's ability to resist a particular disease, so that it does not make you ill.
5. Just as the town thought the danger had passed, a second wave arrived in the autumn — fiercer than the first, and far harder to contain.
a wave (of infection) (n) — a period when a disease rises and spreads rapidly, often one of several such surges.
6. The figures were grim reading: of every hundred who fell ill, several would not recover, and that mortality climbed steeply among the old and the very young.
mortality (n) — the number of deaths, especially the death rate from a particular cause.
7. Viruses do not stay the same: each time they copy themselves they can mutate, changing a little, until the new form behaves quite unlike the old one.
to mutate (v) — to change form through small genetic alterations, sometimes becoming more dangerous.
8. So many patients arrived at once that the small clinic was overwhelmed: beds in the corridors, no spare nurses, and supplies running out by noon.
overwhelmed (adj) — completely overloaded; given far more than it can cope with.
9. The nurse moved down the long hospital ward, past row after row of beds, checking each patient before reaching the far end and starting again.
a ward (n) — a large room or section in a hospital where patients are treated, usually with many beds.
10. The government allowed no bad news to reach the public; under wartime censorship, every newspaper report of falling morale or rising deaths was quietly cut.
censorship (n) — the official control or suppression of news, writing or speech, removing what the authorities don't want seen.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A sickness with no home

In the spring of 1918, the world was already exhausted. The First World War had ground on for nearly four years, and millions of soldiers were crammed into trenches, troopships and crowded army camps. It was in exactly these conditions — tired bodies, no space, constant movement — that a new strain of influenza found the perfect place to spread. Where it truly began is still debated; one of the first large outbreaks was recorded at an army camp in Kansas, in the United States, in March 1918. From there, soldiers carried it across the Atlantic to the battlefields of Europe, and the virus began to infect almost everyone in its path.

This first wave was widespread but, on the whole, mild. Most people recovered. Yet the disease had a strange name attached to it — "the Spanish flu" — though it had almost nothing to do with Spain. The reason lay in censorship. Britain, France, Germany and the United States were all at war, and their governments suppressed any news that might damage morale, including reports of soldiers falling sick. Spain, however, was neutral. Its newspapers reported the spreading illness freely and openly, even covering the king's own infection. To the rest of the world, reading only Spanish accounts, it looked as though the disease had come from Spain. The whole world blamed a country that was simply telling the truth.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the conditions of the First World War help the virus to spread?

2. Why was the pandemic called "the Spanish flu" if it did not start in Spain?

1. Millions of soldiers were packed together in trenches, ships and camps, exhausted and constantly on the move — ideal conditions for an airborne illness to pass from person to person.

2. The warring countries censored news of the illness; neutral Spain reported it freely, so the rest of the world wrongly assumed the disease had come from there.

Discuss:
  • A country was blamed for an epidemic simply because it reported the news honestly. What does that tell us about how blame attaches to a disease?

Part 2 — The second wave

If the spring wave had been the whole story, the world might have forgotten it. But in the autumn of 1918 the virus returned, and it had changed. It is thought to have mutated into a far deadlier form, and this second wave became the great killer of the pandemic. Each new wave seemed deadlier than the last, and this one struck with terrifying speed. People described victims who were healthy at breakfast and dead by nightfall, their lungs filling with fluid, their skin turning a dark blue-purple as they struggled to breathe.

Strangest of all was who it killed. Ordinary flu is most dangerous to the very old and the very young. This one did that too — but it also cut down healthy adults in the prime of life, those aged roughly twenty to forty. Soldiers fit enough for war died in their thousands. One theory is that the strong immune systems of young adults overreacted to the virus and, in fighting it, flooded and destroyed their own lungs. Hospitals everywhere were overwhelmed. There were not enough beds, not enough nurses, not enough coffins. In some cities every ward was full, bodies piled up faster than they could be buried, and exhausted doctors could do little but watch.

Almost every country on Earth was struck. The virus reached crowded cities and remote islands alike, from the great capitals of Europe to villages in the Arctic and the Pacific, where in some places it killed a large share of all the people living there. No corner of the inhabited world was truly spared, and for a few terrible months the whole world held its breath.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What made the second wave of autumn 1918 so much worse than the first?

2. In what way was this flu unusual in who it killed, and what is one theory for why?

1. The virus is thought to have mutated into a deadlier form; it killed extremely fast, hospitals were overwhelmed, and deaths far outran the supply of beds, nurses and coffins.

2. Unlike ordinary flu, it killed many healthy young adults (about twenty to forty). One theory is that their strong immune systems overreacted and damaged their own lungs.

Discuss:
  • A disease that kills the young and strong, not just the weak, frightens people in a different way. Why do you think that is?

Part 3 — The reckoning, and the silence after

With no vaccine and no effective medicine, the world fell back on the simplest defences. Cities ordered people to wear masks, closed schools, churches, theatres and bars, and banned public gatherings. Some places quarantined the sick; others quarantined whole towns. The measures helped, but they came late and were unevenly kept. A third, milder wave rolled through in 1919, and the pandemic finally faded in 1920 — not because anyone defeated it, but because so many had either died or gained immunity that the virus ran out of new victims.

The numbers are almost beyond grasp. About a third of all the people alive — perhaps 500 million — were infected. Estimates of the dead range from 50 million to as many as 100 million, more than the First World War itself had killed. Long afterwards, scientists identified the culprit as a strain of H1N1 influenza, and even recovered fragments of the virus from bodies frozen in the Arctic, helping to explain why it had been so lethal.

And yet, for decades, almost no one spoke of it. There were few monuments, few memorials, little place in the history books. A war had a clear story — heroes, villains, victory — but a virus had none of that, and a grieving world seemed to want only to look forward. It took a later age, and later pandemics of its own, to make people return to 1918 and ask how the whole world could lose so many, so fast, and then so nearly forget.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the pandemic finally fade, if no cure was ever found?

2. Why was the Spanish flu so little remembered for so long afterwards?

1. So many people had either died or developed immunity that the virus ran out of new people to infect; simple measures like masks and quarantines had also slowed it.

2. A virus offered no clear story of heroes and victory the way the war did, and a grieving world preferred to look forward — so it left few monuments or mentions in the history books.

Discuss:
  • A pandemic that killed more than the war beside it was almost forgotten. What makes a society remember some catastrophes and quietly bury others?

Grammar — Determiners (all / every / each / none)

This is a story about totality — a sickness that touched everyone, reached every country, spared no one, and held the whole world in its grip. To tell it accurately you need the determiners that talk about quantity and scope: all, every, each, none, no, both and the whole. Their meanings overlap, but their grammar does not — and getting the grammar wrong is one of the most common slips at this level.

The rules in one place:
• every and each take a singular noun and a singular verb: "every country was struck," "each wave was deadlier." (Each stresses the individuals one by one; every stresses the group as a whole.)
• all and none link to a noun with of the…: "all of the beds," "none of the patients." Before a plain noun, all needs no of: "all countries," "all the people."
• no sits directly before a noun (singular or plural) to mean "not any": "no vaccine existed," "no corner was spared."
• the whole + a singular noun = "all of one thing, complete": "the whole world held its breath," "the whole town was quarantined."
• both = the two together, with a plural verb: "both waves were dangerous."
Singular or plural — the trap:
• Every/each/the whole → singular verb: "Almost every country was struck." (NOT "were struck")
• None of can take singular or plural, but a singular verb is safest in careful writing: "None of the medicine was effective."
• All + plural noun → plural verb: "All the wards were full." / All + uncountable → singular: "All the news was censored."

1. Singular or plural? Choose the right verb form

Each gap needs the verb in the form the determiner demands.

1. Almost every country (be) struck by the virus.

2. Each wave (seem) deadlier than the one before.

3. The whole world (hold) its breath.

4. All the hospital wards (be) full.

1. was (every + singular verb)

2. seemed (each + singular verb)

3. held (the whole + singular noun + singular verb)

4. were (all + plural noun → plural verb)

2. Pick the right determiner

Choose from all / every / each / none / no / both / the whole — the grammar of the sentence will guide you.

1. one was truly immune; the virus reached almost everyone.

2. There was vaccine and cure, so cities relied on masks.

3. of the warring countries censored the news.

4. waves were dangerous, but the second was far worse than the first.

1. No ("No one was truly immune" — no + singular noun)

2. no … no (no + singular noun = "not any")

3. All (all + of the + plural noun) — "Each" also works if you stress them individually: "Each of the…"

4. Both (the two waves together → plural verb "were")

3. Fix the mistake

Each sentence has one determiner error. Write the corrected sentence.

1. "Every soldiers were exhausted." →

2. "None of the patient was saved." →

3. "The all world held its breath." →

1. "Every soldier was exhausted." (every + singular noun + singular verb)

2. "None of the patients was saved." (none of + plural noun; singular verb is safest)

3. "The whole world held its breath." (the whole, not "the all")
