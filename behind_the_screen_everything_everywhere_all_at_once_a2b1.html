Everything Everywhere All at Once

A tired mother learns she can jump between many universes — but the hardest journey is back to her own daughter.

Want more detail? Read this lesson at B2/C1 level →

Today: tell the story of the film Everything Everywhere All at Once — a mother learns she can visit many universes to save her family — learn what is true and what is invented, and talk about imaginary situations with the second conditional.
Before we begin — talk about it: Do you ever think about a different life you could have had? If you could visit another universe, would you want to see a different version of yourself?

The film, in three parts

Daniel Kwan & Daniel Scheinert's Everything Everywhere All at Once (2022). Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one. (There are spoilers — that is fine, we want to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A busy, difficult day

Evelyn and her husband Waymond own a small laundromat. Life is hard: the tax office wants to check their money, Evelyn's father is visiting from China, and her daughter Joy is unhappy. Waymond wants to talk to Evelyn about their marriage, but she is always too busy to listen.

In the lift, on the way to the tax meeting, something very strange happens. A different Waymond, from another universe, takes control of her husband's body. He tells Evelyn that there are millions of universes, and only she can stop a terrible danger.

To fight, Evelyn must do strange, unusual actions to borrow skills from other versions of herself — a film star, a fighter, a chef. Something dangerous is coming, and it is coming to her tax meeting.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What problems does Evelyn have at the start of the film?

2. What does the strange Waymond tell Evelyn in the lift?

1. The tax office is checking her money, her father is visiting, her daughter is unhappy, and her marriage has problems.

2. He tells her there are many universes, and she is the only person who can stop a dangerous enemy.

Talk about it:
  • Evelyn's normal life is full of problems. Do you think a difficult life can also make someone strong? Why, or why not?

Part 2 — The daughter who feels nothing matters

The dangerous enemy is called Jobu Tupaki. Evelyn learns something shocking: Jobu Tupaki is her own daughter, Joy, from another universe. Joy has visited so many universes that she believes nothing matters at all.

She has built a strange bagel that can destroy every universe. People from other universes want Evelyn to stop Joy — even if that means fighting her. Evelyn jumps to many strange universes to learn new skills for the fight — some funny, some frightening.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is Jobu Tupaki, really?

2. What has she built, and why is it dangerous?

1. She is Joy, Evelyn's daughter, from another universe.

2. An "everything bagel" that can destroy every universe at once.

Talk about it:
  • Joy feels that nothing matters because she has experienced everything. Do you think having too many choices in life could make you feel worse, not better?

Part 3 — Choosing kindness

In one quiet universe, Evelyn and Joy are just two rocks, sitting side by side, with no bodies and no voices — only words on the screen. For a moment, Evelyn almost agrees that nothing matters.

But then she changes her mind. She decides that even if nothing matters, she can still choose to be kind. She stops fighting and tells Joy that she loves her, no matter what universe they are in. Slowly, the family starts to reconnect. At the end, they are back in their small laundromat — but their relationship feels different now.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happens to Evelyn in the "two rocks" universe?

2. How does the story end?

1. She almost believes that nothing matters, but she chooses kindness instead.

2. The family is back in the laundromat, but their relationships have improved.

Talk about it:
  • Evelyn chooses kindness, not fighting, to solve the problem. Do you think kindness can solve big problems in real life?

Grammar: the second conditional

This film is full of imaginary situations — other universes, other choices, other lives. English has an easy way to talk about imaginary situations now.

if + past simple, would + base verb = an imaginary situation, and its imaginary result: "If Evelyn weren't so busy, she would listen to Waymond." "If Joy could feel everything at once, nothing would surprise her."
Feel the difference:
We use the second conditional for something imaginary or unlikely now — not something that really happened.

1. Complete with would/wouldn't + verb

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence.

1. If Evelyn weren't so busy with the laundromat, she (spend) more time with her family. (imaginary result)

2. If Joy didn't feel so alone in the universe, she (not / build) something so destructive. (imaginary result)

3. If Evelyn's father accepted Joy's girlfriend, the family (be) much happier. (imaginary result)

1. would spend (imaginary situation)

2. wouldn't build (imaginary situation)

3. would be (imaginary situation)

2. Make the sentence

Put the verb in the correct form after would. Then read it out loud.

1. If people were kinder to each other, the world (be) a better place.

2. If Evelyn could choose again, she (do) things differently.

3. If Waymond left, Evelyn (feel) very alone.

1. would be (would + base verb)

2. would do (would + base verb)

3. would feel (would + base verb)

3. Complete the short text

Fill each gap with the correct second conditional form. Read it aloud when you finish.

If Evelyn (not / have) so many problems, her life (feel) calmer. And if Joy (know) her mother truly accepted her, maybe she would never have become Jobu Tupaki.

didn't have · would feel · knew

Use if + past simple for the imaginary situation, and would + base verb for its result.

The Real Story — is any of this true?

Here is the surprising part: the idea of many universes is not just for films — and the family story underneath is very real.

🌌 A real idea from physics — but not proven

The idea of many universes is not just from films. In 1957, a scientist called Hugh Everett had an idea: every time something can happen in more than one way, the universe splits into different versions. Some other scientists also think there could be many universes, from a theory called "eternal inflation." But nobody has proven this. It is a real, serious idea in science — but it is still just an idea, not a fact.

A real family story underneath

Under all the universes, this film tells a very real story: an immigrant family with money problems, generation differences, and a mother who finds it hard to accept her daughter. The directors are called "the Daniels" — Dan Kwan and Daniel Scheinert. In 2023, the film won seven Oscars, including Best Picture. Michelle Yeoh became the first Asian woman to win Best Actress. The laundromat and the multiverse are not real, but the family's problems feel very real.

Talk about it:
  • Did you know the multiverse idea comes from real science, or did you think it was only for films?
  • Do you think a film about family problems needs a fantasy story like this to feel exciting? Why, or why not?
