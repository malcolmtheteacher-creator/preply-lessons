Check your idea CLICK
"Could well have forgotten" is the weightier guess — well adds confidence, as if the speaker has a real reason to think so (she's done it before). "Might just" does the opposite: just shrinks the guess down to something modest and tentative. Tiny words like "well" and "just" are doing real work — that's exactly what today is about.
Today: fine degrees of certainty — about now (must, can't, may well, might just, could well), about the past (must have, can't have, should have, would have, might/could have), and about the people you know (had better, would rather, may as well, bound to, likely to, supposed to, meant to, and stressed will) — so you can judge, guess and describe with real precision.

Judging the Present

How sure are you, right now? Five small words carry five different amounts of confidence.

Spot the pattern

Three guesses about what's true right now:

  • "That can't be Tom's car — his is red, not blue."
  • "She must be back from holiday — I saw the lights on last night."
  • "He might just be joking; you never know with him."

Before you check: "can't" and "must" don't really mean "impossible" and "obliged" here — so what ARE they doing?

Check the pattern CLICK
All three are guesses about the present, not facts — but each one signals a different amount of confidence. Can't = I'm sure it's NOT true. Must = I'm sure it IS true. Might just = a modest, hedged guess. The modal isn't about ability or obligation here at all — it's a confidence dial.
can't + be → certain something is NOT true
must + be → certain something IS true
may well / could well + be → a confident, reasoned guess
might just + be → a modest, hedged guess

Guessing the Past

You didn't see it happen — but you can still sound confident (or not) about what did.

Spot the pattern

Three guesses about something that already happened:

  • "She must have missed the train — she's never usually this late."
  • "He can't have read my message; he'd have replied by now."
  • "You should have told me you were coming — I'd have cooked more!"

What changes in the grammar when you're guessing about the past instead of now?

Check the pattern CLICK
You add have + past participle after the modal. The meanings shift a little too: "should have told me" isn't a guess at all — it's a comment on what didn't happen but ought to have. "I'd have cooked more" imagines a different, unreal result. Same building blocks, several different jobs.
must + have + past participle → certain it happened
can't + have + past participle → certain it didn't happen
should + have + past participle → it didn't happen, and that's the problem
would + have + past participle → an imagined result, not what really happened
might / could + have + past participle → maybe — not sure

From a plain guess to a past deduction

"I don't know for sure, but I think he left."
→
"He must have left."

Expectations, Habits & Preferences

For advice, likelihood, plans that don't always happen, and the things people just always do.

Spot the pattern

  • "You'd better leave now, or you'll be stuck in rush hour."
  • "He's bound to be late — he always is."
  • "She will leave her keys in the door; it drives me mad."

None of these use a single modal word like "must" or "will" for the future — so why do they feel like modals?

Check the pattern CLICK
They carry the same kinds of meaning as true modals — warning, near-certainty, typical behaviour — even though the grammar looks different: 'd better + bare verb, is bound to + infinitive, and a stressed will + bare verb for something that's just typical of a person, not really about the future at all.
had better (not) + verb → strong advice, with a consequence
would rather + verb → preference
may as well + verb → a weak, "nothing better to do" decision
be bound to + verb → near-certain, inevitable
be likely to / unlikely to + verb → probable / improbable
be supposed to / be meant to + verb → expected, by rule or plan (doesn't always happen)
will / would + verb (stressed) → typical, characteristic behaviour

From a strong modal to a warning with a consequence

"You really must leave now — it's getting late."
→
"You'd better leave now — it's getting late."
