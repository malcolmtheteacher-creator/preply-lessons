Sub-Saharan Africa: Stereotype vs Reality

8 lessons on the cultures of Sub-Saharan Africa — each one opens with a stereotype and finds out what is really true.

Safaris, poverty, drums, “one country”: Sub-Saharan Africa attracts some of the most sweeping stereotypes in the world, even though it holds nearly fifty countries and well over a billion people. Start with the Background lesson, then work through the topics. Each lesson asks three questions — what does the stereotype say, what is the reality, and why does the idea exist? — and finishes with practical tips for working, travelling and making friends across cultures.

📖 Background1

Read this first: who lives in Sub-Saharan Africa, and how the many cultures connect.

BackgroundSub-Saharan Africa: Nearly Fifty Countries, Not One
Background
Sub-Saharan Africa has nearly fifty countries, more than a billion people and some of the world's youngest populations. Its economies, climates, languages and cities differ at least as much as those of Europe or Asia.
The stereotype: “Africa is one country: poor, wild and all the same.”

🧭 Stereotype vs Reality7

Culture, cultural differences and the stories we tell about each other.

1Is It All Safaris and Wild Animals?
Wildlife & tourism
Parks such as the Serengeti and Kruger are famous, and wildlife matters for tourism and conservation. But a great deal of the population lives in cities, farms and villages, and conservation works best when local communities share in it.
The stereotype: “Africa is mostly wilderness full of lions and elephants, and everyone there lives close to wild animals.”
Quantifiers and Noun Phrases — A Great Deal Of, The Vast Majority Of, A Handful OfOpen →
2Behind the Times? Mobile Money in Africa
Technology & money
In many countries, mobile phones are the main way to save, send and receive money. Sub-Saharan Africa leads the world in mobile money, thanks to a lack of banks and a very practical solution.
The stereotype: “Africa is poor and technologically behind, so nothing there is modern.”
Cause and Result — So … That, Such … That, As a Result, Thanks ToOpen →
3Thousands of Languages: Multilingual by Default
Languages & communication
There is no language called “African”: the continent has about 2,000 languages from several large families, including tonal languages and those with click sounds. Being multilingual is normal, and many people use a local language, a regional one and an international one daily.
The stereotype: “Africans speak ‘African’ — and communicate in simple tribal languages.”
Wishes and Preferences — Wish, If Only, Would RatherOpen →
4More Than Drums: Africa's Music Goes Global
Music & culture
Traditional instruments such as the kora and the mbira live alongside highlife, Congolese rumba, Afrobeat, Afrobeats and amapiano. Today the region's artists top charts, win awards and shape music around the world.
The stereotype: “African music is just drumming and traditional dance.”

5Never Rush a Greeting
Manners & customs
Greetings in the region are often long, ask after family and health, and follow rules about age, hands and eye contact. The rules differ from country to country and between the city and the village.
The stereotype: “Africans are always friendly and relaxed about everything, and they all greet in the same way.”
Impersonal Passive — It Is Considered, Is Expected To, Is Said ToOpen →
6Sharing the Plate: Food Across a Continent
Food & daily life
Sub-Saharan Africa has a rich, varied range of staples, stews, grains and fermented foods, and sharing meals is central to family and community life. Hunger is a real problem in some places and times, but it does not describe a whole region.
The stereotype: “African food is all the same, and there is not much to eat except in a famine.”
Ellipsis and Substitution — So Do I, One, Ones, Does TooOpen →
7Huts or High-Rises? Africa's Fast-Growing Cities
Cities & change
Sub-Saharan Africa is changing from a mainly rural region to an urban one, faster than anywhere else. Its cities are centres of business, music and technology, with serious challenges of housing, transport and jobs.
The stereotype: “Africa is all villages and huts — or all slums.”
