Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. He never went near a church service, yet people travelled for days to hear the mystic speak, certain that this strange, wild-eyed man could see straight into the soul and touch God in ways a priest never could.
a mystic (n) — a person believed to have a direct, spiritual connection with God or hidden truths, beyond ordinary religion.
2. He was born a peasant, in a village of muddy lanes and wooden huts, where families worked the land by hand and had never owned more than the few animals in the yard.
a peasant (n) — a poor farmer or farm worker of low social rank, especially in the past.
3. When the doctors gave up, the family sent for the healer — a man with no medical training at all, who laid his hands on the sick child and murmured prayers until, somehow, the fever broke.
a healer (n) — someone believed to cure illness by spiritual or natural means rather than ordinary medicine.
4. Beyond the palace gates was hunger and war, but inside the court the gold gleamed, the music played, and the ruling family and their nobles dined as if nothing in the world had changed.
the court (n) — the household, family and officials surrounding a king, queen or emperor.
5. He held no official title and commanded no army, yet his quiet word in the right ear could make or break a minister — such was his influence over those in power.
influence (n) — the power to affect what other people think, decide or do, often without holding any formal authority.
6. At first it was just a whisper passed between two servants; within a week the rumour had spread through every drawing room in the city, growing wilder with each retelling.
a rumour (n) — a story passed from person to person that may or may not be true, and is not yet proven.
7. When the newspapers printed what he had done, the scandal was so great that respectable families refused to say his name aloud, and his enemies finally had the weapon they wanted.
a scandal (n) — a shocking event or behaviour that causes public outrage and damages reputations.
8. The aristocrat had been born into a family older than the throne itself, with vast estates, a palace in the capital and centuries of titles behind his name.
an aristocrat (n) — a member of the highest social class, usually with an inherited title, land and wealth.
9. Unable to remove him any other way, the plotters decided to poison him — they slipped a deadly substance into his wine and waited for it to take hold.
to poison (v) — to harm or kill someone by giving them a substance that damages the body.
10. As the crisis deepened, the city tipped into a frenzy — crowds surging through the streets, voices screaming over one another, all reason swept away by raw emotion.
a frenzy (n) — a state of wild, uncontrolled excitement, fear or activity.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — From a Siberian village to the palace

Grigori Rasputin was born in 1869 in Pokrovskoye, a small farming village in Siberia, on the cold eastern edge of the Russian Empire. He grew up a peasant, poor and barely educated, and for much of his early life there was nothing remarkable about him at all. Then, in his late twenties, he made a pilgrimage to a monastery and came back a changed man. He began to wander from village to village as a kind of holy man — praying, preaching and, people said, healing the sick with his hands and his prayers.

Word of this strange, magnetic wanderer travelled ahead of him, and in 1903 it carried him all the way to the capital, Saint Petersburg. There his intense eyes and rough peasant manner made him a curiosity in the drawing rooms of the rich, who were fascinated by mystics and faith healers. In November 1905 he was finally introduced to the two most powerful people in the empire: Tsar Nicholas II and his wife, the Tsarina Alexandra. It was a meeting that would change all their lives.

The royal couple were carrying a terrible secret. Their only son, the young Tsarevich Alexei — the boy who would one day inherit the throne — suffered from haemophilia, a disease that stops the blood from clotting. The smallest cut or bruise could start bleeding that no doctor could stop, and any episode might kill him. The Tsarina lived in constant fear for her son, and the doctors of the court could do nothing for him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What kind of background did Rasputin come from, and what changed him?

2. What was the secret illness that frightened the royal family so deeply?

1. He was a poor, barely educated Siberian peasant; a pilgrimage to a monastery in his late twenties transformed him into a wandering holy man and healer.

2. Their son Alexei had haemophilia — his blood would not clot, so any small injury could cause bleeding that might kill him.

Discuss:
  • The richest people in the empire were fascinated by a rough peasant mystic. Why do powerful, comfortable people sometimes long for exactly that?

Part 2 — The man the Tsarina could not do without

In late 1906 Rasputin was brought to the bedside of the sick boy, and what happened next made his name. Again and again, when Alexei was bleeding and in agony, Rasputin would arrive, pray over him, speak to him gently — and the bleeding would seem to ease. He did appear to calm the child and stop the attacks when the trained doctors had failed. Historians still argue over how: perhaps his calm steadied the frightened boy, perhaps he told the doctors to stop their useless treatments, perhaps it was simply chance and timing. But to Alexandra there was no mystery at all. She was certain that God had sent this holy man to save her son.

From that moment, Rasputin held such power over the Tsarina that she would hear no word against him. He had no title and no office, yet his influence reached into the running of the whole empire. He whispered advice about ministers and policy; he was too useful to the family to cast aside, and Alexandra protected him fiercely. Meanwhile, rumours about his private life spread through the city — wild drinking, women, and behaviour no holy man should allow himself. The nobles and the public were scandalised. How could a drunken peasant have the ear of the Tsar?

The timing could not have been worse. When the First World War broke out in 1914, it went badly for Russia, and in 1915 Nicholas left to command the army at the front. That left Alexandra — and, behind her, Rasputin — effectively running the government in the capital. To a country sliding into hunger, defeat and chaos, it looked as though a mad peasant was steering the empire onto the rocks. The aristocracy decided that Rasputin had to go.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the Tsarina come to believe Rasputin was essential?

2. Why did Rasputin's influence become so dangerous during the war?

1. He seemed able to ease Alexei's bleeding and pain when the court doctors could not, so she believed God had sent him to save her son.

2. With Nicholas away at the front from 1915, Alexandra and Rasputin were left running the government while Russia lost the war and slid into chaos — so he was blamed for the disaster.

Discuss:
  • Was Rasputin really steering the empire, or just a convenient face to blame for problems far bigger than him? How would you tell the difference?

Part 3 — The night he would not die

In December 1916 a group of conservatives decided to kill him to save the throne. They were led by Prince Felix Yusupov, one of the richest aristocrats in Russia, together with a grand duke of the royal family, Dmitri Pavlovich, and a politician, Vladimir Purishkevich. On the night of 30 December 1916, Yusupov lured Rasputin to his palace in Saint Petersburg with the promise of meeting his beautiful wife.

What happened next became one of history's most famous — and most exaggerated — death scenes. According to Yusupov's later account, the plotters fed Rasputin cakes and wine laced with poison, yet he ate and drank and seemed entirely unharmed. In a panic, Yusupov shot him; Rasputin fell, then suddenly rose and attacked him. They shot him again, beat him, and finally wrapped his body and pushed it under the ice of the freezing Neva river. Yusupov claimed the "mad monk" was so hard to kill that only the river finished him — and the legend of the man who would not die was born.

The truth, the autopsy suggests, was simpler and grimmer. The doctors found no clear sign of poison and no water in his lungs, which means he did not drown. He died of gunshot wounds — most likely a final, close-range shot to the forehead. Yusupov, who lost his fortune in the revolution, retold the story for the rest of his life, growing it with each version. Rasputin's murder did nothing to save the monarchy. Within weeks the country fell into a frenzy of strikes and protest; within three months Nicholas had given up the throne, and the three-hundred-year Romanov dynasty was finished. Rasputin himself had reportedly warned that if he died, the royal family would not long survive him — and, whatever the truth of that, they did not.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who plotted Rasputin's murder, and how did they lure him to the palace?

2. How does the legend of his death differ from what the autopsy actually showed?

1. A group of conservatives led by Prince Felix Yusupov, with Grand Duke Dmitri Pavlovich and Vladimir Purishkevich; Yusupov invited him to his palace with the promise of meeting his wife.

2. The legend (from Yusupov) has him survive poison and shooting and finally drown under the ice; the autopsy found no clear poison and no water in his lungs — he died of gunshot wounds, probably a shot to the forehead.

Discuss:
  • Yusupov made the death wilder every time he told it. Why do dramatic, exaggerated versions of events so often beat the plain truth — and survive longer?

Grammar — Emphasis: do/did + so / such / too / enough

This is a story built on big claims — he did seem to heal the boy; his power was so great that ministers feared him. When you want a listener to feel the weight of something, English gives you tools to turn the volume up. Here are four of the most useful, all drawn straight from tonight's story.

1. Emphatic do / does / did — insisting it's true.
Drop a stressed do, does or did in front of a plain verb to push back against doubt — "you say he was a fraud, but he did appear to ease the boy's suffering." The main verb stays in its base form: do appear, does seem, did heal (never "did healed"). Use it to contradict, to concede, or simply to underline: "He did seem to calm the child." / "She does believe it." / "They did kill him in the end."
2. so + adjective/adverb  ·  3. such (a) + noun.
Both mean "to a high degree", and both can be followed by a that-clause showing the result.
• so goes with an adjective or adverb: "His influence was so great that ministers feared him." / "He recovered so quickly."
• such goes with a noun (add a/an with singular countable nouns): "He held such power over the Tsarina that she heard no word against him." / "He was such a scandal that families refused to say his name."
4. too … to  ·  … enough to — when the degree decides the outcome.
• too + adjective/adverb + to = more than is acceptable or possible; it blocks the result: "He was too useful to the family to cast aside." (so they didn't)
• adjective + enough + to = as much as is needed; it allows the result: "He was brave enough to walk into the palace." Note the word order: too comes before the adjective, enough comes after it.

1. Add emphatic do / does / did

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Rasputin had no title and no army, yet he shaped an empire. Where does that kind of informal power come from — and why is it so hard to remove?
  2. His enemies killed him to save the throne, and the throne fell anyway within months. Were they wrong about how much he mattered?
  3. We remember the wild legend of his death far better than the plain truth of it. Does it matter that the famous version is largely invented?
