Alexander the Great

A young king who conquered most of the known world — and never lost a battle.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of an empire built in a single lifetime, and discuss what real greatness means — using comparatives and superlatives (and degree) to weigh and rank what made him extraordinary.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. When the old king died, every eye turned to his eldest son, the heir who would now wear the crown.
an heir (n) — a person legally entitled to inherit a title, position or property when its holder dies.
2. It took months of patience, but in the end the wild stallion grew tame enough for a child to ride.
to tame (v) — to make a wild or dangerous animal calm and obedient.
3. Drunk on ambition, the young king set out to conquer his neighbours, taking their cities one by one.
to conquer (v) — to take control of a country or people by military force. (noun: conquest)
4. Three hundred defenders faced an army of thousands — they were hopelessly outnumbered.
to be outnumbered (v, often passive) — to have fewer people, especially soldiers, than the opposing side.
5. Knowing the battle was lost, the soldiers turned to flee, throwing down their shields as they ran.
to flee (v, past: fled) — to run away from danger or defeat.
6. Past the last farm the road petered out and the wild frontier began, where few travellers dared to go.
a frontier (n) — the furthest edge of a known or settled region; a border with the unknown.
7. Cold, unpaid and far from home, the crew finally rose up in mutiny and refused every order the captain gave.
a mutiny (n) — a refusal by soldiers or sailors to obey the orders of those in command.
8. The moment the strong leader was gone, the alliance began to fragment, splitting into smaller and smaller rival camps.
to fragment (v) — to break apart into many separate, smaller pieces.
9. Long after the composer died, his music lived on — the legacy he left to every generation that followed.
a legacy (n) — something handed down from the past, or left behind by a person or event, that still has effects today.
10. So many wild tales were told of him that, within a century, the man had become a legend.
a legend (n) — a famous story from the past, often part true and part exaggerated; or a person who becomes the subject of such stories.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The making of a king

Alexander was born in 356 BC in Macedonia, a kingdom in the north of the Greek world, the son of King Philip II. From the start he was raised to rule. Philip hired the greatest mind of the age, the philosopher Aristotle, to tutor the boy, and under him Alexander learned medicine, philosophy, science and literature — an education that would shape how he saw the world for the rest of his short life.

The famous story of his boyhood says everything about him. A magnificent but uncontrollable horse called Bucephalus was brought to the court, and not one of the king's men could mount it. The young Alexander, watching closely, noticed that the animal was simply frightened of its own shadow. He turned its head towards the sun and calmed it — and tamed the horse that no grown man could ride. Bucephalus would carry him into battle for the next twenty years.

Then, in 336 BC, Philip was assassinated, and Alexander became king of Macedonia at just twenty years old. He inherited his father's hardened, professional army and his father's enormous ambition: to invade and conquer the Persian Empire, the greatest power on earth. Most heirs would have been content to hold what they were given. Alexander had no intention of stopping there.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who tutored Alexander as a boy, and what did the story of Bucephalus reveal about him?

2. What did Alexander inherit when he became king at twenty?

1. The philosopher Aristotle tutored him. Taming Bucephalus — by noticing the horse feared its own shadow and turning it to the sun — showed his intelligence, observation and nerve even as a boy.

2. A powerful, professional army built by his father Philip II, and Philip's ambition to invade and conquer the Persian Empire.

Discuss:
  • Alexander was handed everything — a kingdom, an army, a plan. Does inheriting so much make greatness easier, or harder to prove was truly your own?

Part 2 — The conqueror who never lost

In 334 BC, Alexander crossed into Asia and never came back. What followed was one of the most astonishing military careers in history. At Issus in 333 BC he met the Persian king Darius III in person and broke his army; Darius fled the field so quickly that he left his own mother, wife and children behind in the camp. Two years later, at Gaugamela in 331 BC, the two kings met again. Heavily outnumbered, Alexander drove straight at the Persian centre, and once more Darius fled. The Persian Empire, which had stood for two hundred years, was finished.

City by city, region by region, Alexander pressed on — through Egypt, Babylon and the Persian heartland and out into the far east. Along the way he founded new cities and named many of them Alexandria, after himself; the greatest, in Egypt, would become one of the most important centres of learning in the ancient world. He kept going until 326 BC, when he reached India and fought a hard, rain-soaked battle at the Hydaspes River against the local king, Porus.

He had now reached the frontier of the world the Greeks knew, and he wanted to go further still. But his soldiers had been marching and fighting for eight years, thousands of miles from home, and they had had enough. On the banks of a river in India they refused to take another step — a mutiny that no speech of Alexander's could break. For the first and only time, he was forced to turn back. He had never lost a battle. He had simply run out of army.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened at Issus and Gaugamela, and what was the result for the Persian Empire?

2. Alexander never lost a battle — so what finally stopped his advance?

1. Alexander defeated Darius III at both battles (Issus, 333 BC; Gaugamela, 331 BC), and Darius fled each time. These victories destroyed the Persian Empire.

2. Not a defeat, but his own exhausted soldiers: after eight years and thousands of miles, they mutinied in India and refused to go further, forcing him to turn back.

Discuss:
  • His men stopped him, not his enemies. What does it tell us about even the greatest leader that he depends entirely on the people who follow him?

Part 3 — The empire that outlived the man

Alexander led his weary army back west, but he never reached home. In 323 BC, in the ancient city of Babylon, he fell ill with a fever and, after days of sickness, died. He was just thirty-two years old. The cause has been argued over ever since: many historians point to disease, such as typhoid or malaria, which were common in the region; others, then and now, have suspected poison. What is certain is that the most successful commander in history was gone, far too soon, with no grown heir to follow him.

And that absence proved fatal to everything he had built. Asked on his deathbed who should rule, Alexander is said to have answered only, "the strongest." His leading generals took him at his word. They turned on one another, and over the following decades the vast empire fragmented into rival kingdoms, each ruled by a former general and his descendants. The single greatest empire the world had seen was torn apart almost as fast as it had been made.

Yet what Alexander left behind outlasted any throne. Everywhere his armies had gone, they had carried Greek language, art, science and ideas, which blended with the cultures of Egypt and Asia to create what we now call the Hellenistic world — an age of learning that endured for centuries. The empire vanished within a generation; the legacy did not. And the man himself became a legend, the model of the conqueror that ambitious rulers would measure themselves against for two thousand years.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How and when did Alexander die — and why is the cause still debated?

2. What happened to his empire after his death, and what survived it?

1. He died in Babylon in 323 BC, aged thirty-two, after a fever. The cause is debated because ancient sources disagree: it may have been disease (typhoid or malaria), while some suspected poison.

2. With no grown heir, his generals fought one another and the empire fragmented into rival kingdoms. What survived was the spread of Greek (Hellenistic) culture — and his legend.

Discuss:
  • His empire fell apart in a generation, but his culture and his legend lasted for centuries. Which is the truer measure of a person's "greatness" — what they build, or what they leave behind?

The grammar this story runs on: comparatives & superlatives (and degree)

A life as outsized as Alexander's is a story of measuring and ranking — bigger armies, harder battles, the single greatest empire of all. To weigh one thing against another, and to crown the very top of the scale, you need comparatives, superlatives and the language of degree.

The three tools:
• Comparative — one thing against another: "Persia was richer than Macedonia," "he marched further than any Greek before him."
• Superlative — the top of the whole group: "Alexander built the largest empire the ancient world had ever seen," "Aristotle, the greatest mind of the age."
• Emphasisers (degree) — to sharpen the gap: "by far the most driven of all commanders," "far more ambitious than his father," "nowhere near as cautious as his generals."

1. Comparatives & superlatives — weigh it, then crown it

Use -er / more … than to compare two things, and the -est / the most to mark the top of a group. Mind the irregulars: good → better → the best, far → further → the furthest.

1. Persia was (rich) than Macedonia, but Alexander's army was (disciplined).

2. Aristotle was (great) mind of the age, hired to tutor the boy.

3. At Gaugamela the Macedonians were (heavily outnumbered) than ever, yet Alexander won (decisive) victory of his career.

1. richer than / more disciplined (short adjective + -er; longer adjective + more)

2. the greatest (top of the group)

3. more heavily outnumbered / the most decisive

2. Emphasisers — sharpen the gap

Strengthen a comparison or a superlative with by far, far more, nowhere near as … as, easily. Read each aloud and feel how the emphasiser raises the stakes.

1. He was the most driven of all commanders — no rival came close. (emphasiser + superlative)

2. Alexander was ambitious than his father, who had only dreamed of Persia. (emphasiser + comparative)

3. His exhausted men were as eager as he was to march on. (negative emphasiser)

1. by far (by far the most driven)

2. far more (far more ambitious than)

3. nowhere near as (nowhere near as eager as)

3. The + comparative, the + comparative — two things that rise together

To say one thing grows as another grows, use the pattern the + comparative, the + comparative: "The more he conquered, the more he wanted." Build your own about Alexander.

1. The further he marched, his men grew. (tired)

2. the empire grew, it became to hold together. (big / hard)

3. The more cities he founded, his legend spread. (wide)

1. the more tired

2. The bigger / the harder (The bigger the empire grew, the harder it became to hold together.)

3. the wider

Notice the shape: two clauses, each opening with "the + comparative" — the first sets the cause, the second the result.
