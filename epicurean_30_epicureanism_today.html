Epicureanism Is Back (Sort Of)

For most of history, Epicureanism was marginalized or despised. But in the last 50 years, something surprising happened: philosophers started taking Epicurus seriously again. Not as a hedonist or a cautionary tale, but as a profound thinker about how to live.

This isn't a return to Gassendi's Christian Epicureanism. It's a secular recovery. But it's also not a defense of excess. Modern Epicureanism is surprisingly close to what Epicurus actually taught.

Modern Epicurean Philosophy

Catherine Wilson (b. 1951)
Contemporary Epicurean Scholar
Wilson has written the definitive modern defense of Epicureanism: The Pleasure Principle (2018). She argues that pleasure (properly understood) is a legitimate good and that Epicurus offers valuable guidance on how to live. She's not naive about hedonism — she carefully distinguishes base from refined pleasures, and shows that virtue and wisdom lead to greater pleasure than excess. Her work rescues Epicureanism from centuries of slander and makes it philosophically respectable.
Michel Onfray (b. 1959)
French Philosopher & Public Intellectual
Onfray has written extensively on hedonistic philosophy and Epicureanism. His Tractatus Epicureus and other works promote a secular, materialist epicureanism — pleasure without guilt, without the need for God, without shame. For Onfray, Epicureanism is a radical philosophy of freedom: freedom from religious authority, from guilt, from ascetic denial. He's more explicitly hedonistic than Catherine Wilson, but still careful to distinguish baser from higher pleasures.
✨
Why the Revival?
Modern philosophy has become more secular and naturalistic. Without Christian doctrines constraining thought, Epicureanism looks more attractive. Also, psychology and neuroscience have validated some Epicurean insights: pleasure is real, important, not shameful. Happiness research shows simple living and friendship do matter more than wealth.

Key Ideas in Modern Epicureanism

  • Realism about Pleasure: Pleasure is not sinful or base. It's fundamental to human flourishing and should be taken seriously as a philosophical good.
  • Hierarchy of Pleasures: But not all pleasures are equal. Intellectual, social, and spiritual pleasures are more stable and satisfying than bodily ones.
  • Simplicity and Sufficiency: The best life is one of moderation — enough to live well, but without excess or dependency on luxuries.
  • Friendship as Central: Friendship is the greatest good. Epicurus was right: the examined life with good friends is the goal.
  • Rational Epicureanism: Use reason to evaluate what brings real pleasure vs. what merely seems pleasurable. This is where virtue enters.

What Can We Learn From Epicurus Today?

Practical Wisdom for the Modern Age

Your turn first: What do you think Epicureanism offers that modern life lacks? What problem might Epicurus help us solve?

1. Against Consumerism

Modern capitalism wants you to always want more. Epicureanism teaches: Enough is enough. Want modest food, drink, shelter, and company. Wanting less is freedom. This is radical in a consumer economy.

2. Against Busyness

Modern life prizes productivity and achievement. Epicureanism says: Slow down. Spend time with friends. Enjoy conversation, wine, simple meals. Not everything is about accomplishment. Some things are worth doing for their own sake.

3. Against Fear

The modern mind is anxious: fear of missing out, of failure, of death. Epicurus offers philosophy as therapy: Most of what we fear won't happen. Most of what happens is manageable. Peace of mind (ataraxia) is achievable through reason.

4. For Friendship

Modern life is isolating: we work in competitive environments, scroll through screens, move far from family. Epicureanism insists: Friendship is the greatest good. Invest time in deep relationships. This is not peripheral to a good life; it's central.

5. For Intellectual Life

In an age of entertainment and distraction, Epicureanism rehabilitates the examined life. Reading, conversation, philosophy, science — these are sources of the deepest pleasure. Not guilty pleasures; real pleasures.

6. For Freedom

Epicureanism is profoundly freedom-loving. Freedom from fear, from authority, from compulsion, from shame. This resonates in an age of surveillance and control.

Science Confirms Epicureanism

Happiness Research and Positive Psychology

Over the past 30 years, psychologists have studied happiness and well-being. The results would delight Epicurus:

📊
What Research Shows:
• Beyond a certain income threshold (~$75k/year), more money doesn't increase happiness
• Social connection is the strongest predictor of well-being
• Meaning, autonomy, and engagement matter more than luxury
• Simple pleasures (good food, friends, nature) are more satisfying than expensive ones
• Contemplation and learning bring lasting joy

The Epicurean Thesis Validated

This is exactly what Epicurus said. He wasn't wrong about human flourishing. Modern science is catching up to ancient wisdom.

The only difference: Epicurus used reason and observation. Modern psychologists use controlled studies and statistics. Same conclusions.

A Final Thought

Epicurus offers something rare: a philosophy that takes pleasure seriously while honoring virtue, that embraces nature without denying meaning, that seeks freedom without falling into excess. He reminds us that the good life is not complicated—friendship, simple food, philosophical conversation, freedom from fear.

In a world of anxiety, consumption, and distraction, that wisdom is more timely than ever.
