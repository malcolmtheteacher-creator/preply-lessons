How do you think about history?

♦ ♦ ♦

Is history cyclical or does it have direction?

Do civilizations rise and fall endlessly? Or is history moving toward something?

A note: The Greeks thought time was cyclical — history repeating in endless patterns. Augustine changed this. He was the first to argue that history is linear — that it has a beginning, a direction, and a destination. This idea has shaped how we think about everything since.

II. The Story — Rome Falls, and the World is Forced to Think Differently

In August 410 AD, the unthinkable happened. For eight centuries, Rome had been the center of the known world. Its legions were invincible. Its law was absolute. Its gods had blessed it with dominion over all nations. Then, in three days, Alaric and his Visigoths sacked the city. The greatest civilization on earth was humbled.

The Accusation

Pagan philosophers were quick to assign blame. Rome, they said, had abandoned the old gods. The state religion had been Christianity for a century now. And what was Christianity? A weakness. A religion of slaves and cowards. It taught forgiveness instead of virtue, humility instead of greatness. It had softened Rome’s martial spirit. That is why Rome fell.

Augustine, now in his mid-fifties and bishop of Hippo in North Africa, heard the accusations. Christian refugees were flooding into North Africa. They too were confused, traumatized. They had believed God would protect Rome. How could Rome fall if God was on its side?

Augustine decided to answer. The result was the City of God, begun in 413 AD and completed in 426 AD — a work of 22 books, over a thousand pages, one of the greatest philosophical and theological works ever written.

Two Cities, Two Loves

Augustine begins his answer with a startling claim. Rome did not fall because it abandoned the gods. Rome fell because Rome was always destined to fall. There are not, he argues, many histories. There is one history, and it follows a single principle:

By two cities I mean, to speak more precisely, two loves: the love of God even to the disregard of self is the characteristic of the City of God; whereas self-love even to the disregard of God is the characteristic of the City of the World. — Augustine, City of God, Book XIV, Chapter 28

All of human history, Augustine claims, is the interplay between these two loves. There is the City of God — those whose ultimate love and allegiance is to God. And there is the City of Man — those whose ultimate love is to themselves, to power, to glory, to domination.

These two cities have existed since the beginning. Cain and Abel represent them. David and his enemies represent them. And they will exist until the end of time.

History Has a Direction

Here is where Augustine revolutionizes how humans think. The Greeks thought time was cyclical. Everything that happens has happened before and will happen again. The Stoics thought time was an eternal return — infinite cycles repeating the same pattern.

Augustine says: No. History is linear. It has a beginning, when God created the world. It has a trajectory, as God unfolds his purposes through time. And it has an end, when time itself will cease and eternity will begin.

Within this linear history, everything that happens has meaning. Events are not random. They are not purposeless cycles. They are part of a divine narrative that is moving toward a conclusion.

This changes everything. After Augustine, Western civilization stops thinking of history as cyclical and starts thinking of it as a story. A story with meaning. A story going somewhere. This is why the idea of progress becomes possible. This is why we believe the future can be better than the past. Augustine invented this way of thinking.

Why Rome Fell

So Augustine’s answer to the pagan accusation is this: Rome did not fall because it became Christian. Rome fell because Rome was always a city built on the love of power and glory. Rome was part of the City of Man. And the City of Man is not permanent. It rises and falls according to divine law. Rome rose when its love of power served, however unknowingly, divine purposes. Rome fell when it became purely self-serving.

Christianity did not weaken Rome. Christianity exposed what Rome always was — a human city, not an eternal city. Only the City of God is eternal.

Just War Theory

In defending Christianity, Augustine also develops one of philosophy’s most influential doctrines: just war theory. Augustine asks: is war ever morally justified for a Christian state?

His answer is yes — but only under specific conditions. A just war must:

1. Have just cause: Not revenge or conquest, but defense or restoration of justice

2. Be declared by legitimate authority: Not a private person, but a ruler

3. Have right intention: The cause must truly be what you say it is

This doctrine, controversial though it remains, shapes all subsequent Western thought about war and peace. Augustine refuses the Stoic answer (endure all suffering) and the Epicurean answer (avoid conflict). He insists that justice sometimes requires force.

III. The Ideas — History is a Story, not a Cycle

Idea 1: Two Cities, Two Loves

All human history is divided by what people ultimately love.

Not by their conscious beliefs or stated ideologies. But by what they actually love — what they are willing to sacrifice for, what they truly seek.

This is a deeply philosophical move. Augustine is arguing that the ultimate shape of history is determined not by economics or geography or military might, but by the orientation of the human soul. What does the soul love? That determines everything.

Idea 2: Linear History

History is not cyclical. It is linear, moving from creation toward an end.

The Greeks inherited from myth the idea that time circles endlessly. But Augustine says: time is an arrow. It begins with creation. It proceeds through ages. It terminates in the final judgment. Every moment is unique, unrepeatable, meaningful.

This idea — that history has direction, that the past is genuinely past and the future is genuinely open — becomes foundational to the entire modern world. The idea of progress, the idea of innovation, the sense that the future can be different from the past — all of this depends on Augustine.

Idea 3: Justice Requires Sometimes Using Force

A state committed to justice may use war to defend the innocent or restore right order.

Augustine refuses both the Stoic answer (accept everything) and the Epicurean answer (avoid conflict). He insists that a truly loving response to injustice sometimes requires force.

This is controversial. Many argue Augustine opened the door to wars falsely claimed to be “just.” But Augustine himself is careful: the conditions are strict, the intention must be pure, and the goal is peace, not conquest.

What Augustine Opens

Augustine transforms philosophy from a search for eternal truths into a search for meaning in history. The question becomes: where do I stand in history? Which city do I belong to? What does my love reveal about my true allegiances?

This idea — that history has meaning, that the human story is going somewhere — shapes everything that comes after. Dante will use it to structure the Divine Comedy. Aquinas will use it to reconcile faith and reason across time. Hegel will make it the foundation of his philosophy of history. Marx will invert it but keep its structure. Even modern secular thought inherits Augustine’s belief that history is linear and meaningful.

And for this course, Augustine plants another seed: the City of God is the community of those forgiven, those whose ultimate love is to God. How does one enter that city? Through forgiveness. Through grace. Through the dissolution of the self-love that keeps us in the City of Man.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 questions to sit with.

Two cities in your own life

Augustine says history is divided by what people ultimately love. In your own life, what are you building the city of? God or self?

Is history linear or cyclical?

Augustine says history is linear, moving toward a destination. Do you think that is true? Or do we live in cycles?

Why Rome really fell

Augustine rejects the pagan explanation. Rome fell not because of Christianity but because Rome was always the City of Man. Is this convincing?

Just war theory

Augustine argues that force can be justified in defense of justice. Is this right? Where is the line between justice and vengeance?

What love shapes my life?

If Augustine is right, your life is shaped by your ultimate love. What do your choices reveal you ultimately love?

Progress as an idea

The modern belief in progress depends on Augustine’s idea that history is linear. Is progress real, or an illusion?

Permanence and change

Augustine says only the City of God is permanent. All earthly cities eventually fall. What does this mean for how we live?
