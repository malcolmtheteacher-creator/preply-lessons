Handling Challenging Questions

During speeches and presentations, audiences may ask difficult questions. Here's how to respond:

Challenging Situation Recommended Approach
Too many people want to ask questions Explain you'll try to answer as many as possible, but apologize if anyone is missed. Provide your contact details for follow-up questions later. Optionally, have someone note questions for emailed responses.
You don't understand the question Ask for clarification or ask the person to rephrase. If still unclear, be honest and take their contact details to research and respond later.
Asked a negative question Paraphrase it more positively. Keep your answers factual and avoid too much subjectivity. Frame response constructively.
Asked a multi-part question Acknowledge all parts, but focus on the most important one. Explain there isn't time to go into excessive detail on each part.
Question not related to your topic Thank the questioner politely, but explain you want to stay on topic. Suggest where they might find that information. Move to another questioner.
Personal question unrelated to topic Thank them, but explain you prefer to focus on the topic. Move to the next question professionally.
Hostile or aggressive question Stick to facts; avoid hypothetical answers and emotional responses. Move to another questioner quickly. Offer to speak with them separately after the session.
Persistent questioner when time is running out Take control professionally. Say you need to wrap up, but invite them to get in touch or speak with you afterwards.
No one responds to "Any questions?" Have a couple of questions prepared in advance. Introduce them: "Two things I'm often asked are..."

Key Principles

  • Stay calm and composed, no matter the question
  • Listen fully before responding
  • Buy yourself time with phrases like "That's a great question..." or "Let me think about that..."
  • Never get defensive or emotional
  • It's okay to say "I don't know" or "I'll get back to you on that"

Techniques for Impromptu Speeches

When you need to give a speech with little preparation, use these structures to quickly organize your thoughts:

1. Patterns of Three

How it works: Divide your speech into three clear parts and note key words for each.

Three templates to choose from:

Stories Sequences Time
Beginning, Middle, End Cause, Effect, Remedy Past, Present, Future
Setting, Problem, Solution Point, Reason, Example Before, During, After

Tip: Use the first letter of each heading as your memory aid (e.g., B-M-E for Beginning, Middle, End).

2. Five W's and One H

How it works: Ask and answer these questions to ensure you cover all main ideas:

  • Who? — The people involved
  • What? — What happened or the main idea
  • Where? — The location or context
  • When? — The time frame
  • Why? — The reason or importance
  • How? — The method or process

3. Choose a Theme

How it works: Pick a word that describes the subject and use it as an anagram to generate ideas.

Example: If paying tribute to a colleague, start with "FUN":

  • F — Fantastic, Fiery, Forward-thinker
  • U — Understanding, Unbelievable energy, Uncompromising standards
  • N — Nifty ideas, Natural, Nice person

4. Three Questions

How it works: Divide your speech into three sections by answering key questions. Great for farewell or tribute speeches.

Three Questions What to Consider Example Phrases
What has this person achieved here? When did they start? How have they changed? What has the company accomplished with them? "I remember when [name] joined us..." / "Since then, we've accomplished..."
What is the person doing next? What's their next role? What are they excited about? "[Name] is leaving us to..." / "They're especially excited about..."
What will we remember about them? Their qualities, impact, memorable moments "So I wish [name] well..." / "We'll always remember..."

Matching Structures to Speech Types

Business summary at a meeting: Patterns of Three (Cause-Effect-Remedy) or Five W's and H

Thank you speech: Three Questions or Patterns of Three (Stories)

Farewell for an employee: Three Questions

Update on your project: Patterns of Three (Past-Present-Future)

Birthday tribute: Patterns of Three (Stories) or Choose a Theme

Accepting an award: Thank you + Patterns of Three

Impromptu Speech Practice: Speaking Task

You have 5 minutes to plan and deliver an impromptu speech on a topic of your choice. Use one of the techniques you've learned.

Time remaining: 5:00

Speech Type Options:

Option 1: Business Situation

Give a brief update at a meeting about a project or situation in your work

Suggested structure: Past-Present-Future or Cause-Effect-Remedy

Option 2: Welcome or Farewell

Welcome a new colleague or say goodbye to someone leaving

Suggested structure: Three Questions or Patterns of Three (Stories)

Option 3: Thank You or Tribute

Give a short speech thanking someone for their help or paying tribute to their character

Suggested structure: Choose a Theme or Three Questions

Option 4: Proposal or Pitch

Briefly pitch an idea or suggest a solution to a problem

Suggested structure: Problem-Solution or Five W's and H

During Your Speech:

  • Start with a clear introduction
  • Follow your chosen structure
  • Use specific examples or details
  • Speak at a natural pace
  • End with a strong conclusion

Important Tips:

  • Don't apologize for being unprepared
  • Pause to think if needed — silence is better than "um" or "uh"
  • Make eye contact and use natural gestures
  • If you lose your place, take a breath and continue
  • Confidence comes from structure — trust your framework

Discussion Questions

Reflect on public speaking and handling the unexpected:

1. Speaking Confidence

How confident are you when speaking in front of others? What situations make you most nervous?

2. Past Experience

Have you ever had anything go wrong during a speech or presentation? What happened and how did you handle it?

3. Difficult Questions

What type of question do you find most challenging to answer? How do you typically respond?

4. Preparation

Do you prefer carefully prepared speeches or spontaneous presentations? What are the advantages of each?

5. Emergency Situations

What unexpected situations could happen during a presentation? (e.g., technology failure, power outage) How would you respond?

6. Observation

Describe a time something unexpected happened in a presentation or speech you were watching. How did the speaker handle it?

7. Learning from Experience

What's the most valuable lesson you've learned from giving speeches or presentations? How has it helped you develop as a communicator?

8. Virtual Speaking

Is it easier or harder to give a presentation online versus in person? What are the unique challenges of each?
