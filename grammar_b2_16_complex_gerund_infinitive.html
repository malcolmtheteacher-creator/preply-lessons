Warm-up

Have a go, out loud, before anything else — don't worry about mistakes.

SPEAK

Think of one thing you've done recently that you'd rather not own up to. How would you start that confession — "I admit..." or "I have to admit..."? Say the whole sentence.

Listen in on this conversation

Priya: You look exhausted. What's wrong?

Tom: The landlord finally admitted having ignored my messages for three weeks. There's a leak above the kitchen and it needs sorting before it ruins the ceiling.

Priya: Ugh. Did he say when someone's coming?

Tom: He wants a plumber to look at it tomorrow. Honestly, I just don't like being kept waiting like this.

Look at what Tom says in four short lines. Three different verb patterns, all doing real work in one everyday complaint. Can you spot all three? Say them before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "admitted having ignored" — having + past participle, for something that happened clearly before the admitting.
2. "wants a plumber to look at it" — a verb + person + to-infinitive, for what you want someone ELSE to do.
3. "don't like being kept waiting" — being + past participle, for something being done TO you.

These three patterns are exactly how real complaints, plans and favours get talked about — master them and you'll sound completely natural handling everyday situations like this one.
Today: three grammar patterns for talking about the past honestly, telling people what you want them to do, and describing things being done to you — admit / deny / regret + having done, want / expect / allow someone + to do, and being told / needs doing — so you can handle a complaint, a favour, or a plan without stopping to think.

Owning Up to the Past

When you look back and own up to (or deny) something, the timing needs to be crystal clear.

Spot the pattern

  • "She admitted having lost the spare key months ago — she'd been too embarrassed to say."
  • "He denied having read the email, even though everyone had watched him open it."
  • "Looking back, I regret having said that — it came out all wrong."

What's the shared machinery, and why do they all reach further back than usual? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
having + past participle — the perfect gerund. It places the action clearly BEFORE the admitting, denying, or regretting. Compare: "I regret saying that" (a general regret, common enough) vs "I regret having said that" (the same idea, but the earlier timing is unmistakable — useful whenever the order of events matters).
admit / deny / regret + having + past participle  (the action came clearly before this one)
You'll also hear the simple gerund without "having" — "admit losing", "deny reading", "regret saying" — and it's perfectly natural. The perfect form just makes the "before" unmistakable, which is exactly what we're practising here.

Quick check

1. Your neighbour finally owned up — weeks after it happened. Use admit + having + past participle of break:

"He finally admitted my garden fence last month."

2. Your little brother denied it — but there were crumbs on his jumper. Use deny + having + past participle of eat:

"He denied the last biscuit, and there were crumbs everywhere."

3. Looking back now, you wish you'd thought it through. Use regret + having + past participle of leave:

"I regret my old job so quickly — I should have planned it better."

Now say it

SPEAK

Tell me about something you regret having done — or having not done — in the last year or so. Use having + past participle at least once, out loud.

Getting Things Done

Sometimes the interesting thing isn't what YOU do — it's what's done TO you, or what still needs doing.

Spot the pattern

  • "I don't like being told what to do in my own kitchen."
  • "The car needs to be serviced before the trip."
  • "Actually, the car needs servicing too — same meaning, shorter and more spoken."

Three ways of putting the action onto the thing or person receiving it. What's different about the third one? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
being + past participle (passive gerund) — for something happening to you: being told, being interrupted, being kept waiting.
to be + past participle (passive infinitive) — mostly after need/want/deserve: needs to be fixed.
needs / wants + verb-ing — a shorter, very spoken shortcut that ONLY works with need and want: the car needs servicing = the car needs to be serviced. Same passive meaning, active-looking form.
being + past participle  (passive gerund — it's happening to you)
to be + past participle  (passive infinitive — after need / want / deserve)
need / want + verb-ing  (spoken shortcut, same meaning as "to be done")

Quick check

1. Your boss corrects your work in front of everyone, and you hate it. Use being + past participle of correct:

"I really don't like in front of the whole team."

2. Your bike's brakes are worn out. Use need + to be + past participle of check:

"My brakes before I cycle to work again."

3. The gutters are overflowing already. Use the shorter spoken form — need + verb-ing of clean:

"Honestly, the gutters before winter."

Now say it

SPEAK

Complain, nicely, about three things in your home or workplace that need attention. Use at least one "needs -ing" and one "needs to be done", out loud.

What You Want From Others

Telling someone what you want THEM to do — and getting the trickier verbs right.

Spot the pattern

  • "I want you to call the plumber before Friday."
  • "We didn't expect the tickets to be so expensive."
  • "My parents never allowed us to stay out past ten."

What comes straight after the person in each one? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
want / expect / allow + object + to-infinitive. The person (or thing) comes first, then "to" + the verb — never "want that..." (that's a direct translation trap from other languages).
want / expect / allow / tell + person + to + infinitive

Two verbs that change meaning

  • "After finishing the starter, he went on to order dessert." (a new, next action)
  • "She just went on talking, completely ignoring the time." (the same activity, continuing)
  • "We regret to inform you that the flight has been delayed." (a fixed, formal announcement — present tense only)

Say the difference between the first two, then check.

Check the idea CLICK
go on + -ing = carry on with the SAME activity. go on + to-infinitive = finish that, then move to something NEW. And regret to inform / tell is a fixed present-tense opener for breaking bad news right now — there's no natural past form like "he regretted to inform us"; for looking back, you say "he regretted telling us" instead.

Quick check

1. Your sister keeps losing her keys. Use want + her + to-infinitive of carry:

"I really want her a spare key at all times."

2. The tour guide answers one question, then moves on to a completely different topic. Use go on + to-infinitive of explain:

"She answered my question about the church, then went on the whole history of the town square."

3. You work in a shop and the jacket's out of stock. Use regret + to-infinitive of tell (the fixed present phrase for bad news):

"I you that we no longer have that jacket in your size."

Now say it

SPEAK

Tell me three things you want, expect, or don't allow someone in your life to do (a flatmate, a colleague, your kids, anyone). Use "want / expect / allow + person + to..." for each one.

What Do You Remember?

From earlier lessons

1. This is an old favourite that still trips people up. After a health scare, he quit for good. Use stop + verb-ing of smoke:

"After the scare at the doctor's, he finally altogether."

2. Quick reminder of why that one matters. Say the difference out loud, then check.

"He stopped the car to check the map" vs "He stopped checking his phone at the table" — what's the difference? CLICK
stop + to-infinitive = you paused one thing IN ORDER TO do something else (he stopped the car so he could check the map). stop + -ing = you ended a habit or activity completely (he no longer checks his phone at the table). Same verb, opposite idea.
