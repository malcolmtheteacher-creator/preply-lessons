The Advert That Became a Promise

A company promised £100 to anyone who caught influenza after using its product. One woman took the promise literally — and the law agreed with her.

Today: you'll work through the case that decided when marketing language becomes a binding promise — and you'll sharpen the structures that make an obligation conditional: provided that, unless, in the event of, subject to, on condition that, so long as.
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • When did you last see an advertisement make a specific promise — a guarantee, a refund, a "or your money back"? Did you believe it was enforceable?
  • Where is the line between confident selling and a commitment you would have to honour? Give the clearest example you can from your own market.
  • Has your organisation ever had to honour something it did not intend to promise? What was the wording that trapped it?

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. The company argued that nobody had given it anything in return for its promise. The court disagreed: she had gone to the trouble of using the device three times a day for weeks, and the company had gained the sales that its promise attracted. That was enough to constitute consideration.
consideration (n) — the price of a promise: something of value given or done in exchange for it. In common-law systems a bare promise is generally unenforceable without it. Fixed phrases: good / valuable consideration, in consideration of…, past consideration is no consideration. Civil-law systems get to the same place by other routes.
2. Counsel for the company insisted that no reasonable reader takes an advertisement literally. "The best in the world", "unbeatable value" — nobody sues over that. It was, he said, mere puff.
mere puff (n; also puffery) — extravagant sales talk that no one is expected to rely on, and which therefore creates no obligation. The test is objective: how would a reasonable reader take it? The moment a statement becomes specific and verifiable, it stops being puff.
3. She could not simply claim the money for having bought the product. First she had to use it three times daily for two weeks in the specified way. Only once that was done did any right to payment arise — the use was a condition precedent.
a condition precedent (n) — something that must happen before an obligation becomes due. Contrast a condition subsequent, which ends an obligation that already exists. In drafting you will meet it as "subject to…", "provided that…", "upon…" — and the fights are almost always about whether it was satisfied.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: to revoke · binding · to construe. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which verb means to withdraw an offer or a power before it has been acted on, so that it can no longer be taken up?
to revoke (v) — noun revocation; adjective revocable / irrevocable. "The offer was revoked before acceptance." Related but distinct: to rescind (unwind a contract), to terminate (end it going forward), to repudiate (refuse to perform).
2. Which adjective means legally enforceable, so that a party can be made to keep to it?
binding (adj) — a binding agreement, binding on both parties, legally binding. Its useful opposite in commercial drafting is non-binding — heads of terms and letters of intent are often expressly stated to be non-binding, except for a few clauses that are not.
3. Which verb means to interpret the meaning of words in a document, as a court does?
to construe (v) — noun construction (not "construing"): "on the proper construction of the clause…". Standard collocations: construed strictly, construed against the party who drafted it (the contra proferentem rule — which is the quiet threat behind every ambiguous promotion).

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Ordinarily two parties exchange promises. Here, only one side promised anything: the company said it would pay, and the reader gave nothing back except doing what was asked. That is a unilateral contract. It is one in which…
  (a) only one party is identified, and the other remains anonymous forever   (b) one party promises, and the other accepts not by promising but by performing the act requested   (c) either party may withdraw at any time without consequence
(b) — a unilateral contract. The classic examples: a reward for a lost dog, a prize for a competition, a cashback promotion. Acceptance is the performance itself, which is why no reply is needed. Contrast a bilateral contract: promise exchanged for promise.
2. Before any agreement binds, the law asks whether the parties meant it to have legal consequences at all — an arrangement to meet for dinner does not. This requirement is the intention to create legal relations. In a commercial advertisement it is…
  (a) presumed to be present, unless the wording clearly shows otherwise   (b) presumed absent, because advertising is not serious   (c) irrelevant, because only consideration matters
(a) — in commercial contexts the intention is presumed, and the burden falls on the party denying it. In social and domestic arrangements the presumption runs the other way. This is why "this is not an offer" and "subject to contract" are written so often: they are attempts to rebut a presumption.

D · Collocation & word building

1. Complete the sequence every contract lawyer recites: offer, ___, consideration, and intention to create legal relations. Then give three verbs that go with the first item — one for making it, one for taking it, one for withdrawing it.
acceptance — the four elements: offer · acceptance · consideration · intention. Verbs with offer: to make an offer, to accept it, to withdraw / revoke it. Also collect: to reject, a counter-offer (which destroys the original), and an invitation to treat — the thing a shop window usually is.
2. The judgment used a memorable phrase for an offer made to nobody in particular and everybody at once: an offer to the whole ___. Complete it, and then say the phrase lawyers use for the moment such an offer is taken up by conduct rather than by words.
an offer to the whole world. It is taken up by acceptance by performance (or by conduct) — no notification required, because the offer itself dispenses with it. Modern descendants: reward posters, loyalty schemes, cashback offers, bug bounties.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — A hundred pounds, and a woman who read carefully

The influenza pandemic that swept Europe from 1889 killed on a scale that made people willing to try almost anything. Into that market came the Carbolic Smoke Ball: a small rubber ball filled with carbolic acid, attached to a tube, which the user squeezed into the nostril so that the vapour made the nose run. The theory — that flushing the nose expelled the disease — was wrong, but the marketing was superb.

In November 1891 the company placed an advertisement in the Pall Mall Gazette. It promised £100 to any person who contracted influenza after using the ball three times daily for two weeks according to the printed directions. And it added a sentence that would cost it the case: £1,000 had been deposited with the Alliance Bank in Regent Street, "showing our sincerity in the matter". A hundred pounds in 1892 was more than a year's wages for many working people.

Louisa Elizabeth Carlill, a woman in her early fifties living in Paddington, bought a ball and used it exactly as instructed, from the middle of November until mid-January. Then she caught influenza. Her husband — a solicitor — wrote to the company. The company did not pay. It suggested, among other things, that she might care to come to its offices and use a ball daily under the supervision of its secretary. She sued.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What exactly did the advertisement promise, and on what conditions?

2. Which sentence in the advertisement did the company most regret, and why?

1. £100 to any person who caught influenza after using the smoke ball three times a day for two weeks in accordance with the printed directions.

2. The statement that £1,000 had been deposited with the Alliance Bank to show its sincerity. That sentence was the company's own evidence that the promise was meant to be taken seriously — which destroyed the argument that it was mere puff.

Discuss:
  • The company added the deposit sentence to be more persuasive, and it was the sentence that bound them. Where in your own organisation's public wording does credibility-building risk turning into commitment?

Part 2 — Five defences, none of them foolish

The company's arguments deserve to be taken seriously, because each of them describes a genuine problem in the law of contract. First, the advertisement was mere puff — vague, promotional, not intended to bind. Second, you cannot make a contract with the whole world; an offer must be made to a person. Third, even if there were an offer, Mrs Carlill had never told them she was accepting it, and acceptance must be communicated. Fourth, she had given no consideration: she had bought the ball from a chemist, not from them. Fifth, the terms were hopelessly uncertain — for how long after using it did the protection last? A fortnight? A year? A lifetime?

The vagueness point was the strongest, and the court did not pretend otherwise. There were, the judges accepted, several possible readings of the period covered. But an ambiguity does not automatically defeat a promise; the court asks how an ordinary person reading the advertisement would reasonably have understood it, and adopts a construction the words can bear. Read that way, the promise covered someone who fell ill while using the ball as directed — which was exactly Mrs Carlill's position.

Behind all five defences lay a commercial reality the company could not say out loud. If the promise bound them, it bound them to every purchaser in the country who fell ill that winter — during an epidemic. The floodgates argument was not theoretical. It was the business model.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Name three of the company's five defences.

2. How did the court deal with the argument that the terms were too vague?

1. Any three of: it was mere puff; you cannot contract with the whole world; acceptance was never communicated; there was no consideration; the terms were too uncertain to enforce.

2. By asking how an ordinary reader would reasonably understand the words, and adopting a meaning the language could bear — here, protection while using the ball as directed. Ambiguity alone does not destroy a promise that a reasonable person would have relied on.

Discuss:
  • "How would an ordinary reader understand this?" is the test your own customer-facing wording will be measured against. Take one sentence your organisation publishes and read it as a hostile ordinary reader would.

Part 3 — An offer to the whole world

The Court of Appeal decided for Mrs Carlill in December 1892, and its answers have been quoted ever since. On puff: the deposit at the bank showed sincerity, and a specific, verifiable promise supported by money set aside is not extravagant sales talk. On contracting with the world: an offer may indeed be made to the whole world, but a contract arises only with the limited number of people who come forward and perform the conditions. On notification: where the offer, by its own nature, invites acceptance by conduct, the offeror is taken to have dispensed with notice — nobody expects to be told in advance that a stranger is searching for your lost dog.

On consideration the reasoning is the most elegant, because it works in two directions at once. Mrs Carlill had suffered the inconvenience of using an unpleasant device three times a day for two weeks at the company's request; and the company had received a real benefit, in the sales that its promise generated. Either was enough. The promise was binding.

What happened next is the part usually left out. The company's founder was not destroyed by the case. He formed a new company and advertised again — this time offering £200, and requiring claimants to register with the company first, to use the ball for a stated period under stated conditions, and to produce specified proof. In other words, having lost on an unconditional promise, he came back with a promise wrapped in conditions precedent. The modern promotional terms and conditions — enter by this date, retain your receipt, one per household, subject to availability — descend directly from a company that learned, expensively, what an unqualified sentence costs.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. How can an offer be made to the whole world without creating a contract with everybody?

2. What did the company change in its next advertisement, and what does that tell you?

1. Because the offer is made generally, but a contract is formed only with those who come forward and perform the stated conditions. The class of contracting parties is defined by performance, not by the size of the audience.

2. It required registration in advance, use for a stated period on stated conditions, and specified proof — conditions precedent that control who can ever claim. The lesson learned was not "promise less" but "condition everything".

Discuss:
  • Is a promise wrapped in six conditions more honest than an unconditional one, or less? Argue it from the customer's side as well as the company's.

Conditional obligation

The company lost because it made a promise with nothing attached to it. Everything it should have written — and everything you will ever draft in a promotion, a warranty, a bonus scheme or a service level — lives in the small set of structures that attach conditions to an obligation.

The core set, from loosest to tightest:
• if — neutral, and perfectly good English: "If the user contracts influenza, the company will pay £100."
• provided that / on condition that — the obligation exists only where the condition is met: "£100 will be paid, provided that the ball has been used three times daily for two weeks."
• so long as / as long as — a continuing condition, not a single event: "…so long as the directions continue to be followed."
• unless — the negative gate: "No payment is due unless the directions have been followed." Note that unless and if not are not always interchangeable in drafting; unless creates a single clean exception.
• in the event of + noun · in the event that + clause: "In the event of illness during the period of use, the sum becomes payable."
• subject to — the great qualifier: "subject to availability", "subject to clause 7", "subject to contract". It subordinates the whole obligation to something else.
• where + clause — the drafter's preference over if for situations expected to recur: "Where a claim is made, the claimant must produce the receipt."
Two refinements worth having.
• Inverted conditionals, formal and useful in correspondence: "Should the claimant fail to register, no sum shall be payable." · "Were the offer to be withdrawn, existing claims would be unaffected." · "Had the directions been followed, the position would be different."
• The verb of obligation. Shall is the traditional drafting form ("the company shall pay") and is still everywhere in contracts; must is the modern plain-language preference and is harder to misread; will states what happens rather than what is required. Whichever you choose, use one consistently — mixed shall and will in the same clause is where arguments start.
The reverse skill: reading conditions from the other side. Every condition you insert is a defence you are building, and a customer will experience it as one. Before you add the fifth condition, say the promise out loud as the customer will hear it: "So — you'll pay, unless."

1. Choose the structure that carries the meaning

A phone retailer is running a cashback promotion. Complete each gap with one conditional structure — one to three words.

1. Payment is due only where the customer has kept the receipt. → "Cashback will be paid the original receipt is retained."

2. No payment at all if the phone goes back. → "No cashback is payable the handset is returned within the cooling-off period."

3. The obligation continues only while the contract is live. → "The discount applies the airtime contract remains in force."

4. If a fault occurs, this is what follows. → " a manufacturing defect, the retailer will replace the handset."

5. The whole promotion depends on stock. → "All offers are availability."

1. provided that / on condition that   2. if / where   3. so long as / as long as   4. In the event of   5. subject to

Sentence 2 is the one to watch: written with unless, it would say the opposite — that cashback is payable unless the handset is returned. Negative gates need reading twice.

2. Condition the promise

A hotel group has published this sentence: "Stay three nights with us and your fourth night is free." Rewrite it as a conditioned obligation. Complete each gap.

"A complimentary fourth night be provided the guest has completed three consecutive paid nights at the same property. a booking is cancelled or amended, the entitlement lapses. No entitlement arises the stay is booked through the group's own website. the guest fail to check in on the first night, the reservation shall be treated as cancelled." (gap 5: inverted conditional)

shall / will · provided that (or on condition that) · Where / If · unless · Should

Now count what the customer lost between the poster and the clause: four conditions, none of them visible in the original sentence. That gap is where regulators and complaints live.

3. The meeting — complete the advice

A marketing director wants to run a campaign promising a full refund to any customer who is not satisfied. Complete your reply so that it warns without obstructing. Read it aloud when you finish.

"I'm not going to tell you not to run it. But as it stands the sentence is an offer to the world, and it will be construed against us, so let's make it deliberate. First, the entitlement should arise only the customer has used the product for the trial period — that gives us a condition precedent rather than an open door. Second, a refund is requested, we need a defined window and proof of purchase, or we cannot administer it. Third, no refund the goods are returned in a resaleable condition. Fourth, the whole campaign should be to stock and to a closing date. And take-up exceed our forecast, I'd rather we had a cap that we published from the start than a change of terms halfway through." (gap 5: inverted conditional)

once / provided that / after · where / if · unless · subject · should

Notice the last sentence: the professional move is not to hide the cap but to publish it. A condition disclosed at the start is a term; the same condition introduced later is a broken promise.

Going deeper

A note before we argue: the case is summarised here, figures are approximate, and legal systems differ — this is material for discussion and for language, not legal advice on any real matter.

What the case actually tests. The easy reading is that a fraudulent company was caught by its own advertisement, and the customer won. But nothing in the judgment turns on the product being useless. The reasoning would apply identically to an honest company with an effective product and one careless sentence — which is why the case is taught to commercial lawyers rather than to consumer campaigners. What it decides is that the law reads your public words the way an ordinary reader would, not the way you privately meant them. Intention is measured objectively, from the outside.

The second lesson is about where certainty actually comes from. The company argued that the promise was too vague to enforce, and it was genuinely vague. The court's answer — take a meaning the words can reasonably bear — puts the risk of ambiguity on the party who chose the words. That is a rule with a strong policy behind it and a real cost: it makes drafting adversarial. A promise that a marketing team would call warm and human, a lawyer will read as an unbounded liability, and both are right.

And a third, which is where the argument gets genuinely difficult. The company's response to losing was to come back with the same promise, larger, wrapped in registration requirements and proof conditions. Everyone reading this has met the descendants: the cashback that requires a claim within a fourteen-day window six months after purchase, the guarantee with conditions that only a careful person will satisfy. Conditions can be honest engineering — without them, promotions are uninsurable — or they can be designed to be failed. The words are identical either way; the difference is in whether the conditions are ones a reasonable customer will actually meet.
Talk it through:
  • Argue the company's side properly: no reasonable person treats an advertisement as a contract, and holding otherwise makes ordinary commercial enthusiasm dangerous. What is the strongest version — and where does it fail?
  • Ambiguity is construed against the party who wrote the words. Is that fair, or does it simply advantage whoever can afford the better drafting?
  • Take a promotion or guarantee your own sector runs. Which of its conditions are genuine risk control, and which exist to reduce claims from people who are technically entitled?
  • The company published a bank deposit to prove its sincerity, and the proof became the trap. What does your organisation publish to be believed — and would you be comfortable being held to it word for word?
