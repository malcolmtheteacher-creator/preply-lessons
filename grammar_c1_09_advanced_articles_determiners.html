Listen in on this conversation

A: How's your sister doing? I heard she was in hospital.

B: She's out now, thank goodness. Actually, the hospital she went to has just opened a brand new children's wing — very impressive.

Look closely. "Hospital" appears twice, but only the second time does it get "the". Why, do you think? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
The first time, "hospital" describes the experience of being a patient — no particular building is in the speaker's mind. The second time, "the hospital" points to one specific, identifiable building — the one the sister actually went to. Same word, two completely different jobs. That's the kind of fine control we're building today.
Today: sharpen your control of "the" and no article — for talking about people, places and society — plus small words like "such", "quite", "rather" and "what a" that add reaction to what you say.

People & Things in General

Speaking about a whole category versus one particular thing you and your listener both have in mind.

Spot the pattern

Three general statements:

  • "Money can't buy happiness, but it certainly helps."
  • "Museums are usually free for under-18s in this city."
  • "Patience is a skill you can actually train."

What do "money", "museums" and "patience" have in common — and what's missing from in front of each one? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
No article at all. Each one is spoken about as a whole category or idea — money in general, museums in general, patience in general — not one particular example of it. Plural nouns and abstract uncountable nouns both take zero article when you're generalising.
(no article) + plural noun / abstract noun → a whole category or idea in general

Now compare

"Silence makes some people uncomfortable."
→
"The silence in the waiting room made me uncomfortable."

Left: silence in general, an idea. Right: one particular silence, made specific by "in the waiting room" — so it needs "the". "The" also appears once something has already been mentioned, or once the situation makes it obvious which one you mean.

the + noun → one particular, identifiable thing (already mentioned, pinned down by a following phrase, or obvious from the situation)

Purpose or Place?

Fixed everyday phrases with no article at all, versus "the" for an actual, identifiable building or vehicle.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "She's at school until three, then she's got swimming practice."
  • "We went by train because the roads were closed for the marathon."
  • "He's been in hospital since the accident, but he's recovering well."

What do "school", "train" and "hospital" have in common here — and why do you think none of them has an article? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Each word names an institution or a way of travelling by its purpose — being a student, getting somewhere, being a patient — not a particular building or vehicle. English has a set of fixed phrases like this: at school / at work / at university / at church, in hospital / in prison / in bed, by car / by bus / by train / by bike / by plane. No article at all.

(American English often says "in the hospital" even for a patient — the pattern below is the one used in British and international English, and the one most exams expect.)
at school / at work / in hospital / by car / by train ... → purpose or activity, no article

Now compare

"She's at school right now."
→
"The school she attends has just built a new library."

Left: attending school, a purpose — no article. Right: one particular building, identified by "she attends" — so it needs "the".

the + noun → the actual, physical, identifiable building or vehicle

Groups, Extremes & Reactions

"The" for whole groups of people and for extremes — plus small words that add reaction.

Spot the pattern

Three statements about society:

  • "The elderly are often the last to receive support after a disaster."
  • "Charities argue that the unemployed need more than just financial help."
  • "This policy was designed to protect the homeless during winter."

Do "elderly", "unemployed" and "homeless" take an -s anywhere? What do they mean here? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
No -s, ever — these are adjectives, not nouns. The + adjective (elderly, unemployed, homeless, disabled, rich, poor, young...) means "people who are [adjective]" — a whole group, always plural in meaning, always with a plural verb.
the + adjective (no noun, no -s) = people who are [adjective]

Now compare

"People who are elderly often feel invisible in cities built for the young."
→
"The elderly often feel invisible in cities built for the young."
the + superlative / the + first, second... / the + only → almost always "the"
but: "a second chance" (= another one) vs "the second chance" (= specifically number two)

Reacting and emphasising

Three reactions:

  • "It was such a relief when the results finally came through."
  • "That's quite an achievement, considering you'd never played before."
  • "What a difference a good night's sleep makes!"

Where does "a/an" sit — before "such/quite/what", or after? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
After. such / quite / rather / what + a/an + (adjective) + noun. With a plural or uncountable noun, "a/an" simply disappears: "such good news", "quite remarkable results", "what beautiful flowers".
such a(n) / quite a(n) / rather a(n) / what a(n) + (adjective) + noun

4. Fix the word order:

"It was a such long meeting that half the room fell asleep."

2. Say it out loud: before today, when do you use "a/an", and when do you switch to "the", the very first time you're describing something?

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
One possible answer: use "a/an" the first time you mention a singular countable thing your listener doesn't know about yet ("I saw a dog"), then switch to "the" every time after, once you both know which one you mean ("the dog was..."). Today's lesson builds finer cases on top of exactly this basic switch — generalisations, fixed phrases, groups, and reactions.
