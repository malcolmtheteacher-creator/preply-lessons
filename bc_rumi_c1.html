MINDS WORTH MEETING · C1 · 50 min

Rumi

The Persian mystic who turned the ache of separation into the highest form of knowledge — and made longing itself the road home.

You talk first — before you read anything

This is a talking lesson, not a reading one. So before a single fact, the floor is yours.

Start here · 2 minutes

Just his name and this line: “he turned the ache of separation into the highest form of knowledge”. How did you first come across Rumi — a poem, a quote online, music, somewhere else? What image of him do you carry, and where did it come from? Just talk.

Now meet him — one beat at a time

Three short beats. After each one, you do the talking.

Rumi was born in 1207 on the eastern edge of the Persian-speaking world — most likely near Balkh, in what is now Afghanistan. His family were scholars and preachers, and they were on the move: as the Mongol armies pushed west, they migrated for years before settling in Konya, in Anatolia — then part of the Sultanate of Rum, which is where the name “Rumi” comes from.

Over to you:
  • He was, in a sense, always in exile — a refugee child fleeing the Mongols. Does that change how you might read a poet obsessed with longing and return?

For the first half of his life he was a respected, rather conventional figure: a learned Islamic scholar, a jurist, a teacher with students and a reputation. Nothing in that life predicted the poet he would become. Then, around 1244, he met a wandering dervish named Shams of Tabriz — and everything broke open.

Over to you:
  • A settled, successful man at forty, undone by one friendship. Have you ever seen a single relationship completely change the direction of a person's life?

The friendship was extraordinary, intense, and to outsiders almost scandalous. When Shams disappeared — and probably died, perhaps violently — the loss did not silence Rumi. It transformed him. Out of that grief poured thousands of lyric verses and later the vast spiritual epic the “Masnavi”, sometimes called “the Persian Qur'an”. The whirling dervishes trace their order back to him.

Over to you:
  • His greatest work came straight out of his worst loss. Do you buy the idea that grief can be a doorway to something — or is that just a comforting story we tell the bereaved?

The reed's cry — and you do the thinking out loud

Rumi's most famous image holds the whole of his thought in miniature. I will hand it to you in small pieces. After each piece, you talk — I will mostly listen.

Picture a reed flute — a “ney”. To make it, someone cuts a length of reed away from the bed where it grew, bores holes in it, and pushes breath through its hollow centre. The sound it makes, Rumi says, is not happy. It is a cry — the reed wailing about being torn from the bed it came from. And everyone who hears that music is, without quite knowing it, hearing their own homesickness in it.

Put yourself there

Before I tell you what he means by it: what do you hear in that image of a cut reed crying? What might the “reed-bed” stand for — and have you ever felt that nameless homesickness even when nothing was wrong?

Here is what he means. We are all the cut reed. We were once joined to the source — call it God, the divine, the whole — and we have been set down in a world of bodies and time and distance. The ache, the restlessness, the sense that something is missing even in our happiest moments: that, for Rumi, is not a flaw. It is the memory of where we came from, and the pull to return.

Over to you:
  • Most of us treat that restlessness as a problem — something to numb, fill, or swipe away. Rumi treats it as the surest sign of where you belong. Who do you think is right?
  • Do you recognise that feeling at all in yourself, or do you think it has more ordinary explanations?

And the destination is strange to modern ears. The goal is not to get what you want. It is the dissolving of the separate, grasping self — the ego — into something larger, the way a drop returns to the ocean and stops being a drop. This is “fana” in the Sufi tradition: the self does not win, it disappears, and in disappearing it finds everything. Love is the force that does the dissolving — which is why the grief of losing Shams was not, for him, simply a tragedy. It was the doorway.

The wound, in his way of thinking, is exactly the place where the light is able to get in — the break is not the opposite of wholeness but the road to it.

Take a side · 3–4 minutes

“The dissolving of the self is freedom.” Argue for it or against it — and bring real examples: a moment the “I” got quieter and lighter, or a world (your work, social media) that screams the opposite. Build your case, then I will push back and you defend it.

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that the longing he describes has a purely psychological root.
  • It might well be that we cannot separate the poetry from the faith.
  • I suspect the truth is somewhere in between.
  • You could argue that modern readers have remade him in their own image.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say the longing matters more than the destination.
  • What strikes me most is how he refuses to treat grief as a problem.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, his message is more needed now than in the 13th century.

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure I fully buy that.
  • I take the point, but doesn't that assume…?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…

A · The Reed Test (3–4 min)

Rumi says the restlessness we feel even in good times is homesickness for a source we have forgotten. Take that idea seriously for a moment and apply it to your own life: think of a time you felt something was missing even when, on paper, everything was fine.

Argue one side: is that ache really pointing at something — a "home" you are pulled toward — or is it just a trick of the mind that wisdom should teach us to quiet? Build a real case — give your example, raise the strongest objection to your own view, then answer it.

B · The Dissolving Self (3–4 min)

Rumi's goal is not to get what you want but to lose the grasping "I" entirely — the drop returning to the ocean. Modern life pushes the opposite: build your brand, assert yourself, stand out.

Pick a part of your own world — your work, social media, a relationship. Where, if anywhere, have you actually felt the self get quieter and lighter — and was that loss or freedom? Make the case with one concrete moment from your real life.

Your take — the conversation that matters most

Open discussion

  1. After today, has anything shifted in how you see Rumi — or has it confirmed why you were drawn to him?
  2. His great theme is that longing is the road home. Is that an idea you find comforting, or one you resist?
  3. Rumi is hugely popular in the West today, but often with the Islam quietly removed. Is it fair to take the love and leave the faith behind, or does that distort him?
  4. He turned the worst loss of his life into his greatest work. Do you believe suffering can genuinely make us deeper — or is that a story we tell to survive it?
  5. If you could ask Rumi one question, what would it be? And what do you think he would say back?
  6. Who is the next writer or thinker you would put in front of me — and what is the one idea of theirs I need to understand?
