The Ulysses Contract

C1/C2 Level · Why senior leaders bind their future selves — and their organisations

The discipline of the past self

Homer's Ulysses had himself bound to the mast of his ship so that he could safely hear the sirens' song without leaping to his death. Two and a half thousand years later, well-run boards introduce dividend policies, conflict-of-interest rules, and lock-up periods; thoughtful executives block their own calendars, ring-fence decision time, and quietly take certain options off the table that their tired Friday-afternoon self should not be asked to evaluate from scratch. The mechanism is identical. The phenomenon has a name: a Ulysses contract. The serious question is no longer whether to operate them, but which ones, against which forces, and how well-designed they actually are.

Speak · press on the position

The working claim of this lesson is straightforward: in modern conditions, binding the future self — personally and organisationally — is what mature leadership looks like. Defend, complicate, refine. Two or three full sentences per answer.

  1. Where, in your own working week, are you already operating Ulysses contracts — written or unwritten? What are you binding yourself against?
  2. Which of your organisation's hard rules strike you, on reflection, as well-designed Ulysses contracts — and which are outdated relics whose original logic no longer applies?
  3. Senior leadership often consists in deciding, in advance, what your future self will and will not be allowed to negotiate. Argue that this is not a constraint but a precondition for serious work.
  4. The most expensive failures of judgement tend to come from a tired, optimistic, late-in-the-day version of the self. What does that imply for how the working day, and the working week, should actually be designed?
Speak · sharpen the claim

Pick the one you most want to defend, and defend it for two minutes. Then pick the one you find weakest, and shore it up — even if you'd rather discard it.

  • (a) "Any leader who needs to rely on willpower at seven in the evening has, by that hour, already lost the day."
  • (b) "Every well-run organisation is, in the end, an architecture of Ulysses contracts wearing the costume of policy."
  • (c) "The senior executive who claims to make every decision 'on its merits, in the moment' is either misinformed or about to make a serious mistake."
  • (d) "A board exists, in essence, to be the older, wiser self of the company — saying no, in advance, to things the future executive team will be tempted to do."

Eight chunks to put to work

Read each definition; close it; move on. The point is not to learn them on the card — it is to use them, out loud, in the speaking drill below and in the tasks throughout.

a Ulysses contract A pre-commitment device by which the present self constrains a future, more vulnerable self — named after the Homeric image.
to bind one's future self To restrict, in advance, what the later version of you will be able to do. The verb phrase at the heart of the topic.
decision fatigue The deteriorating quality of decisions made later in the day, after a long sequence of smaller ones. A structural feature of senior roles, not a personal failing.
calendar discipline The deliberate, hard-won practice of treating one's diary as a Ulysses contract — a constraint, not a wish list.
to ring-fence (time / a decision / a budget) To protect something from being raided later by competing demands. A core leadership manoeuvre, and an excellent verb to deploy in meetings.
a hard line A position declared, in advance, to be non-negotiable. Its strength comes from the fact that it was decided before the moment of pressure.
a binding policy An organisation-level rule that cannot be easily overridden — the same instinct as a personal Ulysses contract, scaled up.
to lash oneself to the mast The central metaphor — usable as a vivid idiom for any deliberate, irreversible pre-commitment, in either personal or organisational life.

One paragraph. Then we talk.

Read this once, slowly, aloud. It is here to sharpen a position we already broadly hold — not to reopen it. The conversation that follows is the point.

The Norwegian philosopher Jon Elster, writing in 1979, treated the Ulysses contract as a curious philosophical puzzle: the present self binding the future self, fascinating and faintly suspect. The picture has since changed beyond recognition. Senior decision-makers today operate inside conditions Elster could only partially imagine — calendars optimised by other people, attention harvested in real time, decisions of consequence routinely taken at the end of long, fragmented days. In such conditions, decision fatigue is not a personal failing but a structural feature of the role; raw willpower is a luxury available in unpredictable, exhausting flashes. The mature response is no longer to summon character on demand. It is to operate, deliberately, a portfolio of well-designed contracts — personal calendar discipline, ring-fenced decision time, hard lines declared in advance — and to recognise that good governance, at the level of the organisation, is essentially the same instinct scaled up. Every binding policy, every conflict-of-interest rule, every escalation protocol is a thoughtful past self lashed to the mast on behalf of a future leadership the conditions of work would otherwise gradually consume.

Phrasing · read aloud, pausing at each dash and comma. The sentences are deliberately long and need to breathe at their joints.

Two quick orientations before we open it out:

1. What changed between 1979 and today, and what does that change do to the moral status of the Ulysses contract for senior decision-makers?

Show Answer
The environment in which the future self operates has been comprehensively redesigned: calendars are optimised by other people, attention is harvested professionally, and consequential decisions routinely arrive at the worst hour of the day. That shift moves pre-commitment from "interesting paradox" to "basic operating discipline" — a clear-eyed response to conditions, not a small drama of mistrust between two versions of the same person.

2. Why does the writer call good governance "essentially the same instinct scaled up"?

Show Answer
Because a well-designed binding policy — a conflict rule, an escalation protocol, a lock-up period — performs at the organisational level exactly the function a personal Ulysses contract performs at the individual level: a thoughtful past self lashing the future to the mast, so that the team operating under pressure does not have to discover discipline from scratch in the moment.
Speak · sharpen, don't relitigate

The position is settled: in modern conditions, binding the future self — personally and organisationally — is mature, not weak. The conversation now is about how, where, and against what.

  1. Pick one binding policy currently operating in your organisation. Was it well-designed when it was written? Is it still well-designed now? What, specifically, would you change?
  2. Where in your own working week are you flying without a Ulysses contract you should have signed?
  3. What is the difference between a leader who treats their calendar as a Ulysses contract and one who treats it as a wish list? Which kind of organisation do you think each produces?
  4. The writer says governance is the same instinct scaled up. If that's right, when should a binding policy be loosened — and when should the very desire to loosen it be read as a warning?

Formal hypothetical structures & the mandative subjunctive

Senior conversations in English live in a higher register: careful, hedged, structurally formal. Three clusters do most of the work. Read the cards, close them, then go straight into the oral drill.

Inverted conditionals

Drop the if; invert the auxiliary.

Were we to reopen this question every quarter, no decision would ever stand.

Had the policy not been in place, the loss would have been substantially larger.

Should the board revisit the matter, the original reasoning still holds.

Mandative subjunctive

After suggest / insist / propose / recommend / essential / vital / crucial that: bare infinitive, no -s.

It is essential that the rule not be reopened at the request of any individual director.

I would propose that the committee consider the matter as a whole.

Formal concessives & lest

The carriers of a careful, senior register.

Be that as it may, the policy stands.

The framework was set in advance, lest any single quarter's pressure unsettle it.

However compelling the case for relaxing the rule, …

Speak · oral transformation drill

Your teacher reads a flat sentence in everyday English. You reformulate it on the spot, aloud, using the structure indicated. Each item has a model hidden below.

1. Flat: "If we hadn't put that policy in place, we would have spent half the year arguing about it case by case."   →   use inverted Had…

Show Answer
Had we not put that policy in place, we would have spent half the year arguing about it case by case.

2. Flat: "If a regulator ever questioned the rule, we'd have a clear, defensible position."   →   use inverted Were…

Show Answer
Were a regulator ever to question the rule, we would have a clear, defensible position.

3. Flat: "If you ever find yourself reconsidering the framework, do not weaken."   →   use inverted Should…

Show Answer
Should you ever find yourself reconsidering the framework, do not weaken.

4. Flat: "It's really important that the board doesn't reopen this between now and the end of the quarter."   →   use mandative subjunctive after It is essential that…

Show Answer
It is essential that the board not reopen this between now and the end of the quarter. (Bare subjunctive: not reopen, not doesn't reopen.)

5. Flat: "He set the framework in advance because he didn't want any single quarter's pressure to destabilise it."   →   use lest…

Show Answer
He set the framework in advance, lest any single quarter's pressure destabilise it. (Bare subjunctive: destabilise, not destabilises.)

6. Flat: "The rule may look rigid. Still, the original reasoning was sound."   →   use Be that as it may…

Show Answer
The rule may look rigid. Be that as it may, the original reasoning was sound.
Speak · live micro-task

Pick a Ulysses contract currently operating in your own working life or organisation that someone might reasonably want to relax. In sixty seconds, defend it — using at least two of the structures above (inversion, mandative subjunctive, lest, be that as it may). Speak; don't write.

Three speaking tasks — do all three

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · personal monologue (4 minutes)

Tell the story of a specific Ulysses contract you currently operate in your own working life. This is not a confessional; it is a closing argument.

  1. What were you binding yourself against, and what about the modern executive day made willpower-in-the-moment a non-starter?
  2. What mechanism did you use — calendar architecture, hard line, ring-fenced time, formal policy — and why was that one well-designed for the threat?
  3. What did the contract make possible that would otherwise have been impossible?
  4. If a peer told you they were going to handle the same problem on willpower alone, what would you say?
  5. What's the next contract you ought to sign — and what's quietly stopping you?

Weave in at least three vocabulary chunks from Tab 2 and at least two formal structures from Tab 4.

Task B · demolish the cliche (3 minutes)

A reasonable-sounding peer says to you over dinner:

"Honestly, all this rules-based discipline is overdone. If you genuinely know what you are doing, you should be able to take each case on its merits, in the moment. Locking yourself out of your own choices is what people do when they don't trust themselves — and at our level, that's not the posture I'd choose."

Demolish this, in full sentences, for three minutes. Be concrete about modern conditions of senior work. Use at least one inversion and one mandative subjunctive. Do not concede that "trusting yourself" is the more sophisticated position; argue that the cliche has it backwards.

Task C · board-room role-play (4 minutes)

Your teacher plays a fellow board member who has decided that a long-standing binding policy at your organisation has, in their view, outlived its usefulness and should now be relaxed. They argue it confidently and well.

You make the case for keeping the policy as it stands — even though, privately, you may have some sympathy with the position. Speak as you would in the room: measured, structurally formal, conceding what must be conceded but holding the line on what matters.

Then swap. You argue for relaxing the policy; your teacher defends it. Notice, in your own mouth, where the argument gets harder and easier.

Final open discussion

  1. Modern conditions reward the leader who has thought hardest in advance. What does that imply for how senior people should spend their non-meeting hours — and what is currently consuming yours instead?
  2. There is a category of binding policy that quietly outlives the reason it was created and quietly costs the organisation more each year than anyone admits. How do you spot one? How do you retire one without setting off the wrong signals?
  3. The personal Ulysses contract is invisible to your colleagues; the organisational one is highly visible. What does that asymmetry do to leadership credibility, and how should a senior person manage it?
  4. If you imagine the version of yourself sitting in the same chair five years from now, what is the single Ulysses contract you would most want present-you to put in place this quarter — and what would five-years-from-now-you most thank you for?
