Moving In

She's found a two-bed off Fern Street and she wants an answer by Friday. You want to say yes. You also want to say four unromantic things first, out loud, while they're still cheap to say.

The Scene 🎬

It's a Tuesday, it's late, and Marta's laptop is open on the arm of the sofa with the same listing on it she's had open since about six. Two bedrooms, off Fern Street, a kitchen that gets the morning light. She's walked past it twice. She's worked out the rent on the back of an envelope, and she's decided the second bedroom counts as a bedroom.

And she wants an answer this week, because the agent wants an answer this week. Not a long think. Not "let's see how the next month goes". Friday.

Here's the thing: you want to. You've wanted to for a while. But there are four or five small, deeply unromantic questions sitting in your chest — the money, the paperwork, whether you'll ever again have an hour to yourself, what on earth happens to your own flat — and you know exactly how this goes if you don't ask them now. They don't disappear. They wait, and they come out in eighteen months' time as an argument about a dishwasher.

So tonight you say yes. You just say a few other things first.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of a big decision you've made with somebody else — a home, a job, a trip, a pet. Did you two ever properly sit down and talk it through, or did it just sort of happen to you? Which was it, honestly?
  2. Some people think raising money and paperwork early kills the romance of a thing. Others think it's the most loving thing you can do. Where do you actually stand — and where do the people you live with stand?

Who's who

You
The One With The List 📋

You're going to say yes. You know that. But you'd like the yes to be a real one, which means saying the awkward bits out loud tonight rather than storing them up for later.

The other voice
Marta 🔑

She's found the flat, she's done the sums, and she wants an answer this week. She's not being pushy — she's just further down the road than you are, and every question you ask sounds, at first, a bit like a no.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's not a smooth conversation, and it isn't meant to be. Listen for how the doubts get raised without ever sounding like a refusal.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Marta pushes back properly only once, and she calls one of the requests a sign of hesitation. Which request was it, and how did the speaker turn her objection around?

2. Two of the worries got sorted almost instantly. One of them clearly landed badly at first. Which one, and how could you tell from Marta's reply?

3. Right at the end, the speaker says the things they're nervous about are all small — and then says that's the worrying part. Why is that not a contradiction?

1. The six-month trial run, which she heard as someone keeping one foot out of the door. The speaker didn't argue that she was wrong — they said "maybe it does", and then reframed it: six months isn't a way out, it's a way of finding out early instead of dragging something unhappy on for three years. She finished that sentence herself, which is how you know she'd got there too.
2. The question about keeping the flat. Marta started to say it was a bit something — insulting, presumably — and stopped herself, then asked why. The room and the money went through without a fight; that one she had to swallow first.
3. Because big problems get talked about and small ones don't. Nobody sits down over the bins, so the small stuff just accumulates quietly until it isn't small any more. That's the whole reason the awkward questions came out on the sofa on a Tuesday rather than in eighteen months' time.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for saying yes with your eyes open — raising the unromantic things without turning the evening into a negotiation. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. Every one of these phrases is a small risk, and your voice decides how it lands. Said fast and flat, "can we talk about money" sounds like an accusation; said slowly, a bit apologetically, with a shrug in it, it sounds like someone who's on your side and would just rather get this bit over with. Keep the tune light even when the question isn't — and don't rush into the next sentence to fill the silence, because the silence is them thinking.

🎯 Making it a real conversation

Phrase 1

You've both been drifting around this subject for weeks without ever landing on it, and now there's a deadline. You want to ask, kindly, whether this is an actual decision or just more drifting. Which opener does that without sounding like an accusation?

A. "Are you serious about this or not?"
B. "So — are we talking about this properly, or just talking about it?"
C. "I don't think either of us really means it."
B — "So — are we talking about this properly, or just talking about it?"
A puts the other person on trial, and C answers the question before they've had a chance to. B works because "we" spreads the drifting across both of you, and because the two halves are almost the same words — talking about it properly, or just talking about it — which is a light way of naming something rather heavy, that a conversation can go on for weeks and still not be a decision.
Phrase 2

Your first question has landed like a no, and you can see their face. Before you raise anything else, you need to say the yes plainly — and then flag that what follows is about money and paperwork, not about feelings. Put the pieces in order:

I do.practical worries.I want to,I've just gota couple of
"I want to, I do. I've just got a couple of practical worries."
The repeated "I do" is doing the heavy lifting: in speech we echo ourselves like that when we're worried we weren't believed the first time, so it sounds like reassurance rather than argument. Then "practical" quietly tells them which department the worries are in, and "a couple of" makes it sound like a short list, which stops them bracing for an hour of it.

💷 The unromantic bit

Phrase 3

You have to raise money — the least romantic subject available — and somebody's going to look like the killjoy. Which line puts that on yourself instead of on them?

A. "We ought to discuss the financial side of things."
B. "Before anything else, I want to go through your numbers."
C. "Can I be the boring one and talk about money?"
C — "Can I be the boring one and talk about money?"
A is stiff enough to sound like a meeting, and B makes them the subject of an inspection. C works because you take the unflattering role for yourself — you're volunteering to be the boring one, which is a small joke at your own expense — and because asking permission gives them a second to catch up rather than being ambushed by the topic.
Phrase 4

You're not asking who hands over the rent each month. You're asking who the landlord thinks the flat belongs to — the paperwork question nobody has looked at. Put the pieces in order:

bewouldon it?Whose nameactually
"Whose name would actually be on it?"
"On it" means on the tenancy agreement — the contract with the landlord — and the vagueness is deliberate, because naming the document makes it sound like a solicitor's question. The "actually" is where the work happens: it's the word English uses to get underneath a nice assumption to the real arrangement, and it's the reason the question sounds curious rather than suspicious.

🚪 Asking for what you need

Phrase 5

You need a room of your own, and you know how that sounds on the night someone asks you to live with them. So you describe it as a thing you'd do, not a thing you'd own. Complete the chunk: "I'd need somewhere I can shut ___ ___, if I'm honest."

"I'd need somewhere I can shut the door, if I'm honest."
Asking for "my own room" sounds like a claim on territory, and territory is exactly what people get frightened about when they move in together. Shutting the door is smaller and much more human — everyone knows the feeling of wanting an hour where nobody needs anything — so the same request arrives as a mood rather than a demand. "If I'm honest" signals that this one cost you something to say.
Phrase 6

Now the awkward one. You own a flat, or you rent one, and you're not sure whether hanging onto it for a few months is prudent or a bit of an insult. Ask about it out loud, in your own words — and find a way to admit, in the same breath, that you might be being ridiculous. What would you say?

e.g. "What happens to my flat — do I keep it for a bit, or is that mad?"
Look at what the last three words do. Adding "or is that mad?" hands them the chance to say yes, that's mad, which means you've raised the idea without planting a flag on it — and it admits you already know how it might sound. "For a bit" does the same job on the other side, keeping it temporary and small. Ask this one without that little escape hatch and it becomes a statement about your intentions.

🕰️ Building in a way out

Phrase 7

You want to say why you're asking any of this: not because you expect it to fail, but because you'd rather know early than discover it slowly and painfully. Complete the chunk with a three-word idiom meaning "by suffering it for ourselves": "I don't want us to find this out ___ ___ ___."

"I don't want us to find this out the hard way."
To learn something "the hard way" is to learn it through the damage instead of through the warning — the person who finds out the hard way always knew, they just didn't ask. The line works because "us" makes the worry shared and the whole thing protective: you're not defending yourself against them, you're both trying to avoid the same bad year.
Phrase 8

Here's the actual proposal — a fixed period, agreed openly, after which you both talk again. It has to sound like a suggestion you'd cheerfully drop, not a term of business. Put the pieces in order:

a trial runbe alright withWould you— six months?
"Would you be alright with a trial run — six months?"
"Would you be alright with" is softer than "can we" or "I want", because it asks about their comfort rather than announcing your requirement, and it leaves them somewhere easy to say no. A "trial run" is the phrase English uses for trying something once to see whether it works, without committing — and then the six months arrives afterwards, almost as an afterthought, which is far lighter than leading with the number.

💛 The honest wobble — and the yes

Phrase 9

You've listed your worries and, said out loud, none of them is serious. That should be reassuring. It isn't, quite — and you want to say why in a way that turns back on itself. Which line does that?

A. "None of my worries are that serious, so honestly we'll be fine."
B. "The stuff I'm nervous about is all small stuff. That's what worries me."
C. "If I'm honest, I've got some fairly big concerns about all of this."
B — "The stuff I'm nervous about is all small stuff. That's what worries me."
A and C are both straightforward reports — one cheerful, one gloomy — and neither says anything you didn't already know. B is the only one that catches itself: it notices that small worries are the dangerous kind precisely because nobody thinks they're worth a conversation, so they never get one. Saying it out loud is the whole trick, since it's the only thing that stops the small stuff staying quiet.
Phrase 10

Everything's been said and you're going to say yes. It should sound like a person deciding on the spot, warm and slightly reckless — not a sigh of surrender, and not a formal declaration. Which one?

A. "Alright, I suppose we can give it a try."
B. "Yes. I've thought it through carefully and I'm fully committed."
C. "Go on then. Let's do it."
C — "Go on then. Let's do it."
A is a yes that's already half sorry about itself, and B is so careful it sounds like a contract being signed rather than a life being chosen. "Go on then" is what British English says when someone has talked you round and you're glad they did — it's the tone of accepting a second drink — so it carries warmth and a bit of a leap, which after twenty minutes of paperwork is exactly the note the evening needs to end on.

🔑 Make them yours

These aren't only for flats. They're for any moment where you want to say yes to something and still say the awkward part out loud. Pin three of them to your own life, so they're there when you need them. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I want to, I do. I've just got a couple of practical worries." — something you're being asked to commit to right now, where the answer is yes with conditions. What is it, and what are the worries?

"Can I be the boring one and talk about money?" — where have you gone quiet about money because raising it felt rude?

"I don't want us to find this out the hard way." — something you can see coming that nobody has said out loud yet.

The Twist

😬 The list goes both ways

One more round, no script. You get to the six months, you're rather pleased with how reasonable you've been — and Marta says: "Fine. Then I've got one. My mum's had a fall. If it gets worse she comes and lives with us, in the small room, for as long as she needs. That's my condition. Yours for mine." No trial run on that one. No six months. The room you just asked for is the room she needs, and she wants an answer tonight, same as the flat. Start the timer. You can't have everything on your list and refuse the only thing on hers — so decide, out loud, what you're actually willing to give up, and say it without turning it into a bargain. One rule: "we'll cross that bridge when we come to it" doesn't count. She's asking now.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

There's an old idea that love shouldn't need terms — that if you have to discuss the lease and the money and who gets the small room, something has already gone wrong. It's a lovely idea, and the people who believe it tend to end up having the same conversation anyway, only later, and louder, and about a dishwasher.

The truth is duller than that. Two people can adore each other and still want completely different things from a Sunday. Nobody leaves over the big stuff — the big stuff gets talked about, because it's obviously worth talking about. It's the small, unsayable stuff that quietly does the damage, precisely because it never seemed worth a conversation.

Which is why the list isn't the opposite of the yes. It's what makes the yes worth anything.

Two bigger questions

  1. Is there such a thing as a condition that shouldn't be allowed — something you could ask for while moving in with someone that would, on its own, tell you the answer should be no? Where's that line for you, and would the person you live with draw it in the same place?
  2. A trial run makes the exit easier, and some people say that's exactly what's wrong with it — that things last partly because leaving is hard. Do you think that's true, and if it is, is that a good enough reason to make anything harder to leave?
