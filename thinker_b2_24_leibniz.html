If you could remove all the bad things from the world, would the world really be better?

Imagine an app that could delete every bad day from your life: every failure, every argument, every rainy holiday. It sounds wonderful. But would you still learn, grow, or enjoy the good days as much? A German genius once argued that, even with all its problems, our world is the best one possible.

Today: You'll meet Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz and his idea of the best of all possible worlds, so you can weigh good and bad and explain why he thought a world with some evil could still be the best.

Meet Leibniz

Read one part at a time. After each part, close your eyes and answer the question before you check.

I

A boy in his father's library

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz was born in Leipzig, Germany, in 1646. His father, a professor of philosophy, died when Gottfried was six. According to Leibniz himself, he taught himself Latin as a boy so that he could read the books in his father's library. He finished a doctorate in law while still very young. Instead of teaching at a university, he worked for princes as a lawyer, diplomat, librarian and adviser.

According to his own story, how did the young Leibniz educate himself?

He taught himself Latin so that he could read the books in his late father's library.
II

A mind for everything

In 1672 Leibniz went to Paris as a diplomat and met the great scientists of Europe. Around 1675 he developed calculus, the mathematics of change. Isaac Newton had found it too, and the two men argued bitterly about who was first. Today both get the credit, but we still use Leibniz's symbols. He also built a calculating machine and studied binary numbers, made only of 0 and 1, which computers use today.

What did Leibniz and Newton argue about?

About who invented calculus first. Today both get the credit, but we still use Leibniz's symbols.
III

Why is there evil?

Leibniz asked a hard question: if God is good and all-powerful, why is there so much suffering? In Theodicy (1710) he gave his answer. God looked at every possible world and chose the best one. A world with free people, who can choose bad as well as good, is better than a world of puppets. So this is 'the best of all possible worlds'. Leibniz died in Hanover in 1716, almost forgotten. Later, Voltaire mocked his idea in the comic novel Candide.

Why did Leibniz think a world with evil could still be the best?

Because God chose the best possible world, and a world with free people, who can also choose bad things, is better than a world of puppets.
Talk: Voltaire laughed at Leibniz's idea instead of arguing with it. Is humour a fair way to attack an idea? Can it be more powerful than an argument?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. The war caused terrible suffering: thousands of families lost their homes and loved ones.
suffering (n) — physical or mental pain. Example: "The new medicine has reduced the pain of many patients and ended years of suffering."
2. The job is stressful, but the good salary outweighs the long hours, so she's staying.
outweigh (v) — to be greater or more important than something else. Example: "For me, the benefits of living in the city outweigh the noise."
3. Living in the countryside is a trade-off: you get fresh air, but you lose easy access to shops.
trade-off (n) — a situation where you accept something bad in order to get something good. Example: "There's always a trade-off between speed and quality."
4. Even after losing three games, the team stayed optimistic and believed they could still win.
optimistic (adj) — expecting good things to happen in the future. Example: "Doctors are optimistic that she'll make a full recovery."
5. The diamond looked perfect, but under the microscope you could see a tiny flaw inside it.
flaw (n) — a mistake or weakness that makes something less than perfect. Example: "The plan had one serious flaw: it was far too expensive."
6. The comedian mocked the politician's speech by copying his voice and his silly hand movements.
mock (v) — to laugh at someone or something in an unkind way. Example: "Some children mock classmates who are different."
7. If you drive too fast on ice, an accident is almost inevitable.
inevitable (adj) — certain to happen and impossible to avoid. Example: "With so much rain, flooding was inevitable."

See it in action

Here is the idea in action. A football coach could stop her players from ever making a mistake by controlling every move they make, like a video game. There would be no bad passes, but also no creativity, no learning and no real victories. Leibniz would say the coach is right to let them play freely. Some mistakes are the price of something better. In the same way, he thought, some evil is the price of a world with real freedom.

Thought experiment: The perfect world machine

A machine can remove all pain, failure and sadness from the world. Nobody would ever be hurt again. The only cost: nobody could ever choose to do anything wrong either, because every choice would be made for them, always correctly.

Now give two reasons for your choice. Then argue for the other side for thirty seconds. Which side was harder to defend?

Connect

Compare

Spinoza and Leibniz: Leibniz visited Spinoza in The Hague in 1676. Spinoza thought the world follows from God's nature and could not be any other way. Leibniz thought God chose this world from many possible ones. What difference does it make whether the world was chosen or simply had to be?
For Spinoza, there is no choice: everything follows necessarily, like the rules of geometry. For Leibniz, God had many options and picked the best, so the world has a reason and a purpose. Leibniz's view keeps a good God who chooses; Spinoza's makes God the same as Nature itself.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From John Locke

What did Locke say about natural rights and government?

Everyone is born with rights to life, liberty and property. A government exists to protect them, rules only with the people's consent, and may be replaced if it abuses its power.
From Thomas Hobbes

Why, according to Hobbes, do people agree to be ruled?

Because life without government would be "nasty, brutish and short", so we give up some freedom to a strong ruler in return for peace and safety.
From Thomas Aquinas

How did Aquinas build every argument in the Summa?

He asked a question, listed the strongest objections against his own view, gave his answer with reasons, and then replied to each objection.
From Zeno of Citium

What was Zeno's main lesson about things we can't change?

Divide things into what is up to you and what is not. Accept calmly what is not up to you, and put your energy into your own choices.
