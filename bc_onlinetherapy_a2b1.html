The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then answer the questions before you read the next part.

Part 1 — A real problem

Start with the part that the later scandals made easy to forget. The problem these companies wanted to solve was enormous, and it still is.

In most countries, mental healthcare is the queue that never gets shorter. Waiting lists of months for a first appointment. Country regions with no therapist within a hundred kilometres. Costs above what most households can pay. And a shame that stops millions of people from even joining the queue.

Estimates suggest hundreds of millions of people worldwide have depression or anxiety and receive no treatment.

So "make therapy as easy to reach as a food delivery" was not a cynical idea. For many people it genuinely worked: a counsellor within days instead of months, from your sofa instead of a waiting room, at a fraction of clinic prices.

Platforms signed up thousands of qualified therapists as flexible workers. A computer matched them to subscribers. And the whole thing — video, voice, and above all unlimited messaging — went inside an app.

Investors loved the shape of it: a huge unserved market and monthly subscriptions. The advertising went through the new personal channels — podcast hosts reading therapy adverts between episodes, influencers describing their own sessions.

Then March 2020 exploded the demand. Lockdowns created a mental-health emergency and closed the clinics at the same time. Regulators suspended old rules, including the rule that you must see a prescriber in person.

Money followed. One company reached the stock market in 2021 valued at about 1.4 billion dollars. A newer one passed a valuation of 4.8 billion within two years of being founded.

And now two completely different professional cultures were sharing one product.

Therapy's culture: fifty minutes, strict confidentiality, progress measured in months, and success possibly meaning the patient leaves.

The growth culture: sign-up funnels, conversion rates, engagement dashboards, keeping the customer.

For a while the tension was invisible. Growth was access, access was the mission, and the mission was going magnificently.

Part 2 — Three collisions

The collisions came quickly, each along a different line.

The platform had shared users' health information with advertising platforms, including Facebook and Snapchat. That information included email addresses, IP addresses, and answers from the first questionnaire — including the fact that the person was looking for therapy. It was used to target adverts and to find similar people.

It had promised confidentiality, repeatedly.

The fine was small — 7.8 million dollars, returned to users. The precedent was enormous. The intake form, the most sensitive document in the whole process, had been treated as marketing data.

The company admitted no wrongdoing and said the practice was normal for the industry. Uncomfortably, investigations suggested that was accurate: researchers found trackers all across mental-health apps.

The second was prescribing. One company had grown at startup speed with a simple flow: online questionnaire, video assessment, medicine by post — including, under the suspended rules, controlled medicines.

Reporting through 2022 described clinicians pushed towards thirty-minute assessments and high prescribing rates, and nurses describing targets that medicine should never have. Major pharmacy chains started refusing the platform's prescriptions. Federal prosecutors opened an investigation. The founder was removed, and the company largely stopped prescribing those medicines.

No court ever decided the model was criminal. The investigation and the pharmacies' refusal did the damage anyway.

Notice the mechanism. It is the same one as Volkswagen. No memo orders harm. The targets, the queue and the funding round order it indirectly.

The third was money. The company valued at 1.4 billion missed its numbers within months. Both founders left in late 2021. The share price fell by around ninety per cent.

Part 3 — Who sets the boundaries?

It would be wrong to end with only the scandals, because the honest record answers both ways.

Access really did improve. People in small towns got help. People who would never have walked into a clinic typed instead. Employers added services that did not exist before. For plenty of users, this was the thing that worked.

And the newest question is now arriving on top: AI. There have been cases of AI-written replies sent to users who did not know they were partly talking to a machine, and lawsuits claiming that companion chatbots harmed vulnerable teenagers.

The accountability problem is the same one the translation industry met — but here the stakes are at maximum. An unaccountable system is cheapest exactly where a mistake costs most.

So the living question is not "was online therapy good or bad?" The record measurably answers both.

The question is structural, and every health system, employer and investor is answering it right now, mostly by accident.

The most confidential conversation in medicine is now carried by companies whose economics reward scale, engagement and data.

So who sets the boundaries that the therapist's office used to set with its walls? The market that pays for access? The professions that carry the duty? Or the regulators, who arrive — as this case shows — about three years after the funnel?

Adding information: who, which, that, where

This story is full of sentences that need extra information inside them: the company that promised confidentiality, people who live far from a clinic. That is a relative clause, and it lets you say more in one sentence instead of three.

who — for people. → "Therapists who worked flexibly."

which — for things. → "The questionnaire, which was very personal, was shared."

that — for people or things, in the common kind of clause. → "The company that made the app."

where — for places. → "Regions where there is no therapist."

whose — for possession. → "Companies whose profits depend on data."

The comma rule, which really matters: no commas = the clause identifies which one you mean ("the app that shared data" — that one, not the others). With commas = extra information you could remove ("BetterHelp, which is an American company, …"). Note: use which, never that, after a comma.
