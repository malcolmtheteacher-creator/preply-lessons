Vocabulary Cards: Key Concepts

Click each card to reveal the definition:

UDHR (Universal Declaration of Human Rights)
UN document (1948) listing basic rights that all humans possess: life, freedom of religion, education, freedom from torture
Inalienable
Rights that cannot be taken away; they belong to every human automatically
Prejudice
Pre-judging someone based on their group (race, religion, gender); a belief or attitude
Discrimination
Treating someone unfairly because of prejudice; prejudice in action
Zakah
Islamic obligatory charity: 2.5% of wealth given annually to those in need
Sadaqah
Islamic voluntary charity given from compassion, beyond the obligatory Zakah
Sewa
Sikh practice of selfless service to others without expecting reward
Langar
Sikh free community kitchen open to all, regardless of religion or status; embodies equality
Stewardship
Christian belief that wealth/resources are gifts from God to be managed responsibly, not hoarded
Liberation Theology
Christian theology teaching that God has a "preferential option for the poor" and calls believers to fight injustice
Riba
Islamic prohibition of interest on loans; lending money should not exploit the borrower
Dasvandh
Sikh practice of giving 10% of income to help others or the community
Adl (Justice)
Core Islamic value: justice and fairness toward all people
Laudato Si'
Pope Francis's 2015 encyclical calling Catholics to care for the environment as a spiritual duty
Effective Altruism
Humanist approach: using reason and evidence to determine which actions reduce suffering most effectively

Review Questions: All Lessons

1. When was the UDHR created and why?
1948, after World War II and the Holocaust. The world committed to preventing such atrocities by protecting universal human rights.
2. How do Christianity and Islam differ in their approach to poverty?
Christianity: stewardship (wealth is a gift to manage). Islam: Zakah (obligatory 2.5% charity). Both see wealth as a responsibility, but Islam formalises the obligation.
3. What did Guru Nanak teach about equality?
Rejected the caste system. All people are equal before Waheguru (God), regardless of birth, status, or background.
4. Name one religious leader who fought discrimination and explain their approach
MLK: Non-violence based on Christian love. Malcolm X: Islamic equality of all races. Desmond Tutu: Ubuntu (shared humanity). Guru Nanak: Langar (practical equality).
5. What is one modern social justice issue and how do religions respond?
Climate: Laudato Si', Islamic Declaration. Refugees: Matthew 25:35, Hijra, Khalsa Aid. Inequality: Zakah, Dasvandh, effective altruism.
6. How can religious and secular approaches to justice work together?
Both share goals (reduce suffering, protect rights). Faith provides moral motivation; secular evidence-based approaches ensure effectiveness. Combined: powerful.
