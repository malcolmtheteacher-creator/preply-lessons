Twelve Times the Same Question

One interviewer asked one question twelve times, and the audience remembered the dodge. Years earlier, a company chief answered first, and then said what mattered.

Today: you'll see why an answer that is not heard is worse than a hard answer, build a three-part 'answer, bridge, message' reply, and use it to stay honest and on track when a question is difficult.
Before we start — talk these through:
  • Think of a time you asked someone a direct question and did not get a direct answer. How did you feel about them afterwards?
  • When a question is difficult, what do you usually do first: answer, explain, or change the subject?
  • What is the hardest kind of question to answer in your work or studies, and why?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — One question, asked again

On 13 May 1997, a former British Home Secretary named Michael Howard sat down for a live interview on the BBC programme Newsnight. He had left the Home Office two weeks earlier. Two years before that, as Home Secretary, he had been in a dispute with the head of the Prison Service, Derek Lewis, about the possible dismissal of a prison governor.

The interviewer, Jeremy Paxman, put one question: did you threaten to overrule him? Howard did not say yes or no. He answered with sentences about what he had and had not done, among them that he had not overruled Mr Lewis, which was a slightly different thing.

Paxman asked again. And again. Most counts say the same question was asked twelve times, though some count fourteen, depending on how the first questions are measured.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was being interviewed, and what position had he held?

2. What was the question?

3. How many times was it asked?

1. Michael Howard, a former British Home Secretary.

2. Did you threaten to overrule him? (him being the head of the Prison Service).

3. About twelve times; some people count fourteen.

Discuss:
  • Howard's answer was technically true: he had not overruled the head of the Prison Service. Why did that not satisfy the audience?

Part 2 — What the audience heard

People remember the interview today, but few remember the dispute. What stayed with them was the pattern: a clear question, a reply that sounded as if it came from a different one, and then the same question again. For many viewers, the answer to the question mattered less than the evasion itself.

Years later, Howard joked about it. In 2014, on the last Newsnight that Paxman presented, he told the interviewer that he had not threatened to overrule, and invited him to ask another eleven times. Paxman himself admitted that part of the reason he repeated the question was that the next item in the programme was not ready.

Whether Howard did or did not threaten anyone is not the point of this lesson. The point is what listeners do with an answer that does not answer: they stop hearing the content and start hearing the avoidance.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What do people remember about the interview today?

2. What did Howard say to Paxman in 2014?

3. What does Paxman say was partly the reason for repeating the question?

1. The repetition and the sense of an evasive answer, more than the details of the dispute.

2. That he had not threatened to overrule, and that Paxman could feel free to ask another eleven times.

3. The next item in the programme was not ready, so he needed to fill time.

Discuss:
  • A listener who spots an evasion stops trusting the rest of what you say. Can you remember a time when this happened to you? What did the speaker do next?

Part 3 — Seven deaths and a decision

In September 1982, seven people in the Chicago area died after taking Tylenol capsules that someone had filled with cyanide. The person responsible was never found. The product was made by Johnson & Johnson, whose chairman at the time was James Burke.

Burke put himself in front of the cameras. The company and the authorities told the public not to take Tylenol. Within days, Johnson & Johnson withdrew about thirty-one million bottles from shelves across the country, at a cost of around a hundred million dollars, even though the poisoning seemed to affect only some capsules in the Chicago area.

In effect, he answered the question the public was asking, which was whether Tylenol was safe, and he answered it directly: not now. Then he moved to the message he wanted people to hear, which was that the company's first concern was public safety. Some writers say the legend is tidier than events were, but the shape of his replies remains the standard example of the answer first, then the message.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What had happened to the Tylenol capsules in 1982?

2. What did Johnson & Johnson do?

3. What was the order of Burke's replies?

1. Someone had put cyanide in some capsules, and seven people died.

2. It withdrew about thirty-one million bottles nationwide, at a cost of around a hundred million dollars.

3. He answered the question honestly first, then moved to the message that public safety came first.

Discuss:
  • Burke's answer was uncomfortable for the company, because it meant a big loss. Why can an honest, costly answer build trust faster than a safe one?

How the tool works

Media trainers teach a technique called bridging: answer the question, then link to the point you most want to make. Used well, it keeps you honest and on track. Used badly, it is the dodge from Part 1. The tool below keeps the three moves in order so that the answer always comes first.

Using it, step by step

  1. Answer the question you were asked, in one sentence. If the answer is yes, say yes. If it is no, say no. If you do not know, say that: I don't know yet. We should have the figures on Thursday.
  2. Bridge with one short linking phrase: What matters more is…, The important thing for our customers is…, Let me put that in context…
  3. Message: say the one thing you most want the listener to take away. One point, in one sentence.
  4. Stop and let the listener respond. If the same question comes again, give the same honest answer in fewer words, then bridge to a different aspect of the message.

Try it yourself

These sentences come from replies to difficult questions. What job does each one do? Choose, then check.

1. Yes, the project is about three weeks late.
2. I don't think that's the right question to be asking.
3. That's an important point, and what I would add is this.
4. Every customer affected will be refunded in full this month.
5. No. The earlier forecast was wrong.
6. Well, there are many ways of looking at it.
7. Let me put that in context.
8. Our first priority is that nobody is put at risk.
9. I can't say yet — we won't have the figures until Thursday.
10. As I have already said, I'm not going to get into that.

1. Answer — a direct reply to the question, with no softening.

2. Dodge — it rejects the question instead of answering it, and the listener hears the avoidance.

3. Bridge — it acknowledges the question and moves to a new point; it only works after an answer.

4. Message — the one thing the speaker most wants remembered.

5. Answer — a short, honest reply, even though it is uncomfortable.

6. Dodge — it sounds like an answer but contains none.

7. Bridge — a short phrase that moves from the answer to the message.

8. Message — one clear point, stated once.

9. Answer — an honest answer when you do not know, with the date it will be known.

10. Dodge — it refuses without explaining why, which sounds as though something is being hidden.

Where the tool breaks

Yes-or-no questions about something serious. If the listener has been harmed, or has asked a plain question of fact, the answer is all they want. Bridge later, if at all.

Listeners who are upset. Before the message, they need to hear that you understand. A bridge that arrives too quickly sounds cold: I'm sorry. I understand why you're angry.

When the bridge is the only thing you say. A message repeated in every reply, whatever the question, is the dodge from Part 1. The listener hears the avoidance, not the point.

Talk it through:
  • Where is the line between a bridge and a dodge? How would a listener know?
  • When did you last answer a difficult question honestly and then regret it? What would you do differently now?

Language — Concession, then focus

A bridge is a small piece of grammar: it admits one thing and moves attention to another. The structures below let you do that without sounding evasive.

Concession clauses: Although it is true that…, the more important point is… / While we did miss the deadline, what matters now is… The first clause admits; the second clause moves.
Wh-clefts to focus attention: What matters most is… / What I would add is… / All I can say is… The first part announces a point, and is delivers it.
Content clauses with that: The thing to bear in mind is that… / The important point is that… / What we know is that… After is, a that-clause names the message.

1. Build the bridge

Use each word from the box once: although · what · which · that.

1. it is true that the project is late, the budget is safe.

2. matters most to our customers is a clear date.

3. The point to bear in mind is nobody has been harmed.

4. Delivery slipped by three weeks, is why we are offering refunds.

1. Although

2. What

3. that

4. which

2. Which reply answers first?

In each pair, choose the reply that gives the answer before anything else.

1. Did the delivery arrive on time?
(a) Well, there were weather problems in the north, and the carrier changed hands last month, and as it happens…
(b) No, it was two days late. The carrier had weather problems, and we have a new schedule from Monday.

2. Will you refund me?
(a) Yes. You will have the money within five days, and I'm sorry for the delay.
(b) I understand how frustrating this must be, and we take every complaint seriously, and…

3. Is the product safe?
(a) Safety is our first priority and we always follow strict procedures, and…
(b) Not at the moment. We have stopped sales until the checks are finished.

4. Do you know who is responsible?
(a) I don't know yet, and I won't guess. We will tell you as soon as we do.
(b) That's a very good question, and what I would say is that we are looking into every possibility.

1. (b) — the answer comes first, then the reason.

2. (a) — it begins with the answer and adds an apology.

3. (b) — a direct answer, even though it is uncomfortable.

4. (a) — it admits what is not known and gives a time.

The questions

A customer is unhappy

They want a straight answer and some respect.

  1. Why has my order not arrived?
  2. Why does it cost so much more than last year?
  3. Why can't I speak to a manager?
  4. Will I get my money back?
  5. How do I know this won't happen again?

A manager is concerned

The listener has authority and little time.

  1. Why are we behind schedule?
  2. Did you know about this problem a month ago?
  3. Whose fault is it?
  4. Will we still hit the target?
  5. Why should I trust this forecast when the last one was wrong?

A public question

The audience is listening for evasion.

  1. Do you accept that mistakes were made?
  2. Why did it take so long to respond?
  3. Are people at risk?
  4. What do you say to those who feel let down?
  5. Will anyone lose their job over this?

An interview

A direct question about a weakness.

  1. Why did you leave your last job?
  2. What is your biggest weakness?
  3. Tell me about a time you failed.
  4. Why is there a gap in your CV?
  5. Why do you think you are ready for this role?

A colleague or friend

It is personal, and the honest answer is uncomfortable.

  1. Did you read what I sent you?
  2. Why didn't you tell me earlier?
  3. Do you think I made the wrong decision?
  4. Are you angry with me?
  5. Can you help me with this, even though you're busy?

How it sounds

Six examples of the shape. Try the question first, then open the answer.

Why has my order not arrived?

Answer: Because it was dispatched two days late, and that was our mistake.

Bridge: What matters now is getting it to you.

Message: It will arrive by Thursday, and if it does not, we will refund the full amount.

Did you know about this problem a month ago?

Answer: Yes, we knew about a smaller version of it.

Bridge: What I want to be clear about is what we did next.

Message: We tested the fix, and it did not work well enough. That is why we are changing the process now.

Why is your price higher than last year?

Answer: Our costs rose by about twelve per cent, and we have passed on most of it.

Bridge: The important thing for you is what that buys.

Message: The price includes a longer warranty, and the average repair under it costs more than the increase.

Why did you leave your last job?

Answer: The company was cutting my department, and I decided to leave before the cuts.

Bridge: That gave me time to think about what I really want.

Message: What I want is a role where I can build things, and that is why I applied here.

Do you accept that mistakes were made?

Answer: Yes. We were slow to act, and that was wrong.

Bridge: What matters most now is what we do about it.

Message: Every affected family will be contacted this week, and an independent review will start on Monday.

Will we still hit the target?

Answer: Not at the current rate, no.

Bridge: Here is what would change that.

Message: If we move two people to the final stage, we reach it in six weeks.

Make it harder

  • Shorter: answer in ten seconds, bridge in one sentence.
  • Again: your partner asks the same question three times. Give the same honest answer each time, shorter each time, and bridge to a different point.
  • Hostile: your partner interrupts after your answer with That's not an answer! Reply calmly, without raising your voice or repeating yourself word for word.
  • Opposite: answer the same question again with the opposite answer. Where would your message come from?
  • Upset listener: say one sentence that shows you understand how the listener feels before you bridge.
