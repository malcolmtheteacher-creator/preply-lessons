Islam's Abrahamic Roots and Distinctive Path

Islam as an Abrahamic Faith

Islam means "submission" or "surrender" in Arabic. A Muslim is "one who submits" to God (Allah in Arabic, which simply means "God"). Islam emerged in 7th-century Arabia, but it is deeply rooted in the Abrahamic tradition — Abraham himself is honoured in Islam as a prophet and the spiritual father of all believers.

In Islamic tradition, Abraham (Ibrahim) is one of the most revered figures. The Quran mentions Ibrahim over 70 times, more than any other person except Moses. But Islam's understanding of Abraham and God's revelation differs markedly from both Judaism and Christianity.

The Central Islamic Claim: Islam teaches that it is not a new religion, but a return to the original, pure monotheism that Abraham taught. Judaism and Christianity, in the Islamic view, preserved some truth but also became corrupted or incomplete over time.

Muhammad: The Final Prophet

Muslims believe that God has sent many prophets throughout history to guide humanity. These include Abraham, Moses, and Jesus. But Muslims believe Muhammad (c. 570-632 CE) was the final prophet — the seal of the prophets. He brought God's final and complete revelation.

Muhammad was born in Mecca (in present-day Saudi Arabia). According to Islamic tradition, when he was about 40 years old, he received revelations from God through the angel Gabriel. These revelations continued for about 23 years until his death.

Risalah — Arabic term meaning "prophethood" or "prophetship." The Islamic belief that God sends prophets to reveal divine guidance to humanity.

The Quran: God's Final Word

Muslims believe that Muhammad's revelations were collected into the Quran (sometimes spelled Koran), which they consider to be the literal word of God, not Muhammad's own words. The Quran is arranged into 114 chapters (surahs), and it addresses everything from law and ethics to stories of past prophets.

The Quran repeatedly affirms that God is one — absolutely, utterly one. There is no trinity, no incarnation, no division of God's nature. This is called Tawhid.

Tawhid — Arabic for "oneness" or "unity." The fundamental Islamic doctrine that God is absolutely one, without partner or equal. God (Allah) has no form, no body, and cannot become human. Tawhid is the core of Islamic faith.

Ishmael and the Line of Muhammad

In Jewish tradition, the covenant passes through Abraham's son Isaac, and then to Isaac's son Jacob (later called Israel). But Islam teaches that the spiritual line also flows through Abraham's other son, Ishmael (Ismail), born to Abraham's servant Hagar.

In Islamic tradition, it is Ishmael and his descendants — not Isaac — who represent the true continuation of Abraham's monotheism. Muhammad is understood as a descendant of Ishmael. This genealogy is crucial to Islam's identity: they are not a new religion but the continuation of Abraham's line and covenant.

"Our Lord, and make us both (Abraham and Ishmael) submitters unto Thee, and make of our seed a community that shall submit unto Thee, and show us our ways of worship, and turn toward us. Lo! Thou, only Thou, art the All-Returning, the Merciful."
Quran 2:128

Jesus in Islam

Islam honours Jesus (Isa in Arabic) as a great prophet — one of the most important prophets. He was born of the Virgin Mary (Maryam), and Muslims respect his moral teachings and miracles.

However, Islam explicitly rejects the Christian doctrine of incarnation. Muslims believe that claiming Jesus is God's son or part of God is the gravest sin in Islam: shirk — associating partners with God. The Quran affirms Jesus's message but clarifies that he was a human prophet, not God in flesh.

"O People of the Scripture, do not commit excess in your religion nor say about Allah except the truth. The Messiah, Jesus, the son of Mary, was but a messenger of Allah."
Quran 4:171
Shirk — Arabic for "association" or "partnership." In Islam, it means associating any partner with God, or implying that anything else shares in God's divinity. Shirk is considered the only unforgivable sin in Islam.

Key Concepts: The Five Pillars

The foundation of Islamic practice rests on five core duties, called the Five Pillars:

1
Shahada

Declaration of faith: "There is no god but God, and Muhammad is his messenger"

2
Salah

Prayer (five times daily, facing Mecca)

3
Zakat

Almsgiving; giving to the poor and in need

4
Sawm

Fasting during Ramadan (the month of revelation)

5
Hajj

Pilgrimage to Mecca (at least once, if able)

The Ummah: Community of Believers

Islam emphasizes the ummah — the global community of all Muslims. Unlike Judaism (which is tied to Jewish ethnicity and heritage) or Christianity (which has many denominations), Islam sees itself as a single, unified community united by faith in God and submission to God's will.

Ummah — The global community of Muslims. Literally "nation" or "people." All Muslims, regardless of ethnicity, nationality, or language, are part of one ummah united by their submission to God.

The Quran's View of Other Faiths

The Quran is complex in how it presents Judaism and Christianity. On one hand, it honors the Torah and the Gospels as revelations from God. On the other, it claims that these scriptures were misunderstood or corrupted over time, and that Islam represents the final, uncorrupted revelation.

The Quran refers to Jewish people and Christians as "People of the Book" — people who received scripture from God. It calls for mutual respect, but also asserts that Islam is the final and complete path to God.

How They Connect (and Differ)

Ibrahim/Abraham

All three faiths revere Abraham. Muslims see him as the first Muslim — the first to submit to God. Christians see him as the father of faith. Jews see him as the covenant-maker. Key difference: Islam traces Muhammad's line through Ishmael; Judaism and Christianity through Isaac.

Musa/Moses

Muslims deeply honor Moses as one of God's greatest prophets. He received the Torah (Tawrah in Arabic), which Muslims respect as God's word. Key similarity: All three traditions see Moses as the lawgiver. Key difference: Muslims believe Muhammad superseded Moses as the final prophet.

Isa/Jesus

Muslims honor Jesus as a great prophet and messenger, born of the Virgin Mary. But Muslims explicitly reject the incarnation and trinity. Key similarity: All three faiths see Jesus as important. Key difference: For Christians, Jesus is God; for Muslims, he is a prophet; for many Jews, he is neither.

Tawhid/Monotheism

The Islamic doctrine of Tawhid (oneness of God) is Islam's core. It emphasizes that God is absolutely one, with no partners, no parts, no human form. This is similar to Judaism's insistence on monotheism, but directly opposed to Christianity's trinity (Father, Son, Holy Spirit as one God).

Muhammad — A Unique Figure

Muhammad has no parallel in Judaism or Christianity. Muslims believe he is the final prophet, sealing all prophecy. Christians don't believe in Muhammad; Judaism predates Muhammad by over 1,000 years. This is Islam's most distinctive claim: that revelation did not end with the Torah or Jesus, but continued through Muhammad.

Islam: Return or Revolution?

Muslims claim that Islam is not a new religion, but a return to Abraham's original monotheism. Yet it also makes new claims (Muhammad as the final prophet). You need to synthesize this tension.

Your Task: Create a comparison showing how Islam honours previous prophets (especially Abraham, Moses, and Jesus) while also claiming to be the final, complete revelation. Write 150-200 words explaining:

• What does Islam preserve from Judaism and Christianity?
• What claims does Islam make that are new or different?
• How can Islam be both a "continuation" and a "completion"?

Sample Direction

A strong answer might say something like: "Islam honours Abraham, Moses, and Jesus as prophets sent by God, and respects the Torah and Gospels as divine revelations. However, Islam claims that previous revelations were incomplete or misunderstood, and that Muhammad brought God's final, perfect revelation through the Quran. Islam also emphasizes strict monotheism (Tawhid) — rejecting the incarnation and trinity. In this way, Islam sees itself as continuing the prophetic tradition while correcting what it believes are errors or corruptions in Judaism and Christianity. It's both a return to Abraham's original monotheism and a claim to have moved beyond earlier revelations."

Does Islam Replace Judaism and Christianity, or Complete Them?

This is one of the most important theological questions in interfaith dialogue. Muslims, Jews, and Christians answer it very differently.

The Islamic View: "Completion"

Islam teaches that God has been revealing himself throughout history through prophets. Abraham, Moses, and Jesus all brought messages from God. But Islam claims that: (1) previous revelations became corrupted or misunderstood, (2) Muhammad brought the final, uncorrupted revelation, and (3) Islam is the completion of the prophetic tradition.

In this view, Islam doesn't "replace" Judaism and Christianity so much as it supersedes them — like a final, corrected edition of a book.

The Jewish Perspective: "Not Our Path"

Most Jewish people see Islam (and Christianity) as separate paths that emerged from Jewish roots but went in different directions. They don't see themselves as incomplete or corrupted. Judaism continues to believe that the Torah is God's eternal law and that Jesus and Muhammad were not God's prophets. Islam is respected as a legitimate monotheistic faith, but it's not seen as Judaism's fulfillment.

The Christian Perspective: "Jesus is Final"

Christians believe that Jesus is God's final revelation — that through Jesus, God has said all that needs to be said about salvation and human destiny. From this perspective, Muhammad cannot be a later prophet because Christ has already come. Christians may respect Islam as a monotheistic faith, but they see the Christian gospel as the complete answer to human spiritual need.

A Historian's View

Historians point out that all three faiths claim finality for their revelation. Each believes it has the most complete understanding of God. This isn't a problem to be "solved" — it's a genuine theological difference that reflects each faith's core identity and values.

The Core Question

If Islam is the "final revelation"...

...then how do we explain that Judaism and Christianity claim their own revelations are complete? Must one of them be wrong? Or can they coexist as genuinely different (and valid) paths?

Can all three faiths be right?

They make contradictory claims about Jesus, about God's nature, about who God's final prophet is. Can they all be true, or must we say that (at least on these points) some are wrong?

What does "Abraham's children" mean?

All three trace themselves to Abraham and monotheism. But they answer the question "What does God want from us?" in different ways. Are they siblings with different interpretations, or are they fundamentally opposed?

Your Perspective

Question: In your view, does Islam replace Judaism and Christianity, complete them, or coexist with them as a separate path? Why?

Remember: This is a real theological debate. There's no neutral "right answer." What matters is that you think carefully about the evidence and explain your reasoning clearly.
