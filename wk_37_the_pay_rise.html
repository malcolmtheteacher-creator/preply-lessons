The Pay Rise

Three years, two new responsibilities, one salary that hasn't moved. Everyone tells you to 'just ask' — nobody tells you the words. Today, in a booked half-hour with your manager Ingrid, you ask: with evidence, with a number, and without a single apology.

The Scene 🎬

The best-paid people you know aren't better at their jobs than you. They're better at this meeting.

The facts: you joined three years ago at a fair salary. Since then you've absorbed the reporting that used to be a whole job, trained two new hires, and become the person clients ask for by name. Your pay has had two cost-of-living nudges. The market, which you've actually researched, now pays your role noticeably more than you're getting.

The obstacles are not Ingrid — she's fair, busy, and answerable to a budget. The obstacles are in your own head, and every second-language professional knows them by name: the feeling that asking is greedy, that good work should speak for itself, that the conversation might damage something. So hear the truths that dissolve them. Asking is normal — managers expect it, and budget for it. Work does not speak for itself — it needs a spokesperson, and you're the only candidate. And done properly, the ask doesn't damage the relationship; it upgrades it, because you become someone whose value has been said out loud.

The shape of the meeting: book it honestly (no ambushes), make the case in changes-since-my-number-was-set (not in years served or rent owed), say the number plainly (ranges are for negotiating down), and — whatever the first answer is — leave with something concrete: the yes, the date, or the written path to yes. "We'll see" is the only losing outcome, and it only happens if you accept it.

  1. Why does asking for money feel shameful when working for money doesn't? Where did you learn that?
  2. "Good work speaks for itself." Who benefits most from you believing that?

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for asking to be paid what the job is worth — raises, rate increases, freelance pricing, promotions. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The register is businesslike warmth with zero apology — every "sorry to ask" and "I know money's tight" is a discount you're offering before negotiations open. You are not asking a favour. You're proposing a price correction, with evidence. Practise the number out loud until it comes out flat and easy — the number is where voices crack.

📋 Opening the case

Phrase 1

The job: booking the meeting — honestly labelled. Complete it:

"I'd like half an hour this week — the subject is my ___ and ___."

"I'd like half an hour — the subject is my role and pay."
The ambush ask — money sprung at the end of a project chat — puts managers on the defensive and gets reflex-noes. The honestly labelled meeting does the opposite: Ingrid arrived having already thought about it ("at least you don't ambush people"), which is the only version of surprised you don't want her to be. Booking it also does something for you: it makes the ask real, dated, and unescapable-from.

Take it anywhere: "Half an hour — subject: my role and pay." Also rate reviews, contract renewals, scope talks. Label the tin.
Phrase 2

The job: the opening frame — a correction, not a favour. Which version?

A. "So, I know budgets are really tight, and I feel awkward even asking, but I was sort of wondering if maybe—"
B. "I'd like to talk about my salary — I think it's fallen behind my actual job, and I'd like us to fix that."
C. "I've been headhunted twice this month, just so you know. So. Let's talk money."
B — "My salary has fallen behind my actual job, and I'd like us to fix that."
A negotiates against itself before Ingrid says a word — awkwardness, tight budgets, "sort of wondering": three discounts, zero case. C opens with a threat, and threats get called ("the ones who hint at offers make it hard"). B frames the whole meeting correctly: a gap has opened between job and pay, and closing gaps is normal business. "I'd like US to fix that" makes her a partner in the correction, not a dragon guarding gold.

Take it anywhere: "___ has fallen behind ___ — I'd like us to fix that." Salaries, rates, titles, terms.
Phrase 3

The job: the evidence structure. Complete it:

"Three things have ___ since my number was ___."

"Three things have changed since my number was set."
The strongest architecture a pay case can have — because it answers the question under every raise conversation: why is the old number wrong NOW? Years served don't answer it (time isn't value), loyalty doesn't, rent doesn't (your costs are not her business case). Changes do: absorbed jobs, trained hires, grown accounts — each one checkable, each one something she can repeat upstairs. Count them on your fingers; three is the number that sounds like a case.

Take it anywhere: "Three things have changed since the price was set." The re-pricing argument for salaries, rates, and rents you're on the other side of.
Phrase 4

The job: heading off the comparison trap. Complete it:

"I'm not comparing myself to anyone — I'm comparing my ___ to my ___."

"I'm not comparing myself to anyone — I'm comparing my pay to my job."
The sentence that keeps the meeting clean. Colleague comparisons ("I know what Marc makes") drag managers into conversations they legally and emotionally cannot have, and turn your case into office politics. Pay-versus-job keeps it on ground where you're unbeatable — nobody knows your job better than you, and Ingrid herself wrote two of your planks into your review. Market comparisons, note, are fine: the market isn't a colleague.

Take it anywhere: "I'm not comparing myself to anyone — just my ___ to my ___." The de-politicising frame for every fairness conversation.

💷 The number and the path

Phrase 5

The job: the number itself — flat, sourced, unapologetic. Put the pieces in order:

The market is running fifty-two to fifty-eight —I'm asking for fifty-four.fifty-four is right in the middle of that range.
"I'm asking for fifty-four. The market is running fifty-two to fifty-eight — fifty-four is right in the middle of that range."
Say the number. Not "somewhere in the fifties", not "whatever you think is appropriate" — Ingrid's had three of those this year "and they all left unhappy", because a missing number gets filled in by the budget, and budgets are shy. Source it like evidence ("the survey and three live postings — not a friend in a pub") and place it defensibly in the range. Practise until it comes out as flatly as your name. The number is the ask; everything else is packaging.

Take it anywhere: "I'm asking for ___ — here's what that's based on." Raises, rates, budgets, quotes. The named number, always.
Phrase 6

The job: the question that converts a no into a map. Complete it:

"What would need to be ___ for the answer to be ___?"

"What would need to be true for the answer to be yes?"
The best question in all of negotiating, deployed at the exact right moment: after the first "I don't have it in this year's budget." It refuses the dead end without fighting it, and makes the manager design your route: Keller holding through Q4, ammunition for Bergmann. Now the raise isn't a hope — it's a checklist, half of which you can personally deliver. Most people hear the budget answer and stand up. The question is why you stay seated.

Take it anywhere: "What would need to be true for yes?" Every no that might be a not-yet — which is most of them.
Phrase 7

The job: nailing the not-now to a calendar and a page. Complete it:

"If it can't be now, ___ — and can we put that in ___?"

"If it can't be now, when — and can we put that in writing?"
"We'll see" is where raises go to die — a good feeling that evaporates by Thursday, unrecorded, undated, deniable by everyone including memory itself. The email you ask for isn't a contract; it's just the number, the conditions, and the decision date, between the two of you — and notice Ingrid's reaction: "it protects me too; Bergmann respects a paper trail." A written not-yet is worth ten spoken warm feelings. It's the difference between a raise deferred and a raise dissolved.

Take it anywhere: "If not now, when — and can we put that in writing?" Promotions, reviews, promised anythings.

🤝 The relationship, upgraded

Phrase 8

The job: recruiting the gatekeeper as your coach. Complete it:

"Which part of my case is ___? I'd rather hear it from you."

"Which part of my case is weakest? I'd rather hear it from you."
The move that changes the room's chemistry: you've just invited your manager to stop defending the budget and start strengthening your argument — and she did, instantly and specifically ("everyone claims training — get Priya and Marc's numbers"). Managers usually know exactly where the case will wobble upstairs; almost nobody asks them. The question also signals the thing that makes people want to pay you more: you treat hard feedback as fuel.

Take it anywhere: "Which part of my case is weakest?" Pitches, proposals, applications — ask the gatekeeper to coach, and most will.
Phrase 9

The job: defusing the threat nobody said. Complete it:

"I'm not threatening to leave — I ___ to stay. That's why I'm asking."

"I'm not threatening to leave — I want to stay. That's why I'm asking."
Every pay conversation has a ghost at the table: the other offer, the resignation, the counter-bid. Naming and dismissing it relaxes the whole room — Ingrid can advocate for someone who wants to stay far more easily than she can negotiate with someone holding a hidden door handle. And it reframes the ask truthfully: you're negotiating the terms of staying, not the terms of leaving. (If you ARE leaving without the raise — different conversation, different lesson, and don't bluff this one; bluffs get called.)

Take it anywhere: "I'm not threatening to go — I want to stay. That's why I'm asking." The de-escalation that strengthens the ask.
Phrase 10

The job: the close — gratitude without surrender. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Thank you — for the straight answers most of all. You'll have the Keller numbers and the training data before you see Bergmann."
Close on the two things that keep the machine moving: appreciation aimed at what she actually gave (straight answers, a route, coaching — NOT the raise, which hasn't happened yet), and your deliverables restated with timing. You leave as a colleague executing a plan, not a supplicant awaiting news. Then execute it: the January email that says "as promised — Keller held, here's the data" is the raise, arriving.

Take it anywhere: thank them for the process, restate your homework, and deliver early. The close that makes the yes easy in January.

The Echo 🕰️

"I am the greatest." He said it before he was — that's the part everyone forgets. When Cassius Clay started announcing his own worth, loudly, in rhyme, he was a light-heavyweight nobody from Louisville whom the boxing establishment found ridiculous. The sport's culture said fighters should be humble, let their fists do the talking, take what the promoters offered. Ali looked at that arrangement and understood exactly whom it served — and it wasn't the fighters.

What made it work wasn't the volume; it was that the claim came with a case. He didn't just say greatest — he predicted rounds, named his evidence, studied opponents like an accountant, and then delivered, fight after fight, until the outrageous self-valuation became the market price. And when the money men still tried to underpay the biggest draw in sport, he negotiated like he fought: publicly, specifically, and without a single apology for knowing his number.

Your meeting with Ingrid needs three percent of Ali's volume and one hundred percent of his method: the worth stated plainly, the evidence attached, the delivery that makes the claim undeniable. Good work does not speak for itself — Louisville's finest proved that the work needs a spokesperson, and the job is permanently vacant until you take it.
