The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — Cities in the forest

The Maya were a people who lived in the rainforests of Central America. Their land is now part of Mexico, Guatemala, Belize and Honduras. Their great time, which we call the Classic period, was from about 250 to 900 AD. This was the same time as the Roman Empire, and the Maya were still building after Rome fell.

There was never one big Maya empire. Instead, there were many cities, and each one ruled itself. Big cities like Tikal and Calakmul had thousands of people. Each city had its own king, its own gods, and often a war with the city next door.

Above the trees, the Maya built huge stone pyramids with a temple on top. They built wide squares and palaces too. And they did all of this with no metal tools, no wheels and no big animals to carry things. This was very hard work in a hot, wet forest.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Was there one big Maya empire? Explain.

2. Why was it so hard for the Maya to build their cities?

1. No. There were many cities, and each city ruled itself, with its own king and its own gods.

2. They built huge pyramids and cities in a hot, wet forest, with no metal tools, no wheels and no big animals to carry things.

Talk about it:
  • Many small cities, no one big king, and lots of wars — but the same culture. What is good, and what is dangerous, about a land like this?

Part 2 — Reading the sky, writing in stone

The Maya were great scientists. From tall towers, they watched the sun, the moon and the stars for many years. They knew the planet Venus very well, and they could say where it would be years before. They made a calendar that was almost perfect, and they understood the number zero — something many other people did not know yet.

The Maya also had writing. They were the only people in the ancient Americas with a full writing system. They wrote with small picture-signs called glyphs. They wrote on their stone buildings, on pots, and in folding books made of bark-paper. These books are called codices.

The Maya made many of these books. But in the 1500s, when Spanish priests came, they burned almost all of them, because they thought the books were bad. Today, only four old Maya books are left in the whole world.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name two things the Maya knew about the sky and numbers.

2. What happened to almost all the Maya books?

1. Any two: they could say where Venus would be years before; they made an almost perfect calendar; they understood the number zero.

2. In the 1500s, Spanish priests burned almost all of them. Today only four are left.

Talk about it:
  • Only four Maya books are left. What do we lose when old books and writing are destroyed?

Part 3 — The cities go quiet, but the people stay

Then, over about a hundred years, something went wrong. In the south, one big city after another stopped building. People stopped carving dates in stone. Slowly, they left. By about 900 AD, the great southern cities like Tikal and Palenque were almost empty, and the forest grew back over them.

Why did this happen? Scientists think the biggest reason was the weather. There were very bad droughts — long years with little rain. In this land there were no big rivers, so the farms needed the rain. There were also too many people, the soil was tired, and the cities were always at war. All of these problems came together, and the system broke.

But the Maya did not disappear. In the north, cities like Chichén Itzá grew strong for hundreds of years more. And the people are still here today. More than seven million Maya live in Mexico and Central America now. They speak Maya languages, and they keep some of the old festivals. For a long time, no one could read the old glyphs. Then a Russian scientist, Yuri Knorozov, showed that the glyphs were the sounds of the Maya language. People laughed at him, but he was right. Now we can read most of the writing, and the old stones tell us about real kings, real wars and real dates.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the southern cities go quiet around 900 AD?

2. Give two reasons why "the Maya disappeared" is not true.

1. The biggest reason was very bad droughts with little rain. There were also too many people, tired soil, and many wars. All these problems came together.

2. Any two: northern cities like Chichén Itzá grew strong after that; more than seven million Maya live in the region today; they still speak Maya languages and we can now read most of the old writing.

Talk about it:
  • We often say old peoples "died". But here, the people are still alive today. How does that change the story?

Grammar: relative clauses — who / which / where / whose

The Maya story is full of nouns we want to say more about: a people, a city, a calendar, a king. A relative clause lets you add more information to a noun in the same sentence. You do not have to start a new sentence.

Which word do I use?
• who = for people: The Maya, who lived in the rainforest, built huge temples.
• which = for things: a city which is now empty; a book which the priests burned.
• where = for places: the forest where the cities stood.
• whose = for "his / her / its / their": a people whose writing was lost.
Examples from the story:
• The Maya were a people who built great cities.
• They made a calendar which was almost perfect.
• Tikal is a city where the forest grew back over the temples.
• Yuri Knorozov was a scientist whose idea was right.

1. Choose the right word

Put who, which, where or whose in each gap.

1. The Maya were a people lived in the forest.

2. They built pyramids are still standing today.

3. Tikal is a city many people once lived.

4. The Maya, books were burned, wrote with glyphs.

5. Knorozov was a scientist read the old writing.

1. who (people)

2. which (things — pyramids)

3. where (a place — the city)

4. whose ("their books")

5. who (a person)

2. Make one sentence

Join the two short sentences into one. Use who, which, where or whose. Then read it out loud.

1. The Maya were a people. They watched the stars.

2. A drought is a long time. There is little rain.

3. Chichén Itzá is a city. The Maya built it in the north.

1. The Maya were a people who watched the stars.

2. A drought is a long time which has little rain. (Also good: a long time when there is little rain.)

3. Chichén Itzá is a city which the Maya built in the north. (Or: a city where the Maya lived in the north.)
