Today we are working on imagery and symbol so you can show how a pattern of images builds an argument, not just decorate a poem.

The big idea

One image is decoration. A pattern of images (an image cluster) is an argument. Examiners reward the student who spots the pattern and explains what it makes the reader believe.

Read the whole poem aloud once. Notice how many sentences are questions.

Image clusters

TermWhat it isExample
ImageA word-picture that appeals to the senses.“burning bright”
Image clusterA group of related images that keeps returning.hammer, chain, furnace, anvil
SymbolAn image that stands for an idea beyond itself.the Tyger as energy, danger, beauty

The method: 1) find the images, 2) sort them into clusters, 3) ask what the cluster implies about the subject, 4) explain the effect on the reader.

Weak versus strong

Weak: Blake uses lots of imagery about fire and metal. This makes the poem vivid.
Strong: By building a cluster of blacksmith’s imagery (“hammer”, “chain”, “furnace”, “anvil”), Blake pictures the creator as a labouring craftsman, which unsettles the reader by suggesting that the tiger’s terrifying beauty was made with effort, and so poses an uncomfortable question about what kind of maker would choose to forge it.

SPEAK: What does “vivid” fail to do? What three things does the strong version add?

“Vivid” is a vague adjective that names no effect. The strong version names the cluster, quotes four words, explains what it implies (the creator as craftsman), uses an exact verb (unsettles) and links to a larger question the poem raises.

Sort and interpret

1. Sort the images

Group these phrases into three clusters, then name each cluster.

“burning bright”, “twist the sinews of thy heart”, “the hammer … the chain … the furnace”, “stars threw down their spears”, “distant deeps or skies”, “fearful symmetry”, “seize the fire”, “water’d heaven with their tears”

Fire and forge: burning bright, hammer, chain, furnace, seize the fire. Body and creation: sinews, heart, fearful symmetry. Cosmos and conflict: distant deeps or skies, stars threw down their spears, water’d heaven with their tears. The three clusters together make the creator both a cosmic power and a craftsman, and the tiger both an animal and a force.

2. Interpret the cluster

SPEAK: What does the forge imagery suggest about the creator? Is he admirable, frightening or both? Give a one-sentence interpretation using an exact effect verb.

Possible: “The forge imagery presents the creator as a labouring blacksmith, which makes creation seem deliberate and violent rather than gentle, and positions the reader to fear as much as admire the force behind the tiger.”

3. A change worth noticing

The first stanza says “Could frame thy fearful symmetry?” The last says “Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?”

SPEAK: One word changes. What does it do to the meaning?

“Could” asks about ability; “dare” asks about courage and morality. The final question shifts from how was it possible? to who would dare? So the poem moves from awe to challenge, and the repeated stanza now sounds more fearful than wondering.

Looking back

SPEAK: Which was more useful today: sorting images into clusters, or interpreting the cluster? Why?

I can: sort a poem’s images into clusters and explain what the pattern makes the reader think or feel.
imagery (noun) — descriptive language that appeals to the senses. Example: "The imagery of the forge is violent."
image cluster (noun) — a group of related images that recurs in a poem. Example: "The image cluster of metalwork builds the idea of a craftsman."
symbol (noun) — an image or object that stands for a larger idea. Example: "The tiger is a symbol of fierce, dangerous energy."
rhetorical question (noun) — a question asked for effect, not to get an answer. Example: "The poem is built from rhetorical questions."
juxtaposition (noun) — placing two different things side by side for contrast. Example: "The lamb and tiger are in juxtaposition."
sublime (adjective) — inspiring awe that mixes beauty and fear. Example: "The tiger is both beautiful and sublime."
imply (verb) — to suggest something without saying it directly. Example: "The forge imagery implies deliberate, violent creation."
unsettle (verb) — to make someone feel uneasy. Example: "The unanswered question unsettles the reader."
