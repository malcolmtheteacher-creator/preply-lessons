The Power of Boredom

B2/C1 Level · Why doing nothing is the cradle of creativity

When were you last properly bored?

Not the mild restlessness of a dull meeting — the kind we now reach for our phones to escape — but a long, slow, almost uncomfortable stretch of nothing in particular to do. For most of human history, boredom was simply part of life. Children stared at clouds; adults walked, sat, queued, waited. We have, in the space of about fifteen years, almost entirely engineered it out of our days — and it now looks rather as if this may have cost us more than we realised.

Think about this:

1. When were you last properly, deeply bored — not just understimulated for ten minutes, but for a long stretch with nothing to escape into?

2. Children today have far fewer "empty" hours than children twenty years ago. Is that a kindness or a quiet loss?

3. A growing number of writers, scientists and artists insist their best ideas arrive only in idle moments. Is that mystical thinking, or is something real going on?

Reading: A Word We Have Almost Lost

For most of human history, boredom was simply part of life. Children stared at clouds. Adults walked, sat, waited for buses, queued at counters and tolerated long evenings with nothing in particular to do. We took that ordinary, low-grade restlessness completely for granted. Then, in the space of about fifteen years, the smartphone abolished it. We can now reach for a screen in any spare second — and most of us do, almost without noticing.

Phrasing · read this paragraph aloud and break it into natural thought groups. Pause briefly at each comma; the long sentences are designed to be chunked.

What we have lost, slowly enough that few people noticed, is the discomfort of doing nothing — and the strange productivity that often hid inside it. Researchers studying mind-wandering have found that the periods we used to think of as "wasted" were nothing of the kind. When we are not focused on any particular task, the brain quietly switches to what scientists call the default mode network: a slow, exploratory pattern of activity in which memory, imagination and self-reflection all interweave. That, it turns out, is where many of our best ideas were always coming from.

Sentence stress · read aloud and underline the three or four content words in each sentence that take the heaviest stress. Function words (the, of, with, are) should slip past almost unnoticed.

A 2014 study at the University of Central Lancashire gave one group of participants a deliberately tedious task — copying telephone numbers from a directory — before asking them to come up with creative uses for a paper cup. The bored group consistently outperformed a control group who had skipped the dull stretch altogether. Boredom, it seemed, was not the absence of thinking; it was a creative incubation period the participants were no longer rich enough to ignore. The dull stretch, in other words, had been doing the work all along.

Linking · notice how, in fluent speech, words bleed into each other — "out_of a directory", "creative_uses", "all_along". Practise the linking; don't separate every word.

The trouble is that we now fidget for stimulation almost the moment a pause appears. We reach for our phones at red lights, in lifts, in the half-second between sending an email and starting the next. The technology feels like rest, but it isn't — what it actually delivers is fragmented attention, a constant stream of small, sweet, pointless inputs that pacify the mind without ever releasing it. The hours add up. Whole evenings disappear in a way that no one would willingly choose if asked in advance.

Intonation · this paragraph turns on a contrast — "feels like rest, but isn't". Listen for the falling intonation on each side of the pivot, with a small rise on the contrast word itself.

The implication is uncomfortable, but freeing. Many of the things we rather wistfully call "creativity" or "insight" or "perspective" appear to require a fertile silence we have almost stopped giving ourselves. Walks without podcasts. Showers without playlists. Long, half-purposeless stretches of staring at the wall. They look like nothing — which is exactly the point. The brain, given a true gap, fills it on its own.

Shadow reading · try reading this paragraph aloud half a beat behind your teacher, matching pace, stress and intonation. The aim is rhythm, not perfection — lock onto the music of the sentence.

It's a slow, exploratory pattern of brain activity that switches on when we are not focused on a task — weaving together memory, imagination and self-reflection. It matters because, according to researchers, that is where many of our best ideas have always come from. The brain isn't idle when we are; it's exploring.

3. What did the 2014 University of Central Lancashire study reveal?

Show Answer
A group given a deliberately boring task (copying phone numbers) outperformed a control group on a subsequent creative task. Boredom turned out to act as a creative incubation period — the dull stretch was doing real cognitive work, even though it didn't feel like it from the inside.

4. Why does the writer say that smartphone time "feels like rest, but isn't"?

Show Answer
It pacifies the mind without ever releasing it. Instead of giving us the open, wandering space the brain seems to need, it delivers a constant stream of small, sweet, fragmented inputs that occupy attention without restoring it. We finish drained, not refreshed.

5. Inference: why does the writer call it "freeing" that creativity may require something as humble as boredom?

Show Answer
Because it means the conditions for original thought are not exotic, expensive or rare — they are available to anyone willing to leave the phone alone for a long walk or a slow shower. The "secret" of insight turns out to be something almost every previous generation had simply by default.

Past habits: used to · would · be / get used to

Talking about boredom means talking about how things used to be. Three closely-related structures do most of the work — and one of them is a famous trap for students at every level.

used to + infinitive

A past habit or state that is no longer true.

We used to wait at bus stops with nothing in particular to do.

He used to be quieter as a teenager.

would + infinitive

A past habit only — not a state. Slightly more literary in feel.

My grandmother would sit by the window every evening.

Trap: not would be, would have, would know — states need used to.

be / get used to + -ing

Completely different meaning: be / become accustomed to something.

We are used to having something in our hands at all times.

He's slowly getting used to the silence.

Gap-fill 1: used to or would?

1. As a child, I ____________ (stare) at clouds for hours; now I can barely manage five minutes.

Show Answer
used to stare or would stare — both work, since this is a repeated past action.

2. Twenty years ago, people ____________ (queue) without complaining; today nobody seems to know how.

Show Answer
used to queue or would queue — both fine.

3. He ____________ (be) much quieter as a teenager.

Show Answer
used to be — would doesn't work here because "be" is a state, not a habit.

4. My grandmother ____________ (sit) by the window every evening, doing nothing in particular.

Show Answer
would sit or used to sit — "would" feels slightly more literary, but both are correct.

5. They ____________ (have) a holiday cottage in Cornwall, but they sold it years ago.

Show Answer
used to have — "have" is a state of possession here, not a repeated action, so "would" doesn't work.

Gap-fill 2: be / get used to

6. We are now ____________ (use / fill) every spare second with our phones.

Show Answer
used to filling — "be used to" + -ing. This is the "accustomed to" meaning, not the "past habit" one.

7. It will take him a few weeks, but he will ____________ (get / use / work) in silence.

Show Answer
get used to working — "become accustomed to" + -ing.

Gap-fill 3: rewrite

8. Rewrite using would: "On Sunday afternoons, my grandfather walked for hours with no particular destination."

Show Answer
On Sunday afternoons, my grandfather would walk for hours with no particular destination.

9. Rewrite using used to: "When I was a student, I lived above a bakery."

Show Answer
When I was a student, I used to live above a bakery. ("Live" is a state, so "would" doesn't work here.)
