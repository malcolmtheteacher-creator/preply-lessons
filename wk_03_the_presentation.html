The Presentation

You've rehearsed the talk. You know your slides cold. The part that keeps you up the night before isn't the presentation — it's the hands that go up the moment you finish.

The Scene 🎬

You're standing at the front of a bright room, a clicker in your hand, giving a fifteen-minute talk — maybe to a conference audience, maybe to a client across a polished table. The slides are good. You've practised the words until they're smooth. And for those fifteen minutes, you're fine, because you decide what comes next.

Then you say "so, that's the overview — I'm happy to take any questions," and control quietly leaves your hands. Because the real test of a presentation was never the talk. It's the ten minutes afterwards, when a stranger can ask you anything at all: a detail you didn't prepare, a challenge you didn't see coming, sometimes a question with a sharp edge to it — asked, in front of everyone, in your second or third language.

And here's what nobody tells you: the audience isn't really grading your answers. They're watching how you handle the question. A calm "that's a fair challenge — here's how I'd look at it" earns more respect than a flawless fact fired back in a panic. Poise reads as authority. A rushed, defensive answer reads as someone who's rattled — even when the answer is right.

So today isn't about knowing everything. It's about staying composed when you don't: hearing the real question inside a woolly one, keeping your cool when someone pushes, and saying "I don't have that to hand — let me follow up" without it costing you an ounce of credibility.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Which frightens you more — giving the talk, or the questions afterwards? Why? Be specific about the exact moment your stomach tightens.
  2. Think of a time someone asked you a hard or unfair question in front of others. What did you do — and looking back, what do you wish you'd done instead?

Who's who

You
The Presenter 🎤

You've just given a clear, confident talk. Now the floor is open. Your job isn't to have every answer — it's to stay composed, steer each question well, and leave the room trusting you more than before you started.

The other voices
The Audience 🙋

Curious, sharp, and unpredictable. Most mean well; one has an edge and wants to test you. They can ask anything — and how you handle them, not just what you say, decides what they take away.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Notice how the presenter slows down, breathes, and treats every question — even the hostile one — as something to steer calmly, never to fight.

You (Presenter)Right — I'll keep this brief: three things, then I'll take questions. We grew faster than forecast, we did it in two new markets, and we did it without extra headcount. That's the story. So — I'm happy to open it up. What have you got?
QuestionerThanks. Quick one on the growth — is it, well, real? Or is it just the market lifting everyone?
You (Presenter)Good question — just so I answer the right thing: do you mean are we outgrowing our competitors, or whether the growth will last once the market cools?
QuestionerThe second one, really. Will it hold?
You (Presenter)Then here's my honest read: some of it is the rising tide, yes. But the two new markets are ours — we opened them, and they'd keep growing even in a flat year. So I'd say the core is real.
Second questionerI have to push back on that. You "did it without extra headcount" — which to me just means you've burned out your team and this isn't repeatable. Isn't that the truth you're not putting on the slide?
You (Presenter)I hear you, and it's a fair challenge. The way I'd look at it is this: no extra headcount this year isn't a plan for every year — it's what the numbers happened to be. You're right that we can't run the team hot forever, and hiring is exactly what we're discussing for next year. So I don't think we're as far apart as it sounds.
Second questionerHmm. But you'd admit the pace isn't sustainable as it stands?
You (Presenter)As it stands, without hiring? I'd agree with you — it isn't. That's precisely why it's on the agenda. I'd rather name that openly than pretend it away.
QuestionerCan I come back in? What was the exact churn rate in the second market last quarter?
You (Presenter)The precise figure? I don't have that to hand — and I'd rather not guess at a number in a room like this. Let me follow up with you straight after with the exact quarter-on-quarter figure. Can you grab me at the break?
QuestionerPerfect, thank you.
You (Presenter)If there's nothing else — let me leave you with one thought. The headline isn't the growth number; it's that we now know how to open a market and make it stand on its own. That's the thing worth building on. Thank you all.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. When the first questioner asks something a bit vague ("is it real?"), the presenter doesn't just launch in. What do they do first — and why does that small move make the answer better?

2. The second questioner is sharp, almost accusing — "the truth you're not putting on the slide". The presenter stays calm and does something surprising instead of defending. What is it, and why does it work?

3. Asked for an exact figure they don't have, the presenter doesn't guess and doesn't apologise their way into a hole. What exactly do they do — and why does it protect their credibility?

1. They clarify what's really being asked — "do you mean… or…?" — before answering. A vague question has two or three possible answers; guessing which one wastes your best reply on the wrong target. Checking first buys a breath, shows you're listening, and means the answer actually lands.
2. They agree with the true part instead of fighting the whole thing — "you're right that we can't run the team hot forever". Acknowledging the fair point drains the heat out of a hostile question and quietly turns an attacker into someone you're reasoning with. You don't win a hostile question by pushing back harder; you win it by staying calm and finding the grain of truth.
3. They defer gracefully: name that they don't have it, refuse to guess ("I'd rather not guess at a number"), and promise a specific follow-up. Guessing wrong in front of a room is far more damaging than a confident "let me get you the exact figure". Choosing accuracy over speed reads as senior, not stuck.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the part that actually frightens people — the Q&A. Learn them until they're automatic, so your mind is free to stay calm and think. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. In the Q&A, how you answer matters more than how fast. A calm pause before you speak — a breath, a steady voice, no rush — signals authority far more than a quick, perfect reply. Slow your first three words down on purpose. A composed "let me take that in order…" tells the room you're in control; a flustered rush tells them you're rattled, even when your answer is right.

🗺️ Signposting the talk

Phrase 1

You're opening. You want to promise the audience a short, clear talk and tell them questions come at the end — in one confident line. Put it in order:

three things,I'll keepthen I'llthis brief —take questions
"I'll keep this brief — three things, then I'll take questions."
It sets a promise (short), a shape (three things), and a plan (questions after) in one breath — the audience relaxes because they know the map. Cousins: "I've got three points, then it's over to you.", "Let me walk you through it, and we'll save time for questions."
Phrase 2

You've finished your points and you want to hand the floor to the room — warmly, so people actually feel invited to ask. Complete the chunk: "So — I'm happy to ___ ___ ___. What have you got?"

"So — I'm happy to open it up. What have you got?"
"Open it up" hands the floor generously; "what have you got?" is warm and unafraid, which puts a nervous room at ease. Relatives: "Let me open the floor.", "I'd love your questions — who's first?"

🙋 Inviting & clarifying questions

Phrase 3

Someone asks a woolly, two-part question and you're not sure which half they want. You want to check — without sounding like you're stalling. Which line clarifies best?

A. "Sorry, I don't really understand your question."
B. "Good question — just so I answer the right thing, do you mean…?"
C. "That's not really what my talk was about."
B — "Good question — just so I answer the right thing, do you mean…?"
It compliments, then narrows the question to the exact thing you'll answer — so your reply lands instead of missing. It also buys you a breath. A makes the questioner feel unclear (their fault, not yours); C sounds defensive and shuts them down.
Phrase 4

A question comes at you fast and you want a moment to order your thoughts before you speak — calmly, out loud, not in a panicked silence. What might you say to take that beat and stay in control? (Say your own version.)

e.g. "Let me take that in order." / "Give me a second to do that justice." / "That's worth a proper answer — one moment."
A deliberate pause is a power move, not a weakness. Naming that you're taking a moment ("let me take that in order") sounds considered and buys real thinking time. The room waits happily; a considered answer is worth the two-second wait.

🛡️ Handling a hostile question — calmly

Phrase 5

A questioner challenges you with a bit of an edge. You want to acknowledge the challenge as fair, then calmly give your side — no defensiveness. Complete it: "I hear you, and it's a ___ ___ — the way I'd look at it is…"

"I hear you, and it's a fair challenge — the way I'd look at it is…"
"I hear you" and "fair challenge" concede that the question is legitimate, which lowers the temperature and stops it becoming a fight; "the way I'd look at it" then calmly offers your view as a perspective, not a counter-attack. Cousins: "That's a fair push — here's how I see it.", "I get why you'd ask that — my take is…"
Phrase 6

The hostile questioner has a point buried in the attack. Rather than argue the whole thing, you agree with the true part first. Which response does that — staying calm and keeping the room with you?

A. "You're completely wrong about that."
B. "You're right that we can't run the team hot forever — and that's exactly why…"
C. "I think you've misunderstood the whole presentation."
B — "You're right that we can't run the team hot forever — and that's exactly why…"
Conceding the true part ("you're right that…") drains the heat and turns an opponent into someone you're reasoning with — then "and that's exactly why…" pivots to your point without a fight. A and C escalate and make you look rattled; agreeing where you honestly can is the composed move. Run something hot = push it too hard for too long.

🤷 The question you can't answer

Phrase 7

You're asked for an exact figure you simply don't have. Guessing is dangerous; freezing is worse. You want to admit it cleanly and promise a follow-up. Complete it: "I don't have that figure ___ ___ — let me follow up with you after."

"I don't have that figure to hand — let me follow up with you after."
"To hand" (= available right now) admits the gap without any hint of incompetence, and the promised follow-up shows you'd rather be accurate than quick. That reads as senior, not stuck. Cousins: "I'd rather check than guess — I'll get you the exact number.", "Let me come back to you on that with something solid."
Phrase 8

You want to make clear why you're deferring rather than answering — so it sounds like judgement, not avoidance. What could you say to show you're choosing accuracy over a risky guess? (Say your own version.)

e.g. "I'd rather not guess at a number in a room like this." / "I'd rather give you something accurate than something quick." / "A wrong figure helps nobody — let me get you the real one."
Naming the reason turns "I don't know" into "I'm being careful" — which is exactly what a credible person does. It reframes the gap as good judgement. Never invent a number in front of an audience; the person who guesses wrong loses far more than the one who says "let me check".

🎯 Closing strong

Phrase 9

Questions are slowing down and you want to close the session on your terms — not just let it fizzle out. You want to signal "we're wrapping up, and I've got a final thought." Complete it: "If there's nothing else, let me ___ ___ ___ ___ ___."

"If there's nothing else, let me leave you with one thought."
It takes the room back from the Q&A and lets you have the last word — the thing they'll actually carry out the door. Ending on your message, not a random final question, is a quiet power move. Cousins: "Let me close with the one thing to remember.", "If we're good, I'll leave it there with this…"
Phrase 10

You want your final line to point at the meaning of your talk, not just repeat a number — the one idea worth building on. Which closing does that best?

A. "Anyway, that's everything, thanks."
B. "So, to repeat: growth was up, headcount was flat. Thanks."
C. "The headline isn't the number — it's that we now know how to open a market and make it stand on its own."
C — "The headline isn't the number — it's that we now know how to…"
It lifts the audience from a fact to a meaning — what the result actually proves — which is what sticks. A throws away your last moment; B just recites data they already have. End on the idea, and that's what they repeat afterwards. Stand on its own = keep going without support.

Your Turn 🎭

Now the floor is yours. Speak your answers out loud FIRST — this is fluency practice, so keep talking even when it's not perfect. Use the timer: a real Q&A won't wait while you craft the ideal sentence, and staying composed matters more than being flawless.

The Twist

🎯 The questioner who won't let go

One more round — no script. You've given your talk, and one questioner has decided to make a point of you. They don't ask once and sit down; they push, then push again, twisting your words a little and clearly trying to make you look wrong in front of the whole room: "But you said X… so surely that means Y… so really your whole plan falls apart, doesn't it?" Everyone's watching to see how you take it. Start the timer and handle it live: stay composed, don't get defensive, agree with anything that's genuinely true, calmly hold your line on the rest — and keep the room with you, not just the argument. The goal isn't to crush them; it's to look like the calmest, most reasonable person in the room. One rule: no rising to it, no sarcasm, and no apologising for your English — just stay steady and keep the room on your side.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

It's a strange thing: people will happily rehearse a talk twenty times and never once practise the Q&A — even though the Q&A is the part they're afraid of. The talk feels safe because you control it. The questions feel frightening for the opposite reason: for ten minutes, a stranger decides what you have to talk about, and you can't rehearse an answer to a question you haven't heard yet. That loss of control is the real fear — not the English, not the subject.

But here's the release in that: you were never supposed to know everything. Nobody who's ever stood at the front of a room knew every figure, saw every challenge coming, or had a perfect answer ready. The job was never to be a walking encyclopaedia. It's to stay composed while you don't know — to say "let me check that and come back to you" as easily as you'd say your own name, and mean it. The moment you stop needing to have every answer, the Q&A stops being terrifying.

Because in the end, authority isn't the perfect answer — it's poise. It's the calm pause, the fair "that's a good challenge", the steady voice that doesn't rise when someone pushes. An audience forgets your third slide by lunchtime. What they remember is how you were when things got hard: whether you stayed the calmest person in the room. Get that right, and they'll trust you long after they've forgotten the numbers.
