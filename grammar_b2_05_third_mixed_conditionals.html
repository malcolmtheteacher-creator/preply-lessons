Warm-up

Out loud, before anything else — don't worry about mistakes, just talk.

SPEAK

Think of one small decision from years ago that quietly changed everything — a job, a move, a person you met or didn't. What if you'd chosen differently? An unfinished sentence is fine — just start talking.

Listen in on this conversation

Sam: Do you ever think about what would've happened if you'd stayed at your old job?

Alex: All the time, actually. If I hadn't left, I wouldn't have met my husband — he was my replacement's friend, weirdly.

Sam: Wow. Any regrets though?

Alex: Money-wise, yes — if I'd stayed, I'd probably be earning double what I make now. But if I weren't so restless by nature, I would have stuck it out anyway. So maybe it was always going to end the same way.

Look at what Alex does in three lines. Three ways of imagining a different past — but they don't all point at the same moment. Can you tell them apart? Say it before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "If I hadn't left, I wouldn't have met my husband" — a pure past regret. Unreal past condition, unreal past result. Nothing here is about today.
2. "If I'd stayed, I'd probably be earning double what I make now" — the condition is past, but the result is happening right now.
3. "If I weren't so restless by nature, I would have stuck it out" — a lasting quality (true in general), linked to one specific unreal moment in the past.

These small differences are exactly why English has more than one shape for imagining the past.
Today: imagining a different past — the pure past regret, and the two mixed forms that connect an unreal past to today — so you can talk about what-ifs, near misses, and lucky escapes the way people actually do.

If Only I'd Known

For one unreal moment in the past — a regret, a lucky escape, a near miss. Both halves stay in the past.

Spot the pattern

  • "If I'd left the house five minutes earlier, I would have caught the bus."
  • "She wouldn't have missed the flight if she'd set an alarm."
  • "If we hadn't taken the shortcut, we'd have been stuck in that traffic for hours."

What's the shared machinery, and where does it point in time? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
had (not) + past participle, ... would (not) have + past participle. Both halves point at exactly the same unreal moment in the past — nothing here is about today. Use it for the shortcut you shouldn't have taken, the alarm you didn't set, the bus you nearly caught.
If + subject + had (not) + past participle, subject + would (not) + have + past participle
— or reversed: subject + would (not) + have + past participle + if + subject + had (not) + past participle
The classic slip: "If I would have known" or "If I'd have known" — would have never sits in the if-clause. Just: If I had known.

Quick check

1. Your cousin got soaked because she left her umbrella at home. Say what would have happened if she'd taken it — use not get:

"If she'd taken her umbrella, she so wet."

2. Your friend failed his driving test because he never practised reversing. Say the if-clause — use practise:

"If he reversing, he would have passed first time."

Then I'd Be Somewhere Else

The condition is an unreal past — but the result is true right now, today.

Spot the pattern

  • "If I'd taken that job in Berlin, I'd be living there right now."
  • "She'd still be single if she hadn't gone to that wedding."
  • "I'd have way more free time now if I hadn't agreed to run this project."

Compare these to Tab 2. Something's different about the result half. What, and why? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
The if-clause is still unreal past (had/hadn't + participle) — but the result describes something true right now, so it drops the "have": would (not) + base verb. That mix of a past condition with a present result is exactly why it's called a mixed conditional.
If + subject + had (not) + past participle, subject + would (not) + base verb  (+ now / these days / still)
Compare: "If I'd taken the job, I would have moved to Berlin" (a finished past action) vs "If I'd taken the job, I'd be in Berlin now" (true this very minute). Same if-clause — a different meaning in the result.

Quick check

1. Your friend never learned to drive as a teenager, and she still can't drive today. Say what would be true now if she had — use be able to drive:

"If she'd learned to drive back then, she now."

2. Your uncle sold his flat years ago, which is why he's paying rent today. Say the if-clause — use not sell:

"If he that flat, he wouldn't be paying rent now."

If I Weren't Like This

The condition isn't one moment — it's a lasting truth about someone. The result is still one specific unreal moment in the past.

Spot the pattern

  • "If I weren't so disorganised, I wouldn't have missed the deadline."
  • "She wouldn't have panicked if she weren't so afraid of flying."
  • "If he didn't hate confrontation so much, he would have said something at the time."

This time, is the if-clause about one moment, or something more permanent? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
The if-clause isn't a specific past event — it's a lasting truth about someone (were/weren't, or a simple present habit put in the past tense). The result is still one specific unreal moment, so it keeps would (not) have + past participle.
If + subject + past simple  (a lasting truth), subject + would (not) + have + past participle
Quick test: could you swap the if-clause for "in general" or "usually"? "If I'm usually disorganised..." Yes → this kind of mixed conditional, not the pure third.

Quick check

1. Your brother is naturally very calm — that's why he didn't get stressed when his flight was delayed six hours. Say what would've happened if he weren't like that — use get stressed:

"If he weren't so calm, he during that six-hour delay."

2. Your colleague always says yes to everything — that's just who she is — which is why she agreed to work last weekend even though she was exhausted. Say the if-clause — use be better at saying no:

"If she , she wouldn't have agreed to work last weekend."

Now say it
