Warm-up

Out loud, before anything else — don't worry about mistakes, just talk.

SPEAK

Think about your own friends, family, or colleagues. Say one thing that's true of all of them, and one thing that's true of only one or two of them.

Listen in on this conversation

Sam: How was the barbecue?

Alex: Good, but there was hardly any food left by the time I got there — everyone was starving. Most of the neighbours came, actually.

Sam: Did everyone bring something?

Alex: Each person brought a dish, so there was loads of variety. Neither of the vegetarian options went down that well, though.

Look at what Alex says. Four small quantifier words are doing a lot of work in that short exchange. Can you spot all four? Say them before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "hardly any food" — an amount close to zero.
2. "most of the neighbours" — a specific, known group, so it needs "of the".
3. "each person" — one at a time, individual attention.
4. "neither of the vegetarian options" — exactly two things, and the answer is no for both.

These small words carry huge amounts of meaning — get them slightly wrong and you say something you didn't mean at all.
Today: precise quantifiers — most / all / none (and when they need "of the"), amounts like plenty of / a great deal of / a large number of / hardly any, and every / each / both / either / neither — so you can talk exactly about groups, amounts, and pairs, instead of guessing.

Most, All, None

The words that describe a group — and the tiny rule that decides whether "of" belongs.

Spot the pattern

  • "Most people don't like getting up early."
  • "Most of the people in my office cycle to work."
  • "None of my neighbours have met each other."

Two of these talk about people in general; one talks about a specific, known group. Which is which, and what changes in the grammar? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
"Most people" talks about people everywhere — general, no specific group, so no "of". "Most of the people in my office" and "none of my neighbours" both name a specific, known group — a particular office, particular neighbours — so "of" appears, followed by something definite: "the", "my", "these", or a pronoun like "them"/"us". "Of" is never followed directly by a bare plural noun.
most / all / none + plural noun  (general — no "of")
most / all / none + OF + the / my / these / us / them  (specific, known group)
One trap: "of" is never followed straight by a bare noun. "All of shops were shut for the holiday" ✗ — you need either "all shops were shut" or "all of the shops were shut".

Quick check

1. You and seven friends run a book club. Six of the eight finished this month's book. Talk about that specific group with most + of + them:

finished before the meeting.

2. Now make a general comment, true of people everywhere — not one particular group:

enjoy a lie-in at the weekend if they can get one.

3. You personally know all six candidates who applied for a job. Not one of them got an interview. Say this with none:

got an interview, which surprised everyone.

Now say it

SPEAK

Out loud: say three true things about people in general, starting with "most people..." Then say three things about a specific group you actually know — your family, your colleagues, your class — starting with "most of them...", "all of them...", or "none of them...".

How Much, How Many

Matching the right amount-word to countable things you can number, and uncountable things you can't.

Spot the pattern

  • "There's plenty of time before the train — relax."
  • "A great deal of planning went into that wedding."
  • "A large number of parking tickets were issued that week."

One of these amount-words works for almost anything. The other two are pickier. Which noun in each sentence is countable (you could count them one by one), and which is uncountable? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
"Plenty of" is relaxed — it works with countable and uncountable nouns alike (plenty of time, plenty of seats). "Planning" is uncountable, and "a great deal of" only ever combines with uncountable nouns (money, effort, planning). "Parking tickets" is countable and plural, and "a large number of" only combines with countable nouns you could count one by one.
plenty of + countable or uncountable noun
a great deal of + uncountable noun only
a large number of + countable plural noun only
hardly any + countable or uncountable noun  (= almost none)
One trap: "hardly any" already means "almost none" — never add another negative. "There's hardly no time to finish this" ✗ → "There's hardly any time to finish this" ✓.

Quick check

1. Your fridge is nearly empty — just a couple of drops left. Use hardly any:

There's milk left — we need to shop today.

2. Everyone on the team stayed late all week to get the office ready for a move. "Effort" is uncountable — use a great deal of:

went into getting the office ready in time.

3. After a flight was delayed six hours, the airline's phones didn't stop ringing. "Complaints" is countable — use a large number of:

The airline received that day.

Now say it

SPEAK

Out loud, tell me: something there's plenty of in your life right now, something there's hardly any of, and one thing that took a great deal of effort recently.

Every, Each, Both, Either, Neither

Talking about a whole group at once, one member at a time, or exactly two things.

Spot the pattern

  • "Every flat in the building has a balcony."
  • "Each guest gets a different seat, so check your name card."
  • "Neither of my brothers can cook."

One of these treats the group as a single whole. One focuses on individuals, one by one. One is about exactly two people. Which is which? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
"Every flat" states a general rule about the whole building, no exceptions — every always takes a singular noun straight after it, never "of". "Each guest" zooms in on individuals one at a time — each can also take "of" before a specific group: each of the guests. "Neither of my brothers" is only possible with exactly two people; both/either/neither all work this way — both (the two together), either (one of two), neither (not one of two).
every + singular noun  (no "of" — general rule, whole group)
each (of the) + singular noun  (one at a time, individual attention)
both / either / neither (of the / us / them)  (exactly two things)
One trap: "every" never takes "of". "Every of the shops closed early" ✗ → "Each of the shops closed early" ✓.

Quick check

1. You're stating a rule that's true of a whole hotel, no exceptions — speaking about the group as a whole. Use every + room:

in this hotel has sea views, without exception.

2. A tutor wants to speak to students one at a time, not as a group, giving individual feedback. Use each:

student gets five minutes alone with the tutor after the test.

3. You asked two university friends to lend you a printer. Both said they didn't have one. Use neither + of + them:

had a printer to spare.

Now say it

Out loud: say one rule that's true for every day of your week, describe a task where you dealt with people each one at a time, and tell me — of your two closest friends, does either of them, neither of them, or do both of them live near you?

What Do You Remember?

Quick recall first

1. Fill the gap with some or any: "Is there milk left in the fridge?"

2. Fill the gap with much or many: "How time do we have before the meeting?"

Say first, then check — fresh situations, no looking back

3. You've served hundreds of tourists at the shop this summer, but you don't know them personally — speak generally, no "of":

tourists just want quick directions — they're not looking for a long chat.

4. Your street recycled far more than usual this week. "Bottles" is countable — use a large number of:

We recycled this week — the bin was much heavier than usual.

jobs pay well, so the decision isn't really about money.
