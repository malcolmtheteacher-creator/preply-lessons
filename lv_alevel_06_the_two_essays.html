Watch the trap of this paper: essay one overruns and essay two starves. The two essays are worth the same 30 marks. Set the halfway line before you start and obey it like a referee.

The 12-minute annotation method for a PAIR

Sweep each text twice, fast, pen in hand:

SweepHunting for
1 · Featureslexis, grammar, sounds/graphology, discourse — name each find in the margin
2 · Person + eraidentity claims (Section A) or era markers (Section B); context facts; the audience

Then the move that decides your grade: draw the lines between the texts. Literally — connect a feature in one text to its partner or opposite in the other. Every line you draw is a paragraph. Four good lines = a plan.

Which plan wins?

Plan A: 1. Text 1 lexis 2. Text 1 grammar 3. Text 2 lexis 4. Text 2 grammar 5. Comparison
Plan B: 1. How each performs authority (both texts) 2. Register and mode contrasts (both) 3. What the audiences are given to do (both) 4. The continuity nobody expects (both)
SPEAK Say exactly why Plan B beats Plan A — use the phrase "mark strand" in your answer.
Plan A quarantines the texts, so the connections mark strand starves until paragraph 5 — a quarter of the marks resting on one paragraph. Plan B feeds that strand in every paragraph because each angle already contains both texts.

The comparative paragraph engine

1 · Claim tied to the question focus 2 · Evidence from text one 3 · Hinge 4 · Evidence from text two 5 · Concept + land on the focus

"[1] Both writers turn self-mockery into trust, [2] the memoirist demoting his own body to 'the thing you are dragging around the city' in mock-contempt, [3] while [4] the musician's 'knackered old thing… drove everybody mental' aims the same affectionate scorn at her past self — [5] in each idiolect, the laugh is the license for the emotion that follows, and the identity conveyed is precisely the kind that refuses to take itself seriously."

One paragraph, five parts, the hinge in the middle like a spine. Practise until the shape is muscle memory.

Engine practice — aloud

Angle: HOW EACH TEXT USES NUMBERS. Data: the 1790s text's "First… Secondly… Thirdly"; the habits talk's "two press-ups (.) two! (.) two minutes a day". Build the five parts out loud, then compare.

"[1] Both advisers structure persuasion numerically, [2] the 1790s writer marshalling duty into the sequenced 'First… Secondly… Thirdly' of the pulpit, [3] whereas [4] the modern speaker hammers a single small number — 'two press-ups (.) two!' — until repetition makes it a slogan; [5] the numbered list survives two centuries of informalisation intact, but its unit has shrunk from a life's duties to a two-minute habit, a miniature of the authority migration itself."

Weak v strong — same knowledge, different level

Two candidates compare the resignation pair from Lesson 1 ("I tender my resignation" vs "I walked in and went (.) yeah I'm done"):

Answer 1: "Text 1 is formal and uses formal words like 'tender my resignation'. Text 2 is informal and has pauses and doesn't use proper sentences. This shows that writing is more formal than speaking. Also Text 2 uses accommodation."
Answer 2: "The letter's performative formula 'I tender my resignation' adopts the frozen register the workplace demands — a professional self constructed in a single verb — while the spoken account rebuilds the same event for a friend through false starts and reported speech: 'I walked in and went (.) yeah I'm done'. The contrast is not simply formal against informal but one speaker style-shifting between audiences, managing two registers as two faces of one identity; the 'improper' spoken grammar is the intimacy, exactly as the formula is the professionalism."
SPEAK Find at least four upgrades between the answers — then check the examiner-eye view.
Answer 1: features listed not analysed · "proper sentences" sneers at speech (rule 2 broken) · concept name-dropped with no evidence · comparison stated as a banality (writing more formal than speech). Answer 2: quotes worked hard · the hinge inside the sentence · register and style-shifting welded to evidence · non-standard speech framed as intimacy · lands on identity. Same data, two levels apart.

Upgrade drill

Rewrite Answer 1's last sentence ("Also Text 2 uses accommodation") into welded exam voice. Type, then compare.

"Speaking to a friend, she converges toward the register of the friendship — clipped, dramatised, unguarded — exactly as accommodation theory predicts: the listener shapes the language, and the shaping is visible in every pause."

A full answer's skeleton

Intros and conclusions: short, functional, comparative from the first line. Model Section A intro:

"These two texts build identity in opposite modes toward the same end. The written memoir engineers a speaking voice from graphology and register collision, while the podcast transcript captures an unedited voice whose dialect is its signature; both, however, are performances of authenticity for an audience. This response compares how each constructs its speaker across register, mode, audience and self-presentation."

Three sentences: both texts, a contrast, a similarity, and a map of angles. The close mirrors it in two sentences — the strongest single connection restated with your best pair of quotes.

Timed flight

Fresh pair. Question: Analyse and compare how the writer in Text 1 and the speaker in Text 2 use language to convey personal and social identity.

Text 1 — from a food blog by a retired steelworker:

People ask why a man who spent thirty years in a steel mill now writes about sourdough. Simple. Both are about heat, timing and respect for the material. Get any of the three wrong and you have scrap — or, worse, ciabatta. My crumb shots (that's bread photography, keep up) now get more likes than my grandson's holiday selfies. He is NOT happy. Verdict from the mill lads at the reunion: soft lad bakes now. Correct. Softest crumb in Yorkshire.
