IB LESSON 5 · COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS

Comparing, Not Stapling

Making two pieces of evidence talk to each other

The stapling problem

Most students, when asked to compare two texts (or two moments in one text), do this: paragraph about Text A… then paragraph about Text B… then a summary sentence. That is not comparison. That is stapling two descriptions together and hoping the reader spots the relationship.

IB Criterion B rewards integrated comparison — where the two texts sit inside the same sentences, arguing with each other. The connector words are the hinges.

Stapled (Band 3–4)

Baldwin uses a box metaphor to describe the Pandora myth. He says it is heavy and sealed. This shows that myths are old.

Orwell ends Animal Farm by saying pigs and men look the same. This shows that revolutions fail.

Both writers use metaphor to make a point about history.

What is wrong: the texts never touch. The closing sentence is a wish, not an argument. Nothing is actually compared.

Integrated (Band 6–7)

Where Baldwin reaches for a single closed image — a box that cannot be emptied — Orwell reaches for a mirrored one, the pigs and men whose faces have merged. Both writers refuse to give the reader a clean exit: Baldwin's reader is left holding an inheritance; Orwell's, failing to tell one face from another. The difference lies in weight: Baldwin's image is inherited from the past, whereas Orwell's has been manufactured in front of us.

Why it works: every sentence contains both texts. The connector words ("where... Orwell", "both", "the difference lies in", "whereas") are doing the argumentative work.
The rule: if you can delete Text B's sentences and the paragraph still makes sense, you have stapled. Integration means the paragraph falls apart without both.

When does this matter for IB?

In Paper 1 (unseen text analysis), you are comparing two moments in the same text. In Paper 2 (literature comparison), you are comparing two whole works. The move is the same: make the two pieces talk to each other in every sentence.

The Connector Toolkit

Comparison lives in the connector words. Stop writing "Also, in Text B…" — that is a stapler sound. Start writing with phrases that do actual work.

🤝 Showing similarity with depth

Not just "both" — "both" + what they share.

Both writers… though through different means Both writers strand the reader at the edge of understanding, though through different means.
A similar move is made by… A similar withholding is made by Orwell, whose final line refuses to tell us which figure is which.
Like X, Y also… Like Baldwin, Orwell treats symbol as inheritance rather than ornament.
In each case… In each case, the reader is positioned as a witness unable to intervene.

⚖️ Showing difference with precision

"Different" is the weakest comparative word in English. Use these instead.

Where X does A, Y does B Where Baldwin reaches for a closed image, Orwell reaches for a mirrored one.
Whereas / while Whereas Baldwin's metaphor is still, Orwell's is kinetic — faces scanning faces.
By contrast… By contrast, Orwell's violence is administrative; Baldwin's is inherited.
The difference lies in… The difference lies in weight: Baldwin's image carries centuries; Orwell's, minutes.

🔄 Turning on the hinge

Phrases that set up similarity first, then pivot to difference (or vice versa) inside one sentence.

Both X and Y, yet… Both Baldwin and Orwell trap the reader, yet one seals the exit while the other dissolves it.
Similarly… but where… Similarly, Orwell makes his reader complicit, but where Baldwin does so through inheritance, Orwell does so through perception.
If X is A, then Y is its B If Baldwin's box is the past refusing to empty, Orwell's mirror is the present refusing to resolve.
What X makes static, Y makes… What Baldwin makes weighty and still, Orwell makes endlessly scanning.

🏗️ Zooming out together

For the evaluation slot — when you want to name a pattern that holds across both texts.

Together, these choices… Together, these choices suggest that myth and history both resist the reader's wish to be free of them.
Read side by side… Read side by side, the two passages reveal a shared distrust of clean endings.
The two passages converge on… The two passages converge on a single demand: that the reader refuse to look away.
What unites them is… What unites them is a refusal to let the reader finish the text with their conscience intact.
Band-7 pattern: use three different connector moves across one paragraph — one for similarity, one for difference, one for zoom-out. That single mix is what separates a 6 from a 7.

An integrated PACER paragraph — colour-coded

Same five slots from Lesson 4. Two texts. Every sentence contains both — linked by a connector.

Text A: Baldwin, "The Creative Process" Text B: Orwell, Animal Farm closing chapter
P: Both Baldwin and Orwell refuse to let the reader leave their texts clean-handed — though through very different temporal strategies.

A: Baldwin's extended metaphor of a sealed box reframes myth as a weight inherited across generations, stretching its reach across time with the phrase "long before any of us were born".

C: By contrast, Orwell compresses his indictment into a single final clause — "already it was impossible to say which was which" — operating not across centuries but within the reader's own act of looking.

E: Together, these two temporal scales — Baldwin's inherited past and Orwell's collapsing present — expose a shared conviction: that the moral cost of history is borne by whoever is still paying attention.

R: Read side by side, the texts leave the reader positioned as both heir and eyewitness — complicit in the past Baldwin cannot discard, and in the present Orwell refuses to resolve.

What this paragraph is doing that staples are not

  1. Every sentence has both texts in it (or a connector word referring to both). No slot is "about one author only".
  2. The connectors do argumentative work: "by contrast", "together", "read side by side" are not filler — they name the move.
  3. The E slot finds a pattern that neither text produces alone. "The moral cost of history is borne by whoever is still paying attention" — that claim requires both texts to hold it up.
  4. The R slot gives the reader a double role. Not "heir OR witness" — heir AND witness.

A second model — two moments in one text

The same move works if you are comparing two moments within a single passage (Paper 1).

P: Orwell opens the final chapter with familiarity and closes it with estrangement — two moves that, together, reverse the reader's relationship to the farm.

A: The early line "Years passed. The seasons came and went, the short animal lives fled by" uses temporal compression to reassure, whereas the closing clause "already it was impossible to say which was which" uses spatial collapse to unsettle.

C: Where the opening lulls with rhythm, the ending withholds distinction — the same prose surface delivers two opposite readerly experiences.

E: Together, these bookends produce the chapter's real argument: that the hardest thing to notice is the slow exchange of one world for another.

R: Read against each other, the two moments leave the reader retroactively complicit — the peace of the opening was never peace, only the silence before recognition.
Self-test: count how many sentences in your last essay contain both texts. If the answer is < 3 per paragraph, you are stapling.

Rebuild a stapled paragraph

Below are two stapled paragraphs. Your job: rewrite them as one integrated PACER paragraph where every sentence contains both texts.

Text A: Baldwin extract Text B: Orwell extract
Baldwin: "The myth sat on the shelf like a box no one would open, heavy with what had been put inside it long before any of us were born."
Orwell: "The creatures outside looked from pig to man, and from man to pig, and from pig to man again; but already it was impossible to say which was which."

The stapled draft (what a Band-4 essay would do)

Baldwin uses the metaphor of a sealed box to describe myth. He makes the myth sound heavy and old. This shows that cultural stories stay with us.

Orwell uses the image of pigs and men to show the reader that the animals have become like the humans. His sentence is confusing on purpose.

Both writers use images to make a point about society. They show how things do not really change.

Your task: rewrite this as one integrated paragraph, using PACER slots. Aim for 5–6 sentences. Every sentence should include something about both writers or use a connector word that pulls them together.

P — Position that holds across both texts

Start with something like "Both writers…" or "Where X…, Y…"

A — First choice (from Text A) named with an effect

Name Baldwin's choice and what it does to the reader.

C — Turn the hinge — the same move in Text B

Use a connector phrase ("by contrast", "where... does X, Y does...", "a similar move is made by...").

E — Zoom out — the pattern both share

"Together, these choices..." or "Read side by side..."

R — Reader's new position, holding both texts at once

The reader is not "left thinking about both" — they are positioned in some specific way.

Model answer

P: Both Baldwin and Orwell refuse to grant the reader a clean exit from their text — though one writer seals the way with inheritance and the other dissolves it through perception.

A: Baldwin's sealed-box metaphor reframes myth as a weight that arrived "long before any of us were born", placing the reader inside a history they did not choose.

C: Where Baldwin loads the reader with the past, Orwell empties the reader of judgement in the present — his chiastic "pig to man, and from man to pig" mimics the animals' scanning gaze and implicates the reader in the same failure of distinction.

E: Together, inheritance and indistinguishability produce a shared argument: that the price of moral comfort is paid by whoever stops paying attention.

R: Read side by side, the two passages leave the reader positioned as both heir and eyewitness — complicit in a past they cannot discard and a present they cannot clarify.

What to check in yours: count the times each text appears. Aim for both to appear in at least 3 of your 5 sentences.

A faster drill — one-sentence comparison

Write one sentence that contains both quotes above, using a connector from Tab 1.

Three sample sentences

1. Where Baldwin's "box no one would open" holds the reader inside an inherited history, Orwell's "impossible to say which was which" strands them inside an immediate one.

2. Both writers refuse the reader a settled view: Baldwin by making the past heavy, Orwell by making the present blurred.

3. If Baldwin's image seals the reader in, Orwell's melts the wall between oppressor and oppressed — in each case, the reader's position is the point of the writing.

Integration Cheat Card

The staple test

After you draft a paragraph, ask: "Can I delete every sentence about Text B and still have a coherent paragraph about Text A?"
If YES → stapled. Rewrite using connectors.
If NO → integrated. You are in Band 6+ territory.

The 4 connector moves you must have in your essay

MovePhraseUse when…
Pair Both X and Y… You are asserting a shared move
Pivot Where X… , Y… You are naming a difference with precision
Hinge If X is A, Y is its B You are setting up similarity-with-a-twist
Zoom Together / Read side by side… You are naming a pattern that requires both

Phrases to retire

  • "On the other hand…" → replace with "Where… / whereas… / by contrast…"
  • "Similarly, in Text B…" → replace with "A similar move is made by… / Like X, Y also…"
  • "Both writers show…" → replace with "Both writers refuse / foreground / unsettle…" (use an effect verb from Lesson 3)
  • "This is the same…" → replace with "This parallels X's… / echoes X's…"

The 30-second plan before a comparative paragraph

  1. Pick one specific move that both texts make (e.g. both strand the reader, both use temporal compression, both critique their narrator).
  2. Find one quote from each text that shows that move.
  3. Decide: is the relationship pair, pivot, or hinge?
  4. Open with P (the shared move). Use C to turn to the second text. Use E to name what only both texts together reveal.
