The problem writing has to solve

A speaker gets free identity tools: accent, volume, pace, laughter, a listening face to play against. A writer gets 26 letters and some dots. Yet you can pick up two paragraphs by two strangers and instantly feel two different PEOPLE. Everything that produces that feeling was engineered — and Section A rewards you for reverse-engineering it.

SPEAK Prediction: list every trick you can think of that writing uses to fake a voice — ways to "shout", to whisper an aside, to grin. Aim for five.
CAPITALS and italics for volume and stress · brackets for muttered asides · exclamation marks and question marks for intonation · one-word sentences for comic timing · spelling twisted to capture noises (argh, pfff) · direct address to conjure a listener. If you got five, you already own half of today.

The machinery — six tools with names

1 · Graphology — meaning through appearance
Capitals as shouting, brackets as asides, exclamatives in titles, invented spellings for noises. Whenever a page LOOKS unusual, the look is data.
2 · Register shifting
Formal register colliding with chatty register inside one text — jargon in a joke, slang in a serious sentence. The collision is nearly always the writer's identity showing: two worlds, one person.
3 · Minor sentences
Verbless one-worders — Gone. Fact. Never again. — the written equivalent of a comedian's pause-and-stare. Emphasis by starvation.
4 · Simile and allusion
Comparisons that reach for a SHARED shelf — films, games, childhood brands. The chosen reference points at the intended audience: quote a superhero film and you have declared which generation you are talking to.
5 · Semantic fields
Word families that reveal a life: medical vocabulary in a parenting memoir, kitchen vocabulary in a love letter. Two fields tangled together = two parts of a life tangled together.
6 · Direct address + imperatives
"You" summons a reader; commands turn a memoir into advice. Both build a relationship — and defining WHO the imagined "you" is scores context marks.

The extract

From a comic memoir about running a first marathon at 49, in a chapter addressed to other reluctant middle-aged runners:

Nobody tells you the truth about marathons, so I will. Mile 18 is where your soul leaves your body and watches you, disappointed, from a folding chair. Fact. The books all promise a "wall" — singular. There are nine.

I trained for eight months with an app whose robot voice I came to loathe like a Bond villain. My physio spoke of "proprioception" and "glycogen depletion"; I spoke of my knees making the noise a stapler makes when it runs out. We understood each other eventually.

At mile 24 a stranger in a dinosaur costume handed me half a banana, and I am not ashamed to say I nearly wept. You WILL cry at something ridiculous. Allow it. The thing you are dragging around the city — the slow, complaining, 49-year-old thing — turns out to be capable of miracles, provided you feed it bananas.

PS Vaseline. Everywhere. Trust me.

First sweep — find one of each

a) A graphological shout and a muttered aside

"You WILL cry" — capitals as volume. Asides: the dashes around "— the slow, complaining, 49-year-old thing —" work like brackets, a self-mocking whisper mid-sentence.

b) A minor sentence used as a gavel

"Fact." — and the PS runs on minor sentences too: "Vaseline. Everywhere. Trust me." Comic timing by full stop.

c) A register collision

The physio's clinical lexis ("proprioception", "glycogen depletion") against the stapler joke — expert register meets kitchen-drawer simile, and the writer's identity lives exactly in that gap.

d) A depersonalising move with an emotional job

"The thing you are dragging around the city" — the writer's own body demoted to "thing", mock-contempt that flips into tenderness ("capable of miracles"). When a writer depersonalises what they love, the pronoun is doing emotional work — always analyse it.

e) The audience, precisely defined

"Nobody tells you the truth… so I will" + advice imperatives ("Allow it", "Trust me") + the self-deprecating age jokes: the imagined "you" is a fellow reluctant midlife runner. Naming the audience that exactly is a context mark.

Deep dig — from feature to identity

Features alone are Level 2. The question asks what the language conveys about identity — so every feature needs a second sentence: what does it make of the person? Practise the two-step aloud:

1. "Mile 18 is where your soul leaves your body and watches you, disappointed, from a folding chair." — feature, then identity.

Feature: surreal extended image + personification of the soul (that judgemental folding chair). Identity: someone who processes suffering by making it absurd — self-mockery as a defining trait of the idiolect, and an implicit promise to the reader: this guide will never be worthy or preachy.

2. The Bond-villain simile plus the dinosaur-costume stranger — what do the REFERENCES tell you, before the jokes even land?

The shared shelf: Bond films, charity-run culture, fitness apps. Generational and social coordinates — a middle-aged, pop-culture-fluent, app-owning amateur. Allusions are demographic data; the writer signals a tribe with every reference chosen.

3. Track the text's TWO semantic fields and say what their tangling means.

Sports-science lexis (glycogen, physio, training) tangled with domestic-comic lexis (stapler, bananas, folding chair, Vaseline). A life where expertise arrived late and uninvited — the amateur body colliding with professional knowledge — told entirely through vocabulary choice.
SPEAK The two-step is the whole skill: name it, then ask what it makes of the person. Pick any feature we have NOT discussed and run the two-step aloud on it now.

Exam voice: the written-text paragraph

A model paragraph on the extract — read it, then dissect:

"The writer constructs his identity through controlled collisions of register. His physio's clinical lexis — 'proprioception', 'glycogen depletion' — sits beside the domestic simile of knees like 'a stapler when it runs out', the expert and amateur worlds meeting in one sentence exactly as they met in his life. Graphological choices supply the voice print lacks: the capitalised 'You WILL cry' shouts, while the minor-sentence postscript — 'Vaseline. Everywhere.' — times its comedy with full stops. Most revealingly, he demotes his own body to 'the thing you are dragging around the city' before crowning it 'capable of miracles': mock-contempt pivoting to tenderness, a self-deprecating idiolect that lets an emotional truth land only after a laugh. The imagined reader — addressed as 'you', advised in imperatives — is a fellow midlife runner, making the memoir double as a survival manual for a very specific tribe."

Dissect aloud: count the quotes, name every term, find the identity claim each feature serves, and identify the audience sentence.

Your turn

Write one paragraph on a feature-set the model left alone (the surreal soul image, the banana miracle, the nine walls…). Two quotes minimum, two terms, the two-step.

"The writer treats suffering through absurdist imagery: at mile 18 the soul 'watches you, disappointed, from a folding chair' — personification staged with a prop — while the books' promised 'wall' is corrected, with deadpan precision, to 'nine'. The hyperbole works as identity: pain is real but pomposity is forbidden, so the memoir's advice ('Allow it') can be trusted precisely because its author refuses to be a hero."

Recall zone

From Lesson 2: a streamer's "GG"/"nerfed" vocabulary — which concept, and why not register?

Sociolect — a group's shared lexicon signalling membership. Register is about formality level, which is not what that vocabulary marks.

From Lesson 1: the three comparison hinge shapes.

Contrast (While X…, Y…) · similarity (Both… though differently) · pivot (X does A; Y answers it).

2. What is the two-step, and why does step one alone cap your marks?

Name the feature, then say what it makes of the PERSON. Step one alone is feature-spotting — description, not analysis of identity, which is what the question asks.

Every reference assumes a reader who shares the shelf — so the chosen references reveal the writer's generation, culture and intended tribe.
