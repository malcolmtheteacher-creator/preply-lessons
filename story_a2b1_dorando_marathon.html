The Last Lap

In 1908 a small Italian runner came into the Olympic stadium in first place. Then something went wrong — and the world never forgot it.

Today: you'll follow a true race story and tell your own stories with the past simple and past continuous, so you can say what happened and what was happening around it.

Dorando Pietri and the 1908 Olympic Marathon

Read each part. Answer the quick check from memory, then discuss.

Part 1 — A hot afternoon

On 24 July 1908, the Olympic Games were in London. The marathon was starting. The race began at Windsor Castle and finished in a new stadium. The route was about 42 kilometres — 26 miles and 385 yards. After this race, people used the same distance for every marathon.

More than fifty runners started the race. It was a very hot day. Many runners went too fast at the beginning. Later they were tired and slow. Some stopped at the side of the road, and ambulances took them away.

One runner was Dorando Pietri. He was twenty-two and from a small town in Italy called Carpi. He worked in a sweet shop. He was small and not famous. He did not run at the front. He ran at a steady pace. While the other runners were getting tired, he was moving past them, one by one.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Where did the race start and finish?

2. What happened to many runners? Why?

3. How did Dorando run? What was he doing while the others were getting tired?

1. It started at Windsor Castle and finished in a stadium in London.

2. Many went too fast at the beginning, so they became tired and slow in the heat. Some stopped.

3. He ran at a steady pace. He was passing the other runners one by one.

Discuss:
  • What is better in a long race — a fast start or a slow, steady start? What about in other things, like study or work?

Part 2 — The stadium

Dorando came into the stadium in the lead, in first place. About 75,000 people were waiting, and they were shouting and cheering. But Dorando looked terrible. He had no energy left.

Then he turned the wrong way. Some officials ran to him and showed him the right way. A few steps later, his legs stopped working and he collapsed. He got up and staggered forward a little more. Then he fell again. Most people say he fell five times in the last part of the race.

The doctors and officials ran to him. The crowd was shouting. Someone took his arm and helped him stand. With people on both sides, Dorando walked slowly to the finishing line. He crossed it in 2 hours 54 minutes. The stadium was roaring.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Where was Dorando when he came into the stadium?

2. What mistake did he make? What did the officials do?

3. How did he cross the finishing line?

1. He was in first place, and 75,000 people were cheering.

2. He turned the wrong way. The officials showed him the right way.

3. People helped him. They held him on both sides and he walked slowly to the line.

Discuss:
  • You are standing next to Dorando. Do you help him? Why or why not?

Part 3 — The decision

The crowd thought Dorando was the winner. But very soon the American team made a protest. Their runner, Johnny Hayes, came in just after Dorando. The Americans said: “People helped Dorando. That is against the rules.”

The judges agreed. They disqualified Dorando, and Johnny Hayes got the gold medal.

But that was not the end of the story. Everyone in London talked about Dorando. The newspapers wrote about him, and the famous writer Arthur Conan Doyle described the race. The next day, Queen Alexandra gave Dorando a special silver cup. Someone wrote a song about him. Later, he ran in exhibition races in other countries.

The rule was clear, and Hayes kept his gold medal. But today, more than a hundred years later, many people remember Dorando, not Hayes.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the American team protest?

2. What did the judges decide?

3. What happened to Dorando after the race?

1. They said people helped Dorando to finish, and this was against the rules.

2. They disqualified Dorando. Johnny Hayes got the gold medal.

3. He became famous. The Queen gave him a silver cup, people wrote about him and a song was written about him.

Discuss:
  • The judges followed the rules, but people remember Dorando. Why do you think that happened?

Grammar — Past Simple and Past Continuous

To tell a good story you need two kinds of past: what happened (the events) and what was happening (the scene around the events). Look at the story.

1 · Past simple — the events, one after another. “Dorando came into the stadium. He turned the wrong way. He fell.” Each verb is a new step in the story.
2 · Past continuous — the scene, what was happening. Form: was / were + verb-ing. “75,000 people were cheering.” “The crowd was shouting.”
3 · Together. A long action (past continuous) and a short event (past simple): “While the leaders were getting tired, Dorando passed them.” “Someone took his arm while he was lying on the track.”
4 · Time words. while + past continuous (the long action). when + past simple (the short event): “When he entered the stadium, the people were cheering.”
5 · Be careful. We do not use the past continuous for a finished series of events. He was falling five times. → “He fell five times.” And verbs like know and want are usually past simple: “He wanted to finish.”

1. Past simple or past continuous?

Put the verb in brackets in the correct form. Read the whole sentence first.

1. The sun (shine) and it was very hot when the race started.

2. Dorando (come) into the stadium, and the people (cheer).

3. While the doctors (help) him, the crowd (shout).

4. He (fall) five times before he reached the line.

1. was shining (a scene, in the background)

Going deeper — the rule, the numbers and the language

This story is not only about one runner. It asks a question about fairness. It also explains the distance of the races people run today.

Where do 42 km and 21 km come from?

After the 1908 Games, the marathon distance became 42.195 kilometres — about the same as the route from Windsor Castle to the stadium. A half marathon is half of this: 21.0975 kilometres, or 13.1 miles.

Today the half marathon is one of the most popular races in the world. Many ordinary people train for a few months and run it.

1. How long is a half marathon in kilometres?

2. Which race made the marathon distance famous?

3. Say it in the past simple: “Dorando falls.”

1. About 21 kilometres (21.0975 km, or 13.1 miles).

2. The 1908 Olympic marathon in London.

3. Dorando fell.

Was it fair?

People still talk about the decision. Both sides have a good reason.

He should loseThe rule was clear: no help. Johnny Hayes ran the whole race alone. If helped runners can win, the race is not fair.
He should winHe was first to the line. The officials wanted to protect him. He did not ask for help, so it is not fair to punish him.

Talking about a race

These phrases are useful for any race. Complete each sentence, then reveal.

2. I ___ a personal best today. (made / set)

1. off — “set off”.

2. set — “I set a personal best”.

3. hanging on — meaning “I was only just able to continue”.
