2. Murlock lived a solitary life; for days at a time, he saw no other person.

The story, in four short movements

This is an adaptation for B2/C1. Read one movement at a time. After each, close the text with your hand or scroll past it, then answer from memory. The final evidence stays hidden until you make a prediction.

1 · The cabin with one blind eye

In the early years of the American frontier, a man called Murlock built a rough cabin deep in the forest. The nearest settlement was several miles away. His home stood in a small clearing, surrounded by trees that pressed close on every side.

Years later, hunters sometimes passed the place and found Murlock sitting outside. Although he was only about fifty, he appeared much older. His hair and beard were long and grey, his back was bent, and his eyes seemed to look beyond the people who spoke to him. He answered politely but rarely began a conversation.

The cabin had a door and a single window. The door worked. The window did not. Heavy wooden boards covered it from the outside, and nobody could remember when Murlock had put them there. It made the house look as if it had one blind eye.

When Murlock eventually died, the settlement buried him near the cabin. The building slowly collapsed into the forest. Only the story of the boarded window remained.

Memory check — answer before revealing:

1. Which three details make Murlock appear older than he is?

2. What is physically unusual about the cabin?

1. His long grey hair and beard, bent back, and distant-looking eyes.

2. Its only window has been covered completely with boards.

One sentence only: What kind of event could make a man preserve a broken window for the rest of his life?

2 · Before the boards

When he first came to the forest, Murlock was young, strong and hopeful. He did not come alone. His wife shared the hard work of making a life there: planting, cooking, repairing clothes and keeping the cabin alive through winter. The two of them seemed likely to grow old together in that clearing.

Then his wife developed a fever. There was no doctor nearby and no road that could bring help quickly. Murlock did what little he knew, but her breathing became weaker. After several days she lay completely still. He called her name and received no answer. Her skin felt cold. She appeared to have died.

Murlock's grief did not come out as tears. It seemed to empty him. He moved around the cabin mechanically, preparing her body for burial. He washed her face, closed her eyes and placed her on the table. Outside, the forest darkened.

He planned to make a coffin the next morning. Until then, he would keep vigil beside her.

Memory check — separate fact from interpretation:

1. What observations lead Murlock to his conclusion?

2. Which sentence tells us that the conclusion may not be certain?

1. She does not answer, does not move, feels cold and seems not to be breathing.

2. “She appeared to have died.” The verb appeared reports an impression, not a confirmed fact.

Evidence check: Is “appeared to have died” enough evidence? Name one alternative explanation and one thing Murlock could have checked.

3 · The sound in the dark

Murlock sat beside the table with his rifle close at hand. A candle burned between him and his wife. For hours nothing moved except the flame.

Sometime after midnight, exhaustion overcame him. His head fell forward and he slept.

He woke in complete darkness. The candle had gone out. At first he did not know what had disturbed him. Then he heard a heavy sound near the window — wood under pressure, followed by a low movement inside the room.

Something struck the table. Murlock heard feet scraping across the floor and a weight being dragged towards the window. He called his wife's name, but his own voice sounded unfamiliar.

Unable to see, he raised the rifle and fired towards the noise. The flash lit the cabin for less than a second. In that instant he saw a large animal at the broken window. Then the room was dark again.

A cry followed the shot. It seemed to contain both the voice of an animal and something human. Murlock recoiled against the wall. He remembered nothing more until morning.

4 · What the morning revealed

Sunlight entered through a new opening where the boards had been torn away. Near the window lay a panther, wounded by the shot. Murlock's wife was no longer on the table. The animal had dragged her across the floor.

Her body now showed injuries from the panther. That explained the movement in the darkness—but not the cry that had sounded partly human.

Then Murlock saw the decisive detail.

His wife's hands were closed tightly. Between her teeth was a piece of the panther's ear.

After the turn:

1. Which earlier details now change meaning?

2. Tell the final discovery in one sentence using turned out to have been.

1. Her stillness, the uncertain diagnosis, the dragging sound, and the cry that seemed partly human.

2. “The woman who had appeared to be dead turned out to have been alive.”

Grammar — from first impression to later truth

The story depends on the distance between what the evidence suggests and what later becomes known. English handles that distance with seem, appear and turn out.

seem / appear + to be

An impression about a state at the same time.

Murlock seemed to be calm.

impressionnow/then
seem / appear + to have + past participle

An impression now about an earlier event.

She appeared to have died.

earlier eventimpression
turn out + to be / to have been

The result or truth discovered later.

She turned out to have been alive.

earlier realitydiscovery

Notice the difference

Less precise

She seemed dead, but she was alive.

The meaning is clear, but the timing and discovery are flattened.

More precise

She appeared to have died, but turned out to have been alive.

The perfect infinitives place both states before the later discovery.

Going deeper — how Bierce turns evidence against us

The story is not simply hiding information. It teaches us to interpret each detail one way, then uses the final evidence to make us reinterpret it.

1 · The frame

The narrator begins long after the event, with an old man and an unexplained window. This plants a question before the central story begins.

2 · Restricted knowledge

We know only what Murlock can observe. Because he has no medical help, his conclusion feels reasonable but remains uncertain. Bierce limits our evidence.

3 · Sensory narrowing

When the candle goes out, sight disappears. Scraping, dragging and a cry replace it. Bierce narrows the scene to uncertain sounds.

4 · The decisive object

The fragment of the panther's ear is physical proof. One small object recontextualises the woman's stillness, the struggle and the cry.

plants a question limits our knowledge withholds confirmation narrows the scene recontextualises the evidence

Two interpretations, one ending

Interpretation A · Human error

The tragedy comes from isolation and limited knowledge. Murlock mistakes appearance for certainty because no help is available.

Interpretation B · Unbearable memory

The boarded window is a physical act of avoidance. Murlock cannot change the past, so he hides the place where the truth entered.

Write the hinge

Write 60–90 words explaining how the final detail changes the meaning of one earlier moment. Avoid summarising the whole plot; concentrate on the before-and-after interpretation.

At first, the cry appears to be an animal sound distorted by Murlock's fear. The final clue recontextualises it: the cry turns out to have come from a struggle in which his wife was conscious. Bierce therefore converts a vague Gothic noise into human evidence. What seemed supernatural becomes more terrible because it was physical, preventable and real.
