Working From Home

Two people, one good table. Your flat has quietly become an office for two — and nobody ever agreed to that. Time to build something you can both live with.

The Scene 🎬

You've worked from home for years now. You've got it down to a science: the kitchen table by the window, the good light, the chair that doesn't hurt your back by four o'clock. The flat has one properly good table, and for a long time it was, without anybody ever saying so, yours.

Then your flatmate Dani's company went hybrid. Two days a week at home. And suddenly the kitchen is a meeting room. There's a laptop where the fruit bowl used to be, a call happening on speaker while you're trying to think, and a strange new choreography where you both circle the table pretending not to want it. Last Tuesday you took a client call sitting on your bed, apologising for the noise.

Nobody has done anything wrong. Dani isn't inconsiderate — Dani genuinely hasn't noticed, because Dani is new to this and still just relieved not to be on the train. So this morning, kettle on, before either of you opens a laptop, you're going to say something. Not a complaint. Not a set of rules. A conversation about how two people share one table — and an arrangement you'll both agree to try, and then look at again.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Where do you actually work, or study, or think? Describe the spot. What makes it the good one — and who else in your life wants it?
  2. Think of something you share with somebody: a kitchen, a car, a screen, a fridge shelf. Was it ever discussed, or did it just quietly settle into a system? Is the system fair?

Who's who

You
The One Who Was Here First 💻

Five days a week at this table, and quietly territorial about it — though you'd never say so out loud. You want your working day back, but you'd rather have a good flatmate than a good table, and you know it.

The other voice
Dani, Newly Hybrid 📞

Two days a week at home and still slightly amazed by it. Not selfish, not stubborn — just hasn't clocked that the flat now holds two working lives. Say the word and Dani will happily rearrange everything.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how it's done, not just what's said. Nobody wins this conversation. Both of them offer something, neither of them makes a rule, and the whole thing ends with the words "let's try it" rather than "so that's agreed." That's the skill.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how every proposal arrives as a question, and how the ending stays deliberately open.

DaniMorning! Kettle's just boiled. I'm in today — I'll set up here, I've got a call at nine. You don't mind, do you?
YouCourse not. Actually — while you're standing there and neither of us has started yet. Can we work out a system for the days we're both here?
Dani…Oh God. Is this the speakerphone? It's the speakerphone, isn't it. I'm so sorry.
YouIt's genuinely not a telling-off. It's nobody's fault — the flat just wasn't built for two offices. It's one table and a kettle. Honestly, I'd rather sort it now than let it fester.
DaniOkay. Fair. Where do we even start with that?
YouWhat does your week actually look like? I've been guessing, and I think that's half the problem — I've been planning my days around a version of your job I've invented.
DaniTuesdays and Thursdays here. Tuesday's brutal — calls from ten till about four, barely a gap. Thursday's quiet, it's mostly just me writing things nobody reads.
YouRight, so it's really one loud day. Would it work if the table were yours on Tuesdays — the whole day, start to finish, no negotiating at eleven o'clock? I'd rather take the bedroom desk and keep the table free for your calls.
DaniYou'd do that? You're here five days. I feel like I've walked in and taken your office.
YouI'm easy either way, honestly. I just want the noisy day to have somewhere to live, and it can't be everywhere. The only thing I'd really need to protect is Thursday afternoon — that's when I do the work I can't do with a voice in the room.
DaniThursday afternoon's fine, that's my dull admin. I'll take the sofa, headphones on. Actual headphones. No speaker. I hear myself now.
YouThen that's most of it, isn't it. Can we try it for a couple of weeks and see how it feels? Nothing's fixed — if your Tuesdays move, or it turns out I hate that bedroom desk, we just change it.
DaniI like that a lot more than a rota stuck on the fridge.
YouSo do I. One thing, though — if it's not working, say. Don't just put up with it. Two weeks of you being quietly polite and me being quietly annoyed and we're right back at the speakerphone.
DaniDeal. Two weeks, then we compare notes over something stronger than tea. And I'll buy the headphones today, I promise.
YouBuy the good ones. Cheapest bit of furniture this flat will ever need.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Before proposing a single thing, one of them asks the other a question. What was the question — and why does asking it first change the whole conversation?

2. One of them offers to give up the good table — the better spot — and yet somehow doesn't sound like a martyr, and Dani doesn't end up owing anybody a favour. What two things do they do that keep it feeling level?

3. Nothing they agree is permanent. How do they make sure either of them can reopen it later without looking like they're complaining?

1. They ask what Dani's week actually looks like. Everything before that was guesswork — you can't design a system around a job you've invented. It also hands Dani a part in the solution: she supplies the facts, so the arrangement is half hers before anyone proposes anything.
2. First they say they're easy either way, so the offer sounds like a preference rather than a sacrifice. Then they immediately name one small thing of their own to protect — Thursday afternoon. That turns it into a trade, not a gift, and a trade doesn't leave anybody in debt.
3. They set it up as a two-week trial rather than a decision, and they say out loud that if it isn't working, the other person must say so. Reviewing it later is now part of the deal — so raising it isn't breaking the agreement, it's keeping it.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for the conversation where nobody is wrong and something still has to change. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. This conversation dies if it sounds like a complaint and lives if it sounds like problem-solving. The words alone won't save you: the same sentence, delivered with a sigh and a look at the ceiling, becomes an accusation. Keep your voice light and curious, as if you were solving a puzzle together rather than presenting a grievance. Lean towards them, not away. Let the questions sound like real questions — the ones you don't already know the answer to.

🤝 Opening it as a shared problem

Phrase 1

You want to open with something that makes this a joint project from the very first word — not your complaint, but our puzzle. Put the pieces in order:

a systemwe're both here?Can wefor the dayswork out
"Can we work out a system for the days we're both here?"
Every word is doing something. "Can we" asks permission and makes it two people. "Work out" is what you do with a puzzle, not an enemy — you work things out together. "A system" is neutral and practical; it isn't about anyone's behaviour. And "the days we're both here" names the problem as a scheduling fact, not a person.
Phrase 2

Your flatmate has gone straight to apologising, and now you're one wrong sentence away from a row about who's the bad flatmate. Which line takes the blame off the table entirely?

A. "Look, I'm not blaming you, but you have taken over the kitchen."
B. "It's nobody's fault — the flat just wasn't built for two offices."
C. "Honestly, it's fine. Forget I said anything."
B — "It's nobody's fault — the flat just wasn't built for two offices."
A says "I'm not blaming you" and then blames them — everything after "but" is the real sentence. C retreats, so the problem stays and now it's underground. B moves the blame onto the flat: a building can't be offended, and it genuinely is the thing at fault. Now you're both on one side, facing the same wall.
Phrase 3

Before you suggest anything at all, you need the facts — and you want to ask in a way that admits you've been guessing until now. Complete the chunk: "What does your week ___ ___ ___?"

"What does your week actually look like?"
The whole phrase turns on actually. Without it, it's an innocent question. With it, you're quietly confessing that you've been running on assumptions — and inviting the real answer, including the bits you'd never have guessed. It's also strategic: ask this before you propose, and their information ends up inside your proposal, which makes the proposal half theirs.

🪶 Proposing without insisting

Phrase 4

Now you've got an idea — but you want it to arrive as a suggestion someone can freely say no to, not a plan you've already decided on. Complete the chunk: "___ ___ ___ if you had the table to yourself on your busiest day?"

"Would it work if…?"
Compare it with "I think you should…" or "Let's just say that…". "Would it work" makes the arrangement the subject, not you — and it asks about their reality, not their agreement. It's an idea placed on the table rather than pushed across it. If they don't like it, nobody has to back down, because you never insisted in the first place.
Phrase 5

They're hesitating because they think you're being self-sacrificing. You want to show that you genuinely don't mind — both options are fine by you. Which line does that best?

A. "I don't mind. Whatever. You decide."
B. "I'm easy either way."
C. "Well, obviously I'd prefer one of them, but never mind."
B — "I'm easy either way."
A hands the whole decision over and sounds a bit flat, as if you've stopped caring. C is doing the opposite of what it says — it plants a preference and then sulks about it. B is the generous one: "easy" here means relaxed, not fussy, not going to make this hard. You're telling them the door is genuinely open both ways, which frees them to choose honestly instead of trying to read your face.
Phrase 6

Here's the move that makes the whole thing work: you offer up the good spot, and you say why it's the sensible answer rather than a noble one. Put the pieces in order:

free for your calls.I'd ratherand keep the tabletake the bedroom desk
"I'd rather take the bedroom desk and keep the table free for your calls."
"I'd rather" is the quiet genius here — it frames giving something up as your own preference. Not "I suppose I could", not "I'll have to". You want this. Nobody made you. And the reason is given as a practical one — the calls need the space — so the offer sounds like logistics rather than a favour. Favours have to be repaid; logistics don't.

⏳ Making it a trial, not a treaty

Phrase 7

You can feel it turning into A Rule, and rules are how flatmates end up hating each other. You want a version everyone can escape from. Complete the chunk: "Can we ___ ___ ___ a couple of weeks and see how it feels?"

"Can we try it for a couple of weeks and see how it feels?"
"Try" and "a couple of weeks" shrink the stakes to almost nothing — anyone will agree to two weeks of anything. But look at "see how it feels": you're not asking whether it works on paper, you're asking about the lived experience of it, which is the thing that actually decides these arrangements. And a trial has a built-in ending, so nobody's signing away their life.
Phrase 8

You've just given away the good table. Now — in your own words — name the one small thing you'd genuinely need kept safe: the two hours a week that make the difference between a good week and a bad one. Say it out loud, then type it.

e.g. "The only thing I'd really need to protect is Thursday afternoon — that's when I do the work I can't do with a voice in the room."
See how much is packed into "the only thing". It signals that your list of demands is exactly one item long — so whatever comes next will sound reasonable before they've even heard it. "Really need" separates this from everything you merely want. And the effect is a small trade: you gave up the table, you keep two hours. Concede generously, then ask for one thing, and you've built something level instead of something owed.
Phrase 9

The real danger isn't the arrangement failing — it's the other person being too polite to tell you. Give them explicit permission to reopen it. Put the pieces in order:

put up with it.say —If it's not working,don't just
"If it's not working, say — don't just put up with it."
To "put up with" something is to tolerate it in silence, month after month, while it slowly eats at you. The bare "say" — no "let me know", no "feel free to mention it" — is warm and direct, the way you'd talk to a friend. Crucially, this makes complaining pre-approved. You've agreed in advance that raising it is allowed, so when they do, they're keeping the deal, not breaking it.
Phrase 10

They look worried that this is A Big Serious Talk. You want to say the opposite: this is small precisely because you're doing it now, while it's still small. Which line lands that?

A. "We can always come back to it if it gets worse."
B. "Honestly, I'd rather sort it now than let it fester."
C. "I've been meaning to bring this up for about a month, actually."
B — "Honestly, I'd rather sort it now than let it fester."
A postpones it, which is how it gets worse. C accidentally reveals you've been silently annoyed for weeks — now they're rereading a month of breakfasts. B explains your timing and makes it a compliment: you're doing this today because the friendship is worth protecting.

A wound is said to fester when it's left uncleaned and quietly turns bad — that's the picture behind the word, and it's exactly right for a small resentment nobody ever mentioned. "I'd rather sort it now" is not an apology for raising it; it's the reason you did.

🔑 Make them yours

These phrases are not really about tables. They belong to every conversation where two reasonable people want incompatible things and nobody has done anything wrong. Pin three of them to your own life, so you'll reach for them when the moment arrives. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"It's nobody's fault — the ___ just wasn't built for ___." — a friction in your life that isn't anyone's fault, just a situation nobody designed. What is it, and what would you blame instead of the person?

"I'd rather take… and keep… free for you." — something you could genuinely offer to give up, if it bought you the thing you actually care about.

"Can we try it for a couple of weeks and see how it feels?" — a decision in your life that's currently stuck because it feels too permanent to make. How would you shrink it to a trial?

The Twist

🎥 The thing Dani hadn't mentioned

One more round — no script, no safety net. You've just built your neat little arrangement, and then Dani says, slightly embarrassed: "There's a thing I should probably tell you. My manager is… odd about backgrounds. She comments on everyone's. On camera it has to be the kitchen wall — the bedroom's got no light and my bed in the shot and I'd honestly rather not explain that to her every Tuesday." So the elegant solution you both just agreed doesn't survive its first contact with reality: the table isn't a preference for Dani, it's the only room with a face-shaped wall behind it. Start the timer and rebuild it — out loud, from nothing. Don't reach for the arrangement you already made; it's gone. Find the next one. One rule: whatever you land on, it still has to be something you'd both agree to test rather than declare — and you must still ask for one thing of your own.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I opened it as our problem — nobody had to be the one at fault.
  • I asked what their week was really like before I proposed anything.
  • Every suggestion I made left them free to say no without a fight.
  • I gave something up and asked for one thing back, so it stayed level.
  • We agreed to test it and look again — not to sign it into law.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

In the space of a few weeks, millions of people carried their working lives home in a laptop bag and set them down on the nearest flat surface. Kitchen tables became desks. Bedrooms became call centres. Nobody designed any of it; it simply happened, and then it stayed.

The old arrangement had one quiet gift built into it: a line. You crossed a threshold in the morning and you crossed back at night, and the crossing itself told your body which person to be. Take the line away and the two lives leak into each other — you eat where you work, you answer where you rest, and the sofa where you once did nothing at all now has a laptop on it.

So the negotiation over one good table is never really about furniture. It's about what a home is actually for, and how much of it you're prepared to lend to your job. That's a question people used to answer by walking out of a building at six o'clock. Now everyone has to answer it themselves, in the kitchen, with somebody else who wants the light.

Two bigger questions

  1. Should a home have a line in it — a room, a chair, a time of day that work simply isn't allowed to cross? Do you have one, and does it hold? What did the commute give people that they only noticed once it was gone?
  2. Working from home saves time, money and a great deal of standing on platforms. What does it quietly cost — and who pays most? Think about who has a spare room and who has a kitchen table, and whether "work from anywhere" means the same thing to everyone.
