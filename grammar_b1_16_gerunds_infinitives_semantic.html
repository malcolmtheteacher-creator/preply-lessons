Things You Enjoy (and Can't Stand)

Some verbs are always followed by the -ing form. There's no "to" involved anywhere.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "I really enjoy cooking for my friends."
  • "Could you avoid parking in front of the garage next time?"
  • "I can't stand waiting in queues."

Before you check: what comes straight after enjoy, avoid, and can't stand?

Check the pattern CLICK
The -ing form every time (cooking, parking, waiting). These verbs never take "to + verb". Other common ones that work the same way: finish, mind, keep, suggest, imagine.
enjoy / finish / avoid / mind / keep / suggest / imagine / can't stand
+ verb-ing
A very common mistake: "I enjoy to cook" ✗ / "I avoid to drive in busy traffic" ✗ — these verbs never take "to". Always: enjoy cooking ✓ / avoid driving ✓.

From two sentences to one

"I collect stamps. I really enjoy it."
→
"I really enjoy collecting stamps."

Plans, Promises & Favours

Other verbs always take "to + verb". And when there's a person involved, that person goes in the middle.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "I've decided to look for a new flat."
  • "She promised to call me back before six."
  • "He refused to pay for the taxi."

What comes after decided, promised, and refused?

Check the pattern CLICK
to + verb every time. Other common ones that work the same way: want, hope, plan, offer, manage, agree.
want / decide / hope / plan / promise / offer / manage / agree / refuse
+ to + verb
A very common mistake: "She wants going" ✗ — these verbs never take -ing on their own. Always to + verb: "She wants to go" ✓.

What happens when there's a person in the middle

Compare these two:

"I want to leave early."
→
"I want you to leave early."

Same "to + verb", but now there's a person between them — because it's you who's leaving, not "I". It works the same with tell and ask: "She asked me to close the door." "They told us to wait outside."

tell / ask / want / would like + someone + to + verb

Good At It, Interested In It

After any preposition, the verb always takes -ing — never "to".

Spot the pattern

Three everyday sentences:

  • "My brother's interested in learning new recipes."
  • "He's really good at telling jokes."
  • "Always check your bag before leaving the train."

Look at the small word right before each -ing verb (in, at, before). What kind of word is it?

Check the pattern CLICK
A preposition (in, at, before, after, about, for...). After any preposition, English needs a noun-like word — and the -ing form does that job. That's why it's always -ing here, with no exceptions.
preposition (in / at / before / after / about / for...)
+ verb-ing
A very common mistake: "good at to cook" ✗ — a preposition can never be followed by "to + verb". Always good at cooking ✓.

Your turn

1. Your colleague spends every lunch break with a history book.

Explain why: "She's interested in about ancient history."

2. Your colleague has a habit of reading her emails only at one particular moment.

Describe it: "She always checks her emails after breakfast."

3. You keep forgetting people's names at parties.

Admit it: "I'm not very good at names."

Compare with a possible answer CLICK
The -ing form. A preposition needs a noun-like word after it, and -ing turns a verb into something that behaves like a noun — so "interested in swimming", never "interested in to swim".
