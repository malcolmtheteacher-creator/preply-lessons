At the Museum

The tour is full, the rain is horizontal, and you came a long way for this. Time to talk your way in.

The Scene 🎬

It's a filthy wet Saturday in a city you're visiting, and the City Museum has landed the exhibition of the decade: Ancient Egypt, real mummies, the lot. You've wanted to stand in front of a pharaoh since you were about seven.

You shake out your umbrella, join the queue at the ticket desk, and do the maths: you need two tickets, a place on the guided tour, and some inside knowledge — and judging by the queue, half the city has had exactly the same idea. Getting what you want today won't be about asking. It'll be about how you ask.

Behind the desk: a clerk who has heard every question, every excuse and every sob story a thousand times — dry-witted, unhurried, and, if you play it right, quietly on your side.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last set foot in a museum — and did it live up to the fuss, or was the gift shop the highlight?
  2. Imagine you can put ONE object from your life in a glass case, for people in 500 years to puzzle over. What do you choose, and what will they get completely wrong about it?

Who's who

The Visitor 🧳

The other voice

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Listen for the softeners, the hedges, and the moment "no" quietly becomes "well, maybe".

ClerkAfternoon! You look like you've been in a fight with the weather. What can I do for you?
YouYou could say that — I don't suppose you sell umbrellas as well as tickets? Two for the Egypt exhibition, please: one adult, one student, if my sister here still counts.
ClerkShe counts as long as she's got some ID on her. That'll be eighteen pounds all in.
YouCard, if that's alright. Oh — and while I've got you: you wouldn't happen to know whether the two o'clock guided tour still has places, would you?
ClerkAh. I was afraid you'd ask that. Between you and me, it was full by half past nine this morning. Rainy Saturday — the whole city's had the same bright idea.
YouOh, that's a shame — we've come quite a long way for this, to be honest. Is there any flexibility at all? A waiting list, returned tickets, anything of that sort?
ClerkNo official list, I'm afraid… but no-shows happen, especially in this weather. Tell you what — hover by the meeting point at five to two, and if someone doesn't turn up, the guide will usually wave you in.
YouThat's very good of you. And failing that — is there a later tour we could fall back on?
ClerkFour o'clock. And honestly? It's the better one. Smaller group, and you get the mummy room once the crowds have thinned out. Free with your ticket, too.
YouIn that case, would there be any chance of putting two names down for the four o'clock — just so we're covered either way?
ClerkTwo ways in — very wise. Consider it done.
YouBrilliant. And if it's not too much trouble — any insider tips for the mummy room? It's rather the whole reason we came.
ClerkSince you ask: go now, before the school groups descend. It's a bit of a squeeze after lunch, and by three you'll be admiring the back of someone's anorak instead of a pharaoh. We close at six, mind — last entry to the mummy room's half past five, and they're strict about it.
YouNoted — I'd hate to get thrown out mid-sarcophagus. You've been an absolute star; thanks ever so much for your help.
ClerkNot at all. Enjoy the boy king — he's waited three thousand years for you; try not to keep him much longer.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. "I was afraid you'd ask that." What's the news about the two o'clock tour — and what does that little sentence tell you about how the clerk feels delivering it?

2. The visitor hears the word "full" but still leaves the desk with TWO separate routes onto a tour. What are they — and which of the visitor's questions unlocked each one?

3. "By three you'll be admiring the back of someone's anorak instead of a pharaoh." What is the clerk actually advising — and why say it that way rather than simply "it gets crowded"?

1. The two o'clock tour has been full since half past nine. The phrase shows the clerk is genuinely sorry to disappoint them — it cushions the bad news before it lands, instead of just announcing "it's full". The apology comes before the fact.
2. Route one: hover by the meeting point at five to two and hope for a no-show — unlocked by "Is there any flexibility at all?". Route two: two guaranteed places on the four o'clock — unlocked by "failing that…" and "would there be any chance of putting two names down…?".
3. Go to the mummy room straight away, before the school groups arrive after lunch. The comic picture — anorak versus pharaoh — is friendlier and far more memorable than a dry warning; humour lets the clerk give advice without it sounding like an order.

Your Turn 🎭

No script this time. You'll have a real conversation with a real person. They have their own mind, and you have a goal.

1 · Warm up your voice 🎤

Say each step once, out loud, in your own words. No writing, no peeking at the phrases.

  1. Ask about availability. Check what is possible before you ask for it.
  2. Buy and pay. Get the tickets sorted, simply and politely.
  3. React to bad news. Stay friendly and see if there is any give.
  4. Ask for a fallback. Offer an alternative they can say yes to.
  5. Make it easy for them. Soften the request and show you appreciate the effort.
  6. Thank them well. Close on warmth so they remember you.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1922, a man named Howard Carter made a small hole in a sealed door, held up a candle, and saw "wonderful things" — the untouched tomb of Tutankhamun. Within months, people were queuing around the block to see anything Egyptian.

A century later, here you are at a ticket desk, asking if the mummy room is open. You're standing in a very old queue: people have lined up to stare at wonders since the pharaohs built them.

The boy king waited 3,000 years to be famous. You only have to wait until the four o'clock tour.

Two bigger questions

  1. Millions of people queue for hours — and pay real money — to look at very old objects behind glass. Why do we do it? What are we hoping to feel?
  2. Tutankhamun's treasures were made for his eyes only, forever. Now anyone with a ticket can see them. Is that a happy ending for the boy king — or should some doors have stayed closed?
