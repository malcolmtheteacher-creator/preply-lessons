At the Museum

The tour is full, the rain is horizontal, and you came a long way for this. Time to talk your way in.

The Scene 🎬

It's a filthy wet Saturday in a city you're visiting, and the City Museum has landed the exhibition of the decade: Ancient Egypt, real mummies, the lot. You've wanted to stand in front of a pharaoh since you were about seven.

You shake out your umbrella, join the queue at the ticket desk, and do the maths: you need two tickets, a place on the guided tour, and some inside knowledge — and judging by the queue, half the city has had exactly the same idea. Getting what you want today won't be about asking. It'll be about how you ask.

Behind the desk: a clerk who has heard every question, every excuse and every sob story a thousand times — dry-witted, unhurried, and, if you play it right, quietly on your side.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last set foot in a museum — and did it live up to the fuss, or was the gift shop the highlight?
  2. Imagine you can put ONE object from your life in a glass case, for people in 500 years to puzzle over. What do you choose, and what will they get completely wrong about it?

Who's who

You
The Visitor 🧳

Charming, slightly damp, quietly determined. You want tickets, the mummy room and a place on the tour — and you're not above a little gentle persuasion.

The other voice
The Ticket Clerk 🎟️

Seen it all, surprised by nothing. Knows exactly how full every tour is — and exactly which rules will bend for someone who asks nicely.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't just follow what the visitor asks for — watch how they ask once the answer is no. That's the whole game.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the softeners, the hedges, and the moment "no" quietly becomes "well, maybe".

ClerkAfternoon! You look like you've been in a fight with the weather. What can I do for you?
YouYou could say that — I don't suppose you sell umbrellas as well as tickets? Two for the Egypt exhibition, please: one adult, one student, if my sister here still counts.
ClerkShe counts as long as she's got some ID on her. That'll be eighteen pounds all in.
YouCard, if that's alright. Oh — and while I've got you: you wouldn't happen to know whether the two o'clock guided tour still has places, would you?
ClerkAh. I was afraid you'd ask that. Between you and me, it was full by half past nine this morning. Rainy Saturday — the whole city's had the same bright idea.
YouOh, that's a shame — we've come quite a long way for this, to be honest. Is there any flexibility at all? A waiting list, returned tickets, anything of that sort?
ClerkNo official list, I'm afraid… but no-shows happen, especially in this weather. Tell you what — hover by the meeting point at five to two, and if someone doesn't turn up, the guide will usually wave you in.
YouThat's very good of you. And failing that — is there a later tour we could fall back on?
ClerkFour o'clock. And honestly? It's the better one. Smaller group, and you get the mummy room once the crowds have thinned out. Free with your ticket, too.
YouIn that case, would there be any chance of putting two names down for the four o'clock — just so we're covered either way?
ClerkTwo ways in — very wise. Consider it done.
YouBrilliant. And if it's not too much trouble — any insider tips for the mummy room? It's rather the whole reason we came.
ClerkSince you ask: go now, before the school groups descend. It's a bit of a squeeze after lunch, and by three you'll be admiring the back of someone's anorak instead of a pharaoh. We close at six, mind — last entry to the mummy room's half past five, and they're strict about it.
YouNoted — I'd hate to get thrown out mid-sarcophagus. You've been an absolute star; thanks ever so much for your help.
ClerkNot at all. Enjoy the boy king — he's waited three thousand years for you; try not to keep him much longer.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. "I was afraid you'd ask that." What's the news about the two o'clock tour — and what does that little sentence tell you about how the clerk feels delivering it?

2. The visitor hears the word "full" but still leaves the desk with TWO separate routes onto a tour. What are they — and which of the visitor's questions unlocked each one?

3. "By three you'll be admiring the back of someone's anorak instead of a pharaoh." What is the clerk actually advising — and why say it that way rather than simply "it gets crowded"?

1. The two o'clock tour has been full since half past nine. The phrase shows the clerk is genuinely sorry to disappoint them — it cushions the bad news before it lands, instead of just announcing "it's full". The apology comes before the fact.
2. Route one: hover by the meeting point at five to two and hope for a no-show — unlocked by "Is there any flexibility at all?". Route two: two guaranteed places on the four o'clock — unlocked by "failing that…" and "would there be any chance of putting two names down…?".
3. Go to the mummy room straight away, before the school groups arrive after lunch. The comic picture — anorak versus pharaoh — is friendlier and far more memorable than a dry warning; humour lets the clerk give advice without it sounding like an order.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that turn "no" into "well, maybe" — at this museum and everywhere else. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚪 Opening softly

Phrase 1

You want to ask whether the tour still has places — but you suspect the answer is no, and you'd rather sound easy-going than demanding. Which opener manages your expectations AND theirs?

A. "Are there places on the tour or not?"
B. "I need two places on the tour."
C. "I don't suppose there are any places left on the tour…?"
C — "I don't suppose there are any places left on the tour…?"
"I don't suppose…" politely expects a no — which makes it painless to give one, and rather delightful to give a yes. It's the least pushy way in English to fish for a favour.
Phrase 2

You're already holding out your bank card — but you haven't actually asked whether they take cards. There's a three-word tag you can hang on the end that turns your assumption back into a question: "Card, ___ ___ ___."

"Card, if that's alright."
"…if that's alright" (or "…if that's OK with you") retro-fits politeness onto almost anything: "I'll sit here, if that's alright." "We'll come back at four, if that's alright."

🎣 Asking smoothly

Phrase 3

The most elegant way to ask a stranger for information wraps the question in cotton wool. Put the pieces in order:

whetheryou wouldn'twould you?the tour's full,happen to know
"You wouldn't happen to know whether the tour's full, would you?"
Three layers of softening in one sentence: "wouldn't", "happen to", and the little "would you?" tag. It sounds almost apologetic — which is exactly why it works on busy strangers.
Phrase 4

You've paid, the transaction is over, and the clerk is already turning to the next customer — but you have one more question. What short phrase holds their attention a moment longer, warm rather than needy?

"Oh — while I've got you…"
It frames your extra question as a happy accident of timing, not an extra demand. Cousins: "While you're there…", "Before I let you go…"

🌧️ When the answer is no

Phrase 5

"The tour is full." A beginner says "OK" and walks away. You are not a beginner. Show your disappointment in three words, then ask ONE question that gently checks whether the rules can bend. What's your line?

"That's a shame — is there any flexibility at all?"
"That's a shame" registers the blow without drama; "any flexibility at all?" invites the other person to find a solution for you. Variants: "…is there any flexibility on the time / the price / the date?"
Phrase 6

Now make your case — let the clerk know this visit isn't casual, and that a "no" costs you more than most people. Build the sentence:

for this,quite a long wayto be honest.we've come
"We've come quite a long way for this, to be honest."
"To be honest" marks a frank, human moment — you're confiding, not complaining. Understatement does the rest: "quite a long way" pleads your case without a single dramatic word.

🤝 Building plan B

Phrase 7

The no-show trick might not come off — you need a backup. There's a crisp two-word phrase that means "if that doesn't work out" and makes you sound wonderfully prepared: "___ ___, is there a later tour we could fall back on?"

"Failing that, is there a later tour we could fall back on?"
"Failing that…" is negotiation gold — it accepts plan A might die and moves straight to plan B without a flicker of panic. Note the phrasal verb too: something to fall back on = a safety net.
Phrase 8

You want the clerk to book you two places. Which request would you rather receive at the end of a long shift?

A. "Can you put two names down for the four o'clock?"
B. "Would there be any chance of putting two names down for the four o'clock?"
B — "Would there be any chance of putting two names down…?"
A is fine; B is charming. "Would there be any chance of + -ing" treats the favour as their gift, not your right — and note the grammar: chance of doing, never "chance to do" here.

🌟 One last favour — and a proper goodbye

Phrase 9

Everything's sorted, but you want one more favour — some insider advice. Before asking, you soften it with a phrase that means "feel completely free to say no". What is it?

"If it's not too much trouble…"
The classic pressure-release valve for an extra request. Close relatives: "I hate to be a pain, but…" and "Sorry to be a nuisance, but…" — all of them buy goodwill before you spend it.
Phrase 10

The clerk has bent the rules, booked you in and handed you their best tips. Which exit line will they still be smiling about at closing time?

A. "Thanks."
B. "Thank you for your assistance in this matter."
C. "You've been an absolute star — thanks ever so much for your help."
C — "You've been an absolute star — thanks ever so much for your help."
A is bare; B belongs in a lawyer's letter. "An absolute star" praises the person, and "ever so much" turns the volume up on the thanks without sounding formal.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole visit out loud using all ten, in order — soft opening → smooth questions → the bad news → plan B → last favour → goodbye. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

🌩️ The last-day gamble

One more round — no script, no safety net. It's the FINAL day of the exhibition and every ticket sold out weeks ago. But you've just overheard the clerk mention that ONE returned ticket has come back to the desk… and you need two, because you've brought your grandmother, who has wanted to see Tutankhamun since 1972. Start the timer and talk your way through it: make your case, explore every angle — the returned ticket, a supervisor, a cancellation just before closing, even tomorrow's preview of the next exhibition as a consolation prize. Who gets the one ticket if that's all there is? One rule: the moment you turn rude or demanding, you lose the room — and the ticket.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1922, a man named Howard Carter made a small hole in a sealed door, held up a candle, and saw "wonderful things" — the untouched tomb of Tutankhamun. Within months, people were queuing around the block to see anything Egyptian.

A century later, here you are at a ticket desk, asking if the mummy room is open. You're standing in a very old queue: people have lined up to stare at wonders since the pharaohs built them.

The boy king waited 3,000 years to be famous. You only have to wait until the four o'clock tour.
