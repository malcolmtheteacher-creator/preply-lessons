The Roman Republic

For almost 500 years, Rome had no king — until the men who saved the city became the men who ended it.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one.

Part 1 — A city with no king

A very long time ago, in the year 509 BC, the people of Rome sent away their last king. They were angry with him, and they made a promise: never again would one man rule them. So they made a new system, called a republic. In a republic, there is no king. The people choose their leaders, and power is shared.

Each year, the people voted for two leaders called consuls. There were always two, not one, and they had the job for only one year. Each consul could say "no" to the other one. This way, no single man could become too strong. There was also the Senate — a group of important, older men who gave advice and controlled the money.

There was one special rule for emergencies. If Rome was in great danger, the people could choose one man to be a dictator. A dictator had all the power — but only for a short time, about six months. Then he had to give the power back. Every part of the system was made to stop one man becoming a king.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Rome have two consuls, and only for one year?

2. What was special about a dictator in early Rome?

1. So that no single man could become too strong like a king. The two consuls could stop each other, and after one year they had to stop.

2. A dictator had all the power, but only for a short time (about six months) in an emergency. Then he had to give the power back.

Talk about it:
  • The Romans were afraid of one person having too much power. Do countries today still worry about this? How do they stop it?

Part 2 — Rich and angry

Over the next few hundred years, Rome grew bigger and bigger. Its army won war after war. Rome beat its biggest enemy, a city called Carthage, and took control of Greece, Spain and many other lands. A lot of treasure, land and slaves came into Rome. The city became very rich.

However, the money was not shared. The richest families took huge farms and used slaves to work them. Normal people went away to fight in the wars for years. When they came home, their small farms were often gone. Now there were many poor, angry people in the city, and only a few very rich families. This was a big problem.

Some leaders tried to help the poor. In 133 BC a young man called Tiberius Gracchus wanted to give land back to poor people. But the rich men were angry, and they killed him. Ten years later, his brother tried to do the same thing, and he died too. So now there was a terrible new idea in Rome: you could win in politics not by votes, but by violence.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Rome became very rich. So why was this a problem at home?

2. What happened to the two brothers who tried to help the poor?

1. The money was not shared. The rich families became very rich, but normal people lost their farms and became poor and angry. This split the city.

2. Both Gracchus brothers were killed by the rich men who did not want to share the land. This showed that people now used violence, not votes, to win.

Talk about it:
  • In Rome, soldiers started to follow their general, not the country. Why is it dangerous when people are loyal to one leader and not to the system?

Part 3 — The last days of the Republic

Soon, strong generals took control. One general, Sulla, marched his own army into the city of Rome and made himself dictator — but he did not give the power back. Then came Julius Caesar, a brilliant and very popular general. He won many wars, and his soldiers loved him.

The Senate was afraid of Caesar. They told him to leave his army and come home alone. But in 49 BC, Caesar brought his army across a small river, the Rubicon. This was not allowed, and it started a civil war — Romans fighting Romans. Caesar won, and he made himself dictator for life. To people who hated kings, this was too much. So in 44 BC, a group of men killed Caesar. They thought they were saving the Republic.

However, killing Caesar did not save the Republic. It only started another civil war. In the end, Caesar's young heir, a man called Octavian, won. He was very clever. He kept the old words — Senate, consul, republic — but really he held all the power himself. In 27 BC, he took a new name: Augustus. Now Rome had one ruler again, like a king. We call him an emperor. The system built to stop one man ruling had ended with exactly one man ruling.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Caesar do at the river Rubicon in 49 BC, and why was it so serious?

2. Did killing Caesar save the Republic? What happened instead?

1. He brought his army across the river into Roman land, which was not allowed. This started a civil war against the Republic, and there was no going back.

2. No. It started another civil war. In the end, Octavian won, took all the power, and became Augustus — the first emperor. The Republic was over.

Talk about it:
  • Octavian kept the old names but took all the power himself. Why might a leader want to keep the old words and hide what he is really doing?

Grammar: linking words

When we tell a story, we join our ideas with small words. They show how one idea connects to the next. This story uses words like however, also, but, so, then and for example.

What each word does:
• but / however — show a surprise or a different idea. "The Senate had power. However, the generals were strong too."
• also — add one more idea. "Rome was rich. There were also many poor people."
• so — show a result. "The people hated the king, so they made a republic."
• then — show the next thing in time. "Caesar won the war. Then he became dictator."
• for example — give an example. "Some leaders took all the power. For example, Sulla."
One easy tip: but and so usually join ideas inside one sentence ("Rome was rich, but the money was not shared."). However usually starts a new sentence and has a comma after it ("Rome was rich. However, the money was not shared.").
