The Cow That Pays for the Moonshots

Around 1970 a Boston consulting firm drew four boxes that told companies where to put their money. Half a century later, Google’s parent Alphabet runs the purest version of the idea — and its cash cow is under threat.

Easier version: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you’ll work through the most famous tool in corporate strategy — the BCG growth-share matrix — and test it on the company that lives by it; and you’ll practise the language of formal recommendation: we recommend that the unit be sold…, it is essential that…, it’s time we stopped…

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. In the 1960s one American company might own a hotel chain, a car-rental firm, a bakery and a defence supplier at the same time. By the end of the decade it had become a conglomerate.
a conglomerate (n) — a large group that owns many businesses in unrelated industries. Popular in the 1960s and 70s; many were broken up in the 1980s, when investors argued the parts were worth more separately (the conglomerate discount).
2. The advertising business makes enormous profits, while the self-driving car project has lost money for over a decade. Critics say the company uses one to cross-subsidise the other.
to cross-subsidise (v, US: cross-subsidize) — to use profits from one activity to pay for losses in another. Noun: cross-subsidy. It can be a smart long-term bet or a way of hiding a failure.
3. Balloons carrying internet access to remote villages, kites generating wind power, drones delivering parcels — the founders proudly called these projects moonshots.
a moonshot (n) — a hugely ambitious project with a low chance of success but a very large reward if it works. From the 1960s missions to the moon. A moonshot factory, to take a moonshot.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: core business · capital allocation · cash burn. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which term means the rate at which a loss-making project or start-up spends its money?
cash burn (n) — also burn rate. “The unit’s cash burn is around a billion dollars a year.” Investors ask one question about it: how long until the money runs out, or the revenue arrives?
2. Which term means the activity that a company depends on most, and that defines what it is?
the core business (n) — to focus on the core, non-core assets (the parts a company is willing to sell). The strategic danger: the core is often also the thing a company is least willing to question.
3. Which term means how a company’s leaders decide to divide its money between investment, projects, acquisitions and returns to shareholders?
capital allocation (n) — often described as the most important job of a chief executive. Verb: to allocate capital to. The BCG matrix was, above all, a capital-allocation tool.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. The chemicals group decided that its paint division no longer fitted its strategy and agreed to divest it to a private-equity firm. To divest a business is to…
  (a) sell it or give up ownership of it   (b) invest more money in it   (c) divide it into smaller departments
(a) — to divest (v), noun divestment or divestiture. Compare to spin off: to turn a division into a separate company, often with its own shares.
2. The balloon project would not close overnight. Over the following months the company would wind it down, finding new roles for some staff. To wind down a project is to…
  (a) restart it after a pause   (b) reduce its costs while keeping it growing   (c) bring it gradually to an end
(c) — to wind down (phrasal v) — to end something gradually, not suddenly. A wind-down (n). Close in meaning to to phase out, which is used more for products than for organisations.

D · Collocation & word building

1. In its annual report, Alphabet shows Google Services, Google Cloud and Other Bets as separate business seg___. Complete the noun, then give the verb that means dividing customers into groups.
segments (n) — segment reporting, a loss-making segment. Verb: to segment a market; noun: market segmentation. Stress: the noun is SEGment, the verb is often segMENT.
2. In 2024 a US federal judge ruled against Google in an anti___ case about its search business. Complete the adjective, and give the term used more often in Britain and Europe.
antitrust (adj) — US term for laws against monopolies and unfair competition: an antitrust case, antitrust regulators. In the UK and EU: competition law, competition authorities.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — Four boxes in Boston

In 1963 Bruce Henderson started a small consulting unit in Boston that would become the Boston Consulting Group. Its clients were the giants of the age: conglomerates that owned dozens of unrelated businesses and had no clear way of deciding which ones deserved money. Every division manager arrived at the budget meeting with a confident forecast, and the loudest usually won.

Around 1970 the firm offered a simpler answer: a chart with two measures. Up the side, how fast a product’s market was growing. Along the bottom, its share compared with the largest competitor. Leaders in slow markets were cash cows, producing more money than they needed. Leaders in fast markets were stars. Small players in fast markets were question marks, and small players in slow markets were dogs. The logic was financial: milk the cows, use the cash to turn the best question marks into stars, and sell or close the dogs.

The idea spread very quickly. General Electric developed its own nine-box version with McKinsey, and a Harvard Business Review study published in 1982 found that close to half of the Fortune 500 had introduced some form of portfolio planning. But criticism grew too. Managers of businesses labelled dogs found their budgets cut and their best people moved, which made decline almost certain. Critics said the matrix treated a company like a pile of shares rather than a set of businesses that depended on each other. And during the 1980s many conglomerates were broken up by investors who believed the parts were worth more than the whole.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What problem inside conglomerates was the matrix designed to solve?

2. Give two criticisms of the matrix from the 1980s.

1. They owned many unrelated businesses and had no clear, objective way to decide which ones should receive investment; budget decisions tended to follow the most persuasive managers.

2. Any two of: labelling a business a dog could make its decline self-fulfilling (budgets and people removed); it treated a company like a financial portfolio and ignored how businesses depend on each other; conglomerate structures themselves came to be seen as destroying value.

Discuss:
  • Where money is shared out in your organisation, does it follow a clear method, or the most persuasive person in the room? Which would you trust more?

Part 2 — The purest portfolio

Fifty years later, one company looks almost exactly like the matrix. Google’s search engine, and the advertising beside the results, became one of the most profitable businesses ever built. By the 2010s it was also paying for a remarkable collection of experiments: self-driving cars, which Google had started working on in 2009; internet balloons; delivery drones; research into ageing and disease.

In August 2015 the founders, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, reorganised everything. Google became one company inside a new parent called Alphabet, and the experiments became separate companies grouped as “Other Bets”. For the first time, investors could see the numbers. The bets brought in little revenue and, in most years since, have reported losses of several billion dollars. Over the following decade those losses added up to tens of billions — paid for by search.

The new structure came with more discipline. Ruth Porat, who became finance chief in 2015, was known for pressing projects to show a path to revenue. Some bets were wound down: the wind-power kites of Makani closed in 2020 and the balloon project Loon in 2021. Others began to take money from outside investors. Page and Brin could afford this kind of patience partly because their special voting shares gave them control of the company — something most chief executives do not have.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the 2015 reorganisation matter to investors?

2. What changed in how the bets were managed after 2015?

1. Because the experiments were reported separately for the first time, so investors could see how much revenue they earned and how much money they lost.

2. There was more financial discipline: projects were pushed to show a path to revenue, some were wound down (Makani, Loon), and some took outside investment.

Discuss:
  • If every project in your organisation had to publish its own profit and loss, which ones would look very different — and would that be fair to them?

Part 3 — When the cow is threatened

By 2025 some of the question marks were starting to look like stars. Waymo, the self-driving project begun in 2009, was giving hundreds of thousands of paid, driverless rides a week in several American cities. Google Cloud, a separate business, had become a large and growing source of revenue. Search and its advertising, however, still produced more than half of Alphabet’s income.

Then the cow itself came under pressure. When ChatGPT appeared in late 2022, millions of people began asking a chatbot questions they would once have typed into a search engine. Google responded with its own AI models and, from 2024, AI-written answers at the top of search results. But an answer that satisfies the user immediately may lead to fewer clicks on advertisements — the very thing that pays for everything else. In the same year, a US federal judge ruled that Google had illegally maintained a monopoly in search.

This is the dilemma the matrix does not solve. Protect the cow, and a competitor may do to search what search once did to newspapers and directories. Change it too quickly, and the money that funds the future may shrink before the future arrives. Some analysts say Alphabet proves the matrix still works: the cow paid for Waymo. Others say the lesson is the opposite — that the greatest risk to a portfolio is a change the chart cannot see coming. The question is still open: should a company milk the cow that pays for its future, or risk the cow to build the next one?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why could AI-written answers be a risk to Google’s business, even if users like them?

2. Give one sign that some of the bets were becoming stars.

1. If people get their answer immediately, they may click on fewer advertisements, and advertising in search is what produces most of the money that funds the rest of Alphabet.

2. Waymo was giving hundreds of thousands of paid driverless rides a week in several US cities (and Google Cloud had grown into a large, growing business).

Discuss:
  • What is the “cow” in your industry — the product or service that pays for everything — and what is the most realistic thing that could threaten it?

Grammar — formal recommendations

The end of every portfolio review is a recommendation: keep, invest, sell, close. Boards, consultants and regulators express these in a very particular grammar — a verb in its bare form after that, and a past tense that does not refer to the past. Used well, it sounds decisive and impersonal. Used badly, it sounds like a translated contract.

1 · recommend / suggest / propose / insist / demand + that + bare verb
• “The auditors insisted that the report be published in full.” (not is, not was)
• “She proposed that each region set its own targets.” — no -s, even with a singular subject.
• Negative: “…that the scheme not be extended.”
• In British English, should + verb is an equally correct and slightly less formal alternative: “…that the report should be published.”
2 · It is essential / vital / important / imperative + that + bare verb
• “It is vital that the new manager attend the first meeting.”
• “It is imperative that no customer data be shared.”
• Also after nouns: our recommendation that…, the requirement that…, the board’s insistence that…
3 · Unreal past for present pressure
• It’s time / It’s high time / It’s about time + subject + past simple: “It’s high time the committee published its findings.” — the past tense adds impatience: this should already be happening.
• I’d rather / I would sooner + subject + past simple: “I’d rather the press didn’t learn about it today.”
• Compare: It’s time to review (neutral) vs It’s time we reviewed (pointed).

1. The bare verb

Write the correct form of the verb in brackets. You may need one, two or three words.

1. The consultants recommended that the loss-making division (sell) within two years.

2. It is vital that every business unit (report) its own profits and losses.

3. The chair proposed that the drone project (not / receive) any new funding this year.

4. Shareholders are demanding that the company (be) more open about what its experiments cost.

1. be sold   2. report   3. not receive   4. be

British English also accepts should be sold, should report, should not receive, should be. What is never correct here: reports, doesn’t receive, is.

2. The pointed past

Complete each sentence with the correct form of the verb in brackets.

1. That project has lost money for eight years. It’s high time we (stop) funding it.

2. I’d rather the board (not / hear) about this from a journalist.

3. It’s about time the company (tell) shareholders how much each bet really costs.

4. Would you rather we (wait) until the next quarter before we announce it?

1. stopped   2. didn’t hear   3. told   4. waited

All four refer to now or the future. The past form signals distance from reality: it hasn’t happened yet, and the speaker thinks it should.

3. The recommendation — complete it, then deliver it

This is the closing minute of a portfolio review. Complete each gap with the correct form of the verb in brackets, then deliver it aloud as if to a sceptical board.

“Our first recommendation is that the robotics unit (keep), but that it (require) to find an outside investor within eighteen months. It is essential that our core business (remain) the first priority for capital. Frankly, it’s time we (accept) that some bets will fail, and I would rather we (close) two projects this year than watch all six drift.”

be kept · be required · remain · accepted · closed

Gaps 1–3 also accept should be kept, should be required, should remain. Now say the same recommendation without this grammar — “We think you should keep…, you need to…” — and notice what happens to the authority of the speaker.

Take these into the Speak tab: the board wants the formal recommendation; the team whose project is closing wants to hear it in plain words.

Going deeper

A note on the figures: revenues, losses and ride numbers are rounded and taken from public reporting; this is a live case, and the numbers change every quarter.

What the case actually tests. The first reading says the matrix has never looked better. Alphabet did exactly what the four boxes recommend: it milked an extraordinary cash cow, used the money to fund question marks, closed several that failed, and has at least one — Waymo — that may be turning into a star. Reorganising in 2015 so that every bet had its own reported numbers is the matrix turned into company structure. On this reading, most companies fail not because the tool is wrong but because they lack the discipline, or the patience, to use it.

The second reading says the case shows the tool’s limits. The matrix measures market growth and market share — but most of Alphabet’s bets had no market to measure when they started. Nobody could calculate the share of a driverless taxi market that did not exist. The decisions were acts of judgement and belief, described afterwards in the language of portfolios. And the most serious threat to the cow did not come from a competitor inside its box; it came from a technology that changed what the box was. Is Google in the “search” market, the “advertising” market, or the “answers” market? The answer changes where it sits on the chart.

The third point is about power. Alphabet’s founders control the company through special voting shares. That is what allowed a decade of multi-billion-dollar losses without a shareholder revolt. In a company where investors can remove the board, the same strategy might have ended in the 1980s way: break it up, return the cash, keep the cow. So the question is not only whether the matrix works, but who has the authority to wait for a question mark to become a star.
Talk it through:
  • Proof or warning? Take one side and argue it with at least two facts from the case — then name the one fact that most weakens your side.
  • Which activity in your organisation would be called a dog on the chart but is quietly important to something else? What would happen if it were closed?
  • Should AI answers be allowed to damage Google’s advertising business? Argue as the head of search, then as the head of Waymo.
  • How long should an organisation keep funding a question mark before it demands a path to revenue? What evidence would change your answer?

Speak

All of it out loud, against the clock. A portfolio decision is made in the language of recommendation — and then has to be explained to the people who live with it.

Useful language: we recommend that… be… · it is essential that… · it’s time we… · I’d rather we… than… · and in plain words: we’re closing…, here’s why…, here’s what happens to you · and to close: what we are not prepared to risk is…

Task B · The hostile question (4 minutes)

You are the finance chief of a company like Alphabet at the annual shareholder meeting. An investor stands up and says: “You have lost tens of billions on experiments in ten years. Most have closed. Why shouldn’t you shut the rest today and give us the money back?”

Answer properly: acknowledge the losses in numbers, explain the logic of funding question marks, say clearly what would make you close a bet, and make one firm recommendation. Then do it again in ninety seconds — the compression is the exercise.

Task C · Four rooms, one closure (4 minutes)

Take one project or product that is being wound down — real or invented — and announce it four ways, one minute each. Notice where the formal recommendation grammar helps and where it gets in the way.

  • To the board: the recommendation, the cost of continuing and the cost of closing, in formal style.
  • To the team who built it: what is happening, why, and what happens to them next.
  • To the customers who used it: what changes, when, and what they should do.
  • To a journalist: one clear sentence that does not sound like failure — and does not hide it.

Prefer not to use your own organisation? Take one of these instead:

  • A bank closing the innovation lab it opened five years ago.
  • A supermarket ending home delivery outside the big cities.
  • A university closing a small language department.
  • A car maker stopping production of its smallest, cheapest model.
