The Battle of Waterloo

One long summer's day in 1815 that decided the fate of Europe — and ended an emperor.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of a single day of battle that turned hour by hour and ended the Napoleonic Wars — and you'll master the linkers that let you narrate a fast-changing event: however, meanwhile, despite, whereas, by the time.
Warm up — talk before we read.
  • What do you already know about Napoleon, and about the word "Waterloo"? Why might people say "he met his Waterloo"?
  • Picture a battlefield of 1815 — no planes, no radios, just men, horses and cannon. How do you think a commander gave orders and moved thousands of soldiers on a day like that?
  • Weather rarely decides a modern war. Could rain and mud change the result of a battle two hundred years ago? How?

A. Infer from context

Work out the bold word from the situation around it. Say what you think it means, then reveal.

1. Along the low ridge the general placed his men, some kept out of sight below the crest, so that from the valley the enemy could not tell how many he had.
a ridge (n) — a long, narrow, raised strip of high ground, such as the top of a low hill.
2. Only a few hundred men held the walled farmhouse — the small garrison stationed inside it beat off attack after attack and never gave the place up.
a garrison (n) — the body of troops stationed in a fort, town or defended position to hold and protect it.
3. Once the Imperial Guard broke, the ordered withdrawal collapsed into a rout: whole regiments threw down their weapons and scattered into the darkness in blind panic.
a rout (n) — a crushing, disorderly defeat in which a beaten army breaks apart and flees in panic. Also a verb: to rout an enemy.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The return, and the gathering storm

In the spring of 1815, Europe thought its long war was over. Napoleon Bonaparte, the French emperor who had conquered much of the continent, had been defeated the year before and exiled to the small island of Elba. But in March he escaped, slipped back into France, and marched on Paris. Soldiers sent to arrest him joined him instead. Within weeks he was emperor again, and the nations of Europe declared him an outlaw and prepared for war one last time.

Napoleon knew he had to strike first. Two allied armies were forming in the kingdom of the Netherlands, in what is now Belgium: a mixed British, Dutch and German force under the Duke of Wellington, and a Prussian army under the veteran Marshal Blücher. If they combined, they would crush him. So Napoleon moved fast to get between them and beat each one separately. On 16 June, whereas Wellington held his ground, Blücher's Prussians were badly mauled at Ligny and forced to fall back.

Wellington pulled his army onto a low ridge near a village called Waterloo, south of Brussels, and waited. Heavy rain had fallen through the night, turning the fields to mud. By the time the morning of 18 June came, the two armies stood facing each other across a shallow valley — some 68,000 men with Wellington, around 72,000 with Napoleon. Everything now depended on whether Blücher, beaten two days before, could reach the field in time.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Napoleon come to be emperor again in 1815?

2. Why did Napoleon want to fight Wellington and Blücher separately rather than together?

1. He had been exiled to Elba, but he escaped in March, returned to France, and the soldiers sent to stop him joined him instead — so he retook power within weeks.

2. Combined, the two allied armies would greatly outnumber him; by keeping them apart he hoped to defeat each one on its own.

Discuss:
  • Napoleon gambled everything on speed and surprise. When is it wiser to strike first, and when is it wiser to wait?

Part 2 — The long day

The mud gave Wellington a gift: it forced Napoleon to delay his attack until nearly midday, so that the ground could dry enough to move his guns. Those lost hours would matter more than anyone yet knew. When the assault finally came, the fighting fixed itself around a few farms in front of the ridge — above all a walled farmhouse called Hougoumont, which a small allied garrison defended all day against wave after wave of French infantry, and never let fall.

Through the afternoon the battle swung back and forth. French artillery pounded Wellington's line; his men lay down behind the ridge to shelter from the fire. Then came the great French cavalry charges — thousands of horsemen sweeping up the slope. The allied infantry formed themselves into squares, walls of men bristling with bayonets that the horses would not charge into, and held. However, the pressure was relentless, and by early evening Wellington's centre was stretched dangerously thin.

Meanwhile, far to the east, Blücher was keeping his word. Despite the mud, his exhausted Prussians were marching hard toward the sound of the guns. By late afternoon their leading troops began to appear on Napoleon's right flank, and the emperor had to turn part of his army to meet them. Slowly, the numbers that had favoured Napoleon in the morning began to turn against him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was Napoleon forced to delay his attack, and why did that matter?

2. How did Wellington's infantry survive the French cavalry charges, and what changed the balance in the afternoon?

1. The overnight rain had left the ground too wet to move his artillery, so he waited until nearly midday; those lost hours gave the Prussians time to arrive.

2. The infantry formed defensive squares that the cavalry could not break; the balance shifted when Blücher's Prussians reached the battlefield and threatened Napoleon's flank.

Discuss:
  • A few hours of mud, and one army keeping its promise to arrive, decided the outcome. How much of history do you think turns on chance like this?

Part 3 — The last throw, and the end of an era

By early evening Napoleon knew that time was running out. With the Prussians pressing ever harder on his flank, he gambled on one last blow at Wellington's centre. He sent forward his finest troops — the Imperial Guard, soldiers who had never been beaten in a major attack. As they climbed the ridge in the smoke, Wellington's men, who had been lying hidden, rose and fired volley after volley into them at close range. The unthinkable happened: the Guard wavered, then broke and fell back.

A cry ran down the French line — "The Guard retreats!" — and the whole army began to lose heart. At almost the same moment the Prussians broke through in strength on the other side. Wellington, sensing the turn, stood in his stirrups and waved his whole line forward. The French retreat became a rout, and by nightfall Napoleon's army had scattered into the darkness.

The cost of that single day was terrible: tens of thousands of men were killed or wounded, and the fields around Waterloo were left covered with the dead of three nations. It was a decisive victory. Napoleon fled to Paris, abdicated within days, and was exiled again — this time to the remote island of Saint Helena, far out in the South Atlantic, where he died six years later. Waterloo ended the Napoleonic Wars, closed a quarter-century of near-constant fighting, and gave Europe a peace that, in its main outlines, held for decades. To this day, "to meet your Waterloo" means to face the final defeat you cannot escape.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Napoleon's final gamble, and what happened to it?

2. What were the consequences of the battle, for Napoleon and for Europe?

1. He sent the Imperial Guard, his best and previously unbeaten troops, in one last attack on Wellington's centre; they were met by hidden infantry, broke, and fell back — which shattered French morale.

2. The French army was routed with heavy casualties on all sides; Napoleon abdicated and was exiled to Saint Helena, where he died. The battle ended the Napoleonic Wars and brought Europe a long period of peace.

Discuss:
  • Waterloo gave its name to any final, unavoidable defeat. Why do you think this one battle, out of so many, became that symbol?

Grammar — linkers of time & contrast

Waterloo turned hour by hour: one thing happened while another was going on, and again and again the situation flipped to its opposite. To narrate a day like that, you need words that mark time and words that mark contrast. Master these five and your storytelling gains real control.

The contrast linkers:
• however — starts a new sentence and signals a turn to the opposite idea; followed by a comma: "The squares held. However, the pressure was relentless."
• despite / in spite of + noun or -ing — "against" something; not followed by a full clause: "Despite the mud, the Prussians marched on." "Despite being exhausted, they kept going."
• whereas — joins two clauses to point out a direct difference between them: "Wellington held his ground, whereas Blücher was forced back."
The time linkers:
• meanwhile — "at the same time, somewhere else"; starts a sentence: "Wellington's line held. Meanwhile, the Prussians were marching hard toward the guns."
• by the time — "at the point when (something else had already happened)"; the other action is usually finished: "By the time the Guard attacked, the Prussians had already arrived."
Two traps to avoid:
• Use despite / in spite of with a noun or -ing, but although / even though with a full clause: "Despite the rain…" but "Although it was raining…".
• However is not a comma-splice conjunction: end the first sentence with a full stop (or a semicolon), then "However,…" — never join two clauses with only a comma before it.
