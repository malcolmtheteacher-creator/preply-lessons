English at Work · Role Play · B2/C1 · 50 min

The Joke

Rob does an impression of Aisha's accent. It got a laugh the first time — including from you. It's still going, and you've decided to say something.

The Scene 🎬

It started in the pub after the summer thing. Rob Whelan did Aisha — her voice, the way she says "obviously", the little pause before she answers a question — and it was quick and quite well observed and the whole table went. You went too. That's the part you keep coming back to.

It's about six weeks later now and it's still happening, in the kitchen, on calls when she's not on the call, once in front of someone from another team who didn't know who Aisha was and laughed anyway. Nobody has complained. Aisha, as far as you know, has never heard it. You have no injury to report and no authority to report it with, and if you say nothing, nothing at all will happen, ever.

So this is the hard version of speaking up: you're not defending yourself, you're not owed anything, and the person you're going to say it to is somebody you like — somebody everybody likes, who gave you a lift home twice last winter. There's no formal channel here and you don't want one. You want a thirty-second conversation by the lifts that ends with him not doing it any more and still saying hello to you on Monday.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of a time you laughed at something and then wished you hadn't. What stopped you from saying anything at the time — and was it the same thing that stopped you the next day?
  2. Is it easier or harder to raise something when the person is popular and generous and you genuinely like them? Say why. What exactly are you risking that you wouldn't be risking with someone you didn't like?

Who's who

You
The One Who Laughed 🙂

You're not a manager, you're not Aisha's friend particularly, and you've got no complaint of your own. All you've got is the fact that you noticed, and you laughed, and you'd rather deal with it in a corridor than let it turn into something with paperwork.

The other voice
Rob Whelan 🎤

Popular, generous, everybody's mate. Buys the first round, covers your shift, remembers your kids' names. He does impressions of everyone — the MD, the security bloke, you. He keeps doing Aisha's accent, and he honestly doesn't think he's doing anything at all.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't watch how the argument is won — nobody wins anything. Watch how small the speaker keeps it: how they get in without a build-up, hand Rob his good intentions back before he has to fight for them, and stop the moment the point has landed rather than pressing it home.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Rob asks one short question early on that would have changed the entire conversation if the answer had been different. What was the question, and what was he really trying to find out?

2. The speaker admits something about their own behaviour on the night — something that arguably weakens their case. Why put it in?

3. Rob defends the joke twice, on two completely different grounds. What was his second defence, and what ended it?

1. "So who's complained?" He's working out how much trouble he's in, because that decides whether this is a chat or a process. As soon as he hears that nobody has complained, he can stop defending himself, and the speaker gets him back — which is why the answer comes straight away rather than being held back for effect.
2. Because admitting they laughed too turns an accusation into something two people were both in. It costs the speaker nothing they were going to win anyway, and it takes away Rob's best reply, which was going to be "everyone laughed" — he says it a moment later and it has already been agreed with.
3. First he says she wasn't there; then, when that doesn't work, he says it's affectionate and he does everyone, including the MD, including the speaker. What ends it isn't a counter-argument but a question — whether he'd have done it with Aisha in the room — which he answers himself, honestly, and rather badly for him.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for saying an awkward thing to somebody you like, in about ninety seconds, without turning it into an event. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it — guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. The whole difficulty here is tone, because the words are easy and the temptation is to make them heavy. Keep your voice at the volume you'd use to ask him about the football, don't slow down for emphasis, and above all don't build up to it — no "listen, I need to talk to you about something", which turns thirty seconds into a summons and makes him defensive before you've said what it is. And when he goes quiet, let him. The silence is him thinking, and if you fill it you'll end up apologising for the whole conversation.

🚪 Opening it small

Phrase 1

You catch him by the lifts. You want to open this so that he doesn't spend the next ten seconds working out whether he's about to be sacked. Which opener keeps it light and honest at the same time?

A. "Rob, can I have a word?"
B. "Rob — have you got a sec? It's a small thing."
C. "Rob, are you free later? There's something I need to raise with you."
B — "Rob — have you got a sec? It's a small thing."
"A sec" promises this will be short, and "it's a small thing" tells him the size of it before he can guess wrong, which is the whole job of an opener like this. A has a problem: at work, "can I have a word" is almost always the beginning of something serious, so it does the opposite of what you want. C is worse — it schedules the conversation, and anything scheduled arrives with a weight you then have to justify.
Phrase 2

He's asked who complained. Nobody has. You want to tell him this is coming from you and stopping with you — and that you've chosen to speak rather than escalate. Put it in order:

of this.I'd ratherI'm not makingjust say it.a formal thing
"I'm not making a formal thing of this. I'd rather just say it."
"A formal thing" is deliberately vague, because naming the actual process — a complaint, a grievance, HR — would put the idea in the room even as you rejected it. The second half matters as much as the first: "I'd rather just say it" tells him this is a choice you've made and not a favour you're doing him, so he doesn't owe you anything for it and can't feel he's been let off.

🎯 Naming the thing

Phrase 3

Here's the sentence everything rests on. You have to say what it's about, and say that it was wrong, without using any word he'd have to fight. Put it in order:

you saidland well.That thingit didn'tabout Aisha's accent —
"That thing you said about Aisha's accent — it didn't land well."
If a joke or a remark lands, it arrives the way the speaker meant it to; if it doesn't land, it went wrong somewhere between them and the room. That's the useful part, because it lets you say the joke failed without saying he's a bad person, and it's a word comedians use about their own material, so it's a criticism of the work rather than the man. Notice also "that thing you said", which is as small as a description can get.
Phrase 4

He points out, fairly, that everyone laughed — including you. You agree completely, and you still think it's a problem, because a thing can be fine the first time and stop being fine by the ninth. Complete it: "It's the kind of thing that's funny ___ and then ___."

"It's the kind of thing that's funny once and then isn't."
This is the sentence that lets you keep your own laugh and still object, because it says the laugh was real and the repetition is what changed it. It also gives him somewhere to stand: he isn't being told he was wrong that night, he's being told he's still doing it, which is a much easier thing to admit and a much easier thing to stop.
Phrase 5

He's heard "accent" and he's braced for the label — racist, or something near it. You want to take that off the table while still saying the thing that's true. Which line does both?

A. "I'm not saying you're racist or anything, obviously."
B. "I'm not calling you anything. I'm telling you how it sounded."
C. "Some people might take that the wrong way, that's all."
B — "I'm not calling you anything. I'm telling you how it sounded."
It separates the man from the noise he made, which is the only distinction that keeps a conversation like this alive: he can't defend "how it sounded", because that's yours to report, whereas he'd defend a label for twenty minutes. A fails because saying the word puts it in the room, and he'll hear nothing else afterwards. C fails differently — it hides you behind "some people", so he can simply disagree about them and never has to deal with you at all.

🤲 Leaving him his dignity

Phrase 6

Early on, before he's had to defend himself, you want to hand him his good intentions rather than make him claim them. Which line does that most cleanly?

A. "I'm sure you thought it was harmless."
B. "You probably weren't really thinking about it."
C. "I know you didn't mean anything by it."
C — "I know you didn't mean anything by it."
If you don't mean anything by something, there was no intention behind it, good or bad — so the line settles the question of his character before he's had to argue for it, and everything after that can be about the joke. A sounds like you're quoting a defence back at him rather than believing it. B gives him the good intention but takes something else instead, because "you weren't really thinking" is still a criticism, and he'll notice.
Phrase 7

You laughed that night, and he knows it. Rather than hope he forgets, you put it in yourself and split the blame. Complete it: "I laughed at the time, which is my fault ___ ___ ___ yours."

"I laughed at the time, which is my fault as much as yours."
You give away the strongest thing he had before he can use it, and in return you stop being someone who's arrived to judge him. It's also just true, which is why it works — a shared fault is a much better place to stand than a high one, and he can hear it without having to lose.

🪞 Making it land

Phrase 8

He's said it's affectionate and that he does everyone. You could argue about that for ten minutes and get nowhere. Instead you want him to test it himself, by picturing Aisha in the room when he did it. Say your version out loud, then reveal.

"Put it this way — would you have said it if she'd been standing there?"
"Put it this way" announces that you're going to say the same thing from a different angle, which buys you the room's attention for one more sentence. Then the question does the work you can't do: he answers it in his own head, honestly, before he's decided whether to answer it out loud — and once he's seen it, you don't have to prove it, which is why this beats any version where you tell him.

🚶 Closing it

Phrase 9

The point has gone in. He's gone quiet. This is where most people ruin it by carrying on — so instead you stop, and you make the stopping obvious. Put it in order:

I wantedThat's allGenuinely.to say.
"That's all I wanted to say. Genuinely."
It marks the end, so he knows there's no second half coming and doesn't have to stand there waiting for it. "Genuinely" is doing something quite specific: he's half-expecting this to be the small opening of a bigger complaint, and that one word says there isn't one. It only works if you then actually stop talking.
Phrase 10

You've said it, he's taken it, and now there's the risk that he's polite to you for the next three months and never sits with you again. You want to ask for the friendship back — lightly, as a small favour, not a negotiation. Which line manages that?

A. "We're still all right, aren't we?"
B. "Don't be weird with me now, will you?"
C. "I hope this doesn't change things between us."
B — "Don't be weird with me now, will you?"
To be weird with someone is to go quiet and careful around them after something awkward, which is exactly the risk here, and naming it out loud is what stops it happening. The tag — "will you?" — turns an instruction into a small ask between friends, and the joke in it lets you both climb down. A asks him to reassure you, which puts the work back on him at the worst moment. C is too solemn, and solemn is the thing you've spent the whole conversation avoiding.

The Twist

🎯 She's standing right there

Run Round 1 again — except you don't get the corridor. You're in the kitchen at half four, Rob does the voice, four people laugh, and Aisha walks in halfway through it holding a mug. She's heard enough of it to know. She says nothing, makes her tea, and stands there. Rob looks at you, because you're the one who was laughing last time. Start the timer and go, live: everything you'd planned assumed she'd never know and that this could be handled quietly between two of you, and both of those things have just stopped being true. You have to decide, out loud, in front of her, whether you say it now — and if you do, you're no longer having a private word, you're doing it in public, to a friend, in front of the person he was doing. Whatever you choose, you have to mean it and you have to say something. Silence is a choice too, and if you choose it, you'll say why afterwards.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Almost nothing like this ever gets said. Not because people don't notice — they notice — but because the cost of saying it is real and immediate and lands on you, while the cost of saying nothing lands somewhere else, on someone who isn't in the room, in instalments too small to see.

So the thing that stops it is rarely courage in the dramatic sense. It's the size of the conversation. If it has to be a Reckoning, nobody's got one in them on a Tuesday. If it can be thirty seconds by the lifts, more people can manage it than you'd think.

And the small version has one advantage the formal version hasn't: Rob gets to stop, quietly, without anyone knowing he was ever told. That's not a lesser outcome. For a thing like this, it's most of what you actually wanted.

Two bigger questions

  1. You had no standing here — no injury, no authority, and you'd laughed along yourself. Does that make you the wrong person to say something, or the right one? Would Aisha have wanted you to say it at all, and does it matter what she'd have wanted, given nobody asked her?
  2. The whole lesson assumes the quiet word is better than the formal complaint. When is that assumption wrong? Think about who the informal route protects, who has to do the work in it, and what happens to the third or fourth person who tries it with someone who doesn't take it as well as Rob.
