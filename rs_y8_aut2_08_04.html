Islamic Responses to Suffering

YEAR 8 · UNIT 2: THE PROBLEM OF EVIL

What You'll Learn

You will explore Islamic theodicy: how Muslims understand suffering as divine testing, and how concepts of sabr (patience), tawakkul (trust), and the afterlife provide answers to the problem of evil.

Think: Beliefs About Allah

From Unit 1 and earlier lessons:

In Islam, Allah is understood as all-powerful (al-Qadir), all-knowing (al-Alim), and supremely wise (al-Hakim). Allah created everything and has complete control over all events. Given this, how might Muslims understand suffering?

Question: What does the concept of Qadr mean in Islam?

🔒Try to answer from memory!

Answer: Qadr means divine decree or predestination—the idea that Allah has predetermined all events, both good and bad.

Islamic theodicy must reconcile Allah's power and wisdom with human free will and the existence of suffering. The Islamic approach is distinctive and different from Christianity.

Encounter: Quranic Teachings on Suffering

Suffering as a Divine Test

The Quran explicitly teaches that suffering is a test from Allah. This is a key concept in Islamic theodicy:

Quran 2:155: "And certainly We shall test you with something of fear and hunger, and loss of wealth and lives and crops; but give glad tidings to the patient." This verse explicitly frames hardship as a test from Allah to the faithful.

Muslims understand suffering not primarily as punishment (though sin does have consequences), but as an opportunity to demonstrate faith and virtue. A person facing hardship can show steadfastness, trust in Allah, and devotion. This transforms suffering into something spiritually meaningful.

Sabr: Patience and Perseverance

One of the highest Islamic virtues is sabr—often translated as "patience" but meaning more like steadfast perseverance in the face of difficulty. Sabr is not passive acceptance but active, courageous endurance.

The virtue of sabr: Muslims are called to face suffering with dignity, to trust in Allah's wisdom, and to continue fulfilling their duties and obligations even during hardship. Sabr is rewarded by Allah in this life and the next.

Tawakkul: Trust in Allah

Another central Islamic concept is tawakkul—reliance on or trust in Allah. This doesn't mean passivity; rather, it means doing everything in your power while ultimately trusting Allah's plan:

The saying: "Trust in Allah, but tie your camel." This encapsulates tawakkul—you secure your camel (take practical action), but ultimately trust Allah to protect it.

For Muslims facing suffering, tawakkul means: do what you can to address the suffering, seek medical help, work for justice, but trust that Allah's plan is wise even if you don't understand it.

Explore: Suffering and the Afterlife (Akhirah)

Islamic theodicy gives tremendous weight to belief in the afterlife. Suffering in this world must be understood in light of eternal life in the akhirah.

Akhirah: The Day of Judgment

Muslims believe in Yawm al-Qiyamah (Day of Resurrection) when all souls will be judged by Allah. Those who lived righteously and patiently through hardship will be rewarded with eternal paradise (Jannah). Those who rejected Allah or lived wickedly will face punishment in hell (Jahannam).

How This Answers the Problem of Evil

This worldly life (dunya) is temporary and a test. If someone suffers innocently in this life, Allah will compensate them abundantly in the akhirah. If someone causes others to suffer, they will face justice before Allah. Nothing is wasted; all suffering has eternal significance.

An innocent child who dies of disease will be rewarded in paradise. A person who endures oppression with sabr will be honoured by Allah. This transforms the problem: suffering is not unjust if eternal justice will be served.

Allah's Wisdom and Mercy (Hikmah and Rahma)

Islam teaches that Allah is both al-Hakim (the Wise) and ar-Rahman (the Merciful). When Muslims cannot understand why Allah allows suffering, they rely on the belief that:

  • Allah's wisdom is beyond human understanding
  • What appears as evil may be part of a greater good we cannot perceive
  • Allah's mercy is infinite and will ultimately serve justice
  • Suffering can be a means of purification and spiritual growth

Strengths of Islamic Theodicy

  • Suffering has clear purpose (test)
  • Belief in justice in the afterlife
  • Practical virtues (sabr, tawakkul)
  • Recognizes mystery of Allah's wisdom

Challenges

  • Why does an all-wise Allah need to test?
  • Some suffering seems disproportionate to any possible growth
  • Does belief in akhirah fully answer present injustice?
  • How do humans freely choose if Allah predestines all?

Apply: Analyse Islamic Responses

Scenario 1: A teenager losing a parent

A young person's parent dies in an accident. How might Islamic theology help them find meaning? What would the concepts of sabr, tawakkul, and akhirah suggest about this loss?

Scenario 2: Systemic injustice

A group of people faces discrimination and oppression due to their ethnicity or religion. How does the Islamic framework of viewing this as a test affect their response? Does belief in ultimate justice in the akhirah change how they should act?

Scenario 3: Comparing perspectives

Compare the Islamic view of suffering as a "test" with the Christian free will defence. Which do you find more compelling? Why? What are the advantages and disadvantages of each?

Evaluate: Islamic Theodicy Assessed

Why Muslims Find This Compelling

The Islamic framework provides practical guidance (sabr, tawakkul), spiritual meaning (testing and growth), and ultimate justice (akhirah). It encourages both acceptance and action—you accept that Allah is wise while working to reduce suffering. For many Muslims, this offers genuine comfort and direction.

Critical Perspectives

Some philosophers challenge whether calling suffering a "test" truly explains why an all-knowing Allah (who already knows how we'll respond) needs to test us. Others argue that belief in a distant afterlife justice can be used to excuse present injustice. And the tension between divine predestination (qadr) and human free will remains philosophically troubling for some.

Your reflection:

Does the Islamic understanding of suffering as a test resonate with you? What about the emphasis on sabr and tawakkul? How does belief in the afterlife change your perspective on present suffering?

Review: Key Concepts

Suffering as Divine Test (Balaa)

The Quran teaches that hardship and difficulty are tests from Allah. They are opportunities to demonstrate faith, virtue, and steadfastness.

Sabr: Patient Perseverance

A supreme Islamic virtue meaning steadfast, courageous endurance in the face of difficulty. Sabr is rewarded by Allah.

Tawakkul: Trust in Allah

Reliance on Allah's wisdom and mercy. Muslims act to reduce suffering but ultimately trust that Allah's plan is wise, even if incomprehensible.

Akhirah: The Afterlife

Belief in eternal life after judgment by Allah. This worldly suffering is temporary; eternal justice will be served. Innocent suffering will be rewarded in paradise.
