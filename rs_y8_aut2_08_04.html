Islamic Responses to Suffering

What You'll Learn

You will explore how Islam approaches the difficult question of why suffering exists and how Muslims respond to hardship. You will learn about key Islamic concepts: God's sovereignty and predestination (qadr), suffering as a test from Allah (ibtila), patience and trust in God (sabr), and the promise of justice and reward in the afterlife (akhirah). You will also examine the tension between God's all-knowing will and human free choice, and consider how Islamic teaching encourages both spiritual reflection and practical compassion in response to suffering.

Question 1: What is the Problem of Evil, and why is it called a "problem" for religious believers?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: The Problem of Evil asks: If God is all-powerful (omnipotent) and all-good (omnibenevolent), why does evil and suffering exist? It's a "problem" because these three things seem to contradict: if God is all-powerful, God could prevent all suffering; if God is all-good, God would want to prevent all suffering; but suffering clearly exists. Believers must explain how God can have all these qualities while suffering persists.

Question 2: What are the main types of suffering? Give an example of each.

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: (1) Natural suffering: caused by natural disasters and disease (earthquakes, cancer, tsunamis). (2) Moral suffering: caused by human moral evil—cruelty, violence, theft, hatred. (3) Psychological suffering: caused by sadness, anxiety, grief, despair. All three are aspects of the broader human experience of pain and hardship.

Question 3: Explain Augustine's answer to the Problem of Evil. How does his theodicy use the concept of free will?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Augustine argues that God created humans with free will. Moral evil (human wrongdoing) results from misuse of this free will. God allows this suffering because free will is a greater good than a world without choice. We are responsible for our evil actions. This explains moral evil, but Augustine struggles to explain natural suffering fully.

Question 4: How does Irenaeus's "soul-making theodicy" differ from Augustine's approach?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Irenaeus argues that suffering is necessary for spiritual development and character growth. A world without challenge would produce immature, weak souls. God allows suffering not as punishment but as an opportunity for us to develop virtues like courage, compassion, and perseverance. Suffering "makes" us into morally mature beings. This explains why a good God would permit suffering.

Encounter: Islamic Understanding of Suffering

Tawhid and God's Absolute Sovereignty

Central to Islamic belief is tawhid—the belief that there is one God, Allah, and none is worthy of worship but Him. Essential to this belief is God's absolute sovereignty. Allah is al-Qadir (the All-Powerful) and al-Alim (the All-Knowing). Nothing happens in creation without Allah's will. This means that all events—including suffering, hardship, illness, and loss—occur by God's will and knowledge. No detail escapes Allah's awareness or control.

This foundation shapes how Islam understands suffering. Muslims do not see themselves as victims of random, meaningless tragedy. Rather, everything that happens is part of Allah's plan and wisdom, even if humans cannot immediately understand why.

Qadr: Divine Predestination and Will

What is Qadr?

Qadr (قدر) literally means "measure" or "decree." It refers to Allah's divine predestination—the belief that Allah has already determined everything that will occur. This includes times of plenty and hardship, joy and sorrow, health and illness. The Quran states: "Nothing befalls us except what Allah has decreed for us; He is our Protector." (Quran 9:51)

Implications for Suffering

If Allah has predetermined all things, then suffering is not random or meaningless. It is part of Allah's wisdom, even when we don't understand it. This belief brings comfort to many Muslims: they are not abandoned or forgotten by God. Their hardship is known to Allah and is part of a larger purpose.

"Say: 'Nothing will befall us except what Allah has decreed for us; He is our Protector; and in Allah let the believers put their trust.'" (Quran 9:51)

Quran 9:51

Ibtila: Suffering as a Test from Allah

One of the most important Islamic responses to suffering is understanding it as ibtila (ابتلاء)—a test or trial from Allah. The Quran explicitly teaches that God tests believers through hardship. This is not punishment but an opportunity for spiritual growth and evidence of faith.

The Test Concept

Allah tests people through various means: poverty and wealth, illness and health, loss of loved ones, persecution. These trials reveal what is truly in a believer's heart and strengthen their faith. A believer who trusts Allah during hardship demonstrates genuine faith, not faith that exists only during comfortable times.

Scripture on Testing

"Do people think they will be left alone because they say 'We believe' and not be tested?" (Quran 29:2) This verse makes clear that testing is not a sign of God's displeasure but a normal part of being a believer. Those who claim faith will be tested to prove the sincerity of their belief.

Growth Through Trial

Just as muscles grow stronger through exercise, faith deepens through hardship. When a Muslim endures suffering while trusting Allah, their faith becomes stronger, their character more refined, and their closeness to Allah deeper. The test is a blessing disguised in difficulty.

"Do people think they will be left alone because they say 'We believe' and not be tested? We tested those who were before them, so that Allah knows who are truthful and who are liars." (Quran 29:2–3)

Quran 29:2–3

Sabr: Patience and Trust in Allah

In response to suffering, Islam emphasises sabr (صبر)—patience, endurance, and steadfast trust in Allah. Sabr is not passive resignation but active perseverance with faith. It is one of the highest virtues in Islam. The Quran repeatedly praises those who exercise sabr and promises them great reward.

Sabr means: Restraining oneself from complaining, maintaining trust in Allah despite hardship, continuing to obey Allah's commands even through trial, and finding strength in faith. It is both emotional restraint and spiritual determination.

Reward for Sabr

"Those who are patient, their reward will be without limit." (Quran 39:10) The Quran teaches that those who show sabr receive unlimited reward. This is not compensation in this life (those with sabr may continue to suffer) but reward in the akhirah (afterlife). Patience itself becomes its own spiritual achievement.

Ease After Hardship

"Truly, with hardship comes ease. Truly, with hardship comes ease." (Quran 94:5–6) This famous verse is repeated twice for emphasis. It teaches that hardship is not permanent. Allah sends relief and ease, either in this life or the next. The repetition reminds believers that difficulties are temporary and Allah's mercy is certain.

"With hardship comes ease. With hardship comes ease." (Quran 94:5–6)

Quran 94:5–6

The Afterlife (Akhirah) and Justice

A fundamental Islamic belief is that this life is temporary and the afterlife is eternal. This belief profoundly shapes how Muslims respond to suffering in this world. If someone suffers unjustly—through poverty, oppression, illness, loss—the Islamic promise is that Allah will give them justice and reward in the akhirah. The suffering of this life, no matter how intense, is insignificant compared to the eternal life to come.

  • Divine Justice: Allah is perfectly just. Every wrong will be righted. Those who suffer innocently will be rewarded in Paradise. Those who caused suffering will face accountability.
  • Eternal Perspective: This life is like a moment compared to eternity. Believers are encouraged to see suffering not as a permanent tragedy but as a brief test in the context of infinite life.
  • Paradise as Consolation: For those who suffer with patience and faith, Paradise awaits. The Quran describes Paradise in vivid, comforting terms—a place of peace, joy, eternal gardens, reunion with loved ones.

"Every soul shall taste death, and you will be paid in full only on the Day of Resurrection. Whoever is saved from the fire and admitted to Paradise has indeed triumphed." (Quran 3:185)

Quran 3:185

Ikhtiyar: Human Choice Within God's Will

A complex question arises: if Allah has predetermined all things (qadr), do humans have free will? Islamic theology addresses this through the concept of ikhtiyar (اختيار)—human choice. Humans are not passive puppets. Within Allah's overall plan, humans have genuine choice and responsibility for their actions. They choose to obey or disobey, to show patience or complaint, to help others or harm them.

The Balance: Allah's predestination and human freedom are both true, even though they seem contradictory to human reason. Muslims trust that both realities exist in Allah's infinite wisdom. Humans must act as though their choices matter (because they do spiritually and morally) while trusting that Allah knows and has decreed all outcomes.

This means that while suffering may be part of Allah's plan, how a person responds to suffering is their choice. A person can choose sabr (patience) or impatience, faith or despair, compassion or bitterness. These choices are moral and carry spiritual weight.

Sadaqah and Compassionate Action

Islam is not purely a theology of acceptance. Alongside the spiritual responses to suffering (sabr, trust in Allah, patience) comes an active ethical response: sadaqah (صدقة)—charity and compassionate action. Muslims are commanded to help those who suffer, to feed the hungry, care for the sick, and support the poor.

  • Obligatory Charity (Zakat): One of the Five Pillars of Islam is zakat—a mandatory charitable tax on wealth. This ensures that Islamic communities actively address poverty and suffering.
  • Voluntary Charity (Sadaqah): Beyond zakat, Muslims are encouraged to give freely and generously to those in need. The Prophet Muhammad taught that charity can remove sins and bring blessings.
  • Service to Others: Helping a person in distress, visiting the sick, feeding the hungry—these are not merely good deeds but religious obligations that bring one closer to Allah.
Key insight: Islam's response to suffering involves both vertical faith (trust in Allah, sabr, acceptance of qadr) and horizontal compassion (helping others, giving charity, taking action). Believers don't merely accept suffering philosophically; they work to alleviate it in others while trusting Allah for the ultimate outcome.

Evaluate: Comparing Religious Responses to Suffering

You have now studied three religious perspectives on suffering: Christianity (Augustine and Irenaeus) and Islam. Let's compare how these traditions approach the same difficult question.

Comparison: Christian vs. Islamic Responses

Christian Perspective (Augustine & Irenaeus)

Source Bible, theological tradition

Key Idea God is omnibenevolent but allows suffering either because of human free will (Augustine) or for soul-making/character development (Irenaeus).

Response to Suffering Accept suffering as a consequence of free will or a means of spiritual growth. Focus on redemption through Christ.

Islamic Perspective

Source Quran, Islamic theology

Key Idea Allah is all-knowing and all-powerful. Suffering is part of Allah's wisdom (qadr) and serves as a test (ibtila) to strengthen faith.

Response to Suffering Show sabr (patience), trust in Allah, and believe that justice will come in the akhirah (afterlife). Also take compassionate action through charity.

Key Similarities

Theodicy: Making God's Goodness Compatible with Suffering

All three traditions (Augustine, Irenaeus, Islam) attempt to answer the same core question: If God is all-good and all-powerful, why does suffering exist? Each offers a theodicy—a justification of God's goodness in the face of evil.

Spiritual Growth Through Hardship

Both Islam and Irenaeus emphasise that suffering can strengthen faith and develop character. Irenaeus's "soul-making theodicy" and Islam's concept of ibtila (testing) are similar: hardship is an opportunity for spiritual development.

Key Differences

Role of Human Free Will

Augustine emphasises human free will as central to explaining suffering: God allows moral evil because free will is a greater good. Islam balances this: qadr (predestination) coexists with ikhtiyar (human choice), but humans cannot fully understand how both are true.

Divine Action and Predestination

Christian theodicies focus on God's moral character and justice. Islam emphasises Allah's absolute sovereignty and predetermination of all things. The Quran teaches that nothing happens outside Allah's will, whereas Christianity places greater emphasis on human freedom.

Consolation: This Life vs. The Afterlife

Augustine and Irenaeus emphasise redemption and growth in this life. Islam teaches that this life is temporary and the afterlife is eternal. The ultimate consolation comes in the akhirah, where justice will be perfectly realised.

Compassionate Action

While Christianity teaches charity, Islam places it prominently as part of the response to suffering. Zakat (obligatory charity) and sadaqah (voluntary charity) are not merely good deeds but essential religious duties that are part of one's faith.

Summary: The Islamic Response to Suffering

Islam teaches that suffering is:

  • Part of Allah's plan (qadr): Not random or meaningless, but predetermined by an all-knowing God.
  • A test from Allah (ibtila): An opportunity for spiritual growth and evidence of genuine faith.
  • Met with patience (sabr): Trust in Allah, restraint from complaint, and spiritual determination.
  • Temporary in context: This life is brief; the eternal afterlife is the true context for understanding suffering.
  • Addressed through action: Believers respond compassionately by giving charity and helping those in need.

The goal: To help believers find meaning, spiritual strength, and hope in the face of hardship, while also inspiring them to alleviate suffering in others.
