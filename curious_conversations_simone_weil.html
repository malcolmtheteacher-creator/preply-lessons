The Philosopher Who Worked in a Factory

Simone Weil: When Ideas Meet Real Life

C1-C2 Level | 50 minutes | 5 Key Expressions

Would You Give Up Everything to Understand?

Albert Camus called her "the only great spirit of our time." T.S. Eliot said she had "genius akin to the saints." Who was this woman?

In 1928, a 19-year-old woman took the entrance exam for France's most prestigious philosophy school. She came first place. The person who came second? Simone de Beauvoir — who would become one of the most famous philosophers of the 20th century.

But here's what makes Simone Weil truly extraordinary: she didn't just want to think about ideas. She wanted to live them.

At age 5, she refused to eat sugar because French soldiers fighting in World War I had none. At 6, she was already quoting the 17th-century poet Racine. And at 25, as a university professor, she did something shocking: she quit her prestigious teaching job to work in a car factory.

For 12 months, she worked grueling factory shifts, earning minimum wage, just to understand what workers actually felt.

The Factory Year

Weil kept a detailed journal of her experience. She wrote about the exhaustion, the fear of being fired, the "terrible mallet blows" of the machines, and finishing days "weeping violently." But she also discovered something profound: most people who write about workers have never experienced their reality.

She wrote: "One can no more be a revolutionary by words alone than one can be a mason or a blacksmith."

Later, despite being a pacifist, she traveled to Spain during the Civil War to join the fight against fascism. Her comrades spent most of their time trying to keep her away from guns because she was so clumsy. Her time there ended when she accidentally stepped into a pot of boiling cooking oil.

Her Most Famous Idea

Weil believed that truly paying attention to another person is the rarest and most generous act we can perform. She wrote:

"Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity."

In a world of constant distraction, this idea feels more relevant than ever.

Think About It:

  • Would you give up a prestigious career to truly understand something?
  • Have you ever changed your mind about something after experiencing it firsthand?
  • Do you agree that attention is a form of generosity?

Expressing Interest & Fascination

Click each expression to see how it's used

1. "I'm drawn to..."
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: Something attracts or interests you in a compelling way
Example:
"I'm drawn to Weil's commitment to living her philosophy. Most intellectuals just theorize, but she actually worked in a factory."
NOW YOU TRY:
Complete this: "I'm drawn to people who..." or "I'm drawn to the idea of..."
2. "What strikes me about... is..."
Formality: Neutral to Formal
Meaning: Something makes a strong impression on you; you find it noteworthy
Example:
"What strikes me about Weil is how she refused comfort even as a child — refusing sugar at age 5 in solidarity with soldiers."
NOW YOU TRY:
"What strikes me about [a person/idea/situation you know] is..."
3. "It's made me rethink..."
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: Something has changed your perspective or made you reconsider a belief
Example:
"Learning about Weil's factory experience has made me rethink how easily we judge jobs we've never done ourselves."
NOW YOU TRY:
"Reading/Hearing/Experiencing [something] has made me rethink..."

Exploring Complex Ideas

4. "There's something to be said for..."
Formality: Neutral to Formal
Meaning: You acknowledge that an idea has merit, even if you're not fully convinced
Example:
"There's something to be said for Weil's extreme approach — she really understood factory workers in a way academics never could."
NOW YOU TRY:
"There's something to be said for [an approach/idea that you can partly agree with]..."
5. "I can't help but wonder..."
Formality: Neutral
Meaning: You're curious about something and naturally find yourself thinking about it
Example:
"When I read about Weil's life, I can't help but wonder — would she have made the same choices if she'd lived to see our digital age?"
NOW YOU TRY:
"I can't help but wonder [something you're genuinely curious about]..."

All 5 Expressions:

  • "I'm drawn to..." — Express attraction to an idea/quality
  • "What strikes me about... is..." — Share what impresses you
  • "It's made me rethink..." — Show changed perspective
  • "There's something to be said for..." — Acknowledge merit in complex ideas
  • "I can't help but wonder..." — Express natural curiosity

🎓 Theory vs. Experience
Weil believed you can't truly understand something until you experience it. Do you agree? Can we understand poverty, illness, or hardship without living through it?
👀 The Gift of Attention
Weil wrote that "attention is the rarest form of generosity." In the age of smartphones, how often do we give someone our complete, undivided attention?
🔥 Living Your Values
How far would you go to live according to your principles? Would you give up comfort, status, or safety for what you believe in?
🏭 Hidden Lives
Weil discovered that people who write about workers rarely understand their reality. What jobs or lives do we think we understand — but probably don't?
🧠 When Thinking Changes
Has anything ever made you completely rethink your beliefs or assumptions? What was it, and how did it change you?
⚖️ Extreme Commitment
Weil's choices were extreme — even harmful to her health. Where's the line between admirable dedication and self-destructive behavior?
