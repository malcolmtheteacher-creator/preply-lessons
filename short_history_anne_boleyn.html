Anne Boleyn

The clever, ambitious woman for whom a king broke with Rome — and who lost her head a thousand days later.

Today: you'll tell the story of the woman who reshaped a country and then fell faster than anyone at court thought possible — and you'll master the language of looking back at a mystery and weighing up what must have, might have or can't have happened.

Here are the ten words you'll meet in the story. Work through the four activities below out loud, and only press Reveal once you've committed to an answer.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean here?

1. She had spent years at the glittering French court, and she came home speaking of nothing but its fashions, its music and its elegant refinement; beside her, the other ladies suddenly seemed rough and provincial.
refinement (n) — polished good taste and elegant manners.
2. For nearly seven years the king pursued her, and she would not simply yield; her steady refusal only made him more infatuated, until he could think of little else.
infatuated (adj) — overwhelmed by an intense, often irrational, romantic obsession.
3. There was only one obstacle: Henry was already married. Before he could take a new wife, he needed the Church to annul his first marriage, and only the Pope had the power to do that.
to annul (v) — to declare officially that a marriage was never legally valid.

B. Meaning → word — which word from the story fits?

4. Which word means "the crime of trying to overthrow or betray your ruler or country"? It begins with t.
treason (n) — the crime of betraying your country or attacking the authority of its ruler. e.g. She was condemned to death for treason.
5. Which word means "unfaithfulness to your husband or wife, by having a relationship with someone else"? It begins with a.
adultery (n) — a sexual or romantic affair with someone who is not your husband or wife. e.g. The charges against her included adultery.
6. Which word means "the wife of a reigning king (or the husband of a reigning queen)"? It begins with c.
consort (n) — the wife or husband of a reigning monarch. e.g. As queen consort, she was crowned in a lavish ceremony.

C. Synonym / antonym — the sense of the word

7. If a person is shrewd, they are close in meaning to which of these — naive, astute, careless?
shrewd (adj) ≈ astute — sharply clever in a practical, calculating way, quick to judge people and turn a situation to your advantage.
8. A biddable person is the opposite of which — obedient, headstrong, gentle?
biddable (adj) — meekly willing to do what you are told; the opposite of headstrong (wilful and hard to control).

D. Multiple choice in context — pick the meaning

9. "Court gossip said the queen had miscarried a son, and the whole palace fell silent." Here, miscarried most likely means: (a) delivered a healthy baby; (b) lost a pregnancy before the baby could live; (c) hidden a baby away.
(b) — to miscarry (v) is to lose a pregnancy before the baby is able to survive. It mattered enormously here, because the king desperately wanted a son.
10. "The charges were almost certainly fabricated to be rid of her." Here, fabricated most likely means: (a) proven in court; (b) whispered in secret; (c) invented and false.
(c) — to fabricate (v) is to invent something false, especially evidence or a story, in order to deceive.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The king who would change a kingdom to marry her

Anne Boleyn was born around 1501 into an ambitious English family with useful connections at court. As a girl she was sent abroad, and she spent formative years in the household of the French queen, absorbing the refinement, the wit and the fashions of the most glamorous court in Europe. When she returned to England, she stood out at once: dark-eyed, quick-tongued, stylish and clever. She was not conventionally the greatest beauty of the court, but few could match her for spirit.

King Henry VIII noticed her — and wanted her. But Anne would not become simply another royal mistress, as her own sister already had. She held him at a distance, and the more she withheld, the more infatuated the king became. For years he pursued her, showering her with letters and gifts, until his desire became entangled with a much larger problem: his marriage to Catherine of Aragon had produced a daughter but no surviving son, and Henry was desperate for a male heir.

Henry became convinced he must set Catherine aside and marry Anne. The trouble was that only the Pope could annul a royal marriage — and the Pope would not. What followed changed England forever: rather than obey Rome, Henry broke with the Catholic Church altogether, declared himself Supreme Head of the Church in England, and made his own annulment possible. In 1533, Anne, already pregnant, was married to the king and crowned queen. A woman's refusal had, quite literally, helped to remake a nation's religion.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Anne stand out when she returned to the English court, and how did she handle the king's attention?

2. What enormous change did Henry make in order to be free to marry her?

1. Years at the French court had given her refinement, wit and style; she refused to become just a mistress, and her distance made the king more infatuated.

2. Because the Pope would not annul his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, Henry broke with Rome, made himself head of the Church in England, and married Anne in 1533.

Discuss:
  • A whole country's religion changed partly because of one marriage. When is a huge national decision really about the nation — and when is it really about one person's private wishes?

Part 2 — Queen for a thousand days

Anne was now queen consort, and for a moment she stood at the summit. But the marriage that had cost so much rested on a single hope: a son. In September 1533 she gave birth — not to the prince Henry craved, but to a daughter, Elizabeth. Publicly the king celebrated; privately, the pressure only grew. Anne was intelligent and outspoken, with strong opinions on religion and reform, and she made powerful enemies among those who had backed Catherine or resented her influence.

Over the next two years the ground shifted beneath her. She is thought to have suffered at least one miscarriage, and possibly more; a lost pregnancy in early 1536, of what may have been a son, was a devastating blow. Henry's eye had already begun to wander towards one of Anne's own ladies-in-waiting, the quiet, biddable Jane Seymour — in almost every way Anne's opposite, and now her rival. The king who had turned the world upside down to marry Anne was, astonishingly quickly, tiring of her.

Around the king moved men who read his mood and acted on it — above all his chief minister, Thomas Cromwell, once Anne's ally and now, as their interests clashed, her enemy. What exactly passed between the king and his ministers in the spring of 1536 remains uncertain. But the outcome was not: it was decided that Anne would be removed. And in Tudor England, removing an inconvenient queen did not mean a quiet divorce. It meant something far darker.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the whole marriage ultimately depend on, and what did Anne actually give birth to in 1533?

2. What was changing in Henry's feelings, and who had emerged as Anne's rival?

1. It depended on Anne producing a male heir; instead she gave birth to a daughter, the future Elizabeth I. A later miscarriage in 1536 was a serious blow.

2. Henry was losing interest and had turned his attention to Jane Seymour, one of Anne's own ladies-in-waiting, who became her rival.

Discuss:
  • Anne's whole position depended on giving the king a son — something no one can control. How dangerous is it to build your power on a thing you cannot guarantee?

Part 3 — The trial and the Tower

In May 1536 the trap closed. Anne was suddenly arrested and taken to the Tower of London, accused of adultery with several men — and even of a relationship with her own brother — and, because betraying the king was treason, of plotting his death. The charges were almost certainly fabricated, built on court gossip, coincidence and confessions extracted under pressure. The men accused alongside her were condemned; one may have admitted guilt, the others denied everything. Anne, tried before a court that included men who wished her gone, was found guilty. The verdict, in truth, had been decided before the trial began.

She met her fate with a composure that impressed even her enemies. On 19 May 1536, on Tower Green, she was executed — not by the common axe but by a swordsman brought specially from France, a small mercy granted by the king who had once remade a kingdom for her. She was perhaps thirty-five years old, and had been queen for barely a thousand days. Within days, Henry was betrothed to Jane Seymour.

Yet the story did not end at the scaffold. The daughter dismissed as a disappointment, the infant Elizabeth, would grow up to become Elizabeth I — one of the most formidable rulers England ever had, presiding over an age that still bears her name. The woman executed as a traitor became, in time, the mother of a golden age, and one of the most argued-over figures in English history: schemer or victim, home-wrecker or reformer, or something more complicated than any single label allows.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Anne accused of, and how sound were the charges against her?

2. How did the story of Anne's "failure" of a daughter turn out in the end?

1. She was accused of adultery (including with her brother) and of treason for plotting the king's death; the charges were almost certainly fabricated, and the guilty verdict was effectively decided in advance.

2. That daughter, Elizabeth, became Elizabeth I, one of England's greatest monarchs, giving her name to an entire golden age.

Discuss:
  • History remembers Anne in wildly different ways — as a victim, a schemer, or the mother of a great queen. Why do we feel such a strong need to give people from the past one clear label?

Grammar — must have / might have / can't have + past participle

So much of Anne's story is a mystery we can only reason about from the outside. Were the charges real? Did Henry believe them? English has a precise set of tools for exactly this — looking back at the past and saying how certain you are about what happened. They are the modals of past speculation.

The pattern is always the same: modal + have + past participle.
• must have + past participle — you are almost certain it did happen, judging from the evidence: "The verdict was ready in advance, so the trial must have been a formality."
• might have / may have / could have + past participle — it is possible; you are not sure either way: "She might have made powerful enemies at court." "The confession may have been forced."
• can't have / couldn't have + past participle — you are almost certain it did not happen; the evidence rules it out: "A court chosen to convict her can't have given her a fair trial."
Feel the difference:
• must have = near-certain YES (the evidence points firmly to it).
• might / may / could have = MAYBE (it's one real possibility among others).
• can't have = near-certain NO (the evidence rules it out). Note: for the negative of certainty we say can't have, never "mustn't have".

1. Which modal? Choose from must have, might have, or can't have

Read the meaning in brackets and complete the sentence.

1. The evidence was so thin that the judges genuinely believed she was guilty. (near-certain NO)

2. Breaking with the entire Catholic Church been an agonising decision for Henry. (near-certain YES)

3. Anne hoped, right up to the end, that the king would show mercy. (it's possible; we can't be sure)

1. can't have (near-certain the judges did not truly believe it)

2. must have (the evidence strongly implies it)

3. might have (a genuine possibility, not a certainty)

1. Henry must (know) that the charges were weak.

2. The gossip might have (be) enough to condemn her.

3. A court like that can't have (give) her a fair hearing.

1. have known (must + have + past participle)

2. been (might have + past participle)

3. given (can't have + past participle)

Fill each gap with must have, might have or can't have (+ the right past participle). Read it aloud once you're done.

We will never know the full truth. The charges were so weak that they been true in every detail. Cromwell, once her ally, turned against her when their interests clashed — that seems very likely.
