Waterfall to Agile: A Fundamental Shift

If you've been working in traditional (waterfall) project management, agile represents a fundamentally different way of thinking about work.

Reflect:

  • Have you worked in waterfall? What were the pain points?
  • Have you worked in agile? What were the differences?
  • What's your gut reaction to agile? Energising or chaotic?
  • Do you think agile works everywhere, or only in certain contexts?

Waterfall: Plan Then Execute

Traditional project management plans comprehensively upfront. You gather all requirements, build a detailed project plan, estimate everything, lock it down, then execute. Discovery and execution are separate.

This works when: requirements are clear, environment is stable, change is unlikely, the cost of change is extremely high (building a bridge).

This breaks when: requirements aren't clear, the environment is changing, customers want to learn as they go, and speed matters.

Agile: Learn While Building

Agile inverts the process. You have a vision, you start building in small increments, you get feedback, you adjust. You discover requirements through building, not beforehand.

This works when: requirements are unclear, change is constant, learning is part of the process, getting something to market quickly matters.

This breaks when: requirements must be locked down, comprehensive planning is necessary, or the cost of discovering things late is catastrophic.

The Agile Mindset: Key Shifts

From "get it right upfront" to "learn as we go": You won't know everything before you start. That's okay. Build incrementally, get feedback, adjust.

From "comprehensive documentation" to "working software": The goal isn't perfect documentation. It's working product that delivers value.

From "change is bad" to "change is expected": Requirements will change. Technology will change. Customers will learn what they actually want. Flexibility is a feature.

From "individuals and tools" to "people and interactions": Processes and tools matter, but how people communicate and collaborate matters more.

From "command and control" to "self-organising teams": Teams are trusted to figure out how to do the work. The manager's job is to protect them from external chaos, not to control them.

Consider: In your industry/context, does agile make sense? Where would it break? Where would it thrive?

Vocabulary & Ceremonies

Master the language and rituals of agile and Scrum.

Sprint Fixed time-box (usually 1-2 weeks) where a team commits to deliver specific work; fundamental agile building block.
Backlog Prioritised list of work to be done; evolving list of features, fixes, and improvements the product needs.
Stand-up Brief daily meeting (15 min) where team syncs on progress; usually: what I did, what I'm doing, blockers.
Retrospective Reflection meeting at sprint end; team discusses what went well, what didn't, what to improve next sprint.
User Story Description of work from user perspective: "As a [user], I want [feature] so that [benefit]"; drives prioritisation and understanding.
Velocity Measure of work completed per sprint; used for planning future sprints and predicting timelines.
MVP Minimum Viable Product; smallest product that delivers core value to customers; gets feedback early.
Iterate Repeat cycle; build, release, get feedback, improve; fundamental agile pattern for continuous improvement.

The Agile Cycle: Sprints in Action

Sprint Planning (2-4 hours for 2-week sprint): Team looks at backlog, understands priorities, commits to what they can do this sprint. Discussions about how to approach work, what risks exist, how to handle dependencies.

Daily Stand-ups (15 minutes): Each person: what did I do yesterday, what am I doing today, any blockers? Not a status meeting for the manager. It's for the team to coordinate.

Sprint Work (10 days): Team executes committed work. They solve problems, write code, test, collaborate. The goal is to have "done" work at the end—shippable, tested, integrated.

Sprint Review (1-2 hours): Team shows what they built to stakeholders. Gets feedback. Learns what's actually valuable vs. what was assumed.

Retrospective (1-1.5 hours): Team reflects. What went well? What was hard? What should we change? Actions to improve next sprint.

Repeat: Next sprint starts the next day. Continuous cycle of building, learning, improving.

Scenario: Run a Sprint Retrospective

Your team just finished a sprint that didn't go well. A critical feature took longer than expected. A team member was frustrated with unclear requirements. Morale is a bit low. You're running the retrospective and you need to make it constructive, honest, and focused on improvement—not blame.

10:00

Part 1: Setting the Tone

You open the retrospective. Your goal is to create psychological safety so people will be honest. Write your opening statement. How do you frame this? What tone do you set?

Effective opening: "This sprint was tough. We're behind on a key feature, and I know frustration is high. That's okay. That's real. Today I want to understand what happened—not to blame anyone, but to learn. We all own what happened, and we all get to figure out what's next. I'm going to be honest, and I ask you to be too. Sound good?"

Why this works: You acknowledge the reality (tough sprint), you normalise frustration (that's okay), you set the tone (this is learning, not blame), you take accountability, and you invite honesty.

Part 2: The Conversation

You ask: "What went well this sprint?" and "What was hard?" What questions would you ask to dig deeper? How would you handle if someone blames another person or says "nothing went well"?

Follow-up to "what went well": "What did that person/team do to make that successful?" (Builds on positives, gets specific.)

Follow-up to "what was hard": "What would have made it easier?" "What do we control vs. don't control?" (Moves from complaint to solution.)

If someone blames another person: "I hear that. Say more about the situation. What happened?" (Not defending the other person, just getting context. Then later: "What could we have done differently to prevent that?")

If "nothing went well": "I get that it's frustrating. Let me ask differently: what's one thing we did that wasn't broken?" Even small wins matter. Focus there.

Part 3: Actions & Commitments

The retro is only useful if it leads to change. You need concrete actions. Write 2-3 actions you'd commit to as a team based on the problems discussed. They need to be specific and achievable.

Good action items: Not "communicate better" (vague). Instead: "I will write a 1-page spec for each feature before dev starts" or "We will do a requirements walkthrough before sprint planning" or "Whoever's blocked notifies immediately instead of waiting".

Assign ownership: Each action has an owner. Not the team. A person. "Sarah will create the spec template, and we'll use it starting next sprint."

Review them in next retro: Did we do this? Did it help? Keep iterating.

Important: Don't overload. 2-3 actions max. More than that and nothing changes.

Agile in Reality

Agile is powerful, but it's not magic. And it's not universal. Understanding when and how to apply it matters.

Reflection questions:

  • Have you experienced agile burnout? What causes it?
  • When does agile make work better? When does it add friction?
  • How does agile work when teams are distributed across time zones?
  • What's the difference between "doing agile" (following rituals) and "being agile" (embracing the mindset)?
  • Where's agile being misapplied in your experience?

Agile Buzzword Fatigue

Agile has been around long enough that it's suffering from buzzword fatigue. Some organisations claim to "be agile" while still operating in very traditional ways. They have stand-ups but still micromanage. They do sprints but still lock requirements. This is "agile theater"—the rituals without the mindset.

True agility requires trust, autonomy, and willingness to change. If your culture doesn't support those, agile ceremonies just add meetings without changing outcomes.

Agile Outside Software

Agile started in software but it's spreading. Marketing teams do agile sprints. HR does agile hiring. Finance teams iterate on budgets.

The principles are universal: short cycles, feedback, iteration, continuous improvement. Where you have: uncertainty about requirements, a need to learn fast, and ability to release incrementally, agile works.

Where you have: locked requirements, high cost of change, and long validation cycles, agile is friction.

The Future of Project Management

Agile isn't replacing waterfall—they're converging. Hybrid approaches are common: some work that needs structure and planning, some work that needs iteration and learning.

The real shift is that leaders are learning to manage uncertainty. They're building capability to adapt, to learn fast, to iterate. Whether you call it agile or something else is less important than whether you can actually do it.

Action step: If you manage a team doing agile, run a retro focused on "are we actually getting benefit from agile?" Be honest. If the answer is "we're just doing the rituals," what would you change?
