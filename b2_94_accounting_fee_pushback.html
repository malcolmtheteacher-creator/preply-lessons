Handling Pushback on Fees

B2 Level · Accounting · Client Negotiation

Accounting & Audit · Dewey 657

First Thoughts

A client you have worked with for years has pushed back on this year’s fees. The relationship matters. So does the price. How do you hold the line without losing the client?

Fee conversations feel personal, but the best ones are structured. In this lesson, you practise defending the fee with calm, specific language — not apology.

Take a moment with each question below. Share as much detail as you like — specific examples, what you would actually say, what tripped you up last time.

1. When was the last time a client complained about your fees? What did they actually say?

2. What is the difference between a client who wants a discount and a client who wants to feel listened to?

3. Do you think long-standing clients deserve a special fee? Why or why not?

4. How do you feel when you get a bill you think is too high? What do you want the other person to say?

Quick opener — pick whichever you prefer:

  • Someone tells you: “Your fees are higher than last year.” What is the first thing you want to know before you respond?
  • A partner once said: “Never negotiate a fee in the same email the client complained.” Do you agree?

Key Vocabulary

Eight words and phrases you will actually use in this conversation. Click each card to reveal the meaning, then say the word aloud and try to use it in a sentence from your own experience.

to justify (a fee)To explain clearly why the amount is fair, based on scope, complexity, or time.
scopeThe amount and type of work agreed for the fee. Often shown as a list of deliverables.
a fee estimate / a quoteThe price given at the start of the work — usually a prediction, not a fixed contract.
to push back (on something)To disagree politely and ask for a change. Common in business English.
value for moneyThe feeling that what you paid is worth what you got.
line itemA single entry on an invoice — one specific task or cost.
goodwillThe positive feeling built between client and firm over time. Can be spent, damaged, or earned.
to stand behind (a fee / a decision)To defend it confidently because you believe it is fair.

Key Language

Useful chunks of language for this conversation. Read each phrase aloud and think about when you would actually reach for it.

Listening first

  • “Thanks for being direct with me — I would rather hear this now than at the end of the year.”
  • “Before I respond, can I ask you what specifically feels high to you?”
  • “Help me understand what has changed for you this year.”

Defending the fee calmly

  • “The fee reflects the additional complexity we took on in Q2 — let me show you where.”
  • “I stand behind this number. Let me walk you through the line items.”
  • “The scope this year was about fifteen per cent larger than last year — the fee is in line with that.”

Offering flexibility without folding

  • “I cannot move on the fee itself, but I can look at the payment terms.”
  • “What I can do is agree a fixed fee for next year, so you are not surprised again.”
  • “I would rather talk about scope than price. If you need me to take something out, that is a different conversation.”

Closing calmly

  • “I value this relationship — that is exactly why I want us to be clear now, not at year-end.”
  • “Let me send you a written summary of what we discussed, and we pick this up Friday.”
  • “Thank you for being honest. I would rather work through this than have it sitting between us.”

The Scenario

The situation: You have audited this client for six years. The invoice for this year’s audit is about eleven per cent higher than last year. The CFO — who you get on with personally — has emailed: “I need to talk to you about your fees. I am not happy.”

Your task: Call the CFO back. By the end of a ten-minute conversation the CFO should feel (a) listened to, (b) that the fee is defensible, and (c) that the relationship is still strong — whether or not anything changes.

Step 2 · Deliver

When you are ready, role-play the conversation out loud. Be ready to handle the follow-up questions below.

Follow-up questions to handle:

  • Firm X quoted me twenty per cent less. Why are you so much more expensive?
  • You never told me the fee was going up this year.
  • Can you do me a one-off goodwill discount of ten per cent?
  • What will the fee look like next year? I cannot keep having this conversation.

Reflection

The most important part. What did you notice, what surprised you, what will you try differently next week?

1. Which was hardest — staying calm, or staying specific? Why?

2. When you defended the fee, did your voice sound certain? Try it again.

3. What is the difference between “I am sorry the fee is high” and “I understand this feels high”? Which is better, and why?

4. If you had to say yes to a small concession to save the relationship, what would you give up first?
