Crisis Communication

C2 Level · Strategic Communication

When Things Go Wrong

Corporate crises test the limits of communication. Some companies emerge with reputation intact; others disintegrate under scrutiny. The difference is rarely luck. It is strategic communication under pressure.

Consider how different organisations have navigated product failures. Some respond swiftly with transparency and accountability—and recover. Others deny, deflect, or delay—and spiral into reputational catastrophe. The Tylenol crisis (1982) is still taught as a masterclass in doing it right: Johnson & Johnson prioritised consumer safety over profit, withdrew products proactively, and communicated honestly throughout.

Contrast this with examples where communication failed: delayed acknowledgement, contradictory statements, apparent indifference to harm. Credibility, once lost, becomes extraordinarily difficult to restore.

Consider: Think of a recent corporate crisis. How did the company's communication shape your perception? What did they do well, or poorly? When do you distrust a company's response to bad news?

Crisis Communication: Language & Framework

Effective crisis communication operates within a disciplined framework that balances urgency, transparency, and accountability.

Vocabulary Cards

Mitigate To reduce in severity or seriousness; in crisis communication, to minimise harm and demonstrate corrective action.
Accountability Responsibility for actions; the explicit acceptance of ownership and commitment to corrective measures.
Transparent Open and honest; communication that discloses information without concealment or strategic omission.
Liability Legal or financial responsibility for damage or harm; the exposure an organisation faces following a crisis.
Remediation The action of remedying a problem; concrete steps taken to address harm and restore trust.
Contingency A plan prepared in advance for possible future events; crisis plans and backup protocols.
Stakeholder Any party with an interest in the organisation's wellbeing: customers, employees, investors, regulators, communities.
Reputational Pertaining to public perception and standing; damage to reputation is often more costly than financial loss.

The AAA Framework: Acknowledge, Apologise, Act

Acknowledge the crisis swiftly. Silence amplifies panic and breeds speculation. Acknowledgement demonstrates that leadership is aware and engaged. This need not be a full explanation—you don't yet have one—but must be immediate. "We are aware of the situation affecting our customers. We are investigating urgently and will provide updates hourly."

Apologise sincerely for the impact on stakeholders, regardless of intent. This is not a legal admission of guilt; it is an acknowledgement that harm has occurred. "We deeply regret that our product failed to meet the safety standards our customers deserve." Avoid deflection: never say, "We're sorry if anyone was upset." Say, "We are sorry for the harm this has caused."

Act with visible urgency. Communicate specific steps: investigation teams assembled, corrective measures underway, compensation being processed, systems being retrofitted. Vagueness erodes confidence. Specificity—even if the measures are preliminary—demonstrates control and competence.

Throughout: Prioritise transparency over liability concerns (your legal team will advise). Repeated, contradictory statements destroy trust far more severely than acknowledging initial uncertainty. Update frequently, even if updates are "We continue investigating" rather than final conclusions.

The Press Conference: Product Recall

Scenario: Your company manufactures consumer appliances. A manufacturing defect in your flagship model has caused injury to three users. The incidents are being reported by media as "dangerous design flaw." You must hold a press conference immediately to shape the narrative before it spirals.

Your Press Statement Checklist

Open with immediate acknowledgement of the incidents and concern for those affected
Announce the recall decision swiftly—do not wait for full investigation
Explain the specific issue and what made it possible (factual, not defensive)
Describe concrete remediation: how customers can report, what compensation they'll receive, timeline
Communicate systemic improvements being implemented to prevent recurrence
Avoid: legal jargon, deflection, blame-shifting, or appearing to minimise harm
Draft a concise written statement (200–250 words) for distribution
10:00

Your Statement + Delivery

Compose and rehearse your opening statement (3–4 minutes when read). Then imagine a hostile press environment. Prepare for questions about negligence, executive accountability, and whether this reflects systemic failures. Practice staying composed and on-message while seeming genuinely concerned.

Reflection: Cancel Culture & Corporate Responsibility

Modern crises operate in an accelerated information environment. A product failure can become a viral narrative within hours. Corporate response must be equally swift, yet considered.

Questions for Reflection: How do you distinguish between legitimate accountability and disproportionate "cancellation"? When is a company's failure unforgivable? Can reputation be restored, or do some crises permanently damage a brand?

The concept of "cancel culture" complicates crisis communication. Sometimes public backlash is justified—a company genuinely violated ethics or safety. Sometimes it is disproportionate to actual harm. Sometimes it reflects systemic bias: identical failures by similar companies receive vastly different scrutiny depending on political context.

Strategic crisis communication acknowledges this reality. It operates with genuine accountability where warranted, but also with clarity about what you can and cannot control. You cannot manufacture forgiveness. You can only demonstrate authentic commitment to remediation and systemic change.

The companies that recover most successfully from crises are those that learn and implement real structural changes—not merely communicate that they have. Stakeholders eventually perceive whether change is genuine. Communication without action is ultimately hollow.

Final Reflection: Recall a company you once distrusted but whose subsequent actions restored credibility. What specifically changed? Or conversely, a company you lost faith in permanently—was there anything they could have done to recover?
