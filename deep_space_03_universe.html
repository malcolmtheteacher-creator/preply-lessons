Lesson 3 · Space itself

The Expanding Universe

The universe is 13.8 billion years old, yet we can see 46 billion light-years in every direction. Space itself is stretching, and it is the one idea that explains everything else on this page.

The edge of what we can see
46.5 billion light-years
That is the distance to the edge of the observable universe in every direction. But the universe is only 13.8 billion years old, and nothing travels faster than light. How can we see farther than light has had time to travel?
Resolve the puzzle. If light has only been travelling for 13.8 billion years, how can the source of that light now be 46.5 billion light-years away?
The space between us and the source has been stretching while the light was on its way. The light left a galaxy that was much closer to our position then, and by the time it reaches us, expansion has carried that galaxy far beyond. The 46.5 billion is where the source is now, not the distance the light travelled.
If the universe began with a Big Bang, where in space did it happen?
Everywhere. It was not an explosion in a pre-existing space. It was the rapid expansion of space itself, at every point at once. There is no centre to point at, and every place was “the middle”. You will test this yourself in the sandbox.
If the universe contains countless stars in every direction, why is the night sky dark?
The universe has a finite age. This is Olbers' paradox, and it was an important clue. If the universe were infinite, eternal and unchanging, every line of sight would eventually end on a star, and the whole sky would blaze like a star's surface. It doesn't, because light from far enough away hasn't had time to reach us yet, and because expansion stretches what does arrive to longer, weaker wavelengths.
Part 1

The yardstick

A light-year is the distance light travels in a year: about 9.46 trillion km. Look up and you see the past. The Moon is 1.3 seconds ago. The Sun is 8 minutes ago. The nearest other star, Proxima Centauri, is 4.2 years ago. Andromeda, the nearest big galaxy, is 2.5 million years ago, when the first stone tools were being chipped in Africa. The more distant the object, the further back in time you look. An astronomer is also a historian.

To use the yardstick, you need to know how far things are. In 1912 Henrietta Leavitt, working as a human “computer” at Harvard, found that a type of pulsating star, the Cepheid variables, has a pulse rate that tells you its true brightness. Compare that with how bright it looks and you can calculate the distance. It was the first reliable step on what is now called the cosmic distance ladder.

Part 2

Everything is moving away

From 1912 Vesto Slipher measured the spectra of spiral nebulae and found their light shifted towards the red, so they seemed to be moving away from us. In 1927 the Belgian priest and physicist Georges Lemaître worked out from Einstein's equations that the universe should be expanding, and that the further away a galaxy is, the faster it should recede. In 1929 Edwin Hubble, using Leavitt's stars to measure distances, confirmed it with data. Since 2018 the relationship has officially been called the Hubble–Lemaître law:

v = H0 × d

The speed of recession is proportional to distance. H0 is about 70 km/s for every megaparsec (3.26 million light-years). A galaxy 100 Mpc away recedes at about 7,000 km/s. Double the distance, double the speed.

This is not galaxies flying through space away from us. They hardly move through space at all. Space between them is growing. Think of a loaf of raisin bread rising in the oven: every raisin sees every other raisin moving away, and the further away, the faster. No raisin is the centre.

The light is stretched, too, as the space it travels through expands. This is cosmological redshift, and it is measured by z: wavelength observed = wavelength emitted × (1 + z). A galaxy at z = 1 had light that doubled in wavelength on the way here, and the universe was half its present size when the light left.

If you run the expansion backwards, everything converges on a hot, dense beginning. Setting that beginning at 1/H0 gives about 14 billion years, close to the measured age of 13.8 billion years.

Part 3

The afterglow

For the first 380,000 years, the universe was a hot glowing fog of free electrons and nuclei that scattered light, like the inside of a star. When it cooled to about 3,000 °C, the electrons and nuclei combined into atoms, and the light was suddenly free to travel. That light is still arriving from every direction, now stretched by a factor of about 1,100 into microwaves at 2.725 K. It is called the cosmic microwave background, the CMB.

It was found by accident in 1964 by two radio engineers, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson at Bell Labs in New Jersey, who could not get rid of a faint hiss in their horn antenna. They cleaned out the pigeon droppings and the hiss was still there. They shared the 1978 Nobel Prize for the discovery.

The CMB is smooth to about one part in 100,000. Those tiny hot and cold spots are the seeds of every galaxy and cluster in the universe: slightly denser patches that gravity then pulled together. Satellites such as COBE, WMAP and Planck have mapped them in increasing detail.

Three pieces of evidence for the Big Bang. (1) Galaxies recede in proportion to distance. (2) The CMB: the afterglow of the hot early universe, with exactly the spectrum a hot glow should have. (3) The abundance of light elements: in the first few minutes the universe fused about 75% hydrogen and 25% helium by mass, which is what we measure in the oldest gas clouds. No other theory explains all three.
Part 4

Looking back, with new eyes

Because the farthest light is the oldest and most redshifted, it arrives as infrared. The James Webb Space Telescope (launched on 25 December 2021) is built for infrared, which is why it can see the first galaxies. In 2024 it confirmed a galaxy at z = 14.3 whose light left when the universe was only about 290 million years old. Use the lab on the next tab to see the redshift for yourself.

Beyond the observable universe there is more universe, but its light has not reached us yet. The edge is not a wall. It is just as far as the light has had time to come. The whole thing may be much larger than what we can see, perhaps infinite.

Part 5

An argument still going

How fast is the universe expanding now? Two very good methods give two different answers:

MethodWhat it usesH0
Early universeThe CMB, from Planck, run forward with the standard modelabout 67.4
Late universeCepheids and supernovae in nearby galaxiesabout 73

The gap is far too big to be a measurement error, according to the teams doing the work. This is called the Hubble tension. Either something is wrong with a measurement nobody has found, or our model of the universe is missing something. At the time of writing, the argument is not settled. If you want a problem that might be solved in your lifetime, this one is a good candidate.

Rabbit holes

InflationThe theory that the universe expanded by a huge factor in its first tiny fraction of a second explains why the CMB is so smooth. It is widely accepted but not proven. What would count as proof? Ripples in spacetime from that era, which nobody has found.
Faster than light?Galaxies beyond about 14 billion light-years are receding faster than light. That does not break relativity, because they are not moving through space. Their light can never reach us. In the far future even the nearest big galaxies outside our local group will have slipped beyond view.
The Dark AgesBetween the CMB at 380,000 years and the first stars at around 100–200 million years, nothing shone. Astronomers are building radio telescopes to detect hydrogen from this era, the last unexplored stretch of cosmic history.
The multiverse?If inflation never stops everywhere, our universe might be one bubble in a larger structure. This is an idea, not a result, and many physicists doubt it can be tested. Where is the boundary between physics and speculation?
