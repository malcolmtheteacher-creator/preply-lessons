The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — The general

Dwight Eisenhower was an American general. In 1944, in the Second World War, he had a very big job. He had to plan the invasion of France. Thousands of ships, planes and soldiers waited for his decision.

The plan was for 5 June. But the weather was terrible, so Eisenhower had to postpone it. Then the weather experts told him about a short break in the storm. He listened, asked questions, and said yes. On 6 June, the soldiers landed in France.

In his pocket he had a short note. It said: if the plan fails, it is my fault. He never used the note.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Eisenhower's job in 1944?

2. Why did he postpone the plan?

3. What did the note in his pocket say?

1. He was a general. He had to plan the invasion of France.

2. Because the weather was terrible.

3. That if the plan failed, it was his fault.

Discuss:
  • Was Eisenhower a good leader? Why?

Part 2 — Two kinds of problem

After the war, Eisenhower became President of the United States. Every day, hundreds of people wanted his time. He was famous for one idea about work: he protected his time and asked for short, clear reports.

In 1954 he gave a speech. In it he said: “I have two kinds of problems, the urgent and the important. The urgent are not important, and the important are never urgent.”

It is a strange sentence, but think about it. The phone is urgent. Your health is important. The phone always wins — and that is the problem.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What job did Eisenhower do after the war?

2. What are the two kinds of problems?

3. Which one usually wins?

1. He became President of the United States.

2. Urgent problems and important problems.

3. The urgent one — for example, the phone.

Discuss:
  • Is this true in your life? Give an example.

Part 3 — Four boxes

For many years, this was only a nice sentence. Then, in 1989, an American writer called Stephen Covey wrote a famous book about work and time. In the book he drew a picture: a square with four boxes.

One line asks: is it urgent? The other line asks: is it important? Every job goes in one box. Do it now, plan it for later, give it to someone else, or forget it.

People began to call it the Eisenhower Matrix. Today it is on millions of computers and phones — which is funny, because phones are the biggest problem of all.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Who drew the four boxes, and when?

2. What two questions do the boxes ask?

3. What are the four actions?

1. Stephen Covey, in a book in 1989.

2. Is it urgent? Is it important?

3. Do it now, plan it, give it to someone else, or forget it.

Discuss:
  • Why do you think phones make this problem worse?

How the tool works

Two questions, four boxes. Is it urgent (soon)? Is it important (it really matters)? Each box tells you what to do.

Using it, step by step

  1. Write a list of everything you have to do.
  2. For each one ask: is it important? Then ask: is it urgent?
  3. Put it in a box.
  4. Do the “do it now” box today. Put the “plan it” box in your calendar — with a day and a time.

Try it yourself

Which box is it? Choose, then check.

1. You watch short videos on your phone for an hour before bed.
2. Your child is very ill and needs a doctor today.
3. You want to start running, to be healthy in ten years.
4. Your boss needs the report at five o'clock today.
5. A colleague asks you to book a meeting room. Anyone can do it.
6. You want to learn English for a better job next year.

1. Neither — it is not urgent and it is not important.

2. Urgent and important — it is very important and it cannot wait.

3. Important, not urgent — it is important, but nothing says “today”.

4. Urgent and important — a real time and a real problem.

5. Urgent, not important — it feels urgent, but it is not your job — give it away.

6. Important, not urgent — very important, but not urgent — so plan it.

Where the tool breaks

You can't always give work away. A student or a parent often has nobody to give it to.

Important for who? Your boss and you may not agree.

Not everything is work. A long talk with a friend is in no box — but it can be the best part of the day.

Talk it through:
  • Which box has most of your time?
  • Which box do you want more time in?

Grammar — have to / don't have to

This tool is about what you must do and what you don't have to do. Here is the grammar for it.

have to = it is necessary (a rule, a job, a law).
“I have to wear a uniform at work.”  “She has to leave at six.”
don't have to = it is NOT necessary. You can choose.
“It's Sunday. I don't have to get up early.”
Past: had to / didn't have to.
“Yesterday I had to work late.”
Questions: Do you have to…? Does he have to…?
“Do you have to work on Saturdays?”
Careful: mustn't is different! You mustn't smoke here = it is not allowed. You don't have to smoke = you can choose.
