The Time Traveller's Argument

C1 Level · In 1895 a novelist argued that time is a fourth dimension. Was it science, or only a story?

Today: you'll analyse how a Victorian novelist argued for a scientific idea, and use concession and counter-argument to judge it.
Does the past still exist somewhere, in the way that a faraway place still exists, or is it simply gone? Choose one.

A novel, a physicist, a date: true or false?

In 1895, a novel opened with a long argument about the nature of time. Here are six claims about it and the science around it. Decide which are true.

1. H. G. Wells was a professional physicist.
False. Wells studied biology, under T. H. Huxley, and became a writer. He was a keen reader of science.
2. The character uses a weather record, the trace of a barometer, as evidence that time can be drawn as a line.
True. It is one of the most memorable moves in the chapter.
3. The character's listeners accept the argument at once, without objections.
False. Several of them object, and the argument has to survive their questions.
4. In 1908 the mathematician Hermann Minkowski, who had taught Einstein, argued that space and time should be treated together as a single 'spacetime'.
True. His lecture helped make the idea part of physics.
5. A novel published in 1895 has a character argue that time is a fourth dimension, around ten years before Einstein's special relativity.
True. The novel is The Time Machine by H. G. Wells. Special relativity dates from 1905.
6. The Time Traveller says time is a different kind of thing from space, so no machine could ever move through it.
False. He argues the opposite: that time is only 'a kind of Space', and so can in principle be travelled through.
Talk about it:
  • Which of these would you have expected a novelist to be able to do before the physicists did?
  • What can a story do for an idea that an equation cannot?

The argument, in three moves

The speaker is a character called the Time Traveller, talking to a room of sceptical guests. Before each extract, predict what he will say. Then read, name the technique and judge the move.

Move 1 — A question that sounds like a trick

Before you read: How could you argue that a cube must last for some time in order to be real?

“Can a cube that does not last for any time at all, have a real existence?”

… “any real body must have extension in four directions: it must have Length, Breadth, Thickness, and—Duration.”

H. G. Wells, The Time Machine (1895), chapter 1

New words in this move — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

The Time Traveller says a line with no thickness is a mere abstraction: useful in the mind, but not a real thing.
abstraction (n) — an idea or concept that has no physical existence. Example: "A perfect circle is an abstraction; no real object is one."
Any real body, he argues, must have extension in four directions.
extension (n) — the amount that something stretches in a direction; in older scientific writing, how far a body occupies space. Example: "Length is extension in one direction."
Your call:
  • Notice the form: a question, then a conclusion drawn from it. Is the question fair, or does it hide the answer inside itself?

Move 2 — Time as a direction

Before you read: If time is a fourth dimension, what is the one important difference between it and the other three?

“There is no difference between Time and any of the three dimensions of Space except that our consciousness moves along it.”

H. G. Wells, The Time Machine (1895), chapter 1

New words in this move — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

The fourth direction is duration: how long something lasts.
duration (n) — the length of time something lasts or continues. Example: "The duration of the experiment was three weeks."
He says our consciousness moves along time, but not along the other dimensions.
consciousness (n) — awareness of yourself and your surroundings; the mind's experience. Example: "The patient slowly regained consciousness."
Your call:
  • How much work is the word ‘except’ doing here? What would the sentence claim without it?

Move 3 — The weather record

Before you read: How could a barometer’s record over one day be used as evidence about time itself?

“Here is a popular scientific diagram, a weather record. This line I trace with my finger shows the movement of the barometer … But certainly it traced such a line, and that line, therefore, we must conclude, was along the Time-Dimension.”

In reply, a listener asks why we cannot move about in time as we can in space. The Time Traveller answers: “Are you so sure we can move freely in Space?”

H. G. Wells, The Time Machine (1895), chapter 1

New words in this move — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

A weather record is his analogy: time can be drawn as a line, just as the barometer's trace is.
analogy (n) — a comparison between two things that shows how they are alike, used to explain an idea. Example: "He used an analogy with water flowing in a pipe to explain electric current."
The idea is bold and speculative: it goes beyond the evidence the speaker can offer.
speculative (adj) — based on guesses or possibilities rather than proof. Example: "The article was interesting but highly speculative."
Your call:
  • The answer to the objection is another question. Does it answer the listener, or move the argument sideways? Argue one side.

Conceding a point and still disagreeing

A strong critic does not simply say ‘wrong’. They grant what is good in an argument, then show where it fails. Try it on the Time Traveller.

Concede, then counter: Admittedly / Granted / It is true that + point, but / yet / even so + counter-point.
While / Whilst it is true that + clause, main clause. While it is true that the analogy is vivid, it proves nothing.
For all / Notwithstanding + noun. For all its elegance, the idea is untested. (Not for all of.)
Never use although and but in the same clause.

Build it

Join the facts so the concession comes first. Type your sentence, say it, then reveal a model.

1. Wells was not a physicist. His story anticipated a key idea. Join them, beginning with Admittedly. Admittedly
Admittedly, Wells was not a physicist, but his story anticipated a key idea.
2. The analogy is persuasive. It is not proof. Join them, beginning with While it is true that. While it is true that
While it is true that the analogy is persuasive, it is not proof.
3. The argument is charming. It is incomplete. Rewrite as one sentence beginning For all. For all
For all its charm, the argument is incomplete.

Take a side

Five claims about science and fiction. Choose I agree, I disagree or It depends, then defend your choice in one sentence that begins with a concession. Check the reading last.

1. “Fiction that happens to get the science right deserves little credit: it was a lucky guess.”Admittedly
Partly fair. Wells did not predict relativity; his argument is a loose philosophical one about duration. But the credit is for the framing, which made a strange idea thinkable before physics needed it.
2. “A novelist’s intuition can be as valuable as a physicist’s equation.”While it is true that
It depends what for. An equation can be tested and fail; an intuition cannot. But intuition can supply the question that the equation later answers.
3. “If the past and future exist as fully as the present, free will is an illusion.”Granted
Contested. The Time Traveller calls a man's life ‘a fixed and unalterable thing’, which suggests a block view of time. Philosophers disagree on whether that removes choice, or only describes it from outside.
4. “Time-travel stories teach us more about regret than about physics.”For all
Often true. Most such stories use the machine to ask what we would change. The physics is the setting; the human question is the subject.
5. “Science fiction should be judged first as literature, and only afterwards as prediction.”It is true that
Widely held. Predictions date quickly, and most are wrong. What lasts is style, character and the quality of the questions.

Physics does not settle it. Relativity is often read as favouring a ‘block’ picture, in which past, present and future are equally real, and the Time Traveller's ‘fixed and unalterable’ life points that way. Others hold that only the present is real. Careful thinkers disagree, and the disagreement is about what the equations mean, not about the equations.

Give three ways to concede a point before disagreeing with it.

What is the difference between although and despite?
Although is followed by a clause (although the analogy is vivid). Despite is followed by a noun or an -ing form (despite its vividness), never despite of.
What did the Time Traveller use a weather record to show?
That time can be drawn as a line along a dimension, just as the barometer's trace is a line: so time behaves like a direction of space.

Say the word

Say a definition and a sentence for each word, then reveal.

Which word means "to describe or predict something before it happens or is proved"? (a________)
anticipate (v) — to do or imagine something before others do. Example: "The novel anticipated later scientific ideas."
Which word means "seeming likely to be true, even if it is not proved"? (p________)
plausible (adj) — reasonable and likely to be true. Example: "Her explanation sounded plausible, but nobody had checked it."
Which word means "to admit that a point in an argument is true, even though it helps the other side"? (c______)
concede (v) — to admit that something is true or valid, often unwillingly. Example: "I concede that the evidence is limited."
Which word means "an idea or statement on which an argument is based"? (p______)
premise (n) — a statement that an argument starts from. Example: "If the premise is false, the conclusion falls with it."
