At Passport Control

Nine hours in the air, a passport in your hand, and a uniformed man asking quick questions in an accent you've never heard. You have nothing to hide. Now you just have to sound like it.

The Scene 🎬

The one conversation where you can't walk away, can't start again, and can't check your phone.

The flight landed twenty minutes ago and your brain is still somewhere over the Atlantic. The queue snakes forward. At the desk sits an officer who asks the same fifteen questions four hundred times a day, quickly, without looking up much.

Here's what nobody tells you: he isn't trying to catch you. He's trying to get through his shift. Short, true, calm answers are a gift to him — and long nervous explanations are what make him look up. The travellers who struggle at this desk are almost never hiding something; they're just answering the question they guessed instead of the one he asked.

You're here for two weeks, staying with your friend, flying home on the 21st. That's it. That's the whole file. The only real skill is keeping your answers that size — and daring to ask him to repeat himself when the words blur, which is precisely what confident people do.

  1. Why do completely innocent people get nervous at border desks? What is the fear actually of?
  2. In your language, how do officials talk? Is it the same voice English officials use?

Your Turn 🎭

No script this time. You'll have a real conversation with a real person. They have their own mind, and you have a goal.

1 · Warm up your voice 🎤

Say each step once, out loud, in your own words. No writing, no peeking at the phrases.

  1. Answer the first question. Short, true and calm.
  2. Give the details. Say where you are staying and when you leave.
  3. Ask them to repeat. Do not guess at what they said.
  4. Correct a mistake. Fix it simply.
  5. Stay relaxed. Show you are not nervous and not in a hurry.
  6. Finish. Ask where to go next and thank them.

The Echo 🕰️

For twenty-eight years, the hardest border on earth ran through the middle of one city. At Checkpoint Charlie the questions were not friendly, the silence was not about the stamp, and the wrong answer could unmake a life. Berliners learned to cross with their faces still and their answers short.

Then, on the night of 9 November 1989, a flustered official misread a memo at a press conference — and said the border was open, effective immediately. Thousands walked up to desks built to terrify them, and the guards, with no orders left to follow, lifted the barriers and let them through.

Most borders are kinder than that one, and it's worth remembering at the desk: the officer's questions have answers, the queue does end, and the barrier is built to lift.

Two bigger questions

  1. The guards at the Wall were following orders until, suddenly, there were none. How much of any official's power is really the uniform, and how much is our belief in it?
  2. Borders check where you're going and why. Who in your life gets to ask you those questions — and who doesn't?
