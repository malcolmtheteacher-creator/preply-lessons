At Passport Control

Nine hours in the air, a passport in your hand, and a uniformed man asking quick questions in an accent you've never heard. You have nothing to hide. Now you just have to sound like it.

The Scene 🎬

The one conversation where you can't walk away, can't start again, and can't check your phone.

The flight landed twenty minutes ago and your brain is still somewhere over the Atlantic. The queue snakes forward. At the desk sits an officer who asks the same fifteen questions four hundred times a day, quickly, without looking up much.

Here's what nobody tells you: he isn't trying to catch you. He's trying to get through his shift. Short, true, calm answers are a gift to him — and long nervous explanations are what make him look up. The travellers who struggle at this desk are almost never hiding something; they're just answering the question they guessed instead of the one he asked.

You're here for two weeks, staying with your friend, flying home on the 21st. That's it. That's the whole file. The only real skill is keeping your answers that size — and daring to ask him to repeat himself when the words blur, which is precisely what confident people do.

  1. Why do completely innocent people get nervous at border desks? What is the fear actually of?

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for any official desk — border control, visa office, police check, airport security. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Calm is a speed, not a feeling. Answer at your own pace, keep answers the size of the question, and treat "could you repeat that?" as your right — natives use it constantly. The nervous sound isn't an accent; it's rushing.

🎯 Answers the size of the question

Phrase 1

The job: the purpose-of-visit answer — complete, calm, one breath. Which one?

A. "Well, it's a long story, but basically my friend Klara has been saying for years that I should come, and this spring I finally—"
B. "Holiday."
C. "I'm here for a couple of weeks, visiting friends."
C — "I'm here for a couple of weeks, visiting friends."
A is the nervous babble that makes officers look up — you're answering questions nobody asked. B is technically fine but so short it invites a follow-up. C gives the two things every border officer wants in one line: how long, and why. Nothing to add, nothing to probe.

Take it anywhere: "I'm here for ___, ___-ing ___." Duration plus purpose — the complete answer shape.
Phrase 2

The job: showing you're organised — the address, produced, not recited. Complete it:

"I'll be staying with a friend — I've got the address ___ ___ here."

"I'll be staying with a friend — I've got the address written down here."
Trying to pronounce "Loughborough Road" after nine hours of flying is nobody's finest moment. "I've got it written down" solves the problem before it exists and quietly says: I prepared for this conversation. Officials relax around prepared people.

Take it anywhere: "I've got ___ written down here." Addresses, names, medication, reference numbers.
Phrase 3

The job: proving you're going home, without being asked to prove it. Complete it:

"I've got a ___ flight on the 21st."

"I've got a return flight on the 21st."
The word return is the whole answer to the question underneath every border conversation — are you planning to leave? Offering the date before he asks for evidence turns an interrogation point into a settled fact. "The booking's on my phone if you need it" finishes the job.

Take it anywhere: "I've got a return ___ on the ___." Flight, train, ferry — the date does the reassuring.
Phrase 4

The job: the small honesty that humanises the whole exchange. Complete it:

"It's my first time here, ___."

"It's my first time here, actually."
That little "actually" turns a data point into a human sentence — it says and I'm slightly pleased about it. Officials are people; one warm true sentence changes the temperature of the desk. Note what it isn't: it isn't an apology, and it isn't a speech.

Take it anywhere: "It's my first time ___, actually." New job, new gym, new country — say it and watch people soften.

🐢 Slowing the desk down

Phrase 5

The job: you caught the start, lost the end. Put the pieces in order:

that last partcould youSorry —again?say
"Sorry — could you say that last part again?"
Precision matters: that last part tells him you were listening and where exactly you lost him, so he repeats three words instead of restarting the question louder. Asking this is not a mark against you — it's what listening actually looks like.

Take it anywhere: "Could you say that last part again?" Phone calls, announcements, fast talkers everywhere.
Phrase 6

The job: the question was ambiguous — check which meaning before you answer. Complete it:

"Sorry — do you ___ money, or somewhere to stay?"

"Sorry — do you mean money, or somewhere to stay?"
"Means of support" could be either — and answering the wrong one sounds evasive even when it's just confused. "Do you mean A or B?" does two jobs at once: it buys you three seconds, and it shows him a person carefully trying to answer his actual question. Officials trust that.

Take it anywhere: "Do you mean ___ or ___?" The instant cure for every ambiguous question in English.
Phrase 7

The job: you answered the wrong question and just realised. Recover cleanly. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Sorry — I misunderstood the question. From Hamburg, the 14:40."
Name it, fix it, stop. No excuses about being tired, no apology tour — misunderstanding a question is normal and officials see it hourly. What they notice is the recovery: people who correct themselves plainly read as honest, because that is what honest sounds like.

Take it anywhere: "Sorry — I misunderstood the question. ___." Then simply answer the real one.

🧊 When the desk goes quiet

Phrase 8

The job: he's typing, frowning, saying nothing. Grace under silence. Complete it:

"Of course — take whatever ___ you ___."

"Of course — take whatever time you need."
The silence at an official desk is where nervous people start volunteering information, and volunteering is how simple checks become long ones. This sentence does the opposite: it grants him the pause, calmly, like someone with an empty file. Then — crucially — you stop talking.

Take it anywhere: "Take whatever time you need." Interviews, banks, any desk where the typing goes quiet.
Phrase 9

The job: asking directly whether something's wrong — once, calmly. Which version?

A. "Why is it taking so long? Is something wrong with my passport?"
B. "Is there a problem with anything?"
C. "Look, I've got nothing to hide, you can check everything."
B — "Is there a problem with anything?"
A sounds impatient and slightly frightened at once. C is the strangest sentence an innocent person can say — nobody suggested you had something to hide until you did. B is level and open: a person entitled to ask, asking once. And the answer is usually the stamp.

Take it anywhere: "Is there a problem with anything?" — the calm check, asked exactly once.
Phrase 10

The job: the desk is done — get your bearings before you walk. Complete it:

"Which ___ is baggage reclaim ___ here?"

"Which way is baggage reclaim from here?"
End every official encounter by turning it back into an ordinary human one — and get something useful while you're at it. "From here" earns you an answer with landmarks ("down the escalator") instead of a vague wave. He says "Next!"; you walk off knowing where you're going.

Take it anywhere: "Which way is ___ from here?" — the exit, the taxis, platform four, the seaside.

⚡ The twist

Round 1 again — and the officer looks up from your passport and says: "This says you were refused entry to Canada in 2019. Want to tell me about that?"

It's true — a paperwork mistake on a student visa, sorted out a month later, embarrassing and ancient. You have ten seconds to explain it the way you'd want it explained about you: short, true, and without panic.

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you give purpose and duration in one breath?
  • Can you ask for the last part again without feeling small?
  • Can you check which of two meanings a question has before answering?
  • Can you sit through official silence without volunteering your life story?
  • Can you correct a wrong answer plainly and move on?

The Echo 🕰️

For twenty-eight years, the hardest border on earth ran through the middle of one city. At Checkpoint Charlie the questions were not friendly, the silence was not about the stamp, and the wrong answer could unmake a life. Berliners learned to cross with their faces still and their answers short.

Then, on the night of 9 November 1989, a flustered official misread a memo at a press conference — and said the border was open, effective immediately. Thousands walked up to desks built to terrify them, and the guards, with no orders left to follow, lifted the barriers and let them through.

Most borders are kinder than that one, and it's worth remembering at the desk: the officer's questions have answers, the queue does end, and the barrier is built to lift.
