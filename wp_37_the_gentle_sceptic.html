A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XXXVII of L

The Gentle Sceptic

In which the most cheerful philosopher in history politely demolishes causation, the self, miracles, and the foundations of all human knowledge — then goes to dinner

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet David Hume (1711–1776), born in Edinburgh, precocious, brilliant, and cheerful. He wrote the Treatise of Human Nature at twenty-six ("It fell dead-born from the press"). He became the toast of Paris salons. He died peacefully at sixty-five, on his deathbed playing backgammon, unreligious and unafraid. His philosophy is devastating: all knowledge comes from impressions. We never observe causation, only constant conjunction. The self is a bundle of perceptions with no permanent core. Reason is the slave of the passions. Miracles can never be proven. And the future may not resemble the past. These arguments have never been answered. By the end, you will understand why Kant said Hume "woke me from my dogmatic slumber," and you will glimpse a problem that haunts all philosophy.

II. The Story — The Cheerful Demolisher

David Hume was born in Edinburgh in 1711 into a family of moderate means. His father died when David was two. He was educated at Edinburgh and briefly at Leiden. He was precocious. By his twenties, he had drafted most of the ideas that would later appear in the Treatise of Human Nature.

The Treatise and Ambition

In 1739, at twenty-eight, Hume published his masterwork: the Treatise of Human Nature, a three-volume work that attempted to apply the methods of natural science to the study of human thought. It was, he later said, an ambitious attempt to be the Newton of the mind. The book was a commercial disaster. Hume himself wrote: "It fell dead-born from the press."

Why did it fail? The ideas were too radical. They undermined the foundations of knowledge, morality, and religious faith. And the prose was difficult. Hume would spend the rest of his life trying to repackage and popularize his ideas in more accessible forms.

Success in Paris, Failure at Home

Hume moved to France. In Paris, he became famous. The French salons loved his wit, his skeptical philosophy, his irreverence toward religion. He was the toast of Parisian intellectual society. He knew Rousseau (though they later quarreled). He corresponded with the greatest minds of Europe. Yet in Scotland, he was controversial. His skepticism offended the religious. His naturalism offended the metaphysicians. He applied for professorships and was rejected—because of his reputation as an infidel.

The Late Works and Peaceful Death

In his later years, Hume published Enquiries, Dialogues, and essays that presented his philosophy in more digestible form. He made money through his writings. He became a respected public figure in Edinburgh. He served as librarian of the Faculty of Advocates. He traveled as a diplomat.

And then in 1776, he died of a bowel cancer. Hume faced death with remarkable calm. His friend Boswell visited him and reported that he was cheerful, without fear or regret, playing backgammon. When Boswell expressed astonishment that Hume, an open sceptic and non-believer, could be so serene at the approach of death, Hume reportedly replied that he had nothing to fear—he had lived a good life, and there was nothing beyond to worry about. He died peacefully, without God, without faith, and without terror. For centuries, this was held against him. Now, it is seen as one of the most honest deaths in intellectual history.

Kant and Hume. Immanuel Kant, the greatest philosopher of the eighteenth century, said that Hume "interrupted my dogmatic slumber and gave my investigations an entirely new direction." Hume's skepticism about causation forced Kant to rethink the foundations of knowledge itself. This single moment—Kant reading Hume—changed the direction of modern philosophy.

The Core Philosophy: Impressions and Ideas

Hume’s philosophy rests on a simple principle: all human thought comes from experience. But not just any experience. Specifically, from impressions—vivid, direct perceptions of the world (sensations, emotions, passions)—and from ideas—faint copies of impressions, created by memory and imagination.

An idea is nothing but a faint impression. And if an idea cannot be traced back to a corresponding impression, it is meaningless. This principle is radical. It means most of traditional philosophy—talk about God, the soul, essences, abstract universals—is literally nonsense. These terms have no corresponding impressions. They are just words.

1. The Problem of Causation

We see A happen, then B. We say A caused B. But what exactly are we observing? Not causation itself. We observe only what Hume calls constant conjunction—repeated association. We never see the causal force or power connecting A to B. Causation is not an impression. It is a habit of mind.

When we look about us towards external objects, and consider the operation of causes, we are never able, in a single instance, to discover any power or necessary connexion; any quality, which binds the effect to the cause, and renders the one an infallible consequence of the other. We only find, that the one does actually, in fact, follow the other. — Hume, Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, §7

2. The Bundle Theory of the Self

What is the self? Descartes said: "I think, therefore I am." There is an “I” that thinks. But Hume disagrees. When he looks inward, he finds no unified self—only a succession of impressions: thoughts, sensations, emotions, memories. The self is a bundle of perceptions. There is no permanent core, no soul, no unified subject. Just a collection of fleeting mental contents bound together by memory and habit.

For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other... I never can catch myself at any time without a perception. When my perceptions are remov'd for any time, as by sound sleep; so long am I insensible of myself, and may truly be said not to exist. — Hume, Treatise, Book I, Part 4, §6

3. The Is-Ought Problem

Here is one of Hume's most famous observations. In moral and philosophical writings, writers often move from statements about facts ("is") to statements about value ("ought"). But these are fundamentally different kinds of claims. Facts are about what is the case. Values are about what we should do. You cannot derive an “ought” from an “is.” Morality is not discoverable through reason alone. It flows from sentiment—from our feelings and emotions.

4. Miracles Cannot Be Proven

Hume wrote a famous essay on miracles. His argument is simple. A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature. But the laws of nature are established by human experience—by constant conjunctions we’ve observed. A miracle is a supposed violation of those laws. But the testimony for a miracle can never be stronger than the evidence for the law itself. Therefore, rational belief in miracles is impossible. The evidence against miracles (the uniform experience of nature) always outweighs the testimony for them.

5. The Problem of Induction

We expect the future to resemble the past. The sun will rise tomorrow because it has risen every day of recorded history. But on what basis do we make this assumption? Not on rational proof. We use what Hume calls custom or habit. We observe A followed by B many times. Our mind forms a habit. When we see A again, we expect B. This habit is so deep it seems rational. But Hume insists: it is not reason. It is psychological conditioning.

Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them. — Hume, Treatise, Book II, Part 3, §3

The Paradox of Hume's Life

Here is the deepest paradox. Hume demolished causation, the self, and certain knowledge. He showed that all our beliefs rest on custom, not reason. He argued that miracles cannot be proven and that no rational person should believe in God. Yet after his philosophical demolition work, Hume simply went about his life. He dined. He played backgammon. He made friends. He earned money. He cared about his reputation. He lived as if everything he had philosophically argued against was real.

This is not contradiction. It is honesty. Hume admitted it. He wrote: "When I'm away from my books and company, my speculations appear cold, strained, and ridiculous. I dine, I play backgammon, and I converse with my friends. These amusements drive away the philosophical melancholy."

Philosophy cannot sustain life. Life runs on something deeper than argument. On habit. On emotion. On love. On the passions that reason merely serves. Hume lived this truth. He was a gentle skeptic—skeptical about everything reason claimed, but happy to live according to custom, sentiment, and common sense.

III. The Ideas — What Hume Really Proved

Hume’s demolitions are famous. But what did he actually prove? Let’s look at three core insights.

Idea 1: Empiricism and the Limits of Meaning

All meaningful claims must be traceable to impressions (direct experience). If a term has no corresponding impression, it is meaningless.

This is a revolutionary principle. It rules out vast swaths of traditional philosophy and theology. What impression corresponds to "God"? To "substance"? To "the soul"? If none, then these terms are nonsense. This principle later became central to logical positivism and analytic philosophy. Hume was the first empiricist to take the logical consequences seriously.

Idea 2: Causation as Custom, Not Reason

Causation is not an observable quality. It is a habit of mind—a psychological projection we impose on constant conjunctions.

This undermines the entire scientific worldview—or at least its philosophical foundation. Science assumes that causes produce effects. But Hume shows: we never observe causation itself, only regular patterns. Science is thus based not on reason but on custom and expectation. This does not make science false. But it shows that science rests on a foundation that reason alone cannot justify.

Idea 3: Reason Serves the Passions

Reason is not the master of human life. It is the servant. The passions (emotions, desires, sentiments) are primary. Reason's only role is to discover means to achieve the ends set by passion.

This reverses the traditional philosophical order (dating back to Plato). Philosophers had assumed reason should rule the passions. Hume says: this is backwards. We desire, and reason helps us get what we desire. Morality does not come from reason. It comes from sentiment. Justice does not arise from abstract principle. It arises from sympathy—from our fellow-feeling for others.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Thesis Seed: Philosophy vs. Life

Hume raises a question that cannot be evaded: Can philosophy tell us how to live? His answer: No, and yes. No, philosophy cannot derive values from facts. It cannot prove that we should live one way rather than another. But yes, philosophy can show us the limits of reason, and in doing so, it liberates us to live according to nature—according to the passions, sentiments, and customs that actually move us.

The Philosophical Problem: If all knowledge rests on custom and habit, if causation is unobservable, if reason cannot ground morality, then what is knowledge? What is truth? What is right?

The Answer Hume Suggests: Knowledge is stable custom and habit. Truth is what experience repeatedly confirms. Right is what sympathy and fellow-feeling lead us to do. These are not rational foundations. But they are sufficient. They are human foundations. Philosophy cannot transcend them. It can only clarify them. And once clarified, we can go back to dinner and backgammon, no longer troubled by the thought that life lacks philosophical justification.

VI. Your Turn

Can We Know Anything?

Hume says all knowledge rests on custom, not reason. If that's true, is there any difference between knowledge and mere habit?

The Problem of Induction

We believe the sun will rise tomorrow. Hume says this is not rational proof but psychological habit. Does his argument work? Or is there something wrong with it?

Is the Self Real?

Hume says there is no permanent self, only a bundle of perceptions. If true, who are "you"? What does it mean to be a person?

Philosophy vs. Life

Hume demolishes philosophy then goes to dinner. Can skepticism sustain a good life? Or does life require beliefs that skepticism cannot justify?

Miracles and Evidence

Can miracles ever be proven? Hume says no—testimony can never outweigh uniform experience. Is he right?

The Is-Ought Gap

You cannot derive "ought" from "is." Facts don't determine values. Does this mean morality is subjective? Or can sentiment ground objective truth?

Reason as Slave

Reason is the slave of the passions. Is Hume right? Or does reason sometimes rule the passions? When? How?
