A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XXXVII of L

The Gentle Sceptic

David Hume: Causation, induction, and cheerful skepticism

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: David Hume (1711–1776) is the most devastating critic of metaphysics philosophy has ever produced. He dismantled causation, the self, induction, and belief in God. And then he went to play backgammon. He showed that philosophy cannot justify what we all believe. And then he showed that living without those beliefs is impossible. Hume teaches us that reason has limits—profound limits. What lies beyond? Faith. Habit. Friendship. The pleasures of human life. You will place Hume in your palace and learn perhaps the most important lesson of all: that philosophy is not everything.

II. The Story — The Demolition Job

David Hume (1711–1776) was a Scottish philosopher who lived through Enlightenment optimism about reason. And he spent his career showing its limits.

The Problem of Causation

Hume asked: what is causation? We see one billiard ball strike another and assume the first caused the second. But we never actually observe causation. We only observe constant conjunction—one event following another repeatedly.

We have no impression of cause and effect. All that we observe is that one thing follows another, and when many instances of this kind occur, we call it causation.— David Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding

This is devastating. If we cannot observe causation, we cannot prove it exists. We believe in it only out of habit.

The Bundle Theory of the Self

Hume next targeted the self. When I introspect, what do I find? Not a unified self. I find a stream of perceptions, thoughts, and sensations, constantly changing. The self is just a bundle of perceptions—nothing more.

The Problem of Induction

Hume's final blow: you cannot rationally justify induction. Just because the sun rose yesterday and the day before does not prove it will rise tomorrow. The future may be utterly different from the past. We believe in induction only because we are creatures of habit, not because reason supports it.

♦ ♦ ♦

And yet Hume is cheerful about all this. After dismantling reason, he confesses that he cannot actually live as a skeptic. When he leaves his study, he plays backgammon with friends. He enjoys conversation. He believes in causation, the self, and induction—not because he has proven them, but because he must to live. Philosophy cannot answer life's deepest questions. Life itself must answer them.

III. The Ideas — The Limits of Reason

Idea 1: Empiricism Taken to Its Limit

All knowledge comes from experience. But experience cannot prove causation, the self, or God. Hume takes Locke's empiricism to its logical end. If you cannot observe something, you cannot know it. And you cannot observe these fundamental things.

Idea 2: The Is-Ought Gap

You cannot derive moral conclusions from factual premises. This became one of philosophy's most important insights: facts about the world do not determine values. The "is" does not entail the "ought."

Idea 3: The Limits of Philosophy

Philosophy can dismantle our beliefs. But it cannot replace them. We live by beliefs that reason cannot justify. This is not a weakness. It is the human condition. Reason has limits. Beyond those limits lies faith, habit, emotion, and the attachments that make life worth living.

V. Room 37 of Your Philosophy Palace

YOUR PHILOSOPHY PALACE

Location 37: The Study and the Tavern

You arrive at a room divided in half. On one side, a study with books torn apart, pages scattered, a philosopher with a magnifying glass finding nothing. On the other side, a warm tavern where the same philosopher sits with friends, playing cards, laughing, at peace.

The 3 Images for Location 37

1. A chain of billiard balls, but the connection between them is invisible, a ghost — Causation that cannot be observed, only assumed.

2. A mirror reflecting mirrors reflecting mirrors, infinitely, never touching reality — The self as a bundle of perceptions with no center.

3. A philosopher leaving his study, books falling, laughing, sitting down at a table with friends to play cards — The acceptance that reason has limits and life must go on beyond reason.

Walk through Location 37

Test it now: See the torn books. See the infinite mirrors. See the philosopher laughing. This is Hume's lesson: reason can dismantle everything. But life goes on.

VI. Your Turn

Can reason justify induction?

Hume says no. Do you agree? Can you prove the sun will rise tomorrow?

What is the self?

Hume says it's a bundle of perceptions. Does this match your experience? Or is there something that ties your perceptions together?

Philosophy and life

Hume demolishes philosophy, then goes to play backgammon. Is this a contradiction? Or wisdom?

Habit vs. reason

Hume says we believe what we do out of habit, not reason. Is this disappointing? Or liberating?

The problem of causation

If we never observe causation, only constant conjunction, can we ever truly understand how the world works?

Skeptical peace

Hume is cheerful about his skepticism. Can you be skeptical and still at peace? Or does doubt destroy peace?

VII. Final Retrieval

1. The Problem of Causation

We never observe causation itself, only constant conjunction. We see one billiard ball hit another and assume causation, but what we really observe is one event following another. Causation is not something we can observe; it is a habit of mind.

2. The Self

A bundle of perceptions with no permanent center. When we introspect, we find only fleeting thoughts, sensations, and emotions. There is no unified "I" behind them—just a stream of experience.
