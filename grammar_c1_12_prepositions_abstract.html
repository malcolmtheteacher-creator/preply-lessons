Listen in on this conversation

Kit: How's the move going?

Ren: Honestly? We only agreed to it because rent here doubled. It wasn't really a joint decision in the end — I gave in at the expense of staying near my parents.

Kit: That's rough. Did you at least agree on a date?

Look again at "agreed to it" and "agree on a date." Same verb — agree — but two different prepositions, and two different meanings. Why do you think that happens? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
Because in English, some verbs shift meaning depending on which preposition follows them. Agree to means accepting a plan someone else suggested; agree on means two or more people reaching a decision together; there's also agree with, for sharing someone's opinion. The preposition isn't just decoration — it changes what the sentence actually means. That's one of three patterns we look at today.
Today: prepositions after abstract words like aware of and cope with, multi-word phrases that link your reasons like in the face of and on the grounds that, and verbs that change meaning with the preposition like agree with/to/on — so you can explain decisions, problems and plans precisely.

Needs, Feelings & Problems

Nouns, adjectives and verbs that describe needs, awareness and problems each pair with one fixed preposition — no other one works.

Spot the pattern

  • "There's a real need for a proper conversation, not another argument."
  • "I wasn't fully aware of how much the rent had gone up until the letter arrived."
  • "The new flat isn't really comparable to the old one — it's half the size."

Before you check: is there any logical reason each word takes that particular preposition?

Check the pattern CLICK
No — there's no logic to it. Need, aware and comparable each pair with exactly one preposition, and you simply learn the two words together as a single unit, like a mini vocabulary item.
a need for / an interest in  (noun + preposition)
aware of / comparable to  (adjective + preposition)
cope with / resort to / deprive somebody of  (verb + preposition)

From vague to precise

"He gave up and just did what she said."
→
"In the end, he resorted to doing what she said."

"Resort to" adds a nuance the plain version doesn't have: this was a last option, not a first choice.

Linking Your Reasons

Whole phrases that act as a single preposition — for stating a reason, a cost, or a condition.

Spot the pattern

  • "She turned down the promotion on the grounds that it would mean moving cities."
  • "He took the higher-paid job, but at the expense of seeing his kids most evenings."
  • "In the face of rising costs, the club decided to cancel the trip."

What job is each bold phrase doing? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
On the grounds that introduces a stated reason. At the expense of introduces a cost — something lost or sacrificed. In the face of introduces a difficulty someone deals with or stands up to. None of them change form: "on the ground that" and "in face of" simply don't exist.
on the grounds that + clause  (a stated reason)
at the expense of + noun/-ing  (a cost)
in the face of + noun  (a difficulty)
in the light of + noun  (new information)
with a view to + -ing  (a purpose)
subject to + noun  (a condition)

New information changes the decision

"Because ticket sales were low, they cancelled the concert."
→
"In the light of low ticket sales, they cancelled the concert."

"In the light of" isn't quite the same as "in the face of" — it's about new information changing a decision, not about opposing a difficulty.

Same Verb, Different Meaning

The verb doesn't change — the preposition does, and that changes exactly what you mean.

Spot the pattern

  • "I agree with you — the meeting should be shorter."
  • "She agreed to the new working hours, even though she wasn't happy about them."
  • "After a long discussion, we finally agreed on a date for the wedding."

What's different about who or what each preposition connects to? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
With = you share someone's opinion. To = you accept a plan or proposal someone else suggested. On = two or more people reach a decision together. Same verb, three different relationships.
agree with + person/opinion
agree to + plan/proposal  (something suggested)
agree on + joint decision
result in + effect
result from + cause

Cause and effect point in different directions

"Poor planning caused the delay."
→
"The delay resulted from poor planning."

"Result from" points backwards to the cause. "Result in" points forwards to the effect: "Poor planning resulted in the delay" says the same thing the other way round.

Your Turn to Talk

Time to use all three patterns in one real story. It's your turn to talk.

The situation

Think of a real decision — yours, or one made by someone close to you — about a job, a move, money or a relationship. (If nothing comes to mind, invent one.)

Tell the story out loud for one minute. Try to answer:

  • What were you aware of — or not aware of — before deciding?
  • Did the decision come at any cost — at the expense of something else?
  • What did it result from, and what might it result in?
  • Did everyone agree, or did someone only agree to it reluctantly?
