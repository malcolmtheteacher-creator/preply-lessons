The Sceptical Client

You're the outside expert brought in to lead the new technology programme. Across the table: an operations director who has survived three 'transformations', remembers what each one promised, and has one question she hasn't asked out loud yet. Everyone in the room knows what it is.

The Scene 🎬

The consultant's hardest room isn't the hostile one. It's the polite one that has decided not to believe you.

You lead technology integration for a big consultancy, and today is the steering meeting that decides whether the AI programme at this client actually happens. The slides are good. The business case is real. None of that is the problem.

The problem is sitting opposite you: Ms Roca, operations director, twenty years in the company. She has been through a CRM migration that ate two years, a "digital transformation" that transformed nothing but the org chart, and an automation project whose savings never appeared in any number she could find. She is not against you. She is simply done with promises — and behind her politeness stands the question every room like this holds: what happens to my people?

Here's what this lesson teaches, and it works far beyond AI: you don't beat scepticism with a better pitch. You beat it by switching sides — naming the fear before they do, admitting what your product is bad at, shrinking the bet until it's testable, and handing the sceptic the measuring tape. The moment Ms Roca is designing the test instead of resisting the programme, the meeting is won.

  1. Think of something new you resisted at work that turned out fine — and something you were right to resist. What told you the difference?
  2. Why does admitting a weakness make an expert MORE convincing, when logically it should do the opposite?
YouThe consultant. Believes in the programme, knows exactly where the hype ends, and needs a yes that survives contact with reality.
Ms RocaOperations director. Polite, sharp, three failed transformations deep. Protects her people first and her numbers second. Wins every meeting she doesn't trust.
First: read the conversation and mark the three moments You gives ground — the hype, the limits, the last project's failure. Then notice what each concession buys. That trade is the whole method.

The Conversation 💬

The steering meeting. Coffee, projector, and Ms Roca's notebook — closed.

Ms RocaBefore you start — I've seen this deck, or its cousins, three times in ten years. Convince me this one's different, or let's all save an hour.
YouThen let me say the uncomfortable thing first, because everyone's thinking it: this is about people's jobs, and until we've talked about that honestly, nothing else I say will land. So let's start there, not finish there.
Ms Roca…That's not where these meetings usually start. Go on.
YouThe honest answer on jobs is this: some tasks go — the copying, the checking, the re-typing between systems. The jobs change. And how they change is a decision this room makes, not something the software decides. Anyone who tells you otherwise, in either direction, is selling something.
Ms RocaIncluding you. You're literally here selling something.
YouIncluding me — which is why I'm not here to sell you magic. Half of what you've read about AI is marketing, and you know it, which is exactly why you're sceptical. So forget the jargon for a minute. What this actually does, for your operation, is read the incoming claims, sort them, and draft the routine responses — the work your team currently does between four and six when they're tired and the error rate doubles.
Ms RocaOur automation project said something similar. The savings appeared in the vendor's case study and nowhere else. Why is this different?
YouCan I ask what it actually failed on? Not the official version — what really killed it?
Ms Roca…Honestly? It couldn't handle our edge cases, the team never trusted it, and by month six everyone had quietly gone back to the old way.
YouThen those are the first three things we test — the edge cases, the trust, and whether anyone's still using it when nobody's watching. And I'll be straight with you about limits, because this matters: it's very good at the routine eighty percent, and genuinely bad at the weird twenty — the claims that need a human who knows that this customer, this pattern, smells wrong. Those stay human. On day one and on day five hundred.
Ms RocaYou're the first person in one of these chairs to tell me what it can't do. …So what are you actually proposing? Because if the next slide says "enterprise-wide rollout", we're done.
YouThe opposite. I'd rather start embarrassingly small and be right than start big and be sorry. Give me one process and six weeks — claims intake, one team, volunteers only — and we measure it against numbers you choose, not numbers I bring.
Ms RocaMy numbers. Meaning if I pick harsh ones, you live with them?
YouMeaning exactly that. And one more request, and it's a real one: whoever in your team most doubts this — I'd like them on the pilot. Not the enthusiast. The doubter finds the problems in week two that the enthusiast finds in month six, when it's expensive.
Ms RocaThat would be Andrés. He'll eat you alive.
YouGood — better in the pilot than after it. And here's my side of the deal: if in six weeks you can't see the difference in your own numbers, I'll be the one recommending we stop. In writing, to your board. Not "extend the scope", not "one more phase" — stop.
Ms Roca*opens the notebook* …Six weeks. Claims intake. Andrés. My numbers, and your name on the stop recommendation. Send me that in an email today, exactly as you said it — and Mr Consultant, I'll be holding you to the word "embarrassingly".

  2. The old project failed on three things. What were they, and what happens to them in the new plan?

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for selling any change to any room that's been burned before — new technology, new process, reorganisation, merger. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The sceptic's ear is tuned to one frequency: overclaiming. Every phrase here works by giving ground you'd otherwise be forced to give later — the fear, the hype, the limits — and trading it for the one thing you actually need: a small, fair, measurable test. Concede early, concede precisely, and never concede the pilot.

🧨 Defusing the room

Phrase 1

The job: the fear everyone's holding — raised by YOU, first. Complete it:

"Let me say the ___ thing first, because everyone's thinking it."

"Let me say the uncomfortable thing first, because everyone's thinking it."
An unspoken fear runs every meeting it sits in — nobody hears your slides over the question they're privately asking. Naming it first does two things: it drains its power, and it announces you're the rare presenter who isn't managing the room. Ms Roca's "that's not where these meetings usually start" is the sound of a sceptic recalibrating.

Take it anywhere: "Let me say the uncomfortable thing first." Restructures, price rises, bad results — go where the room already is.
Phrase 2

The job: answering the jobs question without lying in either direction. Which answer?

A. "Nobody needs to worry — this is purely about efficiency, not headcount."
B. "Some tasks go, the jobs change — and how they change is a decision this room makes, not the software."
C. "That's really an HR question, and outside today's scope."
B — "Some tasks go, the jobs change — and how they change is a decision this room makes, not the software."
A is the reassurance nobody believes — and when it later proves false, it takes your whole programme's credibility with it. C is the dodge that confirms the fear. B is true in all three parts, and its real power is the third: it returns agency to the room. Fear of technology is mostly fear of having no say; give the say back explicitly.

Take it anywhere: "Some things go, the ___ changes — and how it changes is our decision." Any change where people fear for their place in it.
Phrase 3

The job: separating yourself from the hype you arrived wrapped in. Complete it:

"I'm not here to sell you ___ — half of what you've read is marketing."

"I'm not here to sell you magic — half of what you've read is marketing."
The sceptic has pre-loaded you with every exaggerated claim your industry ever made. This sentence unloads them: you side with her against the hype, which is disarming precisely because the hype is your industry's. You can only afford this phrase if what follows is concrete — which is the point. It burns the fluff so only substance is left standing.

Take it anywhere: "I'm not here to sell you magic." The credibility reset, for every field with a gap between its adverts and its products.
Phrase 4

The job: the jargon-free translation — what it actually does, for them. Complete it:

"Forget the jargon for a minute. What this ___ does is read the claims, sort them, and draft the routine responses."

"Forget the jargon for a minute. What this actually does is read the claims, sort them, and draft the routine responses."
Three plain verbs, aimed at THEIR work — not "leveraging generative capabilities" but reads, sorts, drafts. The detail that lands hardest is the local one: "the work your team does between four and six, when they're tired." Specifics about their world prove you've looked at it; jargon proves you haven't. If you can't say your product in three plain verbs, you don't understand it yet.

Take it anywhere: "Forget the jargon — what it actually does is ___, ___ and ___." The three-verb test, for everything you'll ever pitch.

🔬 Honesty as strategy

Phrase 5

The job: mining the last failure instead of stepping around it. Complete it:

"Can I ask what it actually ___ on? Not the official version."

"Can I ask what it actually failed on? Not the official version."
Every burned client carries a failure story, and most presenters try to talk over it. Asking for it — insisting on the real version — does something better: it turns the room's worst memory into your test plan. "Then those are the first three things we test" converts her scar tissue into the pilot's design. The past failure stops being evidence against you and starts being your requirements document.

Take it anywhere: "What did it actually fail on last time?" Ask it before they tell you — because they were going to tell you anyway, at the worst moment.
Phrase 6

The job: volunteering the limits before they're discovered. Put the pieces in order:

and genuinely bad atIt's very good atthe weird twenty.the routine eighty percent,
"It's very good at the routine eighty percent, and genuinely bad at the weird twenty."
The sentence that made Ms Roca look up — "you're the first person in one of these chairs to tell me what it can't do." A capability claim with a boundary on it is believable in a way an unlimited one never is; the admission is what certifies the rest. And "those stay human — on day one and day five hundred" answers the jobs question a second time, with architecture instead of reassurance.

Take it anywhere: "It's very good at ___, and genuinely bad at ___." Products, plans, people — including you, in interviews.

📏 Shrinking the bet

Phrase 7

The job: replacing the big rollout with the small proof. Complete it:

"I'd rather start ___ small and be right than start big and be ___."

"I'd rather start embarrassingly small and be right than start big and be sorry."
"Embarrassingly" is the word doing the work — it pre-accepts the criticism ("is that all?") and signals you're optimising for truth, not for the size of your own contract. A sceptic can't refuse a bet this small, and doesn't need to: one process, one team, volunteers only. Big promises ask for faith; small tests only ask for six weeks.

Take it anywhere: "I'd rather start embarrassingly small and be right." Pilots, trials, new habits, new hires.
Phrase 8

The job: handing the sceptic the measuring tape. Complete it:

"Give me one process and six weeks — and we'll measure it against numbers ___ choose."

"Give me one process and six weeks — and we'll measure it against numbers you choose."
The trust move the whole meeting turns on. The vendor's case study failed her because the vendor held the ruler; this offer hands it over — her metrics, however harsh. "If I pick harsh ones, you live with them?" "Exactly that." Nothing converts a sceptic like being made the judge; people don't argue with verdicts they were invited to define.

Take it anywhere: "Measure it against numbers you choose." The offer that costs confidence nothing and buys what nothing else can.
Phrase 9

The job: recruiting the enemy. Complete it:

"Whoever most ___ this — I'd like them ___ the pilot."

"Whoever most doubts this — I'd like them on the pilot."
Backwards, and brilliant. Projects staffed with enthusiasts fail in month six, expensively, when reality arrives; the doubter finds the same problems in week two, cheaply. And there's a second effect: the doubter who was recruited — whose objections became the test — has no reason to fight you in the corridor. Andrés will eat you alive exactly where you want to be eaten: inside the pilot, early.

Take it anywhere: "I'd like the biggest doubter on the project." Turns your loudest critic into your cheapest quality control.
Phrase 10

The job: the commitment that makes every other sentence believable. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "If you can't see it in your own numbers, I'll be the one recommending we stop — in writing, to your board."
The consultant's ultimate tell is the project that can only ever grow — every review recommending "phase two". This sentence stakes your name on the opposite: a stop, in writing, recommended by you. It's the single most convincing thing a seller can say, because it's the one thing a bad seller can't afford to. Notice it's the sentence that made the notebook open.

Take it anywhere: "If it doesn't work by ___, I'll be the one recommending we stop." Only say it if you will. Then say it everywhere.

⚡ The twist

Round 1 again — and just as the notebook opens, the CEO puts his head round the door: "Ah, the AI meeting! Marvellous. I've just told the board we'll have it running company-wide by Christmas — they're thrilled. Carry on!" And he's gone.

Your entire credibility in this room is built on "embarrassingly small", and the CEO — who pays your invoices — has just promised the opposite to his board. Ms Roca is watching you with great interest. The next thing you say decides whether she ever trusts a word of yours again.

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you name the room's fear before the room does?
  • Can you answer the jobs question without a lie in either direction?
  • Can you say your programme in three plain verbs?
  • Can you volunteer what your product is bad at — unprompted?
  • Would you stake your name on a stop recommendation? If not — why should they believe anything else?

The Echo 🕰️

On 17 December 1903, on a cold beach at Kitty Hawk, the Wright brothers flew a powered aircraft for twelve seconds. A hundred and twenty feet — shorter than the wingspan of the planes that would follow. It was, by any standard a board meeting would recognise, embarrassingly small.

The world's response was scepticism — and it was earned scepticism: the newspapers had spent years reporting grand flying machines that fell off piers, built by confident men with excellent presentations. So the Wrights did the only thing that beats a burned audience. They didn't argue, and they didn't scale up the claims. They flew again, a little further, measured everything, and let the numbers grow until denial became impossible. The doubters weren't talked round; they were out-evidenced, twelve seconds at a time.

Every sceptical client is a newspaper editor in 1903 — not stupid, just correctly calibrated by years of crashed promises. You don't owe them a bigger pitch. You owe them a Kitty Hawk: small, measured, repeatable, and impossible to argue with by spring.
