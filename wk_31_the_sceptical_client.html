The Sceptical Client

You're the outside expert brought in to lead the client's big change programme. Across the table: an operations director who has watched three of these come and go, and remembers what each one promised. She's not hostile. She's worse than hostile — she's stopped believing.

The Scene 🎬

The consultant's hardest room isn't the angry one. It's the polite one that has quietly decided not to believe you.

You lead change programmes for a large consultancy — new systems, new ways of working, the projects that reshape how a company runs. Today is the steering meeting that decides whether this one goes ahead. The slides are good. The business case is real. Neither is the problem.

The problem is Ms Roca, operations director, twenty years in the building. She has been through a systems migration that ate two years, a "transformation" that transformed nothing but the org chart, and a project whose promised savings never appeared in any number she could find. Behind her politeness stands the question every room like this is holding: what happens to my people?

What this lesson teaches works for any change you'll ever have to sell: you don't beat scepticism with a better pitch. You beat it by being the first honest person in the chair — name what changes, ask what failed before, admit what your programme won't do, start small, and let her hold the measuring tape. The meeting is won the moment she's designing the test instead of resisting the programme.

  1. Think of a change at work you resisted that turned out fine — and one you were right to resist. What told you the difference?
  2. Why does admitting a weakness make an expert MORE convincing, when logically it should do the opposite?
YouThe consultant. Believes in the programme, knows where its limits are, and needs a yes that survives contact with reality.
Ms RocaOperations director. Polite, sharp, three failed projects deep. Protects her people first and her numbers second.
First: read the conversation and mark the three places You gives ground — what changes, what failed before, what the programme won't do. Then notice what each concession buys. That trade is the whole method.

The Conversation 💬

The steering meeting. Coffee, projector, and Ms Roca's notebook — closed.

Ms RocaBefore you start — I've sat through this presentation, or its cousins, three times in ten years. Convince me this one's different, or let's all save an hour.
YouThat's fair. You've every right to be sceptical — I would be, in your chair. Three projects, three sets of promises, and you're still doing month-end the old way.
Ms Roca…You've read the history, at least. Go on.
YouThen let me start where these meetings usually end, with your team. I'm not going to pretend nothing changes — it does, or there'd be no point doing this. But nothing gets decided over your head. Which jobs change and how — that's a decision you're in, every step, or I'm doing my job badly.
Ms RocaEvery consultant says something like that. Then the plan arrives ready-made.
YouThen judge me on this meeting: I haven't brought a plan. I've brought questions. First one — the last project. Where did it fall down?
Ms RocaOfficially? "Adoption challenges." Actually — it couldn't handle our exceptions, the team never trusted it, and by month six everyone had quietly gone back to the old way.
YouThen that's my checklist. The exceptions, the trust, and whether people are still using it when nobody's watching — those are the first things we test, before anything gets rolled out anywhere.
Ms RocaHm. And what is it you're actually proposing? Because the deck I was sent is forty pages of language I refuse to repeat in this room.
YouIn plain terms, what it does is this: the routine work your team re-types between systems every day — it takes that over. The judgement calls stay with your people. And there are things it won't do, and I'd rather you heard them from me now than found them in month three: it won't handle your strange cases, and it won't run itself. Somebody on your team owns it, or it dies like the last one.
Ms RocaYou're the first person in that chair to tell me what one of these things won't do. So — what's the ask? If your next slide says "company-wide", we're done here.
YouIt doesn't. Let's walk before we run: one team, one process, a few weeks. And on what counts as success — you tell me what success looks like, and we'll measure against that. Your numbers, not mine. If you want to pick harsh ones, pick harsh ones.
Ms RocaMy numbers. …And who runs this pilot of yours? Because you'll want my keen ones, presumably. The believers.
YouThe opposite. I'd want your most sceptical people in from day one. They'll find in week two what the enthusiasts would find in month six, when it's expensive. Who's your hardest sell?
Ms RocaAndrés. Fifteen years on exceptions. He'll eat you alive.
YouThen I want Andrés. And one more thing, because it's the part nobody in my job likes saying: if it isn't working, we stop — and you'll hear it from me first. Not "phase two", not "extended scope". Stop.
Ms Roca*opens the notebook* One process. My numbers. Andrés. And you'll be the one to call it if it fails. Put exactly that in an email today — and we'll see, Mr Consultant. We'll see.

From memory — don't scroll back up.

  1. What does You promise about the team — and, just as important, what does You refuse to promise?
  2. The last project failed on three things. What were they, and what do they become?
  3. What are the four items Ms Roca writes in her notebook?
1. Promised: nothing gets decided over her head — she's in every decision about which jobs change and how. Refused: the pretence that nothing changes. "It does, or there'd be no point doing this."

2. The exceptions it couldn't handle, the team never trusting it, and everyone quietly reverting by month six. They become the checklist — the first things tested before anything is rolled out.

3. One process. Her numbers. Andrés — the biggest sceptic — on the pilot. And You calls a stop, first, if it isn't working.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for selling any change to anyone who's been burned before — a client, your own board, a team, a family. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. A burned listener is checking one thing under all your words: does this person overclaim? Every phrase here is plain on purpose. The moment you sound like the deck, you've joined the three consultants who came before you.

🤝 Meeting the scepticism

Phrase 1

The job: answering "convince me or leave" — without defending yourself. Which response?

A. "I think you'll find our approach is genuinely different this time."
B. "You've every right to be sceptical — I would be, in your chair."
C. "I understand your concerns, but let me take you through the deck first."
B — "You've every right to be sceptical — I would be, in your chair."
A is what all three previous consultants said. C asks her to sit through the very thing she's just told you she won't sit through. B does the only move that works on earned scepticism: agree with it. You're not arguing that her experience is wrong — you're joining her side of the table to look at it. Add the evidence that you know the history ("three projects, and you're still doing month-end the old way") and she has nothing left to fight.

Take it anywhere: "You've every right to be sceptical — I would be too." Any audience your industry has already disappointed.
Phrase 2

The job: being honest about impact — before anyone asks. Complete it:

"I'm not going to ___ nothing changes."

"I'm not going to pretend nothing changes."
The reassuring lie — "don't worry, everything stays the same" — is the most expensive sentence in change management: everyone knows it's false, and when it proves false it takes your credibility with it. This phrase pays a small honesty cost now to keep your word worth something later. Follow it with the reason: "it does, or there'd be no point doing this."

Take it anywhere: "I'm not going to pretend ___." The opener for every truth you're tempted to soften out of existence.
Phrase 3

The job: the promise that actually calms a room — about process, not outcomes. Complete it:

"But nothing gets decided over your ___."

"But nothing gets decided over your head."
Over your head = above you, without consulting you. You can't honestly promise people that nothing will change — but you can promise they'll be in the room when it does, and that's the promise that actually matters. Most fear of change is really fear of having no say. Give the say back, explicitly, and mean it.

Take it anywhere: "Nothing gets decided over your head." Teams, clients, and — word for word — teenagers.

🔍 Learning before pitching

Phrase 4

The job: getting the real story of the last failure. Complete it:

"The last project — where did it ___ ___?"

"The last project — where did it fall down?"
To fall down = to fail at a specific point. The question every burned client is waiting to be asked and almost never is — presenters talk over the corpse of the last project instead of examining it. Note what asking buys: the real version ("officially, adoption challenges; actually…"), and the makings of your test plan. Her scar tissue becomes your checklist: "then that's the first thing we test."

Take it anywhere: "Where did it fall down last time?" Ask it before they tell you — because they will tell you, at the worst possible moment.
Phrase 5

The job: escaping the forty-page deck. Complete it:

"In ___ terms, what it does is this."

"In plain terms, what it does is this."
Then you have to actually do it: the routine work goes to the system, the judgement calls stay with the people. If you can't say your programme in two plain sentences, you don't understand it yet — and the client can hear that. Jargon isn't just annoying; to a sceptic it's evidence, because the last three projects arrived wrapped in it too.

Take it anywhere: "In plain terms, what it does is ___." The two-sentence test, for everything you'll ever have to explain.
Phrase 6

The job: the limits, volunteered. Put the pieces in order:

and I'd rather youThere are things it won't do,heard them from me.
"There are things it won't do, and I'd rather you heard them from me."
The sentence that made Ms Roca look up — "you're the first person in that chair to tell me what one of these won't do." A claim with a boundary on it is believable in a way an unlimited claim never is; the admission certifies everything else you've said. And "from me" carries its own promise: no surprises in month three.

Take it anywhere: "There are things it won't do — I'd rather you heard them from me." Products, plans, proposals, and yourself in interviews.

📏 Making the bet small and fair

Phrase 7

The job: replacing the big rollout with a small start. Complete it:

"Let's ___ before we ___."

"Let's walk before we run."
Four ordinary words that kill the forty-page rollout plan on contact. One team, one process, a few weeks — a bet so small a sceptic doesn't need faith to take it. Big promises ask people to believe; small starts only ask them to watch. And "we're done if the next slide says company-wide" never gets said, because the next slide doesn't.

Take it anywhere: "Let's walk before we run." New systems, new hires, new habits — the universal case for starting small.
Phrase 8

The job: handing the sceptic the measuring tape. Complete it:

"You tell me what success ___ ___, and we'll measure against that."

"You tell me what success looks like, and we'll measure against that."
The last project's savings appeared only in the vendor's own case study — because the vendor held the ruler. This offer hands it over: her definition, her numbers, harsh ones if she likes. Nothing converts a sceptic faster than being made the judge, because nobody argues with a verdict they were invited to define.

Take it anywhere: "You tell me what success looks like." Projects, probation periods, any promise someone doubts you'll keep.
Phrase 9

The job: recruiting the enemy. Complete it:

"I'd want your most ___ people in from day one."

"I'd want your most sceptical people in from day one."
Backwards, and right. A pilot staffed with believers fails in month six, expensively, when reality arrives; the sceptic finds the same problems in week two, cheaply. And a doubter who was recruited — whose objections became the test — has no reason to fight you in the corridor afterwards. "He'll eat you alive." "Then I want Andrés." That exchange won more than any slide.

Take it anywhere: "I'd want the sceptics in from day one." Turns your loudest critic into your cheapest quality control.
Phrase 10

The job: the commitment that makes the rest believable. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "If it isn't working, we stop — and you'll hear it from me first."
The consultant's tell is the project that can only ever grow — every review recommending "phase two". This stakes your name on the opposite, and it's the most convincing sentence a seller can say precisely because a bad seller can't afford it. It's the one that opened the notebook. Only say it if you'll do it — then say it everywhere.

Take it anywhere: "If it isn't working, we stop — and you'll hear it from me first." Any commitment where your credibility is the product.

⚡ The twist

Round 1 again — and just as the notebook opens, the CEO puts his head round the door: "Ah, the programme meeting! Marvellous. I've just told the board we'll have it running company-wide by Christmas — they're thrilled. Carry on!" And he's gone.

Your entire credibility in this room is built on "walk before we run" — and the CEO, who pays your invoices, has just promised the board a sprint. Ms Roca is watching you with great interest. The next thing you say decides whether she ever trusts a word of yours again.

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you agree with scepticism instead of defending against it?
  • Can you be honest about what changes — and still calm the room?
  • Do you ask where the last one fell down before you pitch?
  • Can you say your programme in two plain sentences, limits included?
  • Would you stake your name on calling a stop? If not — why should they believe anything else?

The Echo 🕰️

On 17 December 1903, on a cold beach at Kitty Hawk, the Wright brothers flew a powered aircraft for twelve seconds. A hundred and twenty feet — shorter than the wingspan of the planes that would follow. By any standard a board meeting would recognise, it was a very small start.

The world's response was scepticism — and it was earned scepticism: the newspapers had spent years reporting grand flying machines that fell off piers, built by confident men with excellent presentations. So the Wrights did the only thing that works on a burned audience. They didn't argue, and they didn't inflate the claims. They flew again, a little further, measured everything, and let the evidence grow until denial became impossible. The doubters weren't talked round; they were out-flown, twelve seconds at a time.

Every sceptical client is a newspaper editor in 1903 — not stupid, just correctly calibrated by years of crashed promises. You don't owe them a bigger pitch. You owe them a Kitty Hawk: small, measured, repeatable, and impossible to argue with by spring.
