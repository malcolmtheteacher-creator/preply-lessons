The Status Game

B2–C1 Level · Speaking & Discussion Focus

Before we start

Some people say that, under the surface, almost everything humans do is a quiet competition for status — for respect, rank, and the good opinion of others. Today we test that idea, and you do most of the talking.

Warm-up — answer honestly

1. When was the last time you felt a small glow of pride because someone admired or praised you?

2. When did you last feel quietly stung by being ignored, talked over, or looked down on?

3. Who is the highest-status person you know in real life? What gives them that status — money, skill, kindness, power, something else?

A claim to argue with:

“The desire for status is as basic as hunger or thirst. We never really switch it off — we just hide it, even from ourselves.”

Do you agree? Can you think of a moment in your own life that this explains — or one it cannot explain?

Three ways to win

The writer Will Storr argues that we chase status in three main ways. Most situations mix them, but one usually dominates. Read each one, then we will talk.

1. Dominance

“Respect me, or else.”

Status taken by force, fear, or intimidation. The bully, the tyrant, the loud boss who talks over everyone. The oldest and ugliest game.

2. Virtue

“Respect me, I’m good.”

Status earned by being seen as moral, loyal, generous, or pure. The saint, the volunteer — but also the person loudly announcing how kind they are.

3. Success

“Respect me, I’m good at this.”

Status earned through skill, talent, or achievement. The expert, the athlete, the brilliant cook. We tend to admire this game the most.

Talk it through

1. Which game does your own workplace (or your studies) run on most? Give an example.

2. Which game do you find most attractive in other people? Which do you find hardest to respect?

3. Can you think of a single person who plays all three at once?

Check your understanding

A football coach who wins by terrifying his players, a charity worker admired for her sacrifice, and a surgeon famous for her skill — which game is each one playing?

Show Answer
The coach → dominance (fear). The charity worker → virtue (seen as good). The surgeon → success (skill). In real life they often overlap — the surgeon’s skill may also earn her a kind of dominance in the operating theatre.

Status language

Click each card to reveal the meaning. These are the words you will use in the discussion — try to work three of them into your next answer.

a snub A deliberate insult by ignoring or rejecting someone. “Not being invited felt like a snub.”
prestige Widespread respect and admiration earned by reputation or achievement. “a job with real prestige”
to signal (something) To show others a quality about yourself, often indirectly. “The watch signals wealth.”
a humblebrag A boast disguised as a complaint or modest remark. “I’m so tired from my holiday in the Maldives.”
conspicuous Clearly visible; designed to be noticed. “conspicuous spending” = buying things mainly so others see your wealth.
the pecking order The informal ranking of who is more or less important in a group. “She’s near the top of the pecking order.”
one-upmanship The art of making yourself look better than others, point by point. “Their chat turned into pure one-upmanship.”
to look down on To regard someone as inferior. Opposite: to look up to (to admire). “He looks down on people who didn’t go to university.”
to opt out (of) To choose not to take part. “Can you really opt out of the status game?”

Use it

Finish each sentence about your own life, out loud, using the word in bold:

• “Something people often buy mainly to signal success is…”

• “A job that has a lot of prestige in my country is…”

• “The last humblebrag I heard (or made!) was…”

• “A group where I can clearly feel the pecking order is…”

Spot the game

For each situation, decide which game is being played — dominance, virtue, or success — and say how you know. Several answers are defensible; the reasoning matters more than the label.

1.

A man posts a photo of himself handing money to a homeless person, with a long caption about compassion.

Show Answer
Mostly virtue — the kindness is real, but posting it converts goodness into status. The discussion question: does the audience cancel out the good deed, or not?

2.

A teenager spends a month’s wages on trainers that everyone at school will recognise.

Show Answer
A success / belonging signal through conspicuous spending — the trainers say “I have taste and money, I belong here.” Note how the brand only works if others recognise it.

3.

In a meeting, a manager interrupts a junior colleague, finishes her sentences, and never lets her present her own idea.

Show Answer
Classic dominance — status taken by control and intimidation rather than earned by skill.

4.

A famous chef quietly mentors young cooks for free and refuses all interviews.

Show Answer
Tricky. Looks like pure virtue with the status switched off — but the refusal of fame can itself become a kind of prestige (“the chef who doesn’t need fame”). This is the trap we return to in the last tab.

Can you leave the game?

Here is where the idea gets genuinely difficult. Read the short passage, then we debate it.

The most seductive version of the theory says that status explains everything — even generosity, even humility, even the hermit who walks away from society. But that is also its weakness. If every act of kindness can be re-described as a hidden move for status, then the theory can never be proved wrong. And a theory that explains everything may, in the end, explain nothing.

There is a deeper trap. Imagine someone who says, “I refuse to play these silly status games.” Doesn’t that refusal make them seem wise, above it all — in other words, higher status? Opting out can be the highest move of all: the status of the person who is too good for status.

Debate — take a side and defend it

1. Is the desire for status the single engine behind human behaviour, or just one drive among many (alongside love, curiosity, fear, duty)?

2. Can a person genuinely opt out of the status game — or is “opting out” just another way to win it?

3. Is wanting status a bad thing? Or is it the same hunger that drives people to become excellent, generous, and brave?

Useful phrases for disagreeing well

• “I see your point, but I’d push back on…”

• “That holds for X, but it can’t explain Y.”

• “Isn’t that just another way of saying…?”

• “I’d go further than you, actually — I think…”

Final speaking task:

Take the strongest case you can against your own real opinion. If you think we can’t escape the game, argue that we can. If you think we can, argue that we can’t. Start the timer.

One line to take away

Once you have the phrase “status game,” you start seeing it everywhere — which is exactly the thing to be careful about. A lens that shows you everything can also stop you seeing anything else.
