The World as Will

In which the most pessimistic philosopher in history looks behind the curtain of appearances and finds a blind, striving, purposeless Will

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860) looked at Hegel's optimism and said: no. The world is not Spirit ascending toward freedom. It is a blind, irrational force—Will—that generates endless desire and suffering. Escape comes only through asceticism, art, and compassion. This lesson explores pessimism as a philosophy, and asks: might Schopenhauer have seen something Hegel missed?

A disturbing question

Hegel says history moves toward freedom and the realization of Spirit. This is comforting. But Schopenhauer asks: what if Hegel is wrong? What if the Will that drives all existence is blind, irrational, and incapable of being satisfied? What if history is not progress toward freedom, but merely suffering endlessly repeating itself?

Before you dismiss him as too dark, consider: has he seen something in life that Hegel missed?

II. The Story — The Dark Wisdom

Arthur Schopenhauer was born in Danzig in 1788, the son of a merchant family. Unlike Hegel, who became a celebrated professor surrounded by followers, Schopenhauer spent much of his life in relative obscurity, developing a philosophy that ran counter to all the optimism of his age.

Where did his pessimism come from? Partly from temperament. But also from keen observation. Schopenhauer looked at the world and saw something the rationalists had missed: life is fundamentally driven by desire, and desire can never be permanently satisfied.

The Will Behind Appearances

Schopenhauer took Kant's distinction between appearance and reality (phenomena and noumena) and gave it a new interpretation. Behind all the apparent diversity of the world—all the separate things, people, and desires—there is one thing: the Will.

Man can do what he wills, but he cannot will what he wills. — Schopenhauer

What did he mean? You can act on your desires. But you cannot choose what you desire. The Will operates in you, through you, without your conscious choice. Every creature is driven by the Will to survive and to reproduce. This is not a rational goal. It is blind striving.

The Suffering Machine

Here is Schopenhauer's stark vision: all life is suffering. Why? Because desire is the root of suffering. We desire things we don't have. When we get them, we desire something else. Satisfaction is temporary. Boredom and discontent are our natural state.

The Cycle of Suffering

Desire (frustration, pain) → Satisfaction (brief, fragile) → Boredom (void, emptiness) → New Desire (and the cycle repeats)

This is the human condition. We are trapped in an endless cycle. The universe did not create us for happiness. The Will created us to survive and reproduce. Everything else is illusion or distraction.

Three Ways Out

If life is suffering, can we escape? Schopenhauer proposed three paths:

1. Aesthetic Contemplation

When we encounter true art or beauty, we step outside ourselves for a moment. We stop desiring. We simply experience. In that moment, we are free from the Will. Music, in particular, speaks directly to the Will itself—it is the voice of suffering and transcendence.

2. Asceticism

Deny the Will. Renounce desire, comfort, sexuality, ambition. This is the path of the saint or the monk. By denying the Will in yourself, you acknowledge that the Will (life itself) is not worth affirming.

3. Compassion

When you recognize that all beings are expressions of the same blind Will, suffering the same fundamental suffering, compassion becomes possible. You see the same Will suffering in every creature. This recognition can lead to ethical action.

Schopenhauer and the Epicurean answer: Recall Epicurus from earlier in the course. Epicurus said we should minimize pain and seek simple pleasures. Schopenhauer agrees—but he is harsher. For Schopenhauer, all desire is essentially painful, even the simple ones. The only escape is to stop desiring altogether. Epicurus says "enjoy moderately." Schopenhauer says "the fact that you desire proves you are suffering."

III. The Ideas — What Schopenhauer Offers

Idea 1: The Will as the Thing-in-Itself

Behind appearance, there is blind, irrational striving.

Kant said we cannot know the thing-in-itself. Schopenhauer said we can—because we are the thing-in-itself. We experience the Will directly as our own will. The world is the objectification of this Will. It is not rational, not moral, not purposeful. It simply is—blind, endless striving.

Idea 2: Universal Suffering

All life is characterized by suffering.

This is not a mistake or a failure. It is the fundamental condition. Suffering arises from desire. And desire arises from the Will. Every creature that lives must desire in order to survive. Therefore, every creature suffers. This is not pessimism, Schopenhauer would say. It is realism.

Idea 3: Asceticism, Art, and Compassion as Escape

There are moments when we can step outside the Will.

We cannot escape the Will permanently. But we can escape it temporarily through aesthetic experience, through denying our desires, and through recognizing our fundamental unity with all suffering beings. These moments of transcendence are the only real peace we can know.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Is Schopenhauer right about suffering?

Does desire always lead to suffering? Can satisfaction ever be more than momentary? Or are there desires that don't disappoint?

Hegel vs. Schopenhauer

Hegel sees history moving toward freedom. Schopenhauer sees it as endless repetition of suffering. Which seems more true based on what you know?

Can we escape the Will?

Schopenhauer offers three escapes: art, asceticism, compassion. Are these real escapes? Or just temporary distractions?

Music as ultimate art

Schopenhauer said music speaks directly to the Will. Have you experienced music that way—where it seemed to reach something deeper than words?

Is pessimism a gift or a curse?

If Schopenhauer is right about suffering, does knowing this help us live better? Or does it just make us miserable?

The Will we cannot choose

Schopenhauer said "you can do what you will, but not will what you will." Do you feel free? Or are you driven by forces you didn't choose?
