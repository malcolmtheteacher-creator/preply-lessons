The World as Will

In which the most pessimistic philosopher discovers a blind, irrational force behind all existence—and finds strange comfort in denial

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860) looked at Hegel’s optimism and said: no. The world is not Spirit ascending toward freedom. It is a blind, purposeless force—the Will—that generates endless desire and suffering. Life swings between suffering and boredom. The only escapes are art, asceticism, and compassion. This lesson explores pessimism as a serious philosophy, and asks whether Schopenhauer saw something Hegel missed.

A disturbing question

Hegel says history moves toward freedom and the realization of Spirit. Uplifting. But Schopenhauer asks: what if Hegel is fundamentally wrong? What if the Will that drives all existence is blind, irrational, and incapable of being satisfied? What if history is not progress toward freedom, but merely suffering endlessly repeating itself in new forms?

Before you dismiss him as too dark, ask yourself: has he seen something in life that Hegel missed?

II. The Story — The Dark Wisdom

Arthur Schopenhauer was born on February 22, 1788, in Danzig, the son of a wealthy merchant family. Unlike Hegel, who became celebrated, surrounded by students and influence, Schopenhauer lived much of his life in relative obscurity, developing a philosophy that ran radically counter to all the optimism of his age. He was cantankerous, misanthropic, difficult. But perhaps he was onto something.

As a young man, Schopenhauer studied at the University of Göttingen and Berlin. He read Kant carefully. He was influenced by Eastern philosophy—Hindu and Buddhist texts—which was unusual for a Western philosopher. He wrote a dissertation on the principle of sufficient reason. But his real breakthrough came in understanding Kant not as a vindication of reason, but as its limitation.

The Will Behind Appearances

Schopenhauer took Kant’s distinction between phenomena (appearances) and noumena (things-in-themselves) and transformed it. Behind all the apparent diversity of the world—all the separate things, people, objects, and competing desires—there is one fundamental reality: the Will.

Not God. Not Spirit. Not reason. The Will—blind, irrational, purposeless striving. It is in every creature, driving every action. The Will to survive. The Will to reproduce. The Will to power. It operates in you without your conscious choosing. You can do what you will. But you cannot will what you will.

Man can do what he wills, but he cannot will what he wills. — Schopenhauer

The Problem of Desire

Here is Schopenhauer’s stark vision of the human condition: All life is suffering. Why? Because desire is the root of all suffering. We desire things we lack. We suffer in the lacking. When we finally get what we desire, the satisfaction is brief, even illusory. Then we desire something else. And the cycle repeats.

The Eternal Pendulum

Desire (frustration, pain, longing) → Satisfaction (brief, fragile, fleeting) → Boredom (void, emptiness, meaninglessness) → New Desire (and the cycle repeats infinitely)

This is not pessimism, Schopenhauer insisted. This is realism. We are trapped in an endless cycle. The universe did not create us for happiness. The blind Will created us to survive and reproduce. Happiness, fulfillment, meaning—these are illusions or distractions from the fundamental truth: life is suffering.

The veil of Maya: Schopenhauer borrowed the Hindu concept of Maya—the veil of illusion that makes us see the world as divided into separate things. In truth, all is one Will. The appearance of separation is illusion. Once you see through the veil, you see that what you call individuality, what you call your separate desires and happiness, is part of the same blind striving present in all beings.

Three Ways to Escape

If life is suffering, is escape possible? Schopenhauer proposed three paths, in ascending order of difficulty:

1. Aesthetic Contemplation — Temporary Escape

When we encounter true art or natural beauty, something shifts. We step outside ourselves momentarily. The usual grasping, desiring, suffering self falls away. We simply experience. In that moment—brief, rare, precious—we are free from the Will. We are not trying to possess beauty. We are just witnessing it. Music speaks directly to the Will itself. In music, we hear suffering and transcendence made audible. Art is a window to something beyond the endless cycle.

2. Asceticism — Active Denial

This is the path of the saint, the monk, the renunciate. Deny the Will in yourself. Renounce desire, comfort, sexuality, ambition, the desire for recognition. By consciously denying the Will, you acknowledge that the Will (life itself) is not worth affirming. You say “No” to existence. This is the only truly ethical response to suffering.

3. Compassion — Recognition of Unity

When you recognize that all beings are expressions of the same blind Will, suffering the same fundamental suffering, compassion becomes possible—not as sentimentality but as metaphysical insight. You see the same Will groaning in every creature. This recognition can lead to genuine ethical action: to reduce suffering not because of moral rules but because you recognize the other’s suffering as your own.

III. The Ideas — What Schopenhauer Offers

Idea 1: The Will as the Thing-in-Itself

Behind all appearance, there is blind, irrational, purposeless striving.

Kant said we cannot know the thing-in-itself. Schopenhauer says we can—because we ARE the thing-in-itself. We experience the Will directly as our own will, our own drives and desires. The world is the objectification of this Will. It is not rational. It is not moral. It is not purposeful. It simply is—a blind, endless striving that reproduces itself infinitely.

Idea 2: Universal Suffering

All life is characterized by suffering. This is not a mistake; it is fundamental.

Suffering arises from desire. Desire arises from the Will. Every creature that lives must desire in order to survive. Therefore, every creature suffers. Pain is active; pleasure is merely the absence of pain. The universe is not set up for our happiness. It is set up for the continuation of life through reproduction and survival—and these require eternal suffering.

Idea 3: Asceticism, Art, and Compassion as Escape

There are moments and practices when we can step outside the Will.

We cannot escape the Will permanently while we live. But we can escape it temporarily through aesthetic experience, through ascetic denial of our desires, and through recognizing our fundamental unity with all suffering beings. These moments of transcendence are the only real peace we can know. They are not happiness (which would be illusory). They are peace—a cessation of the eternal striving.

Idea 4: The Hedgehog's Dilemma

Community is impossible; solitude is painful.

Schopenhauer's image: hedgehogs huddling together for warmth on a cold night. But as they draw close, they prick each other with their spines. So they move apart. But then they freeze. They must constantly adjust their distance. This is human relationship. We need each other. But we inevitably hurt each other. The best we can do is maintain sufficient distance to avoid excessive pain while still receiving some warmth.

Walk through your Location 42 — Feel the darkness and the weight

Don’t make these pretty. Make them oppressive, dark, real. The pendulum swinging. The beautiful painting leading nowhere. The hedgehogs in futile proximity. The veil revealing not beauty but the same suffering everywhere. This is the kingdom of pessimism.

Feel it now: The blind force. The wheel of suffering turning. The brief beauty and the darkness behind it. The hedgehogs can never be comfortable. The veil shows no escape. You are in Schopenhauer’s world.

VI. Your Turn — Confront the Pessimist

Schopenhauer is hard to dismiss once you take him seriously. Choose 2–3 of these questions and wrestle honestly.

Is Schopenhauer right about suffering?

Does desire always lead to suffering? Can satisfaction ever be lasting? Or are there desires fulfilled that don’t disappoint?

Hegel vs. Schopenhauer

Hegel sees history as progress toward freedom. Schopenhauer sees it as endless repetition of suffering. Which vision seems more true?

Can art really rescue us?

Does experiencing beauty or music truly take us outside the cycle of desire? Or is Schopenhauer naive about art’s power?

The way of asceticism

Is renouncing desire the answer? Is the monk freer than the merchant? Or is asceticism just another form of desire?

Pessimism as honesty

Schopenhauer claimed pessimism is realism, not neurosis. Is he right? Does acknowledging suffering lead to wisdom or despair?

Eastern philosophy in the West

Schopenhauer drew on Buddhism and Hinduism. What does bringing Eastern wisdom into Western philosophy reveal?
