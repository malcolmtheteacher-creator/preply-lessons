B2/C1 Level · Photography, and what happens in the half-second before you press the button.

Today: we're working on the language of describing and speculating about images, so you can talk about any photograph — or any scene in front of you — with precision instead of "it's nice, I like it".

Reading the frame

Photographers talk about composition — where things sit inside the rectangle. You don't need to be a photographer to use this language, and it transfers straight into describing anything you can see.

The rule of thirds
The subject sits off-centre, on an intersection.
Leading lines
Lines draw the eye towards the subject.
Framing
Something in the scene frames the subject.
Symmetry & reflection
The frame mirrors itself across an axis.

The language you need — noticing

Precision comes from saying where a thing is and what it does to your eye. These are the phrases that do the work:

in the foreground off to one side the eye is drawn to framed by cropped out caught mid-stride the light falls across out of focus at eye level from a low vantage point
Two that people get wrong.
• to crop is what the photographer does to the picture — you crop something out. The thing itself doesn't crop.
• candid ≠ honest. A candid photograph is one where the subject wasn't posing and often didn't know. Its opposite is staged or posed.
Try it now — out loud

Look up from the screen at whatever is actually in front of you. Describe it as if it were a photograph, using at least four of the phrases above. Where would you stand? What would you crop out?

Speculating: the half-second you didn't see

A photograph gives you a fraction of a second and withholds everything either side of it. So talking about images is mostly speculation — and English has a precise gear for that. Overstate it and you sound naïve; hedge it well and you sound thoughtful.

The speculation ladder — from most certain to least:
• She must have just… — you're near-certain, and you're reasoning backwards from evidence.
• Judging by…, I'd say… — you're naming the evidence out loud.
• It looks as though / as if… — the appearance suggests it.
• There's a sense that… / You get the impression… — a feeling the image creates, not a fact.
• It's hard to tell whether… or… — you're refusing to decide, on purpose.
• She can't have been… — near-certain in the negative.

Watch how it's done

Scene one A woman stands at a bus stop in heavy rain, holding a folded newspaper flat above her head. She isn't looking at the road. She's looking back down the street, past the camera, and half-smiling.

"Judging by the newspaper, she can't have been expecting rain — nobody carries a paper as an umbrella by choice. It looks as though she's just spotted someone coming, because she's turned away from the road entirely. There's a sense that the bus is now the least interesting thing in the frame."

Now one together — you lead

Scene two An empty swimming pool at what looks like early morning. A single pair of shoes sits neatly at the deep end, toes pointing at the water. The surface is completely still. In the top corner of the frame, a hand is just visible, reaching in from outside the picture.
Speak — build the reading
  • What's the most certain thing you can say? Use must have or can't have, and say what your evidence is.
  • Now the least certain. What does this image make you feel that it doesn't actually show?
  • The hand in the corner: is it a flaw, or the whole photograph? Argue it either way.

Now one on your own

Scene three A boy of about nine sits on a suitcase in an airport corridor, both arms wrapped around a cardboard box. The box has holes punched in the top. Around him, adults walk past in both directions, blurred. He is the only sharp thing in the frame.

First, tighten your language on paper. Fill each gap with one word.

1. He have been waiting a long time — nobody sits on a suitcase when there are seats.

2. It looks though the adults haven't noticed him at all.

3. by the holes in the box, there is something alive inside it.

4. It's hard to whether he is arriving or leaving.

5. The blur means everyone else was moving; he have been perfectly still.

1. must — (certainty about the past, reasoned from evidence)

2. as — (looks as though)

3. Judging — (judging by + the evidence you're naming)

4. tell — (it's hard to tell whether… or…)

5. must — (the only reading the sharpness allows)

Now speak — 60 seconds, no stopping

Read scene three once more, then look away from it and talk for a full minute. Move down the ladder as you go: start with what must be true, end with what you merely get the impression of. Don't resolve it — a good reading of a photograph leaves the question open.

Arguments worth having

Choose two of the four. Take a side, and use the speculation language when you're describing what a photograph does — the hedging is what makes an argument sound considered rather than shouted.

Time remaining: 3:00
Card one

Does the camera steal the moment?

A psychologist walked students round a museum, asking them to photograph some objects and simply look at others. Next day they remembered the photographed objects less accurately — she called it the photo-taking impairment effect. But there was a twist: when people zoomed in on one detail, memory held up, even for the parts outside the frame.

So is the problem the camera — or the lack of attention? Build a case. What does the zoom finding tell you about what memory actually needs?

Card two

Whose face is it?

Street photography has always depended on the candid shot: strangers, unposed, unaware. The tradition produced some of the most humane images ever made. It also means being photographed by someone you never agreed to.

Where exactly does interesting become intrusive? Does it change if the photograph is beautiful? If it's published? If the person is a child, or asleep, or crying?

Card three

Promptography

In 2023 an artist entered an image in the creative category of a major international photography competition and won. He then refused the prize, revealing the picture was generated by AI. He argued the two things should never compete: "Promptography is done with prompts. Photography is done with light."

Was refusing the prize the right move, or a stunt? Is his distinction a real one — and does a viewer who can't tell the difference have any reason to care?

Card four

The unphotographed life

Some people photograph almost nothing and remember their lives in words. Others photograph constantly and can produce an image of any day you name. Both insist theirs is the richer memory.

Which are you, and has it changed? Make the strongest possible case for the opposite habit to your own — then say what it would actually cost you to switch.

Teach it back

Speak — one minute, as if to someone who's never heard it

Explain the difference between saying "she must have just arrived" and "it looks as though she's just arrived". When would choosing the wrong one actually cause a problem — in a job, in a news report, in a courtroom?

Write the rule

In one sentence, and without looking back at tab 3: what is the rule for must have and can't have when you're speculating about the past?

One way of putting it: use must have + past participle when the evidence leaves only one sensible explanation, and can't have + past participle when the evidence rules an explanation out. Both are about your confidence, not about the past being certain.

If your sentence says the same thing in different words, it's right. If it mentions "possibility", check again — that's might have, which sits lower down the ladder.

Odd one out

Which one doesn't belong with the others, and why? There is a defensible case for more than one answer — say yours out loud before revealing.

candid  ·  posed  ·  unguarded  ·  spontaneous

posed — the other three all describe a subject who isn't performing for the camera. Posed is the opposite of all of them.

If you argued for candid on the grounds that it's the only one that's specifically a photography term while the others describe people generally — that also works, and it's the more interesting answer.

Mixed practice — you choose the gear

Each of these needs a different level of certainty. Say each one aloud in full, choosing your structure, then check.

1. Her coat is soaking but her hair is dry — she (wear) a hood.

2. The shadows are extremely long, so it (take) at midday.

3. Everyone in the frame is looking left, so something (happen) just outside it.

1. must have been wearing — (the only explanation the evidence allows)

2. can't have been taken — (long shadows rule midday out; note the passive)

3. must have been happening — (reasoning backwards from where the eyes point)

From memory — no looking

  1. Name three different ways to talk about the future in English, and give a situation where each one is the natural choice.
  2. What is the difference between "I used to walk to work" and "I would walk to work"? Which one works with a state like live or own — and which doesn't?
  3. Give two ways of softening a disagreement so it stays polite but still counts as disagreeing.
  4. Turn this into reported speech: "I've been trying to reach you since Tuesday." — she said…
  5. What's the difference in meaning between "He stopped smoking" and "He stopped to smoke"?

1. will (decision made now, prediction), going to (intention already decided, or evidence you can see now), present continuous (a fixed arrangement with a time and place). Also acceptable: present simple for timetables, be about to for the immediate future.

2. Both describe repeated past actions. Only used to works with states — "I used to live there" is fine, "I would live there" is not. Would needs a repeated action.

3. For example: "I take your point, but…" · "I'm not sure I'd go that far." · "There's something in that, although…" · "I'd have said it was more a case of…"

4. She said she'd been trying to reach me since Tuesday. (present perfect continuous shifts back to past perfect continuous; "you" becomes "me")

5. Stopped smoking = he gave up the habit. Stopped to smoke = he stopped what he was doing in order to have a cigarette. The verb after stop changes the meaning completely.

What actually worked

I can…

I can describe an image precisely — saying where things sit in the frame and what draws the eye — and I can speculate about it at the right level of certainty, from must have down to it's hard to tell whether, instead of just saying whether I like it.
