The Man Who Invented the Identity Crisis

Erik Erikson never knew who his father was. He never went to university. Then he mapped the whole of human life in eight stages, and the world still uses his map.

Today: you'll follow the life of the man behind the eight stages of life. By the end, you'll be able to explain his theory in your own words, and talk about wishes and regrets with wish and if only.
Before we start. Let's see what comes to mind. There are no wrong answers here.
Discuss:
  • People often talk about "stages" of life: childhood, the teenage years, middle age... How many stages would you say there are? Name them.
  • Which age is the hardest to be, in your opinion? Why?
  • Do people really change after the age of 30? Or is our personality fixed by then?

The vocabulary of this story

Ten words. Work through the four activities. Say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. At the synagogue, the boys said he looked too Scandinavian. At school, the boys said he was too Jewish. He didn't fit in anywhere. He was an outsider.
an outsider (n) — a person who doesn't belong to a group, or feels they don't. Opposite: an insider.
2. The soldiers came home from the war, but many didn't know who they were any more. Their old life made no sense. Erikson said they were going through a crisis.
a crisis (n, plural crises) — a time of great difficulty, when an important decision or change has to happen. A mid-life crisis, an identity crisis.
3. A baby can't walk before it can stand. Erikson believed life works the same way: you go through one stage and then move on to the next.
a stage (n) — one step or period in a process. At this stage of my life; the early stages; to reach a stage.

B · Which word means…?

Three words: a degree · a stepfather · a virtue. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which word means a good quality in a person's character, such as courage or honesty?
a virtue (n) — Erikson gave each stage of life its own virtue: a strength you gain when the stage goes well. Adjective: virtuous.
2. Which word means the qualification you get when you finish a university course?
a degree (n) — to get / have / do a degree in something. Erikson became a Harvard professor without one.
3. Which word means the man who is married to your mother but is not your biological father?
a stepfather (n) — also stepmother, stepson, stepdaughter, stepbrother, stepsister.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. At each stage, you face a conflict. You don't win it completely. You find a healthy balance. Erikson called this resolving the conflict. Resolve means…
  (a) to avoid   (b) to start   (c) to find a solution to
(c) — to resolve (v) a problem, a conflict, an argument. Noun: a resolution.
2. At 80, some people look back on their lives with pride. Others feel deep regret. Regret means…
  (a) sadness about something you did, or didn't do   (b) anger at other people   (c) fear of the future
(a) — regret (n and v). I regret leaving; I have no regrets. The grammar tab gives you another way to say it: I wish I hadn't…

D · Word building & collocation

1. You know the verb trust. Build the family: the opposite noun, when you don't trust anyone: ___ · an adjective for a person you can trust: ___ · a person who trusts everyone easily is very ___.
mistrust · trustworthy · trusting. Erikson's very first stage is called Trust vs. Mistrust.
2. One noun completes all three: an ___ card (in your wallet) · to search for your own ___ · a crisis of ___. It means who you are.
identity — identity card, to search for your identity, an identity crisis. Verb: to identify with someone = to feel you are like them.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — The boy who didn't fit

Erik was born in Frankfurt, Germany, on 15 June 1902. His mother, Karla, was a young Danish Jewish woman. She never told him who his father was. When Erik was three, she married a Jewish children's doctor, Theodor Homburger. The boy became Erik Homburger. For years, he was told that Theodor was his real father.

But Erik began to suspect the truth. He was tall, with blond hair and blue eyes. He looked nothing like his parents. At the synagogue, other boys called him "the goy", the non-Jew. At school, other boys called him a Jew. He belonged to two worlds, and to neither. Later in life, he said he had always felt like an outsider.

He finished school in 1920, but he didn't go to university. Instead, he travelled around Europe for several years with a sketchbook. He wanted to be an artist. He slept in cheap rooms, drew people and places, and tried to work out who he was. He was lost, and he knew it.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was Theodor Homburger, and what was Erik told about him?

2. Why did Erik feel like an outsider, both at the synagogue and at school?

1. A Jewish children's doctor who married Erik's mother when Erik was three. He was really Erik's stepfather, but Erik was told he was his real father.

2. With his blond hair and blue eyes, the Jewish boys saw him as a non-Jew. At school, the other boys saw him as Jewish. He didn't fully belong to either group.

Discuss:
  • Erik spent his early twenties travelling and drawing, with no plan. Is that time wasted, or is it an important part of growing up?

Part 2 — Eight stages

In 1927, an old school friend invited him to Vienna. A small school there needed a teacher, and Erik was good with children. The pupils came from families close to Sigmund Freud. At the school, Erik met Freud's daughter, Anna, a pioneer of child psychology. She saw something special in him. She trained him as a psychoanalyst, a man without a degree. He also got a teaching certificate in Maria Montessori's method.

In Vienna he married Joan Serson, a Canadian dancer. In 1933, as the Nazis grew stronger, the family left for the United States. Erik became one of the first child psychoanalysts in Boston. Soon he was working at Harvard, then Yale, then Berkeley. He still had no degree. In 1939 he became an American citizen and chose a new name: Erik Erikson. It means "Erik, son of Erik". The boy with no father had become his own father.

Freud believed that our personality is mostly formed by the age of five or six. Erikson disagreed. He had watched children in Vienna, in Boston and in two Native American communities. He believed we keep developing all our lives. And he believed other people shape us at every step: family, friends, school and society. In 1950, in his book Childhood and Society, he described eight stages of life. At each stage we face a conflict between two forces. If we find a healthy balance, we gain a strength. He called it a virtue.

Part 3 — The crisis with his name on it

Stage five was the one Erikson knew best. He gave the world a new phrase for it: the identity crisis. He first used it for soldiers coming home from the Second World War. They had lost their sense of who they were. Soon the phrase was used for teenagers, and today everyone uses it.

In 1950, the University of California asked all its teachers to sign a loyalty oath, a promise that they were not communists. Erikson wasn't a communist, but he refused to sign, and he left his job. Later, his books made him famous. Gandhi's Truth won the Pulitzer Prize in 1970. Harvard made him a professor, the man who had never been to university.

His own life didn't always follow his theory. In 1944, Erik and Joan had a son, Neil, who was born with Down syndrome. On doctors' advice, as was common then, Neil was sent to live in an institution. The family rarely talked about him, and he died in 1965, still a young man. Years later, Erikson's daughter wrote a book about growing up with a famous father. She described a man who found it hard to be close to his own family.

Erikson never found out who his real father was. He kept searching until old age. In his eighties, he and Joan wrote about the last stage of life from the inside. He died in 1994, aged 91. Joan, then in her nineties, added a ninth stage to the theory: very old age, when the body grows weak and the old conflicts come back again.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was the phrase "identity crisis" first used for, and who is it used for today?

2. Why did Erikson leave the University of California?

3. What did Joan Erikson add to the theory, and why then?

1. Soldiers returning from the Second World War. Today it's used for teenagers, and for anyone who loses their sense of who they are.

2. He refused to sign a loyalty oath saying he was not a communist, even though he wasn't one.

3. A ninth stage for very old age. She was in her nineties herself, so she was writing about it from the inside.

Discuss:
  • He understood families better than almost anyone, but his daughter said he struggled with his own. Does that make his ideas less believable, or just more human?

Grammar — wish & if only

Erikson's last stage is all about looking back: am I happy with my life, or do I wish it had been different? English uses wish and if only for this, and the verb after them moves one step back in time.

Wishes about now:
• wish + past simple — a present situation you'd like to be different: "I wish I had a bigger flat." (I don't have one.)
• wish + could — an ability you don't have: "I wish I could sing."
• wish + would — you want someone else to change their behaviour: "I wish the neighbours would stop shouting." Not for yourself: never I wish I would.
Wishes about the past (regrets):
• wish + past perfect: "I wish I had saved some money last year." (I didn't.)
• Negative: "He wishes he hadn't sold his old guitar."

If only = a stronger, more emotional wish, with the same grammar: "If only it wasn't raining!" · "If only we had left earlier!"

A small trap: with be, careful English uses were for every person: "I wish I were taller." In everyday speech, was is also fine.

1. Choose the form

Complete each sentence with the correct form of the verb in brackets. Look for clues about time.

1. A teenager: "I have no idea who I am. I wish I (know) what I want to do with my life."

2. A 70-year-old: "I stayed in the same town all my life. I wish I (travel) more when I was young."

3. A four-year-old: "I'm too small! I wish I (reach) the biscuits on the top shelf."

4. A parent: "My son leaves his clothes all over the floor. I wish he (pick) them up!"

1. knew (a present situation → past simple)

2. had travelled (a past regret → past perfect)

3. could reach (an ability you don't have → could)

4. would pick (someone else's behaviour → would)

Going deeper

Which stage is this?

Eight people, in mixed order. Decide which stage each one is in, and which side of the conflict they are on. Say it, then reveal.

1. A 50-year-old nurse starts training young nurses at weekends. She wants to pass on what she knows.
Stage 7, Generativity — she's giving something that will last after her.
2. A baby cries, and every time, someone comes quickly to feed and hold her.
Stage 1, Trust — she's learning that the world is safe and people can be relied on.
3. A 15-year-old changes her clothes, her music and her friends three times in one year.
Stage 5, Identity vs. Role Confusion — she's trying on different identities. For Erikson, that's healthy at this age.
4. A two-year-old pushes his dad's hand away, shouts "Me do it!" and tries to put his own shoes on.
Stage 2, Autonomy — he wants to do things by himself.
5. A nine-year-old compares his marks with his classmates' and decides he's "bad at everything".
Stage 4, Inferiority — the negative side of Industry vs. Inferiority.
6. A 78-year-old woman looks through old photos and smiles: "I made mistakes, but it was a good life."
Stage 8, Integrity — she accepts her life as it was.
7. A five-year-old invents a game of "shops" and gives every child in the garden a job.
Stage 3, Initiative — she's planning and leading.
8. A 28-year-old ends every relationship after a few months, because getting close feels dangerous.
Stage 6, Isolation — the negative side of Intimacy vs. Isolation.
What the story teaches. First, the theory grew out of a life. A boy who never knew his father, and never felt he belonged, spent his career asking "Who am I?". His most famous idea, the identity crisis, was his own story.

Second, the theory has critics. Some researchers, such as Carol Gilligan, argued that it was based mostly on the lives of men. Others say the ages are too fixed: today many people study, marry and have children much later than in 1950. And life is not really a ladder. Erikson himself said the old conflicts never disappear completely. They can come back at any age.

Third, it changed how we see adults. Before Erikson, most psychologists thought development stopped after childhood. Now almost everyone agrees that we keep growing and changing all our lives. That idea is his.
