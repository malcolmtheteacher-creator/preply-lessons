The Right to Say Nothing

Four words spoken in every police film in the world began as a 5–4 decision about a confession signed by a man who had never been told he could refuse.

Today: you'll work through the case that put a script into the mouth of every police officer in the United States — and you'll sharpen the modal system that separates what a person may do, what an authority must do, and what nobody need do at all.

The vocabulary of this case

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. He was not free to leave, he was in a closed room with two officers, and the questioning went on for two hours. The Court held that a person in that position is under pressure of a particular kind, and gave the situation a name: custodial interrogation.
custodial interrogation (n) — questioning by law-enforcement officers after a person has been taken into custody or otherwise deprived of freedom of action in any significant way. The two halves matter separately: in custody + interrogation. Neither alone triggers the rule, and litigation about where the line falls has run for sixty years.
2. The constitutional provision at the centre of the case says that no person shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself. In short, the privilege against self-incrimination.
self-incrimination (n) — providing evidence against oneself. Verb to incriminate; adjective incriminating (incriminating evidence). Note that it is described as a privilege rather than a right in older texts — the older word, same protection.
3. Nobody had beaten him. But the Court looked at the manuals officers were trained from — isolate the suspect, project certainty of guilt, offer sympathy, wear the person down — and concluded that an interrogation room is inherently coercive even when no hand is raised.
coercive (adj) — operating by pressure that overbears a person's free choice. Noun coercion; verb to coerce someone into doing something. The Court's move was to hold that the pressure can be structural rather than physical — a claim the dissenters flatly rejected.

B · Which term means…?

Three terms: to waive · admissible · to uphold. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which verb means to give up a right voluntarily, knowing that you hold it?
to waive (v) — noun a waiver. The test the Court set is exacting: a waiver must be knowing, intelligent and voluntary. Watch the spelling and the false friend: to waive a right, to wave a hand. In commercial drafting: "no failure to enforce shall operate as a waiver".
2. Which adjective means capable of being received and considered by a court as evidence?
admissible (adj) — noun admissibility; opposite inadmissible. Distinguish admissibility (may the court hear it?) from weight (how much does it prove?). A confession may be admissible and still be worth very little — or be devastating and inadmissible, which is exactly what happened here.
3. Which verb means for a higher court to confirm a decision or conviction and leave it standing?
to uphold (v) — the conviction was upheld. Its opposites are worth collecting precisely: to overturn or to quash a conviction, to reverse a decision, to remand (US) or remit (UK) a case back for rehearing, and to vacate a judgment.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. The remedy the Court chose was not to punish the officers but to make the confession unusable at trial. This is the exclusionary rule. Its logic is that…
  (a) the officer is fined for the breach   (b) evidence obtained in breach of a protection is kept out of court, removing the incentive to breach it   (c) the trial is held in private
(b) — the exclusionary rule, with its extension the fruit of the poisonous tree (evidence discovered because of the tainted evidence). It is deterrence by design, and its cost is visible: sometimes reliable evidence of guilt is excluded. That trade is the whole argument between the majority and the dissent.
2. Later courts held that staying quiet is not enough — a suspect who wishes to rely on the protection must say so clearly. The verb for asserting a right so that it takes effect is to invoke it. To invoke a right means…
  (a) to claim it expressly, so that the other side must act on it   (b) to lose it by failing to use it   (c) to transfer it to a lawyer
(a) — to invoke a right (also to assert, to rely on). The paradox is genuine and much criticised: to use the right to remain silent, you may have to speak. Collocations: to invoke the privilege, to invoke a clause, to invoke a procedure.

D · Collocation & word building

1. Complete the most quoted sentence in American law enforcement: "You have the right to remain ___." Then give the fixed idiom for the act an officer performs when delivering the whole warning.
silent. The idiom: to read someone their rights (also to Mirandize someone, a verb made from the case name — a rare honour, and a useful example of how a surname becomes vocabulary: compare to boycott). Related noun: the caution, which is what the equivalent warning is called in England and Wales.
2. The constitutional guarantee that the state must follow fair procedures before taking your liberty is called due ___. Complete it, and give the adjective that describes a decision made without any procedure at all.
due process — procedural due process (fair steps) and substantive due process (limits on what may be done at all). The adjective for a decision made without proper procedure: arbitrary (noun arbitrariness). In UK and Commonwealth systems the nearest equivalents are natural justice and procedural fairness.

The case, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — A confession, and a paragraph nobody had read to him

In March 1963 an eighteen-year-old woman was abducted and raped in Phoenix, Arizona. Ten days later the police arrested Ernesto Miranda, a twenty-three-year-old labourer with a criminal record and, according to the later assessments, limited education. He was put in a room with two detectives and questioned for about two hours. At the end of it he wrote out and signed a confession.

The form he signed had a paragraph printed at the top stating that the confession was made voluntarily, without threats or promises of immunity, and with full knowledge of his legal rights. What is not in dispute in the case is that nobody had told him what those rights were. He was not told that he could refuse to answer. He was not told that he could have a lawyer present, or that one would be provided if he could not pay. The detectives later confirmed as much.

The confession was admitted at trial. His lawyer objected and was overruled; Miranda was convicted and sentenced to a term of twenty to thirty years, and the Supreme Court of Arizona upheld the conviction, holding that he had not specifically requested counsel. The case went to the Supreme Court of the United States, where it was heard together with three others raising the same question from New York, California and the federal courts. That grouping matters: the Court was not correcting one bad interrogation. It was deciding what happens in every interrogation room in the country.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the signed form assert, and what was actually missing?

2. Why is it significant that four cases were heard together?

1. The form stated that the confession was voluntary and made with full knowledge of his legal rights. In fact nobody had told him he could stay silent, or that he could have a lawyer, paid for by the state if necessary.

2. Because it turned a single appeal into a general question. The Court was being asked to lay down a rule for all custodial interrogations, not to decide whether these particular detectives had behaved badly.

Discuss:
  • A printed paragraph declared that he understood rights nobody had explained. Where in your own sector does a signature stand in for a conversation that never happened?

Part 2 — What happens in a room with no windows

The most striking passages of the judgment are not about Ernesto Miranda at all. Chief Justice Earl Warren spent pages on the police interrogation manuals then in circulation, and quoted their advice: question the suspect alone, in unfamiliar surroundings; display absolute confidence in his guilt; minimise the seriousness of the offence; offer a sympathetic explanation he can accept; if he asks for a lawyer, discourage it without refusing it.

The point of the exercise was to establish that modern interrogation had moved from physical violence to psychological technique — and that the technique worked precisely by making a person feel that they had no real choice. The old legal test asked whether a confession had been beaten out of someone. Warren's Court held that the pressure of custodial questioning is inherent in the situation itself, and that a protection which depends on the suspect happening to know about it protects only the educated and the well advised.

So the Court did something courts are usually reluctant to do: it wrote the script. Before any custodial questioning, a person must be told, in clear terms, that they have the right to remain silent; that anything they say can be used against them in court; that they have the right to an attorney; and that if they cannot afford one, one will be appointed before questioning. Rights may be waived, but only knowingly, intelligently and voluntarily — and the burden of proving a valid waiver rests on the prosecution. If the warnings are not given, the statement is inadmissible, however true it may be.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the Court quote the interrogation manuals at such length?

2. State the four warnings, and the standard for a valid waiver.

1. To show that pressure had become psychological rather than physical, and that it was built into the design of the interrogation itself — so a test asking only about violence no longer protected anyone.

2. The right to remain silent; that anything said may be used against the person in court; the right to an attorney; and that an attorney will be appointed if they cannot afford one. A waiver must be knowing, intelligent and voluntary, and the prosecution must prove it.

Discuss:
  • A protection that only works if you already know about it protects the well advised. Name one right or entitlement in your own field that has exactly this problem.

Part 3 — Five to four, and everything after

The decision, on 13 June 1966, was 5–4. The dissents were not a formality. Justice Harlan argued that the new rules were not required by the Constitution, that the Court was legislating a code of police procedure it had no business writing, and that the existing test — was this particular confession voluntary in all the circumstances? — was flexible and adequate. Justice White went further: the Court's rule would return killers and rapists to the streets, and its cost would be paid by future victims nobody would ever be able to name.

What happened to Ernesto Miranda is the detail that unsettles both sides. His conviction was overturned, and he was tried again without the confession — and convicted again, on other evidence, receiving the same sentence. He was paroled in 1972. In 1976 he was killed in a bar fight in Phoenix. A suspect was arrested, read his rights from a card of the kind the case had created, and chose to say nothing. Nobody was ever convicted of the killing.

The rule itself has been narrowed, tested, and reaffirmed. Statements taken without warnings may still be used to contradict a defendant who testifies differently; there is an exception for urgent questions about public safety; and in 2010 the Court held that silence alone does not invoke the right, so a suspect who says nothing for hours and then answers one question has not necessarily protected himself. Yet when Congress attempted to legislate the rule away, the Court struck the statute down in 2000, and did so through a Chief Justice who had spent his career criticising the decision. His reasoning was blunt and is worth keeping: whatever its origins, the warning had become embedded in national culture, and part of what people understood their own protections to be.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the dissenters' strongest objection?

2. What happened to Miranda himself after the decision that bears his name?

1. That the Court was writing a code of police procedure rather than interpreting the Constitution, that the existing voluntariness test was adequate and flexible, and that the cost of the new rule would be borne by future victims of people who could not be convicted.

2. His conviction was overturned and he was retried without the confession, convicted on other evidence, and given the same sentence. He was paroled in 1972 and killed in 1976; the man suspected of killing him was read his rights and said nothing.

Discuss:
  • A rule was later upheld partly because people had come to rely on it, not because the reasoning had improved. Is that a good reason to keep a rule? Where else does it apply?

Rights, duties & permission

The whole case is a modal problem. A person may stay silent; the state must tell them so; the person need not answer; and the prosecution shall not compel them. Get one of those wrong in a notice you write and you have either created a duty you did not intend or destroyed a protection you meant to give.

What the individual MAY do — the language of entitlement:
• may — the plainest permission: "You may consult a lawyer before answering."
• have the right to / be entitled to — stronger, and the standard register of a formal notice: "You are entitled to have a representative present."
• be at liberty to / be free to — formal, and useful because they emphasise the absence of constraint: "You are free to leave at any time."

What the individual NEED NOT do — the hardest group, and the one people get wrong:
• need not / are not obliged to / are under no obligation to / do not have to.
• The trap: must not is prohibition, not absence of duty. "You need not answer" gives a choice. "You must not answer" forbids. One word apart, opposite instructions.
What the authority MUST do — the language of duty:
• must — the modern plain-drafting default: "Officers must inform the person of their rights before questioning begins."
• be required to / be under a duty to / be obliged to — impersonal, and the register of a policy document.
• shall — the traditional statutory form, and still the language of constitutional text: "No person shall be compelled to be a witness against himself." Note the passive: it imposes the duty on everybody without naming anyone.
• may not — prohibition on the authority: "Questioning may not continue once the right to counsel has been invoked."

The register ladder, loosest to tightest: has to → must → is required to → shall. Choose one level and stay on it; a notice that mixes has to and shall reads as though two people wrote it, because two people usually did.
The reverse skill: saying a right in language the person will actually act on. "You are under no obligation to respond to any question put to you" is impeccable and is not what you say to a frightened nineteen-year-old. "You don't have to answer. Nothing bad happens if you don't." The professional habit is to give the plain version first and the formal version for the record — and to check, out loud, that the plain version landed.

1. Right, duty, or neither?

An employer is opening a misconduct investigation interview. Complete each gap with one modal structure — one to four words.

1. The employee is allowed to bring someone with them. → "You to be accompanied by a colleague or a union representative."

2. The employee is not required to answer any particular question. → "You answer every question put to you today."

3. The investigator has an obligation to disclose the allegations first. → "The investigator set out the allegations before any questions are asked."

4. The employee is forbidden from discussing the case with witnesses. → "You approach any other employee named in the complaint."

5. The employee can stop the meeting whenever they want. → "You are to end the meeting at any point."

1. have the right / are entitled   2. need not / do not have to / are not obliged to   3. must / is required to   4. must not / may not   5. free / at liberty

Look at 2 and 4 together. Both are negative; only one is a restriction. An investigator who says "you must not answer" when they meant "you need not" has just instructed a witness to stay silent.

2. Raise the register

A data protection notice has been drafted in conversational English. Rewrite it for the formal version. Write only the missing words.

1. "You can ask us for a copy of your data." → "The data subject request a copy of the personal data held."

2. "We have to answer within a month." → "The controller respond within one month of receipt."

3. "You don't have to give us a reason." → "The data subject to provide reasons for the request."

4. "We can't charge you for it." → "No fee in respect of a first request." (passive obligation)

1. is entitled to / may   2. must / shall / is required to   3. is under no obligation / is not required   4. shall be charged / may be charged

Sentence 4 shows why the passive earns its place in drafting: the duty binds whoever might charge, without the drafter having to list them.

3. Brief the interviewer — complete the instruction

You are training investigators before a series of regulatory interviews. Complete what you tell them. Read it aloud when you finish.

"Three things, and they are not negotiable. First, before a single question, you tell the person what the interview is about and what will happen to what they say. Second, you tell them clearly that they answer any question they don't wish to answer, and that they to have someone with them — and you say both of those in ordinary words, not in the wording on the form. Third, if they ask for a representative, questioning continue until that person is present. If you are unsure whether someone has understood, you assume they have; ask them to tell you in their own words. An interview that we later rely on has cost us more than no interview at all."

must · need not (or do not have to) · are entitled / have the right · may not (or must not) · must not · cannot

The last sentence is the argument that actually persuades investigators: the warning is not a courtesy to the suspect, it is what makes the interview usable. Every organisation that questions people learns this the expensive way.

Going deeper

A note before we argue: the case is summarised here, and legal systems differ sharply on this exact question — this is material for discussion and for language, not legal advice on any real matter.

What the case actually tests. The familiar reading is that the Court protected the vulnerable: a right that depends on already knowing you have it is a right for lawyers' children. A bright-line rule fixes that, and does it in a form a tired officer at three in the morning can follow. Against it stands the dissent's objection, which has never been answered so much as outvoted: this is a code of police procedure, written by nine people who do not conduct interrogations, in a case that gave them no evidence about what the rule would cost.

The sharper modern critique comes from neither side. It is that the warning has become the thing that makes interrogation respectable. Most suspects — by the usual estimates, a large majority — waive their rights within minutes and talk anyway. The card is read, the box is ticked, and the confession that follows arrives in court wearing a certificate of validity that a pre-1966 confession never had. On that reading the protection has been converted into a ritual, and the ritual serves the state. Whether that is a failure of the rule or simply the price of any rule that can be reduced to a card is the real question, and it is not comfortable from either direction.

It is worth knowing that a comparable democracy went the other way. In England and Wales, the caution given on arrest tells the person that they do not have to say anything — and then warns them that it may harm their defence if they fail to mention something they later rely on in court. Since 1994 a court may draw an adverse inference from silence. Two systems with a shared legal ancestry looked at the same problem and reached opposite conclusions about whether silence should be free. And then there is the paradox the American courts created for themselves in 2010: staying silent does not invoke the right to silence. To be protected, you must speak.
Talk it through:
  • Argue the dissent properly: a court should not write police procedure, and the cost of the rule falls on victims nobody can name. Then argue the majority. Which was harder to say convincingly?
  • If most people waive the right within minutes, has the warning protected them or legitimised what follows? What would a genuinely effective protection look like?
  • England and Wales allow a court to hold silence against you; the United States does not. Which system would you rather be questioned in — and does your answer change depending on who you are?
  • Bright-line rule or flexible standard: for the interviews your own organisation conducts, which produces better decisions — a script everyone must follow, or a judgement call made in context?
