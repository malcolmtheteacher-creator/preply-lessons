The POW Approach

POW is a framework that helps you think strategically about your presentation before you start creating slides. It stands for Purpose, Outcome, and Benefit.

Understanding POW

Each element answers a critical question about your presentation:

Purpose (P)
Why am I giving this presentation?
To inspire • To persuade • To excite • To reassure • To motivate • To encourage
Outcome (O)
What do I want my audience to do differently as a result?
Adopt a new process • Accept a proposal • Change a behaviour • Take action
Benefit (W)
What new information, learning, or benefit will my audience gain?
Save time • Improve efficiency • Reduce costs • Enhance skills • Gain insight

Example 1: Sales Process Presentation

Let's see how the POW framework works in practice:

Purpose: To motivate the team to use the new sales process

Outcome: Team members will use the new process correctly in their daily work

Benefit: The team will waste less time finding key information and be more productive

Objective Statement:

"To motivate the team to use the new sales process correctly so that they lose less time finding key information and are more productive."

Example 2: Management Initiative Presentation

Purpose: To persuade management to support your new initiative

Outcome: Management will accept and approve the new initiative

Benefit: It will save money and enable more efficient working processes

Objective Statement:

"To persuade management to support the new initiative because it will cut back on costs and improve working processes."

Crafting Your Core Message

Your core message is the one thing you absolutely want your audience to remember. It's short, specific, and directly connected to your audience's needs.

The Power of "Because"

Research shows that including the word "because" followed by a clear reason dramatically increases audience engagement and compliance. Here's why:

Harvard Study Finding: When people hear the word "because" followed by a reason, they're more likely to respond positively — even when the reason seems obvious. The word "because" itself triggers a psychological response.

Examples of "Because" in Action

Weaker: "Please approve this initiative."

Stronger: "Please approve this initiative because it will save us £5 million annually and improve our competitive position."

Weaker: "We need to adopt new software."

Stronger: "We need to adopt new software because it will reduce data entry errors by 40% and free up three hours per week per employee."

Characteristics of a Strong Core Message

Check each one as you write:
  • ✓ Short and simple (one sentence, easy to repeat and remember)
  • ✓ Aligned to your POW framework
  • ✓ Specific to your audience's needs and interests
  • ✓ Uses simple, conversational language (include "we", "you", "us", or "our")
  • ✓ Avoids clichés and statements everyone already knows
  • ✓ Contains "because" followed by a specific benefit

Audience-Specific Core Messages

The same core message may need adjustments for different audiences. What resonates with one group might not work for another. Compare these versions targeting different decision-makers:

For Senior Management: "This initiative will save us £5 million annually because we'll reduce operational costs and improve our competitive advantage."

For Your Team: "This initiative will make your work easier and save you time because you'll spend less time on administrative tasks and more time on creative work."

For Customers: "This initiative means faster service for you because we're streamlining our internal processes to respond to your needs more quickly."

Understanding Your Audience

Before you finalize your presentation, deeply analyse who will be listening. Different audiences have different priorities, concerns, and motivations.

Key Questions to Ask About Your Audience

1. Who are they? (job role, seniority, department, expertise level)

Example: Are they senior managers concerned about ROI, team members worried about job security, customers interested in benefits, or external stakeholders focused on compliance?

2. What do they already know? (their baseline knowledge and experience)

Example: Are they technical experts who need advanced details, beginners who need fundamentals explained, or specialists in a different field who need context?

3. What do they care about? (their priorities and values)

Example: Cost reduction, time savings, quality improvement, career development, environmental impact, customer satisfaction, or risk management?

4. What do they need from you? (what will this presentation solve for them)

Example: Do they need information to make a decision, training to implement change, reassurance about a concern, or evidence to convince others?

5. What will get their attention? (what hooks them emotionally or intellectually)

Example: A surprising statistic, a personal story, a concrete business case, a problem they're experiencing right now, or evidence of risk?

Tailoring Content for Different Audiences

The same topic requires different emphasis and evidence depending on your audience:

Topic: New software implementation

For IT Department: Focus on technical specifications, security protocols, integration requirements, and system architecture.

For Management: Focus on cost savings, time-to-ROI, competitive advantage, and risk mitigation.

For End Users: Focus on how it makes their job easier, training support, minimal disruption, and improved workflow.
