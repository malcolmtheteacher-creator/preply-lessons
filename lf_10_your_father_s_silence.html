Your Father's Silence

He came over at twenty-two with one case, and in fifty-six years he has told you nothing about it. Sunday afternoon, his kitchen, a pot of stewed tea. You're going to ask.

The Scene 🎬

Your dad is seventy-eight. He came over in 1971, aged twenty-two, with one case and the name of a street written on the back of an envelope. That is roughly the sum of what you know, and most of that you got from your mum.

It isn't that he refuses. It's gentler and more annoying than that. You ask, and he says "ah, it was a long time ago", and then he asks whether you want a biscuit, and somehow you're talking about the neighbour's fence. He's done it your whole life. You've stopped noticing that he does it.

Today it's just the two of you. Mum's at her sister's until six. The football's on low in the next room, neither of you watching it. There's a pot of tea that's been sat there long enough to go bitter. And you've decided that this is the afternoon you actually ask — not because anything's wrong, but because he's seventy-eight, and one day the answer won't be available.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of one person in your family who doesn't talk about their own past. What do you actually know about them, and where did you get it from — them, or somebody else?
  2. Some people go quiet about a thing because it hurts; some because they honestly think nobody would be interested. How would you tell the difference from the outside?

Who's who

You
The One Asking 🫖

Not doing a project, not writing anything down. You'd just like to know something about your own father before it's too late to ask — and you'd like to get through it without either of you making a fuss.

The other voice
Dad, 78 ☕

Kind, dry, slightly deaf when it suits him. He isn't hiding anything dramatic. He's just spent fifty years assuming that what happened to him is of no interest to anybody, and he's had a lot of practice at changing the subject.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Watch how little pressure there is in it. The one asking never demands, never argues with him about why he won't say — they just keep making the question smaller and easier to answer until, almost by accident, he answers one.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. It's slow, and it's meant to be. Leave the pauses in; they're doing half the work.

DadThere's tea in the pot if you want it. It'll be stewed, mind. It's been sat there since — well. A while.
YouIn a minute. Dad, can I ask you something you'll probably not answer? … You never talk about that time at all, do you. When you came over. Fifty-odd years and I couldn't tell you one single thing about it, not one, which is a bit—
DadAh, it was a long time ago, love. There's nothing to tell. Do you want a biscuit or not?
YouI'm not after anything deep. I'd just like to know, that's all. Like — I don't know — where you slept. What you ate. Boring things.
DadBoring things. Mm. Well. There was a room. Leeds. And the landlady was — no, hang on, she wasn't the landlady, she was the one downstairs. Doyle. Mrs Doyle.
YouGo on, humour me. What was it like, the room?
DadCold. You put a shilling in the meter and you got — what — an hour of it, or something. Less. And I used to sit in my coat doing the forms, the paperwork, all that kind of thing. There was an awful lot of paperwork. That's it, really. That's the whole of it.
YouWere you frightened? I would've been.
DadFrightened. That's a big word, that is. I was twenty-two, I didn't have the sense to be frightened. … A bit, maybe. On the boat, a bit. I'm not — look, I don't honestly know how you'd say it.
YouYou don't have to tell me the whole thing. … What was she like, before we came along? Mum.
DadYour mother? God. She was — no, that's not it. She wasn't like she is now, put it that way. She used to… sorry. What was I saying?
YouShe used to do something. Take your time. I'm not going anywhere.
DadShe used to laugh at the way I said things. Not nasty, mind. She'd have me say a word about nine times and then she'd go and say it wrong herself, on purpose, so it wasn't only me getting it wrong. If that makes any sense at all. Nobody had done that before. That was — yeah.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Dad's first real answer isn't about how he felt — it's a small, dull detail about a room. What had the one asking requested, just before, that made that possible?

2. Twice Dad starts an answer, stumbles and loses the thread completely. What does the one asking do at those moments — and what do they carefully not do?

3. Dad never tells the story, and he's never asked to. So why does the one asking treat the afternoon as a win?

1. They asked for boring things — where he slept, what he ate — rather than for the big feelings, which meant he could answer without deciding to open up about anything. Small questions are answerable; large ones only get "it was a long time ago".
2. They wait, and when he asks where he was, they hand him back the thread he dropped and tell him there's no rush. What they don't do is fill the gap themselves, guess what he meant, or move on to the next question while he's still thinking.
3. Because he said one true thing he had never said before, and the door was left open rather than shut. They didn't try to get fifty years out of him in one afternoon, which is precisely why there'll be a next Sunday.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for getting something out of someone who has spent a very long time saying nothing. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. The whole thing falls apart the moment it starts to sound like an interview, so keep your voice low and unhurried, and let the questions come out as though you'd only just thought of them. Don't lean forward. Don't rush into the gaps — a silence you leave alone will often be filled by the other person, whereas a silence you fill is one they never have to.

🚪 Asking in the first place

Phrase 1

You're about to ask about something he's dodged for fifty years, and you'd rather admit up front that he might dodge it again than pretend this is a normal question. Which opener does that?

A. "Dad, I need you to be honest with me."
B. "Can I ask you something you'll probably not answer?"
C. "Why won't you ever talk about it?"
B — "Can I ask you something you'll probably not answer?"
A sounds like an accusation waiting to happen, and C makes him defend his silence before he's said anything at all. B does something cleverer than either: by predicting the refusal out loud, it takes the pressure off, because he no longer has to fight you to keep quiet — and, oddly, that's often what makes people answer.
Phrase 2

Now name the silence itself — plainly, as a fact you've both lived with, rather than as a complaint. Put the pieces in order:

about that timedo you.You never talkat all,
"You never talk about that time at all, do you."
The "do you" on the end is a tag question, and notice it's written with a full stop rather than a question mark, because it isn't really asking — it's inviting him to agree with something you both already know. Said flat and gently like that, it observes the silence without accusing him of it.

🪶 Making the question small enough to answer

Phrase 3

He's just told you there's nothing to tell and offered you a biscuit. He thinks you want a confession. Which reply lowers what you're asking for?

A. "I just think you should talk about it more, that's the problem."
B. "I'm not after anything deep. I'd just like to know, that's all."
C. "Why do you always do this to me?"
B — "I'm not after anything deep. I'd just like to know, that's all."
A tells him he's doing it wrong and C turns the afternoon into a row about the two of you. B works because "to be after something" means to want it, so you're saying plainly that you don't want the big emotional version — and "that's all" shrinks the whole request down to something he can hand over without it costing him anything.
Phrase 4

He says there's nothing worth telling. You'd like him to tell it anyway — as a favour to you, not because you've won the argument. Complete it, half-joking: "Go on, ___ me." (starts with h…)

"Go on, humour me."
To humour someone is to go along with them because you're fond of them, not because you agree that they've got a point. So the phrase quietly concedes that he may well be right that it's all very boring, and asks him to do it for you regardless — which is very hard to refuse, because refusing would mean refusing you rather than refusing the subject.
Phrase 5

He's started, then pulled back — you can see him deciding how much of this he's willing to get into. Let him off most of it, so he'll give you some of it. Put the pieces in order:

the whole thing.have toYou don'ttell me
"You don't have to tell me the whole thing."
People go quiet partly because they think the choice is between saying everything and saying nothing, and everything is far too much. This line offers a third option, so a small piece stops feeling like the first instalment of a confession and starts feeling like the end of it — which is exactly why they'll usually give you rather more than the small piece.

🔍 The questions that actually open someone

Phrase 6

You want to ask about your mother — not as your mother, but as the woman she was before either of you existed. Complete it: "What was she like, before we ___ ___?" (c… a…)

"What was she like, before we came along?"
To "come along" is a light, almost casual way of saying to be born or to turn up, and putting it that way matters: "before we came along" quietly acknowledges that your parents had a whole life which had nothing to do with you, which for a lot of people is a genuinely new thought and a rather flattering one to be asked about.
Phrase 7

You want to ask about fear — the thing men his age tend not to admit to. Which version gives him the easiest possible route to saying yes?

A. "Weren't you scared?"
B. "That must have been absolutely terrifying."
C. "Were you frightened? I would've been."
C — "Were you frightened? I would've been."
A can be batted away with a single "no", and B hands him the adjective so that all he can do is agree with you or correct you. C asks the plain question and then answers it about yourself first, which means that when he admits to fear he isn't confessing to a weakness — he's simply agreeing with someone who has already gone first.

⏳ Giving them room

Phrase 8

He's begun a sentence about your mother and lost it entirely. The temptation is to help, to guess, to move on. Don't. Complete it: "Take your time. I'm not going ___." (a…)

"Take your time. I'm not going anywhere."
It's literally true — you're sat in his kitchen — and that's what makes it work, because the ordinary fact does the reassuring rather than any big statement about listening. It tells him the pause costs you nothing, so he can take as long as he needs, which is usually the only thing standing between him and the rest of the sentence.

🫖 Marking it, and keeping the door open

Phrase 9

He's given you one real thing. You want him to notice that he's done it — without making it into a moment he'll be embarrassed by. Put the pieces in order:

you've ever saidyou know.That's more thanabout it,
"That's more than you've ever said about it, you know."
It marks what just happened as unusual, which is a compliment, but the "you know" on the end keeps it offhand rather than solemn, so he isn't put on the spot. Compare "thank you so much for sharing that with me", which would send a man like this straight back into the kitchen to see about the biscuits.
Phrase 10

Last move. You want another go at this, but the moment you make it a big arrangement, he'll dread it. In your own words, ask him to do this again — and keep it light enough that he can say yes without agreeing to anything heavy. What would you say?

e.g. "Can we do this again? Same chair, same terrible tea."
The question is small and the joke about the tea is doing the real work, because it makes the thing you're asking for sound like a cup of tea rather than a conversation about his life. That's the trick of it: name the furniture, not the subject, and he gets to say yes to a Sunday afternoon instead of yes to talking about 1971.

🔑 Make them yours

These work on anyone who's gone quiet — a parent, a grandparent, a friend who's stopped saying how they are. Pin three of them to somebody real, so you'll have them ready when the afternoon turns up. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"Can I ask you something you'll probably not answer?" — who is it, and what's the question you've never asked them?

"I'm not after anything deep. I'd just like to know, that's all." — what small, dull, answerable thing could you ask them instead of the big one?

"Take your time. I'm not going anywhere." — when do you fill other people's silences? Where could you stop?

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your kitchen. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer — but notice that this is the one role play where going slowly is the skill, not a problem.

The Twist

😬 He turns it round

One more round — no script. You've got your gentle little questions lined up and you're rather pleased with them. Then, halfway through, he puts his cup down and asks you: "And what's brought all this on? Are you alright? Is it something with you?" He thinks you're only asking because something's wrong. Start the timer and get through it. The rule: you don't get to say "nothing, I was just curious" — he won't believe it, and if you fob him off he'll close the door for good and be polite about it forever. So you have to give him something true about yourself first, out loud, before he'll give you anything more. Decide what that is while the timer runs, and say it.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I asked for something small and boring rather than something deep.
  • I left his pauses alone — I didn't fill them, guess, or hurry him on.
  • I never argued with him about his silence or made him defend it.
  • When he gave me something, I marked it lightly and didn't make a fuss of it.
  • I left the door open for next time instead of trying to get it all today.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

We tend to assume that the quiet ones are protecting something, and sometimes they are. More often they've simply never been asked in a way that made answering easy, and after enough years the not-talking stops being a decision and becomes a habit, like a door you go past without noticing it's shut.

There's also the small matter of thinking it isn't interesting. A man who spent a winter in a cold room in Leeds knows perfectly well that thousands of other men did the same, and that nobody made a fuss of them either, so why would his version be worth anybody's Sunday?

Which is why the boring questions get the answers the big ones don't. Ask what it felt like and you'll get "it was a long time ago". Ask what the room was like and you might get the meter, the coat, the window that didn't shut — and somewhere behind those, without either of you announcing it, the rest.

Two bigger questions

  1. Whose story is it? He lived that year and he's entitled to keep it. But you came out of it, and one day you'll be the only one left who could pass it on. Does that give you any claim on it at all — and if he'd rather it went with him, is that a loss or simply his right?
  2. Every family has things that go unsaid for a generation and then get asked about too late. Is that carelessness, or is there something in the way families work that makes it almost inevitable? What is currently going unasked in yours?
