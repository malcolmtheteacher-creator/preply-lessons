IB LESSON 3 · ANALYSIS & EVALUATION

Effect on the Reader

The half of your sentence that earns the marks

Why naming the technique is only half the job

In your last essay you spotted techniques — metaphors, repetition, word choice. That gets you to Band 3 or 4. The upper bands need something more: what the technique does to the reader.

The IB formula: Authorial choice + effect on the reader = analysis.
Miss the effect, and you are only describing the text. Include the effect, and you are evaluating it.

Spot the difference

Band 3 — technique only

"Orwell uses the windmill as a symbol of the animals' work."

It names the technique. It stops there. The reader of your essay learns nothing about why that symbol matters.

Band 6+ — technique + effect

"Orwell's extended symbol of the windmill positions the reader to see revolutionary labour as an endlessly receding horizon — something the animals build, rebuild, and never inhabit."

Names the choice and tells us what it does to the reader. The phrase "positions the reader" is doing the work.

Band 3 — technique only

"Orwell repeats the word 'four' many times."

Accurate but inert. No reader response, no interpretation.

Band 6+ — technique + effect

"Orwell's insistent repetition of 'four legs good, two legs bad' reinforces the slogan's status as thought-terminating ritual, inviting the reader to feel how rhythm can replace argument."

Two effect verbs ("reinforces", "inviting"). The reader is now inside the experience Orwell is engineering.

The test to apply to every analysis sentence

Ask: Does my sentence tell the reader what happens to them as they read?
If no → add an effect verb.

The Effect-Verb Toolkit

Stop writing "this shows that..." Start writing with verbs that name what the text does to the reader. These are the phrases IB examiners recognise as Criterion A evaluation.

🎯 Positioning the reader

Verbs that show where the text places us — physically, emotionally, or ethically.

positionspositions the reader as a witness
alignsaligns us with the narrator's view
distancesdistances the reader from the violence
immersesimmerses us in the boy's panic
implicatesimplicates the reader in the silence

💭 Shaping perception

Verbs that describe how the text changes the way we see something.

foregroundsforegrounds the animal's suffering
highlightshighlights the contradiction in the slogan
reframesreframes the myth as an inheritance
revealsreveals the narrator's complicity
exposesexposes the pretence of order

😰 Creating emotion / atmosphere

Verbs that name the mood or feeling the text generates.

unsettlesunsettles the reader with its calm tone
disorientsdisorients us by withholding names
evokesevokes a sense of dread
heightensheightens the tension
temperstempers the horror with humour

🏗️ Structuring the experience

Verbs that describe how the text builds, withholds, or delivers meaning over time.

buildsbuilds pressure across the paragraph
withholdswithholds resolution
delaysdelays the reveal until the final line
reinforcesreinforces the slogan through rhythm
subvertssubverts our expectation of rescue

🎭 Signalling the author's stance

Verbs that show how the writer is leaning — ironic, sympathetic, critical.

critiquescritiques the leader's hypocrisy
mocksmocks the self-importance of the elite
invitesinvites the reader to question the myth
challengeschallenges the comfortable reading
underminesundermines the surface meaning
Pro move: Pair two of these verbs in one sentence for Band 7 texture — one naming the reader's experience, one naming the author's stance. e.g. "The understated diction distances the reader, even as it critiques the polite language used to describe atrocity."

Match the Technique to its Likely Effect

Read the extract, pick the effect you think the technique is producing, then reveal the answer. If you pick the "wrong" one, read the explanation — more than one answer can be defensible. The point is to see how technique and effect link up.

1. Short, declarative sentences
"He fell. The snow did not move. No one came."

Which effect fits best?

✓ reinforces isolation & finality

Why: Each sentence is a closed door. No conjunctions, no dependent clauses, no hope. The structure enacts the abandonment the content describes.

Model sentence: "The three terse, end-stopped sentences reinforce the finality of the boy's collapse and distance the reader from any rescuing gesture."

2. Extended metaphor
"The city was a map that had been torn in half and stitched back together with different thread — you could trace the seam in every silence between strangers."

Which effect fits best?

✓ reframes the city as a site of unhealed division

Why: "Map" is a neutral, everyday object — but the writer loads it with a violent history (torn, stitched, visible seam). The metaphor reframes the abstract idea (social division) as something the reader could literally see, if they looked closely enough at the silences around them.

Model sentence: "The extended map-metaphor reframes the city as a stitched-together wound, implicating the reader in the polite silences that keep the seam hidden."

3. Repetition of a single word
"Four legs good, two legs bad. Four legs good, two legs bad. Four legs good, two legs bad."

Which effect fits best?

✓ mimics indoctrination & replaces thought

Why: The chant is not an argument, it is a rhythm. Orwell's repetition lets us feel how slogans close off thinking — the reader experiences the same hypnotic pull the animals do.

Model sentence: "Orwell's triple repetition of the slogan reinforces its hypnotic quality and invites the reader to recognise how rhythm can substitute for reasoning."

4. Understated diction (calm word choice for violent content)
"Napoleon called softly for the dogs. A few minutes later, the four pigs were waiting, their ears flat."

Which effect fits best?

✓ unsettles by making violence feel routine

Why: "Softly" and "a few minutes later" are the rhythms of ordinary life. By grafting them onto an execution, Orwell makes the horror feel procedural — which is more disturbing than overt description.

Model sentence: "Orwell's deliberately understated diction unsettles the reader by rendering the coming violence as administrative routine, critiquing how regimes domesticate brutality."

5. Delayed reveal / paragraph shape
"They had been together since childhood. They had fought beside each other. They had built the farm with their own hooves. Now he watched her dragged from the yard."

Which effect fits best?

✓ builds intimacy, then withholds rescue

Why: The paragraph first lets us invest in the bond ("since childhood", "fought beside"). Then the final sentence snaps away without intervention. The structure produces the heartbreak — content alone would not.

Model sentence: "The anaphoric build of 'They had...' builds the reader's attachment, which Orwell then subverts with the abrupt shift to 'Now', implicating us in the bystander's silence."

Close-Read: Three Orwell Passages

For each passage below: identify one authorial choice, choose an effect verb from the Toolkit, and write one Band 6+ analysis sentence. Reveal the model answer only after you write your own.

Passage 1 — Boxer's end

"'Fools! Fools!' shouted Benjamin, prancing round them and stamping the earth with his small hoofs. 'Fools! Do you not see what is written on the side of that van?'"

Annotate: What is Orwell doing with repetition, the choice of "prancing", and the unanswered question?

Model analysis sentences

Choice: The repetition of "Fools!" combined with the unanswered rhetorical question.

Sentence A: "Orwell's urgent repetition of 'Fools!' heightens Benjamin's helplessness and implicates the reader in the animals' too-late recognition."

Sentence B: "The unanswered question withholds the comfort of explanation, positioning the reader alongside the illiterate animals who cannot read the van."

Why it works: Both use a clear effect verb. Both tell us what happens to us as readers, not just what the words mean on the page.

Passage 2 — The commandment changes

"There was nothing there now except a single Commandment. It ran: ALL ANIMALS ARE EQUAL BUT SOME ANIMALS ARE MORE EQUAL THAN OTHERS."

Annotate: The choice of capitals, the contradiction inside the sentence, the matter-of-fact framing ("It ran:").

Model analysis sentences

Choice: The internal contradiction of the slogan, held together by the conjunction "BUT".

Sentence A: "The logical self-contradiction of the amended commandment exposes the regime's cynicism and invites the reader to feel how power bends language until it means nothing."

Sentence B: "Orwell's flat reporting verb 'It ran' distances the narrator from the slogan, positioning the reader as the one who must register the horror the animals cannot."

Passage 3 — The final scene

"The creatures outside looked from pig to man, and from man to pig, and from pig to man again; but already it was impossible to say which was which."

Annotate: The chiasmus / mirrored phrase structure, the verb "looked" (passive observation), the final indistinguishability.

Model analysis sentences

Choice: The mirrored "pig to man, and from man to pig" mimicking the reader's failed attempt to distinguish.

Sentence A: "Orwell's chiastic phrasing mimics the reader's own scanning gaze and reinforces the novel's final claim: that the distinction between oppressor and oppressed has collapsed."

Sentence B: "The closing clause 'which was which' withholds a name from either figure, implicating the reader in the moment of moral equivalence the revolution has produced."

Checkpoint: In each of your sentences, underline the effect verb. If you cannot find one, the sentence is still technique-only — rewrite it using the Toolkit.

Effect-on-the-Reader Cheat Card

Keep this open while you draft. Every analysis sentence should pass the two-part test below.

The two-part test

1. Is an authorial choice named? (technique, word, structure)
2. Is an effect verb present? (positions / unsettles / reframes...)
   If both = YES → Band 6+. If only 1 = Band 3–4.

Fast-grab verb list

When you want to say…Use one of these
The text places the reader somewherepositions, aligns, distances, immerses, implicates
The text changes how we see somethingforegrounds, reframes, reveals, exposes, highlights
The text creates a feelingunsettles, disorients, evokes, heightens, tempers
The text builds or withholds over timebuilds, delays, withholds, reinforces, subverts
The author takes a stancecritiques, mocks, invites, challenges, undermines

Phrases to retire (they say nothing)

  • "This shows that…" → replace with an effect verb.
  • "The reader can see…" → replace with positions / reveals / foregrounds.
  • "It makes the reader feel…" → tighten to unsettles / evokes / heightens.
  • "This is important because…" → cut. The effect verb already tells us why.

A Band-7 sentence skeleton

[Authorial choice] + [effect verb] + [what the reader now sees / feels],
   [second effect verb] + [authorial stance / bigger point].

Example: "Orwell's extended windmill-symbol reframes revolutionary labour as an endlessly receding horizon, implicating the reader in a hope we keep building even as it is used against us."
