The Canonic

Epicurus's theory of knowledge: How we know what we know

Before We Begin...

🧠 What Do You Already Know?

How do you know something is true? What sources of knowledge do you trust? Write freely — no "correct" answers needed.

Today: We're exploring Epicurus's canonic (kanon) — his theory of knowledge. Unlike Plato, Epicurus didn't believe in abstract forms. He believed knowledge comes from three sources: sensations, preconceptions, and feelings. Understanding this epistemology is crucial for understanding how Epicurean ethics works.

Why Epistemology Matters

Before we can know what the good life is, we need to ask: How do we know anything at all? Epicurus's answer shapes everything else in his philosophy.

Unlike Plato (who believed true knowledge comes from reason alone), Epicurus grounded knowledge in physical reality. Unlike the Skeptics (who doubted everything), Epicurus believed we can know truth — through sensation, natural concepts, and bodily feelings.

This makes Epicureanism a realistic, materialist philosophy. And it makes his ethical system practical, not abstract.

The Three Criteria of Truth

📐
Kanon = Measuring Rod: The word "canonic" comes from kanon (measuring rod). Epicurus offers three measuring rods for determining truth — three standards by which we can test reality.
1. Sensations (Aisthēseis)
Direct sense perception — what you see, hear, touch, taste, smell. These are always true. The senses don't lie.
Example: You see red. That sensation of red is true. You don't see color the way I do, but your sensation is accurate for you.
2. Preconceptions (Prolēpseis)
Natural concepts formed from repeated sensation. Ideas that naturally arise from sense experience, without deliberate reasoning. These are innate, intuitive understandings.
Example: You've seen many humans. From repeated sensation, you form the concept "human" — this preconception lets you recognize a new person immediately, without reasoning.
3. Feelings (Pathē)
Bodily sensations of pleasure and pain. Physical experiences that guide us toward what is good (pleasure/health) and away from what is bad (pain/disease). These are also infallible guides.
Example: You feel pain when you touch fire. That feeling is true — it's your body's infallible signal that fire causes harm. Pleasure when you eat nourishing food signals health.
Your turn: Think of how you might use all three criteria to determine if water is essential for human health. How does sensation prove it? How does preconception? How do bodily feelings?

Using the three criteria:

  • Sensation: You see water, taste it, feel its wetness. You observe humans drinking it.
  • Preconception: From observing many people, you form the concept that water is necessary. You didn't reason this out — it's intuitive.
  • Feelings: When dehydrated, you feel thirst (pain signal). When you drink, you feel relief (pleasure signal). Your body infallibly guides you to water.

No abstract theory needed. Your senses, natural concepts, and bodily wisdom all agree: water is essential.

All sensation is true. The criterion is either sensation or feeling or a mental projection derived from each of these.

— Epicurus, reported by Diogenes Laertius

If Sensation is Always True, How Do We Make Errors?

The Problem

Epicurus says sensations are always true. But clearly, we make mistakes. We see a ship in the distance as a toy. We misidentify a friend in the crowd. How can sensation be infallible if we constantly misperceive?

Epicurus's answer: The sensation is true. The judgment about the sensation is false.

Key distinction: Sensation vs. Judgment

What you sense is the visual impression of a distant object. This impression is real and true — it's exactly what appears to your senses at that moment. But what you judge about that impression ("It's a toy") is an added belief, which can be false.

Example: The ship in the distance

  • The sensation: You see a small, toy-like shape on the horizon. This visual impression is absolutely true — that's what appears to you.
  • The judgment: You conclude "It's a toy." This judgment is false — it's actually a ship seen from a distance.
  • The confusion: We add expectations. Distance usually means smallness. So we judge what we see as a small thing. But the sensation itself — the visual appearance — was completely accurate.

How to correct error: Epicurus says we must examine our judgments more carefully. Move closer. Add other senses (hear the ship, feel the spray). Let sensation guide your judgment.

🔍
Epicurus's Method: When beliefs conflict with sensation, change the belief, not the sensation. Reason should serve sensation, testing whether our interpretations match reality.

Three Ways Error Occurs

1. Adding to sensation: We interpret sensations through expectations and past experiences. "It looks like a toy because I expect distance to create smallness."

2. Ignoring contrary sensation: We believe something despite contradicting evidence. We want the ship to be a toy, so we ignore the growing sound of its engine.

3. Hasty judgment: We leap to conclusions without sufficient sensory evidence. We identify a friend from behind based on hair color alone, ignoring that we haven't sensed their face.

Solution in all cases: Return to careful sensation. Reserve judgment. Let additional sensory evidence clarify.

When you assume something, examine also whether it follows necessarily, or only by opinion; and if some of it is false, it will lead astray.

— Epicurus, fragment

Practical Epistemology: How We Know What We Know

Applied to Natural Philosophy

Epicurus's epistemology has immediate practical consequences. Let's see how he uses it to understand the natural world — and why this matters for ethics.

Example 1: Gods and Atoms

Epicurus believes in atoms and void. Why? Let's trace his reasoning through the three criteria:

Sensation: We observe things breaking down (wood burns, bodies decay). Sensation shows us change and dissolution.

Preconception: Reason tells us: change must have an ultimate limit. Something must be unchangeable (otherwise infinite regress). These are atoms — particles that cannot be divided.

Feeling: Fear of divine punishment often comes from ignorance. Understanding atoms and void removes this fear — the gods aren't responsible for natural processes, only atoms are. This brings tranquility (pleasure).

Example 2: Divine Beings

Epicurus believed in gods, but gods very different from what traditional religion taught. How did his epistemology shape this belief?

Sensation: We don't observe gods causing lightning or miracles. What we observe is natural cause and effect.

Preconception: The natural human concept of "god" is a blessed, immortal being — happy, unconcerned with human affairs. Not an interventionist figure.

Feeling: Fear of punishment harms our tranquility. Understanding that gods are indifferent to humans brings relief. The gods exist (a natural belief), but they don't judge or punish us.

💡
Key Insight: Epicurus's epistemology directly shapes his ethics. We pursue knowledge (true sensation) to remove fear and pain (false beliefs). When reason contradicts sensation, trust sensation. When natural feelings guide you toward something, pay attention — your body is wiser than you think.

No Divine Revelation Needed

Notice what's absent from Epicureanism: revelation, prophets, sacred texts claiming to be truth beyond questioning. Truth is accessible to everyone through sensation, reason, and bodily feeling. An enslaved person can know truth as well as a king. A woman as well as a man. This was revolutionary.

The Garden welcomed people from all backgrounds because Epicurus's epistemology said: everyone has sensation, everyone has preconceptions, everyone has feelings. Everyone can know truth.

The three criteria of truth:

  • Sensations (aisthēseis): Direct sense perception — always true
  • Preconceptions (prolēpseis): Natural concepts formed from repeated sensation — intuitive, uncultivated ideas
  • Feelings (pathē): Bodily sensations of pleasure and pain — infallible guides to good and bad

Sensation vs. Judgment:

  • Sensations are always true — what appears to your senses is real
  • Judgments about sensations can be false — we add beliefs that may not match sensation
  • Example: You see a distant ship as toy-like (true sensation). You judge it's a toy (false judgment).
  • To correct error: let additional sensations clarify your judgment

Epistemology & Equality:

  • Everyone has sensation, preconception, and feeling
  • No need for priests, prophets, or divine revelation to know truth
  • This makes knowledge accessible to all people regardless of status
  • Justifies welcoming women and enslaved people to the Garden as equals

Practical applications:

  • Atoms: Sensation shows us change and dissolution. Preconception suggests something unchangeable. Reason postulates atoms.
  • Gods: Sensation shows no divine intervention. Preconception suggests blessed immortal beings. Natural concept of god doesn't require punishment or involvement in human affairs.
  • Natural philosophy: Use sensation to test theories. When reason contradicts sensation, trust sensation.
