Warm-up

Out loud, before anything else — don't worry about mistakes, just talk.

SPEAK

Think of something you can honestly say has NEVER happened to you. Say the sentence out loud, starting with "I've never...".

Listen in on this conversation

Sam: You will not believe what happened at the gym this morning.

Alex: What happened?

Sam: This guy dropped a weight so hard the whole building shook. Never have I seen every single person in a room stop and stare at once.

Alex: Was he okay?

Sam: Fine — just red in the face. But seriously, rarely do people apologise that fast. He said sorry about ten times.

Look closely at Sam's two sentences in bold above. Something is unusual about the word order — it isn't how you'd normally build these sentences. Can you say what's different? Try it out loud before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
Normally: "I have never seen..." and "People rarely apologise...". Sam flips it: Never have I seen... and rarely do people apologise... — the subject and the auxiliary swap places, almost like a question.

This is called inversion, and people reach for it when something genuinely surprised them, or when they want an opinion to land harder. It's for the dramatic moments in a story or a strong reaction — not for every sentence you say.
Today: flipping the word order after words like never, not only, and no sooner — so you can tell a story or make a point with a bit more drama, the way people do when something really surprises them.

That's Never Happened Before

Flip the subject and verb after words like never, rarely, and seldom to turn an ordinary opinion into something that sounds like breaking news.

Spot the pattern

Here's the plain version next to the flipped version — same meaning, different impact:

  • Plain: "I have never eaten such spicy food." → Flipped: "Never have I eaten such spicy food."
  • Plain: "We rarely go out during the week." → Flipped: "Rarely do we go out during the week."
  • Plain: "He seldom complains about anything." → Flipped: "Seldom does he complain about anything."

What happens to the subject and the verb each time? And what shows up in sentence two and three that wasn't there in the plain version? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
The subject and the auxiliary swap places, exactly like forming a question. If there's already an auxiliary (have), it moves to the front with the subject following. If there ISN'T one — a plain present tense like "go" or "complains" — you have to build one: do / does / did steps in, and the main verb drops its ending (complains → complain).
Never / Rarely / Seldom / At no point + have/has/had/do/does/did + subject + main verb
Two traps: (1) forgetting to invert at all — "Never I have seen" isn't inversion, it's just wrong. (2) forgetting do-support when there's no other auxiliary — "Rarely we go out" needs to become "Rarely do we go out".

Quick check

1. Your upstairs neighbours have started playing music so loud the windows rattle. Complete with the present perfect of hear:

"Never such awful music in my whole life!"

2. Describe a genuine habit of your family's weeknights — it's rare for anyone to be home before nine. Use the present simple of get:

"Rarely home before nine on a weeknight."

3. Your flight was cancelled and nobody from the airline said a word about it beforehand. Use the past simple of inform:

"At no point us that the flight was cancelled."

Not Only That...

A way to stack two impressive, surprising, or annoying facts on top of each other for extra punch.

Spot the pattern

Plain next to flipped again:

  • Plain: "She's a great cook and she's also a fantastic host." → Flipped: "Not only is she a great cook, but she's also a fantastic host."
  • Plain: "They arrived late and they forgot the gifts." → Flipped: "Not only did they arrive late, but they also forgot the gifts."
  • Plain: "He lost his job and he lost his flat the same month." → Flipped: "Not only did he lose his job, but he also lost his flat the same month."

Which half of each sentence gets the flip — the first or the second? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Only the first clause inverts. "Not only is she..." / "Not only did they..." — subject and auxiliary swap, same as never/rarely. The second half, after but, stays in completely normal order: "she's also...", "they also forgot...". Only one flip per sentence.
Not only + have/has/had/do/does/did/is/are/was/were + subject + verb, but + subject + also + verb
Trap: don't invert the second clause too. "Not only did he lose his job, but also did he lose his flat" is a double flip and it's wrong — the second half just needs "but he also lost his flat."

Quick check

1. Your new colleague is turning out to be seriously impressive — she cooks incredible food, and she grows her own vegetables too. Complete with is:

"Not only an amazing cook, but she also grows her own vegetables."

2. Bad delivery day: the parcel showed up three days late, and the box arrived crushed. Complete with the past simple of arrive, subject "the parcel":

"Not only three days late, but the box was also crushed."

3. Your neighbour is genuinely inconsiderate — he blocks the shared driveway every single day, and he plays loud music at night. Complete with the present simple of block:

"Not only the driveway every day, but he also plays loud music at night."

Now say it

SPEAK

Describe someone you know — a friend, a colleague, a family member — combining two facts about them with not only...but also. Say it plain first, then flipped, so you can hear the difference.

The Moment Everything Changed

The inversions people reach for when telling a story about something that happened suddenly, or right after something else.

Spot the pattern

  • "No sooner had I sat down than the phone rang."
  • "Hardly had we arrived when it started pouring with rain."
  • "Little did I know that my quiet Tuesday was about to get very complicated."

Two of these connect one event to a second event that happened almost immediately after. Which word joins them each time? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
No sooner had + subject + past participle... than... and Hardly had + subject + past participle... when... — two ways of saying "the second thing happened almost the instant the first thing did." They use the same past-perfect inversion, but they pair with different joining words: no sooner pairs with than, hardly pairs with when. Mix them up and it sounds off to a native ear.

Little did I know is its own fixed phrase — little up front + did + subject + know + what you didn't see coming. It's a classic way to open the dramatic turn in a story.

There's also only then, for the exact moment understanding finally arrived: "Only then did I understand what she meant."
No sooner + had + subject + past participle + than + subject + past simple
Hardly + had + subject + past participle + when + subject + past simple
Only then + did/was/were + subject + verb
Little did + subject + know + (that) ...
Trap: no sooner...than (never "when"); hardly...when (never "than"). Easy to swap them when you're talking fast — slow down on this one.

Quick check

1. You'd just closed your eyes for a five-minute nap when the doorbell rang. Complete with the past perfect of close:

"No sooner my eyes than the doorbell rang."

2. You and a friend had just walked into the cinema when the fire alarm went off. Complete with the past perfect of walk into:

"Hardly the cinema when the fire alarm went off."

3. You searched everywhere before finally checking your coat pocket — that's when you understood where your keys were. Complete with the past simple of realise:

"Only that I'd left my keys in my coat pocket the whole time."
