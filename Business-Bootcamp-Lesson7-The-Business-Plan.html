The Real Truth About Business Plans

Here's what people get wrong: they think a business plan is a 50-page document written for banks. Wrong. A business plan is YOUR tool. It answers the fundamental questions:

  • What exactly are we selling?
  • Who wants to buy it and why?
  • How will we make money?
  • What could go wrong?
  • What's our competitive edge?

🏗️ The Business Plan Architecture

A full business plan has 7 core sections. Not all situations need all 7 — but knowing them means you can choose what you need.

1 Executive Summary

One page. The entire business in a nutshell. This is what people read first — if it doesn't grab them, they won't read the rest.

2 Company Description

Who you are, what you do, your mission. Keep it to one page. Your story matters, but only if it's relevant to your business.

3 Market Analysis

Who wants what you're selling? How big is the market? Who are your competitors? What's your positioning?

4 Organization & Management

Who runs this? What's your structure? In early stages, this can be brief — but it matters to investors.

5 Marketing & Sales Strategy

How will customers find you? How will you convince them to buy? What's your customer acquisition cost?

6 Financial Projections

Revenue, costs, profit. Year 1, Year 2, Year 3. Include assumptions. Show when you break even.

7 Funding Request (if needed)

How much money do you need and why? What will you use it for? When will it run out?

The Golden Rule: Length

Full business plans: 10-20 pages if you're raising money from investors. Shorter is always better. For your own use or pitch purposes? A one-page business plan is often more powerful than a 50-page document.

📝 Mastering the Critical Sections

Section 1: The Executive Summary (Most Important)

This is the hook. Write it last. One page maximum. Investors spend 30 seconds reading this — if they're interested, they'll read more.

👆 Click to see what belongs in an Executive Summary

The Formula

1. Problem statement: The problem your customer faces (1 sentence)

2. Your solution: How you solve it (1-2 sentences)

3. Market size: "The market is worth £X billion" or "50 million people have this problem"

4. Your competitive advantage: Why you're different

5. Business model: How you make money (key revenue streams)

6. Financial highlights: Year 1 revenue, break-even point, growth trajectory

7. Funding needed: How much and why (if applicable)

Section 3: Market Analysis

Too many entrepreneurs think "everyone is my customer." No. Great businesses know exactly who they're targeting.

👆 Click to see the Market Analysis framework

Market Research Questions

Market Size: How many potential customers are there? What's the total spending?

Trends: Is the market growing, shrinking, or stable?

Customer Profile: Age, location, income, problems they face?

Buying Behavior: How do they currently solve this problem?

Competitive Analysis

Direct competitors: Companies doing exactly what you do

Indirect competitors: Other solutions to the same problem

Your advantage: Better price? Faster? Better quality? Different market?

Market gaps: What's no one solving well?

Section 5: Marketing & Sales Strategy

This section scares entrepreneurs. It shouldn't. It's simple: how will your first customers find you?

  • Customer Acquisition Channels: How will you reach customers? Social media, referrals, paid ads, partnerships, direct sales?
  • Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC): How much does it cost (in time and money) to acquire one customer?
  • Customer Lifetime Value (LTV): How much will one customer spend with you over time?
  • Sales Process: How do customers actually buy? Online? In-person? Over time?
  • Pricing Strategy: Why is your price what it is? What's your profit margin?

    • 🎤 Think It Through

    Pick a product or service you know. If you were starting that business, how would your first 10 customers find you? Be specific — "social media" is too vague. "Instagram reels targeting pet owners aged 25-40 in London" is better.

    💰 Making Financial Projections Not Terrifying

    This is where many entrepreneurs panic. Don't. You don't need to be an accountant. You need to think clearly about three things: revenue, costs, and profit.

    Revenue: How Much Will You Make?

    Start simple. Pick your assumptions and stick with them.

    👆 Click to see a revenue calculation example

    Example: Pet Subscription Box

    Assumptions: £25 per box, 1 box per customer per month

    Year 1:

    • Month 1: 10 customers = £250
    • Month 6: 50 customers = £1,250
    • Month 12: 150 customers = £3,750
    • Year 1 Revenue = £25,000

    Year 2: Assume 200% growth = £50,000 revenue

    Year 3: Assume 150% growth = £75,000 revenue

    Costs: What Will It Cost to Run?

    Think in categories: fixed costs (same every month) and variable costs (change with sales).

    👆 Click to see cost examples

    Fixed Costs (per month)

    Rent, salaries, insurance, utilities, software subscriptions

    Example: £2,000/month

    Variable Costs (per unit sold)

    Product costs, packaging, shipping, payment processing fees

    Example: £8 per box

    Profit: The Bottom Line

    Revenue - Costs = Profit (Simple as that)

    Year 1 Projection:
    Revenue: £25,000
    Fixed Costs: £24,000 (£2,000 × 12)
    Variable Costs: £14,400 (150 customers × 12 months × £8)
    Total Costs: £38,400
    Profit: -£13,400 (loss)
    💡 Critical Insight

    This business loses money Year 1. That's okay — many do. But you need to show: (1) When do we break even? (2) What does Year 2 and 3 look like? (3) What would need to change to be profitable sooner? This is the conversation investors want to have.

    The Key Metrics Investors Actually Care About

  • Break-Even Point: When do revenues equal costs? (month, quarter, or year)
  • Gross Margin: (Revenue - Variable Costs) / Revenue. Higher is better. Aim for 60%+
  • Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC): Total marketing spend ÷ number of customers acquired
  • Customer Lifetime Value (LTV): Estimated total profit from one customer over their lifetime
  • LTV:CAC Ratio: Should be at least 3:1 (you make £3 for every £1 spent acquiring customers)
  • Burn Rate: How fast are you losing money? (if pre-revenue)

    • 📊 Quick Math

    Create a simple revenue and cost projection for a business idea. Pick: price point, how many customers in month 1, growth rate, and 3-4 main costs. Calculate Year 1 profit or loss.

    🏆 The Business Plan Pitch Challenge

    It's boardroom time. You're sitting across from Lord Sugar with your business plan. You have 3 minutes to convince them your business is worth investing in.

    📺 Your Brief

    You've spent weeks researching, analyzing, and planning. Now you have 3 minutes to present your business plan. You can use visuals (your one-pager), but your words need to be crystal clear. No waffle. No jargon. Just business sense.

    What Your 3-Minute Pitch Must Include

    • The problem your customer faces (what pain are you solving?)
    • Your solution (what you're selling)
    • Your customer (who are they?)
    • Market size (how big is the opportunity?)
    • How you make money (revenue model)
    • Year 1 financials (revenue, costs, profit/loss)
    • Your competitive advantage (why you win)
    • What you need (funding, partnerships, etc.)
    • Why you're the right person to execute this

    How to Structure Your 3-Minute Pitch

    OPENING (15 seconds): Hook with the problem
    Example: "Every year, small businesses waste thousands on design agencies they don't fully trust. They end up with generic work that doesn't stand out."
    YOUR SOLUTION (30 seconds): What you're offering
    Example: "We connect small businesses with vetted freelance designers who understand their industry. They get quality design in 1 week, not 6 weeks."
    MARKET & NUMBERS (45 seconds): Customer + financials
    Example: "Our target is 5,000 small businesses in London. We charge £1,200 per project. Year 1, we project 50 customers = £60,000 revenue."
    EDGE (30 seconds): Why you're different + why you'll win
    Example: "Unlike other agencies, our designers are specialists in specific industries. A restaurant designer knows restaurant design. That's our edge."
    THE ASK (20 seconds): What you need and why
    Example: "I'm asking for £30,000 to build our platform, hire our first 3 lead designers, and fund customer acquisition. We'll break even month 8."

    ⏱️ Your 3-Minute Boardroom Challenge

    3:00

    🎤 Deliver Your Pitch

    You're now in the boardroom. Lord Sugar is listening. Stand up. Look confident. Deliver your business plan pitch under the timer. Your teacher will ask hard questions after — be ready to defend your numbers.

    💭 Tough Questions You Might Get Asked

  • "Why should I believe these numbers?" — Have sources and assumptions clear
  • "What if you only get half the customers you project?" — Show you've thought about downside scenarios
  • "Who's your biggest competitor and how do you beat them?" — Know them better than they know themselves
  • "What would cause this business to fail?" — Show you understand risks
  • "Why are you the right person to run this?" — Have relevant experience or insights
