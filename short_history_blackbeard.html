Blackbeard

For two years one man terrorised the Atlantic — armed less with cannon than with the picture of himself he put in everyone's head.

Today: you'll tell the story of a fearsome reputation — how it was built and how it ended — and discuss why fear can be more powerful than force, using the past simple and past continuous and the language of crime on the high seas.

Key words — guess first, then check

Before we read, let's unlock the words you'll need. Each one is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. In the early 1700s the Caribbean was overrun by sea robbers, and merchants lived in dread of piracy on the trade routes.
piracy (n) — the crime of attacking and robbing ships at sea. (a person: a pirate)
2. From the deck of his flagship, the admiral signalled to the smaller ships strung out behind him and gave the order to advance.
a flagship (n) — the main, most important ship in a fleet, carrying the commander.
3. The warships took up position across the harbour mouth to blockade the town, and soon no merchant ship dared try to slip past them.
to blockade (v) — to seal off a place by surrounding it, so people and goods cannot get past.
4. The raiders dragged the wealthy merchant off as a hostage, locking him below deck while they sent word to his family.
a hostage (n) — a person held prisoner so that the captor can demand something in exchange.
5. The family scraped together every coin they had to pay the ransom, desperate to see their son walk free again.
a ransom (n) — money or goods demanded in return for releasing a prisoner.
6. He cultivated a terrifying image on purpose — the wilder the stories about him grew, the easier his work became.
to cultivate (an image) (v) — to deliberately develop and encourage a particular impression of yourself.
7. His whole performance was pure theatrics — the smoke, the pistols, the slow grin — designed to frighten crews into giving up before a shot was fired.
theatrics (n, pl) — dramatic, exaggerated behaviour intended to create an effect on an audience.
8. Faced with that fearsome sight, most captains chose to surrender at once, handing over their cargo rather than risk a fight.
to surrender (v) — to stop resisting and give yourself or your possessions up to an enemy.
9. After years on the run, the outlaw begged the king for a pardon, hoping his crimes might be wiped clean and forgotten.
a pardon (n) — an official decision to forgive a crime and cancel the punishment.
10. The navy would lay an ambush for a pirate, lying in wait out of sight until the perfect moment to strike.
an ambush (n) — a surprise attack made from a hidden position.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The making of a monster

We know surprisingly little about the man who became the most feared pirate in history. Even his real name is uncertain. He is usually called Edward Teach, though old records also spell it Thatch, Thache and several other ways — pirates often shed their old names, and his true identity has been blurred by time. He was probably born in or around Bristol, in the west of England, in the late 1600s, and is thought to have learned the sea as a sailor before turning to crime.

His moment came during the Golden Age of Piracy, the few decades around 1700 when the Caribbean and the American coast swarmed with sea robbers. Late in 1717, off the island of Martinique, Teach and his companions captured a large French ship. He kept her as his flagship, fitted her with around forty guns, and gave her a memorable name: Queen Anne's Revenge. With that one powerful vessel and a few smaller ones, an ordinary sailor had become a commander to be reckoned with.

But his real weapon was not the cannon. It was his face. Teach grew a thick, wild black beard that covered most of his features, and into it — when he went into battle — he tucked slow-burning lengths of cord, so that smoke curled and drifted around his head. With pistols slung across his chest, he looked, by every account, like something not quite human. He had understood something simple and powerful: if his reputation arrived before he did, many crews would surrender without a fight. The legend of "Blackbeard" was, in large part, a performance he built on purpose.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is it hard to be certain about Blackbeard's real name and origins?

2. What was Queen Anne's Revenge, and why did Blackbeard's terrifying appearance actually make his work easier?

1. Very little was recorded about him, his name is spelled several ways (Teach, Thatch, Thache…), and pirates often dropped their original names — so his true identity has been blurred by time.

2. It was the large captured French ship he made his flagship, armed with around forty guns. His fearsome image — the beard, the smoke, the pistols — frightened many crews into surrendering without a fight, so he often won without firing.

Discuss:
  • Blackbeard won battles mostly by frightening people, not by hurting them. Where else do you see fear or reputation used as a weapon today — in business, politics or daily life?

Part 2 — The blockade and the bargain

By the spring of 1718 Blackbeard was at the height of his power. In May he sailed his small fleet to the busy port of Charleston, in the British colony of South Carolina, and did something extraordinarily bold: he blockaded the entire harbour. For about a week, nothing could safely come or go. He stopped ship after ship, plundered them for supplies, and took a number of prominent passengers hostage — including a member of the colony's council.

Then came his strangest demand. He did not ask for gold. He sent word to the governor that, in exchange for the hostages' lives, he wanted a chest of medicine. Historians still debate why — perhaps his crew was sick, perhaps suffering from the diseases common among sailors of the age. Whatever the reason, the threat behind it was deadly serious, and the authorities, fearing he would carry it out, handed the medicine over. The hostages were released, and Blackbeard sailed away, having held a major British port to ransom and won.

Soon afterwards, though, he changed course. He ran Queen Anne's Revenge aground and lost his great flagship, and he travelled to North Carolina to accept a royal pardon from its governor — officially, at least, becoming an honest man. Many of the most successful pirates took such pardons when the risks grew too high. But for Blackbeard, retirement did not last. Before long he was quietly back at sea, robbing ships again, his fearsome reputation still doing much of the work for him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Blackbeard do at Charleston in 1718, and what unusual ransom did he demand?

2. He accepted a royal pardon — so why did his life of crime not actually end there?

1. He blockaded the harbour for about a week, robbed ships and took hostages, and demanded — instead of gold — a chest of medicine in exchange for the prisoners' lives. The authorities paid, and the hostages were freed.

2. He lost his flagship and took a royal pardon in North Carolina, but he soon returned quietly to robbing ships at sea, so in practice he never really stopped.

Discuss:
  • He held a whole city to ransom and asked only for medicine, not treasure. What does that choice tell you about him — and about what people will pay almost anything to protect?

Part 3 — The trap at Ocracoke

By late 1718, the authorities had had enough. The governor of the neighbouring colony of Virginia, unwilling to wait for North Carolina to act, sent a Royal Navy officer, Lieutenant Robert Maynard, to hunt the pirate down. On 22 November, Maynard's men cornered Blackbeard at Ocracoke, a quiet inlet off the North Carolina coast where the pirate liked to anchor.

The fight that followed was short and ferocious. Maynard, whose ships had run aground and were outgunned, hid most of his men below deck and waited. Seeing only a near-empty vessel, Blackbeard boarded — and walked straight into the trap. The sailors poured up from below and surrounded him. According to the accounts of the day, Blackbeard fought on through a shocking number of wounds before he finally fell. To prove the monster was truly dead, Maynard had his head cut off and hung from the front of his ship for the voyage home.

His death is often treated as the symbolic end of the Golden Age of Piracy; within a few years the great pirate threat had largely been crushed. Yet the legend refused to die with the man. Blackbeard had been a real and dangerous criminal for only about two years — but the terrifying figure he invented has sailed through three centuries of books, films and stories, far more famous in death than almost any pirate was in life.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did Maynard manage to defeat Blackbeard at Ocracoke?

2. Why is Blackbeard's death seen as the end of an era — and what survived him?

1. He hid most of his men below deck so his ship looked nearly empty; when Blackbeard boarded, the sailors rushed up and surrounded him. Blackbeard was killed, and his head was cut off and displayed on Maynard's ship.

2. It is treated as the symbolic end of the Golden Age of Piracy, which was largely crushed soon afterwards. What survived him was the legend — the invented, terrifying figure that has stayed famous for three centuries.

Discuss:
  • Blackbeard was a criminal for only about two years, yet he is one of the most famous pirates ever. Why do you think the image has outlived the man so completely?

The grammar this story runs on: past simple vs past continuous

Storytelling lives on the contrast between these two tenses. One paints the scene that was already unfolding; the other is the sharp event that breaks into it. Get the contrast right and your listener feels the moment everything changes.

The two tools:
• Past continuous (was/were + -ing) — the background, the longer action in progress, the scene that was already happening: "the merchants were sailing calmly through the Caribbean…"
• Past simple — the completed event that interrupts that scene, the sudden thing that happens: "…when a black flag appeared on the horizon."

Two linking words do most of the work:
• when + past simple = the interrupting event ("…when the cannon fired").
• while + past continuous = the background it interrupts ("While the crew were sleeping, …").
And when two long actions overlap, use the past continuous for both: "While his men were robbing the ships, Blackbeard was watching the harbour mouth."

1. Background vs the event that breaks it

Choose the past continuous for the scene already in progress, and the past simple for the shorter, completed event that interrupts it.

1. The merchants (sail) calmly through the Caribbean when a black flag suddenly (appear) on the horizon.

2. While the crew (sleep), Blackbeard (close) in for the ambush.

3. Smoke (curl) around his head as he (climb) onto the merchant deck.

1. were sailing / appeared (the scene in progress / the sudden event that breaks it)

2. were sleeping / closed (while + background; the completed action)

3. was curling / climbed (background detail / the event)

2. when or while?

Use when before the short, completed event (past simple) and while before the longer background (past continuous). Fill the gap with the right word.

1. Blackbeard's ship was blockading the harbour, no merchant dared to leave.

2. The captain was still arguing with his crew the pirates boarded.

3. the lit fuses were smoking in his beard, many crews simply surrendered.

1. While (+ past continuous background)

2. when (+ past simple event)

3. While (+ past continuous background)

3. Put them together — set the scene, then break it

Fill each gap with the tense the meaning needs. Read it aloud once you've finished.

On the morning of the battle, the pirates (rest) on a quiet deck and the sea (lie) flat and calm. While they (wait), Maynard's hidden men (crouch) below. Then Blackbeard (board) the near-empty ship — and just as he (cross) the deck, the sailors (pour) up and (surround) him.

were resting (background) · was lying (background) · were waiting (background) · were crouching (simultaneous background) · boarded (event) · was crossing (background in progress) · poured (event) · surrounded (event)

Notice the rhythm: the scene unfolds in the past continuous, then the past simple snaps the moment shut.
