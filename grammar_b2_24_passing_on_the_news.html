Warm-up

Out loud, before anything else — don't worry about mistakes, just talk.

SPEAK

Think about the last piece of news you passed on to someone — a friend's move, a shop closing, a mix-up at work. Out loud, tell it now, exactly as you told it then.

Listen in on this relay

Jo: Did you hear about Sam?

Marco: No — what's happened?

Jo: Apparently he's moving to Dublin for work.

Marco: Since when? He never said anything to me.

Jo: I know — he must have been planning it for ages and just not told anyone.

Marco: Or maybe he only heard back last week. Priya said he'd been waiting on the visa for months.

Look at what Jo and Marco do in six short lines. Three completely different jobs, all in one everyday chat about someone else's news. Can you spot all three? Say them before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "Apparently he's moving" — a distancing marker, for news you didn't witness yourself.
2. "he must have been planning it for ages" — deduction, for filling in the gaps with logic.
3. "Priya said he'd been waiting on the visa" — reported speech, for passing on someone else's exact words.

This is why these structures matter: they ARE how news travels between people. Master these and you can pass anything on naturally.
Today: turning what you hear into what you tell — reported speech, distancing markers like apparently, and deduction with must/might/can't have — so you can pass on the news naturally, without sounding like you're reading a statement out loud.

She Said, He Said

When you retell someone's words, the grammar shifts one step into the past.

Spot the pattern

  • Direct: "I'm really enjoying the new job." → Reported: "She said (that) she was really enjoying the new job."
  • Direct: "We'll send you the details tomorrow." → Reported: "He said (that) they would send us the details the next day."
  • Direct: "Are you free this weekend?" → Reported: "She asked if I was free that weekend."

What happens to the verb each time, and what happens to the question? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Backshift: the verb moves one step into the past — am/is/are → was/were, will → would. "That" is optional and often dropped in speech.

Reported questions lose the question word order and the question mark. Yes/no questions add if or whether; wh-questions keep the wh-word but go back to normal subject-verb order.
said (that) + subject + backshifted verb
asked if / whether + subject + backshifted verb
asked + wh-word + subject + backshifted verb
One trap: reported questions never invert. "She asked if I was coming" — not "she asked was I coming". And "asked" never takes "that" — that's for statements.

Quick check

1. Your flatmate once told you: "I work better in the mornings." Report it to someone else:

"She said she better in the mornings."

2. A colleague said: "I'll text you the address later." Report it:

"He said he text me the address later."

3. A friend asked you: "Are you still coming to the barbecue?" Report the question:

"She asked I was still coming to the barbecue."

Apparently...

Small words that tell your listener exactly how sure you are — and where the news came from.

Spot the pattern

  • "Apparently, the gym on the corner is closing down at the end of the month."
  • "It turns out the delay was because of a signal fault, not the weather."
  • "According to Priya, the new manager starts on Monday."

Each one hedges the news differently. What's the difference between them? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Apparently / supposedly — secondhand, unconfirmed, no specific source named.
It turns out — a surprising truth revealed, often correcting what everyone assumed.
According to [person] — attributed to a specific, named source.
I heard (that) — casual hearsay, picked up from somewhere.
Apparently, + clause.  /  Clause, apparently.
It turns out (that) + clause.
I heard (that) + clause.
According to [named source], + clause.
One trap: "according to" always needs a specific, named source — a person, a website, a sign. You can't say "according to people say" (that's just "apparently").

Quick check

1. Your friend Leo told you the café is under new ownership. Attribute it to him:

Leo, the café's under new ownership now.

2. Everyone assumed your flight was delayed because of the strike, but actually it was a technical fault — a surprising truth just revealed:

the delay had nothing to do with the strike at all.

3. Your cousin exaggerates constantly, so you're not fully convinced by his latest story — an unconfirmed, slightly doubtful claim, no named source:

He's, , dating a famous singer — I'll believe it when I see it.

They Must Have Known

When you don't know for certain, English lets you guess out loud — with different levels of confidence.

Spot the pattern

  • "She must have known about the surprise party — she kept asking odd questions all week."
  • "They can't have told him about the change of plan — he still thinks it's next Tuesday."
  • "He might have got the dates mixed up — that would explain why he wasn't there."

Three guesses, three different levels of confidence. What's the shared structure, and what changes the confidence level? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Shared structure: modal + have + past participle.

must have = confident it happened (the evidence points one way).
can't have = confident it didn't happen (the evidence rules it out).
might/could have = not sure — one possibility among others.
must have + past participle  (confident: it happened)
can't have + past participle  (confident: it didn't happen)
might / could have + past participle  (possible, not sure)
One trap: it's always "have" after the modal, never "had" — must have known, not "must had known". And "must have" is pronounced "must've" but always written in full.

Quick check

1. You arranged to meet a friend at the café at 10. It's 10:15, no message, no reply. Make a confident guess with leave:

"She home late."

2. Your friend swears she told her sister about the move, but the sister looks genuinely shocked when you mention it. Make a confident negative guess with tell:

"She her — look at her face!"

3. Nobody knows why the parcel arrived a week late — a few things might have happened. Make an uncertain guess with get lost:

"It somewhere in the system."
