Haggling at a Market

The rug is perfect, the price is madness, and the trader is already smiling. The dance has begun.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

You're deep in a covered market on the last day of your trip — narrow lanes, hanging lamps, the smell of spice and dust and old leather. On one stall, half-buried under a hundred others, hangs a hand-woven rug: deep reds, a pattern you've never seen, and not a single machine-made thread in it. You've stopped walking. That's your first mistake.

The trader has already clocked the look on your face. Here, the price on his lips is never the price you pay — it's the opening line of a conversation. Getting a fair deal today won't be about how much money you have. It'll be about how you want it, and whether you're willing to walk away without it.

Behind the stall: a trader who has sold rugs to a thousand tourists and lost to a handful of them. Warm, quick, and endlessly patient — he has all afternoon, and he knows you have a flight to catch.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. When did you last haggle over a price — a market, a car, a flat, a phone contract? Did you enjoy it, or would you rather have paid the sticker price and run?
  2. In some places, haggling is expected and refusing to do it is almost rude; in others, it's mortifying. Where do you sit — and does it change the moment you're the one selling?

Who's who

You
The Buyer 🧳

Charmed, a little out of your depth, but no fool. You want the rug — badly — but you'd rather not say so, and you're ready to walk if the number stays silly.

The other voice
The Trader 🧵

Sold everything to everyone. Opens high, always. Reads your face better than you read the price — and secretly enjoys a buyer who plays the game well.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't just follow what the buyer offers — watch how they admire it, question the price, and drift towards the exit. Walking away is a phrase, not a failure.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the warmth, the pauses, and the moment the price starts to move.

TraderAh, you have a good eye — I saw you stop. Come, come closer, it doesn't bite. Feel the weight of it.
YouIt really is lovely — you don't often see work like this. Did you make these yourself, or…?
TraderMy family, in the mountains. This one, four months on the loom. Every knot by hand — count them if you like, we have until closing time.
YouFour months — I can believe it. Go on then, what are you asking for it?
TraderFor you, my friend, because it is late and you have a kind face — one hundred and twenty. It's a gift, honestly.
YouOoh. That's a fair bit more than I was hoping to spend, to be honest. It's beautiful, but a hundred and twenty is out of my range.
TraderOut of your range! For four months' work? You wound me. But I like you — make me an offer, go on. Don't be shy.
YouAll right — but don't laugh. Would you take sixty for it?
Trader(clutches chest) Sixty! My children, they go hungry tonight. No, no — a hundred, and I throw in a cushion cover. That's my final, my very final.
YouI appreciate that, I do. But is there any movement below a hundred? Could we not meet somewhere in the middle?
TraderThe middle… ninety. Ninety and we shake hands, and you tell all your friends where you found it.
YouHmm. I'll be honest with you — I think I'll leave it and have a look round. Thank you, though, genuinely.
Trader(as you turn) Wait, wait — you break my heart. Come back. For a serious buyer who walks away… what can you really do? Tell me a real number.
YouTell you what — I've got seventy-five on me, and that's honestly my lot. Seventy-five, the cushion cover, and you've got a deal.
TraderSeventy-five and you carry it yourself, no delivery! (sighs, grins) …Go on. You drive a hard bargain. Give me your hand.
YouDone — you've twisted my arm. Pleasure doing business with you, truly. It'll have pride of place at home.
TraderGood, good. Come back next year and I'll charge you double — you're too good at this. Safe travels, my friend.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The trader opens at 120 and the buyer walks out with the rug for 75 plus a cushion cover. Trace the four numbers the price passed through on the way down — and whose move dropped it each time.

2. "I think I'll leave it and have a look round." The buyer clearly wants the rug — so why say this, and what does it actually achieve one line later?

3. "I've got seventy-five on me, and that's honestly my lot." Why is naming a fixed amount of cash a stronger move than simply saying "I'll give you seventy-five"?

1. 120 (trader's opening) → buyer counters low at 60 → trader drops to 100 (with a free cushion cover) → buyer pushes "meet in the middle", trader offers 90 → buyer walks, trader chases and buyer closes at 75. The two big falls came from the buyer's low counter and, crucially, from the walk-away.
2. It's the walk-away — the single most powerful move in any haggle. By looking genuinely ready to leave empty-handed, the buyer forces the trader to decide whether 90 is worth losing the whole sale. One line later the trader chases them back and asks for "a real number" — the price has moved because the buyer's feet did.
3. A number sounds like an offer you can nudge upward; a wallet with exactly 75 in it sounds like a physical wall. "That's my lot" turns a negotiating position into a simple fact of the world — you're not being stubborn, you literally can't. It's very hard to argue with an empty pocket.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that carry you from "just looking" to "sold" — at this stall and any other. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

😍 Admiring — warm them up

Phrase 1

You've stopped at the stall and you genuinely love the piece. You want to praise the craft — which flatters the seller and costs you nothing — without blurting out how much you want it. Which line does that?

A. "I really, really need this — how much?"
B. "You don't often see work like this, do you?"
C. "Is this the cheap one or the expensive one?"
B — "You don't often see work like this, do you?"
It praises the skill, not the object, so it warms the seller without confessing your desire. A shows your hand and invites a high price; C sounds cold and transactional. Cousins: "This is really beautifully made." "There's real craftsmanship in this."
Phrase 2

You want to get the trader talking proudly about where the piece comes from — proud sellers relax, and relaxed sellers deal. Ask, warmly, whether they made it themselves. What's your (gently curious) line?

"Did you make these yourself, or…?"
The trailing "…or?" leaves the door open and invites the whole story. It shows real interest in the work, which buys goodwill you can spend later on the price. Try also: "Where's this one from, if you don't mind me asking?"

💰 Asking the price — on your terms

Phrase 3

Time to ask the price — but here the first number is only ever an opening bid, and you want your question to hint that you know that. Which framing quietly treats the price as negotiable rather than fixed?

A. "What's the price? Is it fixed?"
B. "How much does this cost, please?"
C. "Go on then, what are you asking for it?"
C — "Go on then, what are you asking for it?"
"What are you asking?" frames the number as their request, not a law of nature — it invites a counter-offer before a word is spoken. "Go on then" adds an easy, playful warmth. B is perfectly polite but sounds like a fixed-price shop.
Phrase 4

They've named a high number. You want to flinch — politely — and plant the idea that it's beyond you, without saying a flat "no". Complete the chunk: "That's a fair bit ___ ___ ___ I was hoping to spend."

"That's a fair bit more than I was hoping to spend."
A soft, honest flinch. "A fair bit more" is understatement — it signals surprise without insult — and "than I was hoping to spend" makes it about your budget, not their greed. Follow it with: "…it's beautiful, but it's out of my range."

🤝 Bargaining warmly — move the number

Phrase 5

The first drop wasn't enough. You want to nudge them lower without naming a figure yourself — a smooth phrase that simply asks whether the price can shift. Put the pieces in order:

anyis thereon the price?movement
"Is there any movement on the price?"
Beautifully non-committal: you haven't offered anything, you've simply asked the price to be flexible — and left them to make the next move. Handy everywhere, from markets to salary talks. Sibling: "Is the price negotiable at all?"
Phrase 6

You're apart — they're high, you're low — and you want to suggest splitting the difference, framing it as fair to both of you rather than a demand. Which line does that most warmly?

A. "Just lower the price, come on."
B. "Could we not meet somewhere in the middle?"
C. "Your price is too high, drop it."
B — "Could we not meet somewhere in the middle?"
"Meet in the middle" makes the compromise mutual — we both give a little — so it lands as fair, not as pressure. The negative question ("Could we not…?") sounds gently hopeful rather than demanding. A and C are blunt and put the seller's back up.

🚶 Ready to walk away — your strongest card

Phrase 7

The price has stalled above what you'll pay. Your best move now is to look genuinely ready to leave with nothing — that's what makes the number drop. What do you say, warmly, as you start to drift off?

"I think I'll leave it and have a look round. Thank you, though."
The walk-away — the most powerful phrase in haggling. It's not a bluff you shout; it's a calm, friendly exit that forces them to choose between their price and the sale. Keep it warm so the door stays open: "I'll leave it, thanks — it's just a bit much for me today."
Phrase 8

They've chased you back and asked for "a real number". You name a firm limit — and make it sound like a fact of the world, not a position you'll shift from. Complete the chunk: "I've got seventy-five on me, and that's ___ ___ ___."

"I've got seventy-five on me, and that's honestly my lot."
"That's my lot" = that's all I have, full stop. Naming the actual cash in your pocket turns a negotiating position into a physical wall — you're not being difficult, there simply isn't any more. Variants: "…and that's me maxed out." "…that's the last of my cash."

✅ Closing the deal — seal it with a smile

Phrase 9

They've come down to your number. Don't just grunt "OK" — accept with charm that flatters their effort and marks a happy ending for you both. What's the warm way to say yes?

"Done — you've twisted my arm. You've got a deal."
"You've twisted my arm" cheerfully pretends they persuaded you — a generous, face-saving way to close that leaves everyone feeling they won. "You've got a deal" seals it cleanly. Also lovely: "Go on then — you've got yourself a sale."
Phrase 10

The rug's yours, the price was fair, and you'd both enjoy parting on good terms. Which goodbye respects the trader as a worthy opponent and leaves a smile behind?

A. "Bye."
B. "Thank you for completing this transaction with me."
C. "You drive a hard bargain — pleasure doing business with you."
C — "You drive a hard bargain — pleasure doing business with you."
"You drive a hard bargain" is a compliment between equals — it tips your hat to a good haggler. "Pleasure doing business with you" ends it warmly and grown-up. A is flat; B sounds like a receipt read aloud.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole stall out loud using all ten, in order — admire → ask the price → flinch → nudge → offer → walk away → name your limit → close → goodbye. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

🌩️ The two-rug problem

One more round — no script, no safety net. You've fallen for TWO rugs, not one, but you've only got a hundred in cash, no card that works here, and a taxi to the airport in fifteen minutes. The trader knows it. Start the timer and talk your way through it: can you get both for a hundred as a "pair price"? Is one alone the smarter buy? Could you leave a small deposit and have the second posted home — and would you trust that? Do you play your rush as weakness, or turn "I have to leave" into your own walk-away? One rule: the moment you turn rude or start demanding rather than persuading, the trader folds his arms and the price only goes up.

The Echo 🕰️

For fifteen hundred years, the greatest market on earth had no walls. Silk from China, spices from India, glass from Rome, horses from the steppe — all of it changed hands along the Silk Road, one bargain at a time, across languages nobody fully shared.

Every one of those deals was a version of the dance you've just learned: admire, question the price, offer, threaten to walk, close with a handshake. No common tongue, no fixed prices — just two people, some goods, and the oldest conversation in the world.

Your rug cost you seventy-five and a bit of nerve. On the Silk Road it might have crossed a continent before it reached your stall. You didn't just buy a rug — you took your turn in a two-thousand-year-old haggle.

Two bigger questions

  1. On the Silk Road, buyer and seller often shared no language and would never meet again — yet trade flourished for centuries. What lets two strangers strike a fair deal with almost no words and no trust? Is haggling itself a kind of shared language?
  2. We now live mostly with fixed prices and one-click buying — no dance, no walk-away, no handshake. Have we gained something by killing the haggle, or lost something human in the trade?
