An order no one has ever seen

It is a rainy Sunday and a family is playing cards. Someone shuffles the deck, seven times as they were taught, and puts it in the middle of the table. The cards are no longer in any order a person could name. There is no Ace of Spades first, no King of Hearts at the end. It is a jumble.

Here is something curious. A pack has fifty-two cards, and the number of ways to arrange fifty-two things is fifty-two times fifty-one times fifty and so on all the way down to one. That number begins with eight and has sixty-eight digits. It is so large that a properly shuffled deck is almost certainly in an order that has never existed before, and will never exist again.

Why is a properly shuffled deck almost certainly unique?
The number of possible orders of 52 cards is astronomically large, so the chance that anyone has had the same order before is tiny.
Talk about it. Is it more surprising to you that a deck is unique, or that someone can learn that unique order? Why?

And yet a person can look at a shuffled deck and learn the whole order. People do it as a hobby and in competitions. They do not do it by repeating seven of hearts, queen of clubs, three of diamonds over and over. A card is a poor thing to remember: the same red and black marks, the same faces, fifty-two almost alike.

What they do is turn each card into a picture, and put the pictures in order along a route. In competitions the best of them learn a pack in less than thirteen seconds. The method is the one you already have.

How do people learn the order of a pack?
They give each card its own picture and place the pictures along a route of places.
Talk about it. Would you like to learn a deck? What would you use it for?

To do this you need a fixed list of fifty-two pictures, one for each card, so that you never have to decide. The seven of hearts is always the same scene. In this room you will learn a simple system, and see how champions make it faster.

Fifty-two pictures sounds like a lot. But there is a pattern. Each suit is a family of thirteen, so you learn four families, not fifty-two separate things. You will build this system one suit at a time, and by the end of the room you will be able to run through a short hand.

Why does the card system use a fixed list of pictures?
So the same card is always the same picture and you never have to decide again.
Talk about it. What pattern would you use to give 52 cards 52 pictures? Think of suits as groups.

How champions go faster

Champions do not use one picture per card. They give every card a person (or a person with an action and an object, like PAO), and then take two or three cards at a time: the first card gives the person, the second the action, the third the object. A pack of 52 then needs only about 17 scenes, and a scene takes a couple of seconds to build. This is how a whole pack can be learned in under a minute.
