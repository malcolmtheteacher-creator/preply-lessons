The Scientists' Press Briefing

Your findings are preliminary. The journalists want certainty. Hold the line.

What Went Wrong?

A real example of how science gets distorted in headlines:

Headline: "COFFEE CAUSES CANCER — Scientists Warn of Major Health Risk"
What the study actually said: "A preliminary observation in a lab model suggests a potential mechanism by which one component of coffee might, under specific conditions, show limited effects on cellular markers associated with cancer risk. Further research is needed."

The headline is dishonest. The scientist who said "potential mechanism" got turned into "causes cancer." This happens because journalists want certainty, but honest scientists communicate degrees of confidence. Your job in a press briefing is to hold the line—be honest about what your data shows while resisting pressure to overstate.

Here are the six key dimensions of scientific hedging—the language that matches your actual level of confidence:

1Modal Verbs ▼

What it means: Use modal verbs (may, might, could, tend to) to match the strength of your evidence.

Too strong: "Sleep deprivation causes poor work performance."

Honest: "Sleep deprivation may contribute to reduced occupational performance."

The scale:

  • may/might — low confidence, preliminary evidence
  • tends to/appears to — moderate confidence, pattern observed
  • is likely to — stronger evidence, but not proven
  • has been shown to — well-established in research
2Epistemic Adverbs ▼

What it means: Adverbs like "possibly," "arguably," "apparently" signal uncertainty without saying "I don't know."

"This possibly indicates a shift in behaviour, though further work is needed."

"The mechanism appears to involve three key stages."

"Tentatively, we can say that..."
3Approximating Language ▼

What it means: Limit the scope of your claims. Don't let journalists turn your sample into a universal claim.

Too broad: "Remote work increases productivity."

Honest: "In our sample of 200 knowledge workers, remote work correlated with increased task completion rates."

Phrases: "in the majority of cases," "under certain conditions," "for populations similar to our sample," "in this context," "in the short term"

4Source Attribution ▼

What it means: Make clear where evidence is coming from. Preliminary data, our sample, current literature—attribute it.

"Based on our preliminary data..." (not "proven")

"The current evidence suggests..." (allows for future contradictions)

"According to the broader literature..." (situates your work)
5The Ladder of Certainty ▼

What it means: Know where your claim sits on the certainty scale. Use language that matches.

"X is proven" — only for laws of nature with universal evidence
"X is well-established" — decades of consistent evidence
"X is supported by evidence" — multiple good studies, some disagreement
"X is suggested by our data" — we found it, but it's preliminary
"X warrants further investigation" — initial signal, needs confirmation
6Correcting Misrepresentation ▼

What it means: When a journalist pushes you toward certainty you don't have, correct them clearly and respectfully.

"That's not quite what our findings show. What we found was [precise statement]. We cannot yet say [overclaim]."

"I want to be precise here: we can say X, but not Y."

"The headline you're suggesting goes beyond what the evidence supports. Here's what we actually observed..."

Reflection

Hedging language isn't weakness—it's honesty. The scientists who stand up to media pressure and refuse to overstate findings are the ones the public should trust. Precision under pressure is the mark of genuine expertise.
