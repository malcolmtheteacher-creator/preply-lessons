Making a Decision

Voting & Reaching Group Consensus

Vocabulary for Decision Making

Essential words and phrases for group decision-making meetings:

Proposal A suggested plan or idea presented for consideration
Motion A formal proposal made in a meeting, typically voted on
In favour of Supporting; voting yes for something
Against Opposing; voting no against something
Abstain To refrain from voting; to remain neutral
Unanimous vote A vote where everyone votes the same way
Proxy vote A vote cast on behalf of another person who is absent
Casting vote A deciding vote used when votes are tied; usually by the chair
Motion carried A proposal is accepted; the vote passed
Motion rejected A proposal is not accepted; the vote failed
Show of hands A voting method where people raise their hands to vote
Draw or tie Equal votes for both options; no clear winner

Understanding Voting Terms

Test your understanding of voting terminology:

1. Is a proxy vote a vote that you cast for yourself?

No. A proxy vote is a vote you cast on behalf of someone else who cannot be present. That person authorizes you to vote in their place.

2. Are you in favour of something if you disagree?

No. If you are "in favour of" something, you support it and agree. If you disagree, you are "against" it.

3. When you draw or tie, which side wins?

Neither. A draw or tie means votes are equal. This is why the chairperson's casting vote is needed to decide.

4. When would someone make a casting vote?

A casting vote is made when the votes are equal (a tie). The chairperson uses this vote to break the deadlock and make a final decision.

5. Is a show of hands used to vote or to show who is present?

A show of hands is used to vote. It's a visible voting method where people raise their hands to vote yes or no. (It's not used for attendance.)

6. If a motion is carried, is it accepted or rejected?

If a motion is "carried," it is accepted. The vote passed, and the proposal goes forward.

7. In a unanimous vote, does everybody vote the same or different?

In a unanimous vote, everybody votes the same. There is complete agreement.

8. When you abstain, do you vote for or against something?

When you abstain, you vote for neither. You remain neutral and don't count toward either side of the vote.

9. When you make a proposal, is it always accepted?

No. A proposal is only accepted if the vote passes. People may disagree and vote against it.

Language for Expressing Opinions in Meetings

Use these phrases to participate effectively in group decisions:

Asking for Opinions

  • "What are your views on this?"
  • "What are your ideas on this?"
  • "Does anyone have thoughts on...?"
  • "What do people think about...?"

Expressing Agreement

  • "I completely agree."
  • "You have a good point there."
  • "I can see both sides of the argument."
  • "That makes sense."

Expressing Disagreement

  • "I'm not sure I agree with you there."
  • "I'm afraid I can't agree with you there."
  • "I see your point, but..."
  • "I respectfully disagree because..."

Proposing and Voting

  • "I'd like to propose..."
  • "Let's have a show of hands."
  • "Those in favour of...?"
  • "Those against...?"
  • "Anyone abstaining?"

Example Dialogue

Chair: "We need to decide on the office move. What are your views on this?"

Person A: "I'm in favour of the move. The new office has more space and is more modern."

Person B: "I'm not sure I agree with you there. The travel time is longer, and it's more expensive."

Person C: "I can see both sides of the argument. Let me think about the costs versus benefits."

Chair: "Let's have a show of hands. All those in favour of the move? Against? Anyone abstaining?"

The Office Move Decision

Your company is considering moving to a new office. You must discuss and vote on this decision.

The Situation

The current office is on the 15th floor of an old building. The lift breaks down frequently. The company wants to move to a modern new office. However, the new office is more expensive, further away, and harder to park at.

Current Office vs. New Office

Current Office Advantages

  • Good location, near public transport
  • Easy to park
  • Cheap rent
  • Close to restaurants and sports facilities

New Office Advantages

  • Great views
  • More space, including a kitchen
  • Modern furniture
  • Accessible for disabled staff

Current Office Disadvantages

  • Lift breaks down frequently
  • No kitchen; staff eat out
  • Near busy road (noise)
  • Needs repainting (requires moving)

New Office Disadvantages

  • Longer travel time for staff
  • Expensive lunch options
  • Much higher rent
  • Difficult to park

Your Role in the Meeting

Choose Your Position

Role A - In Favour of Move: Give strong reasons for moving and suggest solutions to disadvantages.

Role B - Against Move: Argue to stay and offer ways to improve the current office.

Role C - Undecided: Listen to both sides and make your decision during the discussion.

Role D - Chairperson: Listen to arguments and make a casting vote if needed.

Meeting Steps

Step 1: Chairperson opens the discussion: "We need to decide whether to move offices. What are your views?"

Step 2: Role A presents arguments for moving

Step 3: Role B presents arguments against moving

Step 4: Role C asks questions and discusses both perspectives

Step 5: Chairperson calls a vote: "Let's have a show of hands. All in favour of the move?"

Step 6: Vote is counted; if tied, chairperson makes casting vote

Sample Dialogue

Chair: "What are your ideas on moving offices?"

Role A: "I completely support the move. Yes, it's more expensive, but we'd have a better working environment with modern facilities. That improves productivity."

Role B: "I'm not sure I agree with you there. The extra cost is significant, and staff will spend more time commuting."

Role C: "You have a good point about commuting time, but I also see the value in having a modern office with a kitchen."

Chair: "Let's take a vote. Show of hands. In favour of the move?"

Reflection Questions

After your meeting, reflect:

  • Did you clearly present your position?
  • Did you listen to other perspectives?
  • Was the decision fair to everyone?
  • What decision did your group make?
  • Would you have voted the same way?

Good decision-making involves listening, reasoning clearly, and respecting different viewpoints.
