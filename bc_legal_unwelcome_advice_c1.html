Delivering Unwelcome Legal Advice

C1 Level · Law · Advisory Communication

Legal · Dewey 340

First Thoughts

Your client wants to do the deal. You, looking at the facts and the law, can see at least two risks they are underestimating — one of which is potentially company-ending.

You do not want to be the lawyer who says no to everything. But you also cannot let this pass with a soft footnote. How do you deliver the advice in a way they actually hear?

Take a moment with each question below. Share as much detail as you like — specific examples, what you would actually say, what tripped you up last time.

1. When was the last time you had to give advice you knew the client did not want to hear? How did they react?

2. What is the difference between being risk-averse and being risk-aware? Which would your clients say you are?

3. Why do some lawyers get the reputation of “the deal-killer”? Is that ever a fair label — or is it usually about delivery?

4. Can legal advice ever be too direct? What are the costs on either side?

Quick opener — pick whichever you prefer:

  • A partner once said: “If the client ignores your advice, you have to decide in that moment whether you wanted to be heard or to be right.” What do you think they meant?
  • Imagine a client has just sent an email saying “We are going ahead anyway.” Do you reply in writing or pick up the phone?

Key Vocabulary

Eight words and phrases you will actually use in this conversation. Click each card to reveal the meaning, then say the word aloud and try to use it in a sentence from your own experience.

to carve outTo agree that something is kept separate from a general rule or a deal — usually for one party’s benefit.
exposureThe degree to which a party is at risk of legal or financial loss.
to mitigateTo reduce the severity or likelihood of a risk, without fully removing it.
a material riskA risk significant enough to affect the decision or outcome. The word ‘material’ is the legal test.
precedentA prior case or decision that guides current thinking — used both in court and in contract negotiation.
on the recordSaid or written formally, so it can be referenced later. The opposite is off the record.
to ring-fenceTo protect or isolate something (a liability, an asset, a subsidiary) from the rest of the business.
red flagA strong warning sign of risk that requires further investigation or a stop.

Key Language

Useful chunks of language for this conversation. Read each phrase aloud and think about when you would actually reach for it.

Framing before you deliver the advice

  • “Before I give you my view, let me lay out the facts as I am reading them, and you can tell me if we are seeing this the same way.”
  • “I want to share something that is harder to hear than the answer you were probably hoping for.”
  • “I am going to give you my legal view, and then I will tell you what I would personally do in your position — if that is helpful.”

Soft but firm risk language

  • “Our view is that this carries a material risk of regulatory challenge.”
  • “I would not characterise this as a grey area — it is closer to the wrong side of a bright line.”
  • “It is defensible, but it is not comfortable, and you should know the difference.”

Separating legal view from commercial decision

  • “This is a commercial call for you, not a legal call. My job is to make sure you take it with your eyes open.”
  • “There is a path through this, but it requires you to accept a specific risk — and I want to be explicit about what that risk is.”
  • “If you decide to proceed, I would like us to agree now on what we would do if the regulator comes back.”

Preserving the relationship

  • “I want to be the lawyer who tells you the hard thing early, not the polite thing late.”
  • “I understand this is not the advice you wanted. I would rather lose this call than lose your trust.”
  • “If you go ahead, I am with you — and I will write the note that gives you the cleanest defence.”

The Scenario

The situation: You are counsel to a fast-growing European fintech. The CEO wants to launch a new product feature in three more markets before securing formal regulatory approval, on the argument that competitors are doing the same. You believe the approach carries material regulatory risk in at least two of the three markets, and personal exposure for the board.

Your task: You have a thirty-minute call with the CEO and CFO. By the end of it they should understand your advice on the record, what you recommend instead, and what you can and cannot do for them if they proceed anyway.

Step 2 · Deliver

When you are ready, role-play the conversation out loud. Be ready to handle the follow-up questions below.

Follow-up questions to handle:

  • Everyone in the market is doing this — are you telling me we are the only ones getting cold feet?
  • If we did proceed, what is the worst realistic outcome and in what timeframe?
  • Can we ring-fence this in a subsidiary so the group is protected?
  • What is it going to cost us — in fees and time — to do it your way?
