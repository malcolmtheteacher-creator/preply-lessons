The Race Nobody Can Leave

In 2001 a famous medicine, Prozac, suddenly got cheap copies — and its maker lost its money very fast. Today’s AI companies have the same problem every few months. Is that why they can’t stop?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then answer the questions before you read the next part.

Part 1 — The medicine and the cliff

Prozac is a medicine for depression. An American company, Eli Lilly, started selling it at the end of the 1980s.

Making a new medicine costs a lot of money. But for many years, the law protected Prozac. Nobody else could make it. This protection is called a patent.

So Lilly could choose its own price. Prozac made about two and a half billion dollars a year.

In August 2001, the patent ended. From that day, other companies could sell copies of the medicine — at a much lower price.

People bought the copies. In just a few weeks, Lilly’s money from Prozac fell very fast, and it never came back.

Business people call this a patent cliff: the money goes over the edge, like a car off a cliff.

True or false? Click your answer.

1. Nobody else could make Prozac while the patent was active.

2. After 2001, the copies were more expensive than Prozac.

3. Lilly’s money from Prozac came back after a few years.

Talk about it
  • Do you buy cheaper copies of medicines, clothes or food? Are they as good?
  • Was it fair for Lilly to keep its price high for so long? Why or why not?

Part 2 — A cliff every three months

Today, AI companies have the same problem as Lilly — but much faster.

The biggest AI companies spend billions of dollars to make the best AI in the world. When their AI is the best, they can ask a high price.

Here is an example: Claude Opus 4.7, an AI model from the company Anthropic. It came out in April 2026. For about three or four months, the price stayed the same.

Then cheaper competitors, many of them from China, made AI that was almost as good. Anyone can download these models and use them.

The price of that kind of AI fell by about three quarters — from about four dollars to about one dollar. Lilly had fourteen years. The AI company had a few months.

In Europe, about 60% of AI use on one popular website now goes to these cheaper models. One year before, it was about 16%.

So the big AI companies want to stay ahead. They want their engineers to build the next, better model — fast.

True or false? Click your answer.

1. Lilly had more time than the AI companies before cheap copies arrived.

2. The cheaper AI models all came from America.

3. The price of Opus 4.7-level AI fell by about three quarters.

Talk about it
  • Which is more important to you when you choose an app: the best quality or the lowest price?
  • Why do you think the AI companies want to go so fast?

Part 3 — Why don’t they stop?

Some people who run AI companies say that very powerful AI could be dangerous. So why don’t they stop?

The problem is simple. If one company stops, the others don’t. Its customers and its engineers go to the competitors.

In September 2026, Anthropic’s boss, Dario Amodei, wrote a long essay. He did not ask his company to stop. He wanted the same speed rules for all companies, and he wanted the US government to make strong rules against China.

But President Trump sees AI as a race — and he wants America to win it. “Whoever wins AI wins,” he said.

There is one more problem. Cheap AI helps a lot of people. Small companies and countries like the UK use it every day. They want safe AI — but they don’t want one company to win and the prices to go up.

So everybody in the race thinks the same thing: “I don’t like this race. But I don’t want to lose — and I really don’t want them to win.”

Can anyone leave the race? And who can change its rules?

True or false? Click your answer.

1. Amodei asked his own company to stop.

2. President Trump sees AI as a race.

3. Cheap AI helps small companies.

Talk about it
  • Should all AI companies follow the same rules? Who should make them?
  • Tell the story again in six sentences. Use: want someone to… and would like someone to….

What do they want? want / would like / ask + someone + to do

Everyone in this story wants other people to do something. For this, we use a special pattern.

want / would like + person + to + verb
My manager wants me to work on Saturday.
I’d like you to call me tomorrow.

After the verb, use an object word: me, you, him, her, it, us, them — not I, he, she, we, they.
She wants him to help. — not She wants he to help.

Never use that after want: I want you to come. — not I want that you come.

The negative: I don’t want them to win. (= I hope they do not win.)
I want them not to be late. is possible, but I don’t want them to be late is more natural.

The same pattern works with ask, tell and need: The teacher asked us to close our books.

Speaking

Take your time. Short sentences are fine.

1 · Tell the story

Tell me the story in your own words. Six or seven sentences is enough.

2 · What everybody wants

Look at the people in the story: the AI companies, their investors, President Trump, small companies, the customers.

What does each one want? Who do they want to do what?

Use these: The investors want the company to…Small companies don’t want…Customers would like the prices to…Amodei wants governments to…
3 · Your wishes

Think about your work, your town or your country.

Tell me three things you would like other people to do — and one thing you don’t want them to do.

Use these: I’d like my boss to…I want the government to…I don’t want my neighbours to…
4 · The meeting

You are the boss of an AI company. Your investors want you to go faster. Your engineers want you to be more careful.

What do you tell each group? What do you decide?

Use these: I understand that you want us to…I don’t want our company to…We need everybody to…

If we have time

  1. Is it better to have one big company in a market, or many small ones? Why?
  2. Would you use a cheaper AI if it was almost as good? Why or why not?
  3. If everyone stops a race, but one person keeps running, what happens? Can you think of an example from real life?
