At the Restaurant

A booked table, a serious allergy, a dish worth arguing for, and a bill that doesn't quite add up. An ordinary dinner — handled beautifully.

The Scene 🎬

It's your sister's birthday, and you've booked the little Italian place everyone raves about — the one that's impossible to get into. Table for two, eight o'clock, and you got there by ringing three times and being extremely charming.

There's just one small complication: your sister has a nut allergy, the serious kind, and this is a kitchen that puts pine nuts in things you'd never expect. So tonight isn't only about a nice dinner. It's about booking well, asking the right questions before the food arrives, choosing wonderfully — and, at the end, making sure the bill is actually correct without turning the evening sour.

Looking after you: a waiter who is warm, quick, and quietly proud of the kitchen. Treat the staff well and the whole night opens up. Get it wrong and you'll spend the evening apologising to a plate of pesto.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. What's the best meal you've ever eaten out — and was it the food that made it, or the people, the place, the moment?
  2. When something's wrong with your food or your bill in a restaurant, are you the type to speak up straight away, or to say "it's fine" and quietly seethe? Why?

Who's who

You
The Diner 🍷

Hosting your sister's birthday. Warm, appreciative, but paying attention — you want a lovely evening, a safe plate for her, and a bill that adds up.

The other voice
The Waiter 🍽️

Sharp, friendly, house-proud. Happy to check with the kitchen, happy to recommend — and, if you flag a mistake kindly, happy to fix it without a fuss.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Notice how the diner raises the allergy and, later, the billing mistake — never blunt, never a scene, but never letting it slide either. That balance is the whole skill.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how the diner asks for things: never a demand, always an invitation to help.

WaiterGood evening, welcome to Da Marco. Do you have a reservation with us tonight?
YouWe do — a table for two under Bello, for eight o'clock. I'll confess we're a shade early; I hope that's not a nuisance.
WaiterNot in the slightest — your table's ready and waiting. Can I take your coats? This way.
YouLovely, thank you. I don't suppose the little table by the window is free? It's my sister's birthday, and she does love to watch the street.
WaiterFor a birthday? Consider it yours. Here are your menus — can I get you anything to drink while you settle in?
YouA jug of tap water for the table to start, if that's alright — and would you mind giving us a moment with the menu? There's something I ought to flag first, actually.
WaiterOf course. Fire away — is it about the menu?
YouIt is. My sister has a fairly serious nut allergy — the kind we can't take chances with. I don't suppose you could check with the kitchen which dishes are genuinely safe for her?
WaiterAbsolutely, and thank you for telling me — it's much easier before you order than after. I'll speak to the chef directly. Off the top of my head, the pesto's out and one of the desserts, but almost everything else we can adapt.
YouThat's a real weight off, thank you. In that case she'll have the sea bass — and I'm torn between the wild mushroom ragù and the lamb. Which would you steer me towards?
WaiterHonestly? The lamb, every time — slow-cooked for six hours, falls off the bone. And I'll get the sea bass confirmed as nut-free with the chef, don't you worry.
YouSold — the lamb it is. You've made that very easy. (later) That was genuinely superb; please pass our compliments to the chef. Could we get the bill whenever you have a moment? And is service included, or is that left to us?
WaiterI'll pass it on, he'll be delighted. Service isn't included, so that's entirely as you see fit. Here you are.
YouThank you — oh, one small thing before you take the card. I think we might have been charged for a bottle of wine, but we only had the two glasses. Would you mind having a quick look?
WaiterYou're quite right — my apologies, that's the next table's bottle crept onto yours. Let me reprint that for you now.
YouThese things happen — no harm done at all. Thank you for sorting it so gracefully. We'll definitely be back.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The diner mentions the nut allergy before anyone has ordered a thing. Why is the timing so important — and how does the waiter show he agrees it was the right moment?

2. The diner can't decide between two dishes and ends up ordering the lamb. Who actually made the decision, and how did the diner get them to do it?

3. There's a mistake on the bill. How does the diner raise it — and why does the evening stay warm instead of turning into an argument?

1. Because a nut allergy is far safer to solve before the food is cooked than after it's on the plate — the diner flags it while there's still time to change everything. The waiter says so outright: "thank you for telling me — it's much easier before you order than after."
2. The waiter did. The diner admitted they were "torn between" the two dishes and simply asked, "Which would you steer me towards?" — handing the decision to the person who knows the kitchen, then happily taking their advice.
3. Gently — "I think we might have been charged…", "Would you mind having a quick look?" — framing it as a possible slip, not an accusation. The soft wording lets the waiter fix it and even apologise, so nobody loses face and the goodwill survives to "we'll definitely be back."

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that carry you from the booking to the bill — polished enough for the finest restaurant, natural enough for any. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🍽️ Booking & arriving

Phrase 1

You're ringing to book a table, but you don't want to sound as though you're demanding one — you'd rather leave them room to say they're full. Which opening manages that best?

A. "I want a table for two at eight."
B. "Give me a table for two tonight."
C. "I was hoping to book a table for two this evening — do you have anything free around eight?"
C — "I was hoping to book a table for two this evening — do you have anything free around eight?"
"I was hoping to…" softens the whole request into a wish rather than an order, and "do you have anything free" quietly gives them permission to say no. Warmer, and far more likely to get a yes.
Phrase 2

You've arrived a little early and you want the nicest table in the house — the one by the window — without sounding entitled if it's already taken. Put the pieces in order:

the little tableI don't supposeis free?by the window
"I don't suppose the little table by the window is free?"
"I don't suppose…" politely expects a no, so it costs nothing to refuse — and feels like a gift to grant. The perfect way to ask for the better table, the earlier slot, the last portion of anything.

🧾 The menu & allergies

Phrase 3

The waiter is hovering, ready to take your order, but you're not ready — you need to read properly and check a few things first. What do you say to buy that time without waving them away rudely?

"Would you mind giving us a moment with the menu?"
"Would you mind + -ing" turns a "go away" into a courtesy. Note the grammar: mind giving, never "mind to give". Useful cousins: "Could you give us a couple more minutes?" / "We're not quite ready, if that's OK."
Phrase 4

Someone at your table has a serious allergy. You need the kitchen to check properly — not just a shrug and "should be fine". Ask so they take it seriously, but stay warm rather than alarming. What's your line?

"I don't suppose you could check with the kitchen which dishes are genuinely safe for her?"
Asking them to "check with the kitchen" signals you want a real answer, not a guess — and "genuinely safe" quietly raises the stakes without panic. Always flag an allergy before you order, never after.
Phrase 5

A dish on the menu is just a name — you've no idea what actually comes with it. Complete the polite way to ask the waiter to fill in the picture: "Could you tell me a little more ___ the sea bass — what does it come ___?"

"Could you tell me a little more ABOUT the sea bass — what does it come WITH?"
"Tell me a little more about…" invites a proper description, and "what does it come with?" is the natural, idiomatic way to ask about the sides — far better than "what is on the plate?"

🍷 Ordering

Phrase 6

You genuinely can't decide, so let the expert decide for you. Which way of asking for a recommendation sounds most confident and most charming?

A. "What's good here?"
B. "I don't know, you choose for me."
C. "Which would you steer me towards?"
C — "Which would you steer me towards?"
"Steer me towards" flatters the waiter as the guide who knows the way — more elegant than the blunt "what's good here?" (which faintly suggests the rest might be bad). Cousins: "What would you recommend?" / "If you were me, what would you have?"
Phrase 7

You like two dishes equally and want to say so out loud, inviting help. Complete the chunk: "I'm ___ ___ the ragù and the lamb."

"I'm torn between the ragù and the lamb."
"Torn between A and B" = pulled equally two ways — a lovely, natural way to admit you can't choose and to open the door to a recommendation. Note: torn between, and it takes two options, not three.

🛎️ The bill & a quiet problem

Phrase 8

The bill has arrived and you want to know whether a tip is expected or already added, without any awkwardness. What's the smooth, natural way to ask?

"Is service included, or is that left to us?"
One tidy question settles the whole tipping question — "left to us" is the graceful phrase for "up to us to decide". Handy anywhere the tip rules aren't obvious.
Phrase 9

There's a mistake on the bill — you've been charged for something you didn't have. You want it fixed, but you don't want to sound as though you're accusing anyone of cheating you. Which line does that?

A. "This bill is wrong. You've overcharged us."
B. "I think we might have been charged for something we didn't have — would you mind having a quick look?"
C. "Why is this so expensive?"
B — "I think we might have been charged for something we didn't have — would you mind having a quick look?"
"I think we might have been…" frames it as a possible slip, not a crime; "would you mind having a quick look?" hands them the chance to fix it and save face. You get the money back and keep the room warm.
Phrase 10

They've apologised and corrected the bill straight away. You want to wave it off graciously — signal there are no hard feelings — in a few easy words. What do you say?

"These things happen — no harm done."
The perfect gracious brush-off: it forgives the mistake and closes the matter in one breath, so nobody keeps apologising. Relatives: "Don't worry about it at all." / "It's easily done."
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole evening out loud using all ten, in order — booking → the window table → a moment with the menu → the allergy → the questions → the recommendation → the bill → the mistake → the gracious close. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

Your Turn 🎭

Now it's your table. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer to keep a real dinner's easy pace behind you.

Round 1 — you are the diner 🍷

The waiter speaks — you answer. No looking back at the phrases; trust your ear. Remember: warm first, then ask.

WaiterGood evening, welcome to Da Marco. Do you have a reservation with us?
WaiterPerfect, your table's ready. Here are your menus — anything to drink while you decide?
WaiterOf course, take your time. Was there something you wanted to ask?
WaiterLeave it with me — I'll check every dish with the chef. Now, do you know what you'd like, or shall I talk you through a few things?
WaiterExcellent choice, you won't regret it. (later) And how was everything for you this evening?
WaiterSo glad you enjoyed it. Here's your bill — take your time, no rush at all.
WaiterOh — you're absolutely right, my mistake. Let me fix that for you straight away.

Round 2 — now YOU are the waiter 🍽️

Swap chairs. It's your restaurant, your kitchen, your menu — invent the dishes, the specials, the chef's temper. Give the waiter a personality.

DinerGood evening — we've a table booked for two under Bello, for eight o'clock.
DinerThank you. Before we order — my partner has a serious nut allergy. Could you check which dishes are genuinely safe?
DinerThat's very reassuring, thank you. I'm torn between the risotto and the lamb — which would you steer me towards?
DinerSold. And could we get the bill whenever you have a moment — is service included?
DinerSorry — I think we might have been charged for a dessert we didn't order. Would you mind checking?
DinerThese things happen — no harm done. Thank you, it's been a wonderful evening.
The Twist

🌩️ The kitchen slip

One more round — no script, no safety net. The mains arrive, everything looks perfect… and then your sister notices a scatter of pine nuts across her supposedly nut-free sea bass. Nobody has eaten yet. This is the allergy you spent the whole evening guarding against, and it's now sitting on the plate. Start the timer and handle it: get the waiter's attention calmly, make absolutely clear what's happened and why it matters, insist the dish is remade properly — and decide, out loud, how you feel about the rest of the meal, the bill, whether you'd trust the kitchen again tonight. One rule: however alarmed you are, you keep the staff on your side — the instant you humiliate them, the evening is beyond saving.

The Echo 🕰️

Eating out is not a modern habit. In Pompeii, before Vesuvius buried it in AD 79, the streets were lined with thermopolia — counters with sunken clay jars of hot food, the fast-food joints of the Roman world. Many Pompeians had no kitchen at home; they ate on the street, from a stranger who cooked for a living.

One thermopolium, dug out only recently, still had traces of duck, pig, goat, fish and snails in its jars — a menu two thousand years old. On the walls next door, diners scratched their reviews and complaints in graffiti, exactly as we'd leave a star rating today.

So when you book a table, quiz the waiter and settle the bill, you're doing something wonderfully ancient. The people of Pompeii would recognise the whole evening — right down to arguing, politely, over what they'd actually been charged for.
