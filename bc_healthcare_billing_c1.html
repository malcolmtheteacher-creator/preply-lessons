Mediating a Clinical-Billing Dispute

C1 Level · Healthcare Administration · Stakeholder Mediation

Healthcare Admin · Dewey 362.1

First Thoughts

An insurer has declined to fund three nights of inpatient stay because the documentation does not, in their view, justify medical necessity beyond day two. The consultant involved is a senior clinician with little patience for paperwork arguments. The patient is already home and fine; the invoice is not.

You are the administrator who sits between them. The clinician wants you to ‘just fight it’. The insurer wants you to accept the coding as given. Your job is to find a path that protects the trust, the patient, and the relationship with the insurer.

Take a moment with each question below. Share as much detail as you like — specific examples, what you would actually say, what tripped you up last time.

1. When two senior stakeholders disagree and both want you to take their side, what is the first question you need to ask yourself?

2. Why is ‘medical necessity’ one of the hardest boundaries in healthcare administration? Who gets to define it?

3. What is the cost — not in money, but in trust — when a clinician feels administration has not fought their corner?

4. Is there a difference between being impartial and being passive in a dispute like this?

Quick opener — pick whichever you prefer:

  • You have ten minutes before the consultant calls. What is the single sentence you most want to land on with them first?
  • An administrator once said: “My job is not to take sides. My job is to make sure the right facts are in the room.” How would you apply that here?

to reconcile (positions)To find where two opposing views can coexist or compromise, often through shared facts.
to triangulateTo cross-check a claim against more than one source — usually the record, the clinician, and the insurer’s criteria.
clinical codingThe formal translation of a patient’s care into codes that insurers use to calculate payment.
to escalateTo raise an issue to a higher authority when the current level cannot resolve it.
a submissionA formal, written case submitted to an insurer — the main vehicle for challenging a decline.
without prejudiceA phrase used to allow negotiation without it being used as evidence later. Useful in formal correspondence.
a clinical justificationA documented reason, in clinical language, for why a decision was made — e.g. why a patient stayed an extra night.

Key Language

Useful chunks of language for this conversation. Read each phrase aloud and think about when you would actually reach for it.

With the clinician — showing you are on their side without promising

  • “I’ve read the notes, I’ve read the insurer’s reason for declining, and I want to walk you through where I think we have a case — and where we don’t yet.”
  • “My job here is not to lose you the argument. It’s to make sure we bring the argument that is going to win.”
  • “I am going to need something from you that the notes, as they stand, don’t give me — and I want to be specific about what.”

With the clinician — asking for what you need

  • “Can you give me, in three lines, the clinical justification for nights two and three?”
  • “If this went to an external review, what is the single fact about this patient you’d most want that reviewer to know?”
  • “I’d rather spend ten minutes with you now than submit something that comes back declined again in three weeks.”

With the insurer — firm but procedural

  • “I’d like to submit additional clinical justification and ask you to reconsider the decision on nights two and three.”
  • “I understand your criteria, and I want to make sure the documentation we provide meets them rather than argues around them.”
  • “Before we move to a formal appeal, I’d like to explore whether a supplementary submission resolves this at case-handler level.”

Holding the middle — with either side

  • “I can’t speak for the insurer, but I can tell you what their criteria actually say and where we sit against them.”
  • “I’m not going to pretend the case is stronger than it is — but I do think there is a reasonable argument here worth putting.”
  • “What I don’t want is for us to dig in on a position that a reviewer won’t support — that costs us more than it saves us.”

The Scenario

The situation: A three-night inpatient stay, private insurer, consultant-led. The insurer has funded night one but declined nights two and three, citing insufficient documentation of medical necessity. The consultant is senior, respected, and has written a curt email asking you to “just push back on them”. The insurer’s case handler is professional but firm. The patient is recovered and uninvolved. Total disputed amount is material but not catastrophic.

Your task: Do two conversations back-to-back. First, a ten-minute call with the consultant — get the clinical justification you actually need. Second, a short, firm, written submission to the insurer — without prejudice. You are not there to win the argument today; you are there to make the argument winnable.
