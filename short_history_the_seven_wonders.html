The Seven Wonders of the Ancient World

Seven fabled monuments the ancients agreed were unmissable — and how six of them vanished from the Earth.

Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Pilgrims and merchants crossed whole seas to reach the great temple, and over the centuries it became one of the most famous monuments in the known world.
a monument (n) — a large structure built to honour a person, a god or an event, or simply to be remembered.
2. The old lighthouse still stood, but earthquake after earthquake had left it a crumbling ruin, half its stones scattered across the harbour.
a ruin (n) — the remains of a building that has been destroyed or fallen apart, usually over a long time.
3. No stone of the palace remains, so historians must rely on a few written accounts by travellers who claimed to have seen it with their own eyes.
an account (n) — a written or spoken description of an event by someone who reports what happened.
4. Historians are still not sure the gardens ever existed; some believe the whole thing may be a beautiful myth, passed from writer to writer.
a myth (n) — a traditional story, or a widely held but false belief, that may not be based on fact.
5. To build a statue that tall, engineers had to construct an iron frame inside it and pour bronze around it, plate by plate.
to construct (v) — to build something large by putting parts together.
6. A single violent earthquake can bring down in seconds what took thousands of workers a generation to raise.
an earthquake (n) — a sudden, violent shaking of the ground caused by movement deep inside the Earth.
7. The tomb was so richly decorated that visitors described it as one of the marvels of the age — a true masterpiece of the sculptor's art.
a masterpiece (n) — a work of outstanding skill, the finest example of what someone can make.
8. When the empire fell, the great bronze figure was broken up and its metal was plundered, carried off to be melted down and sold.
to plunder (v) — to steal valuable things from a place, especially by force during war or after a disaster.
9. From fifty kilometres out at sea, sailors could see the fire at the top of the tower; it was the tallest structure humans had built for their own use.
a structure (n) — anything that has been built, especially something large like a tower, bridge or building.
10. The Pyramid has endured for four and a half thousand years, outlasting every other wonder on the list — and every empire that ruled beside it.
to endure (v) — to last or survive for a very long time, despite difficulty.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Who drew up the list

The idea of "seven wonders" was not handed down by the gods; it was a travellers' guidebook. In the centuries after Alexander the Great, Greek writers began listing the sights that any educated visitor to the eastern Mediterranean simply had to see. The most famous list settled on seven — a number the Greeks loved — and it is usually credited to a poet named Antipater of Sidon, writing around 100 BC. Later a short handbook, once attributed to a mathematician called Philon of Byzantium, described the marvels for readers who would never travel that far.

Every wonder on the canonical list lay within reach of the Greek world: the Great Pyramid of Giza in Egypt; the Hanging Gardens of Babylon; the Statue of Zeus at Olympia; the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus; the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus; the Colossus of Rhodes; and the Lighthouse of Alexandria. They were not chosen for being old or holy so much as for being astonishing — the biggest, the tallest, the most lavish things human hands had made. To stand before one was said to change how a person understood what people were capable of.

There is one enormous catch. Of the seven, only a single wonder can still be visited today. The rest are known to us almost entirely through words — ancient descriptions, a handful of coins and carvings, and educated guesswork. So the "seven wonders" are, in a real sense, a list of things we have mostly lost.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What kind of list was it originally, and roughly when and by whom was the famous version drawn up?

2. Why were these particular structures chosen — and what is the "enormous catch"?

1. Essentially a travellers' guidebook to the must-see sights of the Greek-speaking Mediterranean; the famous version is credited to the poet Antipater of Sidon, writing around 100 BC.

2. They were chosen for being astonishing — the biggest, tallest and most lavish human creations. The catch is that only one of the seven still survives; the rest are known mostly through ancient writings.

Discuss:
  • The ancient list was really a piece of tourism marketing. If you had to choose seven "wonders" of the modern world, what would decide whether something made the list?

Part 2 — Seven marvels, one survivor

Start with the one you can still touch. The Great Pyramid of Giza, built for the pharaoh Khufu around 2560 BC, is by far the oldest wonder and the only one left standing. For nearly four thousand years it was the tallest structure on Earth. The others were younger, and far more fragile. The Hanging Gardens of Babylon — terraces said to be planted with trees and watered by hidden machinery in the desert — are the great mystery: no ruins have ever been securely identified, and some scholars quietly doubt they existed at all.

Three of the wonders were destroyed by human hands or slow neglect. The Temple of Artemis at Ephesus, a forest of marble columns, was burned down, rebuilt, and finally left to decay. The towering Statue of Zeus at Olympia, a seated god of ivory and gold, was lost when the games that honoured him were banned and his temple fell silent. The Mausoleum at Halicarnassus — the tomb so grand it gave us the very word "mausoleum" — stood for centuries before earthquakes cracked it apart and its fine stone was carried away for other buildings.

The last two were killed by the ground itself. The Colossus of Rhodes, a bronze giant of the sun-god that stood over the harbour, survived barely fifty years before an earthquake snapped it at the knees and toppled it. And the Lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the tallest buildings in the ancient world, guided ships with its fire for centuries until a series of earthquakes finally brought it down into the sea. Six wonders, six different endings — war, fire, banning, neglect, and again and again, the earthquake.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which wonder is the only survivor, and what makes it exceptional?

2. Name two wonders brought down by earthquakes, and one whose very existence is doubted.

1. The Great Pyramid of Giza — the oldest of the seven, the only one still standing, and for nearly four thousand years the tallest structure on Earth.

2. The Colossus of Rhodes and the Lighthouse of Alexandria were both destroyed by earthquakes; the existence of the Hanging Gardens of Babylon is doubted, as no ruins have been securely found.

Discuss:
  • Six of the seven were destroyed, yet we still talk about them constantly. Can a building that no longer exists still be "wonderful" — or do we just love the story of it?

Part 3 — How we know, and why they still matter

Because six wonders are gone, almost everything we say about them comes second-hand. Ancient authors described their size and splendour, sometimes in numbers that were surely exaggerated; a few appear on old coins; archaeologists have found column bases at Ephesus and blocks of the Lighthouse on the seabed off Alexandria. From these scraps, scholars reconstruct what the monuments are thought to have looked like — which is why so much about them is phrased with careful caution: it is said, it is believed, it was reported to be. The wonders live now in the passive voice.

That uncertainty is part of why the list has lasted for more than two thousand years. Every age has redrawn it: there are now "new" seven wonders chosen by public vote, "natural" wonders, and countless imitations. The original seven set the template — the idea that human achievement can be collected, ranked and shown off — and that idea proved far more durable than the buildings themselves.

And there is a quiet lesson in the survivor. The Great Pyramid outlasted the others not because it was the most beautiful, but because it was the most solid: a mountain of stone with nothing inside to burn, no thin bronze to melt, no tall thin tower to snap. The wonders that dazzled fastest often fell fastest. The one that simply endured is the one we can still walk up to and touch.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What kinds of evidence do scholars actually have for the six lost wonders?

2. Why has the idea of the seven wonders lasted even longer than the buildings?

1. Ancient written descriptions (often exaggerated), images on old coins, and archaeological remains such as column bases at Ephesus and blocks of the Lighthouse found on the seabed.

2. The list created a lasting template — the idea that human achievement can be collected, ranked and celebrated — which every later age has copied with new lists of wonders.

Discuss:
  • The Pyramid survived because it was solid rather than spectacular. Is there a wider lesson there — do the flashiest achievements tend to last the least?

Grammar — the reported (impersonal) passive

Almost everything we say about the six lost wonders is second-hand: we can't name who claims it, and we can't prove it. English has an elegant set of structures for exactly this — for reporting what people in general think or say, without committing to it as fact. The wonders practically demand this grammar.

The two main patterns:
• It + is/was + reporting verb (passive) + that… — the impersonal opener: "It is said that the gardens were watered by hidden machines." "It was believed that the statue could see the whole stadium." Common verbs: said, believed, thought, claimed, reported, rumoured, known.
• Subject + is/was + reporting verb (passive) + to + infinitive — the tighter, more formal version: "The gardens are said to have been watered by machines." "The statue was believed to be forty metres tall."
Choosing the infinitive — same time or earlier?
• For something happening at the same time as the reporting, use a plain infinitive: "The Colossus is thought to stand over the harbour" (or, for a past state, "was thought to stand").
• For something earlier than the reporting, use the perfect infinitive to have + past participle: "The Lighthouse is believed to have survived for centuries." "The gardens are said to have been built by a king for his homesick queen."

1. Rewrite with It is/was … that …

Turn each plain sentence into the impersonal passive, using the verb in brackets. Keep the tense of the reporting verb the same as the cue.

1. People say the Hanging Gardens never really existed. → the Hanging Gardens never really existed. (say — present)

2. In antiquity, everyone believed the Colossus was one of the tallest statues on Earth. → In antiquity, the Colossus was one of the tallest statues on Earth. (believe — past)

3. Ancient writers reported that the Lighthouse could be seen from far out at sea. → the Lighthouse could be seen from far out at sea. (report — past)

1. It is said that (present impersonal passive)

2. it was believed that (past impersonal passive)

3. It was reported that (past impersonal passive)

2. Rewrite with subject + to + infinitive

Now say the same idea the tighter way. Choose a plain infinitive or the perfect infinitive (to have + past participle) — watch whether the event is at the same time or earlier.

1. It is believed that the Mausoleum inspired later royal tombs. → The Mausoleum inspired later royal tombs. (earlier event)

2. It was thought that the Statue of Zeus was made of ivory and gold. → The Statue of Zeus made of ivory and gold. (state at the same time)

3. It is said that a queen ordered the Hanging Gardens to be planted. → A queen ordered the Hanging Gardens to be planted. (earlier event)

1. is believed to have (perfect infinitive — earlier than the reporting)

2. was thought to be (plain infinitive — a state at the same time)

3. is said to have (perfect infinitive — earlier than the reporting)

3. Tell it like a cautious historian — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap so the paragraph reports the claims without stating them as certain fact. Use a mix of both patterns. Read it aloud once you're done.

The Hanging Gardens of Babylon the most mysterious of the seven wonders. It that a king built them for his homesick queen. The terraces been watered by an ingenious hidden machine — though no ruins have ever been found, so it that the gardens were never real at all.

Sample answers: are said to be (the most mysterious) · is believed / is said (that a king built them) · are thought to have (been watered) · is even claimed / is sometimes said (that the gardens were never real).

Notice the caution the grammar builds in: every claim is reported, never asserted — exactly how we have to talk about a mostly vanished past.
