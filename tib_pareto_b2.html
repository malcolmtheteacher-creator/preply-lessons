The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — The engineer who counted incomes

Vilfredo Pareto was born in Paris in 1848, to an Italian father and a French mother. He trained as an engineer in Turin and spent years managing railways and ironworks in Italy. He was clever, argumentative and very interested in politics — so interested that, in his forties, he changed careers completely and became an economist.

In 1893 he became a professor at the University of Lausanne, in Switzerland. There, he did something unusual for the time: instead of just arguing about how wealth should be shared, he collected numbers about how it was shared. He studied tax and income records from different countries and different centuries.

What he found surprised him. Again and again, the distribution had the same lopsided shape. A small proportion of the people had a very large share of the wealth, and the vast majority had very little. In 1896 and 1897, he published his results in a book called Cours d'économie politique.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

2. What was unusual about his method?

3. What pattern did he find?

1. He was an engineer (railways and ironworks).

2. He collected real numbers — tax and income records — instead of only arguing about how wealth should be shared.

3. In many countries and centuries, a small proportion of people held a very large share of the wealth.

Part 2 — A legend and a number

The famous version of the story says that Pareto found that 20% of the people in Italy owned 80% of the land. Many books add a charming detail: that he first noticed the pattern in his garden, when he saw that 20% of his pea plants produced 80% of the peas. There is no good evidence for the garden story. It is probably a legend added later, because it makes the idea easy to remember.

Pareto himself was interested in wealth, not in a general rule for everything. He died in 1923, long before his name became attached to the phrase “the 80/20 rule”.

That happened because of a quality expert named Joseph Juran. Born in 1904 in what is now Romania, Juran moved to the United States as a boy and grew up poor in Minnesota. He became an engineer at Western Electric, a huge telephone factory near Chicago, where he inspected products for defects.

1. That 20% of the people in Italy owned 80% of the land.

2. Probably not — there's no good evidence; it's likely a later legend.

3. At Western Electric, a big telephone factory near Chicago.

Part 3 — The vital few

Juran noticed that factory problems were lopsided too. A handful of causes produced the vast majority of the defects. If a factory fixed those few causes first, it could solve most of its problems quickly. In the 1940s, looking for a name for this idea, he remembered Pareto's charts of wealth and called it the Pareto principle. He described it as “the vital few and the trivial many”.

Years later, Juran admitted that he had stretched things. Pareto had never stated a universal rule; Juran had given him credit for a much bigger idea than he had actually proposed. Juran also changed his own phrase to “the vital few and the useful many”, because the “many” small causes still matter.

Today, the 80/20 rule is everywhere. Shops find that a small share of their products bring in most of their money. Software companies have found that fixing a small share of the bugs removes most of the crashes. Coaches, doctors and teachers use it too. But the numbers are a rule of thumb, not a law of nature: the real split might be 70/30 or 95/5, and the two numbers don't have to add up to 100.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did Juran notice about factory defects?

1. A handful of causes produced most of the defects.

2. Because the many small causes still matter; they aren't worthless.

3. No — it's a rule of thumb; the real split can be 70/30, 95/5 and so on.

How the tool works

A Pareto chart sorts causes from biggest to smallest. The bars show each cause; the red line adds them up as you go. Where the line crosses 80%, you have found your vital few. Here, 100 complaints at a small café:

Using it, step by step

  1. Collect real data: complaints, faults, sales, hours — whatever you want to improve.
  2. Group it into causes and sort from biggest to smallest.
  3. Add the percentages as you go. Stop when you reach about 80%.
  4. Fix those vital few first. Then look again — the chart will have changed.

Try it yourself

A small online clothes shop had 200 returns last month. Which causes are the vital few (together about 80%), and which are the useful many? Add them up, then choose.

1. Wrong size — 110 returns
2. Customer changed their mind — 12 returns
3. Damaged in the post — 16 returns
4. Colour looked different online — 50 returns
5. Arrived late — 10 returns
6. Poor quality — 2 returns

1. Vital few — the biggest cause, 55% on its own.

2. Useful many — only 6%.

3. Useful many — 8% — the third-biggest cause, but outside the top two.

4. Vital few — 25%; with “wrong size” it makes 160 returns = 80%.

5. Useful many — 5%.

6. Useful many — only 1% — although, if the quality problem is serious, it may still deserve attention.

Where the tool breaks

It's not a law. The split changes from case to case; don't force your data into 80/20.

Small causes can be serious. One rare safety problem may matter more than a hundred common annoyances.

It can be used unkindly. “Twenty per cent of customers bring eighty per cent of the profit” can lead a company to ignore everyone else.

  • When would following the rule be a mistake?

Grammar — quantifiers and proportions

The 80/20 rule is all about how much and how many. At B2, you need a wide range of phrases to describe proportions — not only percentages.

Large parts: the (vast) majority of, most (of the), almost all (of the), nearly everyone.
“The vast majority of voters stayed at home.”  Use most without of the for general statements: “Most teenagers own a phone.”
Small parts: a (small) minority of, a handful of, a few / few, a little / little, hardly any.
“Only a handful of shops were open.”  A few = some (positive); few = not many (negative): “Few people understood the plan.”
Fractions and multiples: a quarter of, a third of, half (of) the, twice as many / much as, three times as many as.
“The new bus carries twice as many passengers as the old one.”
Agreement: the verb agrees with the noun after of.
“The majority of the money is spent on rent.”  “The majority of the students are from abroad.”
