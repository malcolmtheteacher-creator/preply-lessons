Working the Room

The real conference doesn't happen in the sessions. It happens by the coffee, in the ten minutes between them — where a stranger and a good question can change your year.

The Scene 🎬

The morning session has just ended and two hundred people are pouring into the foyer for coffee. This is the part of the conference that actually matters — and, for most people, the part they secretly dread. The talks you can sit through in silence. This you can't. Here you have to walk up to a stranger, open your mouth, and be interesting, in English, with a cup of lukewarm coffee in your hand.

The good news: everyone else in the room is feeling exactly the same. That person standing alone by the window isn't unapproachable — they're relieved you came over. Working a room isn't about being slick or salesy. It's four small skills: opening easily, saying who you are in one clear line, being genuinely curious about the other person, and — the one nobody teaches — getting out of a conversation kindly so you can meet the next person.

Standing near the coffee, half-looking at their phone so as not to seem alone: another delegate, roughly as nervous as you, hoping someone will make the first move.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Networking: energising or exhausting? Be honest. What's the single worst moment for you — walking up, the small talk, saying what you do, or escaping at the end?
  2. Think of someone you've met who's brilliant at making a stranger feel comfortable in seconds. What exactly do they do? Could you borrow one thing of theirs this week?

Who's who

You
The One Making the Move 🤝

Slightly nervous, quietly determined. You're here to meet three or four people worth staying in touch with — which means opening easily, being interested, and moving on gracefully.

The other voice
A Fellow Delegate ☕

Friendly once someone breaks the ice, and just as unsure how to start. Give them an easy way in and they'll happily talk — they were hoping you'd come over.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the easy opener, the one-line answer to "what do you do?", and the warm, unhurried exit.

YouIs the coffee any good, or should I lower my expectations? Mind if I join you?
DelegateHa — please do. It's… conference coffee. You get what you pay for. Are you enjoying it so far?
YouReally am, actually — the talk on supply chains this morning gave me a lot to think about. How are you finding it? Is this your first time here?
DelegateThird year running, believe it or not. It's the one event I always make time for. What about you — what brings you along?
YouI'm Sofia, by the way. I work in logistics — basically, I help mid-size companies move their products across borders without losing their minds or their money.
DelegateMarco. That's a good way to put it. So you must have felt the last couple of years, then — all the disruption?
YouYou have no idea. Oh — how did you get into your side of it, though? You said you're on the software side?
DelegateCompletely by accident, honestly. I trained as an engineer and sort of fell into it. We build the tracking tools half your clients are probably shouting about.
YouThat's funny you say that — a client was raving about exactly that kind of tool last week. We should compare notes properly; I'd love to hear how you see the market moving.
DelegateDefinitely. Shall we swap details? I'll be honest, I never remember to follow up, so pin me down.
YouConsider yourself pinned. I'll drop you a line next week — and I promise it won't be a sales email. Listen, I don't want to monopolise you — I know you're here to meet people. But this has been the best conversation of the morning.
DelegateLikewise, genuinely. Go and brave the room — and enjoy the terrible coffee. Talk soon, Sofia.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Look at the very first line. Why open with a little joke about the coffee rather than "Hello, my name is Sofia and I work in logistics"?

2. When asked "what brings you along?", Sofia answers in one line — "I help mid-size companies move their products across borders." Why is that better than listing her job title and duties?

3. Sofia ends the conversation herself, while it's still going well. Why leave on a high — and what two things does she do to make the exit warm rather than abrupt?

1. A shared, low-stakes joke (the bad coffee) is an easy, human door in — it gives the other person something effortless to react to and signals "I'm friendly, not selling." Names come naturally a moment later; the ice has to break first.
2. "I help X do Y" is instantly clear, memorable and about the value she brings, not her org chart. A job title makes people nod politely; a vivid one-liner makes them ask a follow-up question — which is exactly what happened.
3. Leaving on a high means the conversation is remembered as a good one, and it respects that you're both there to circulate. She makes it warm by (i) locking in the follow-up ("I'll drop you a line next week") and (ii) giving a genuine compliment ("the best conversation of the morning") — so the exit feels like a gift, not an escape.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that turn a room full of strangers into a handful of real contacts. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Warmth carries further than words. Networking language is simple on purpose — what makes it land is a smile you can hear, an unhurried pace, and real interest in your eyes. Say these lightly, like you've got all the time in the world (even in a ten-minute break), and people relax into you. A warm "how are you finding it?" beats a slick pitch every time.

☕ Breaking the ice

Phrase 1

You spot someone standing alone and want to approach without it feeling heavy or awkward. Which opener is the easiest to say yes to?

A. "Hello. I would like to network with you."
B. "Mind if I join you?"
C. "Are you important? Should I know you?"
B — "Mind if I join you?"
Light, warm, and almost impossible to refuse. It asks a tiny permission and instantly makes you a pair rather than a stranger looming. A is stiff and states the obvious; C is a joke that could easily misfire. Cousins: "Is this seat taken?", "Room for one more?"
Phrase 2

You've joined them; now you need a first question that's easy for anyone to answer and gets them talking. Complete the chunk: "How are you ___ ___ so far?"

"How are you finding it so far?"
The perfect low-effort opener — everyone has an opinion on the event they're at, so it always gets a reply. "Finding it" asks for their experience, not just facts. Cousins: "Is this your first time here?", "Have you been to any good sessions?"

🪪 Saying who you are — in one line

Phrase 3

You want to introduce your name naturally, mid-conversation, not as a stiff announcement. There's a two-word tag that slips it in casually: "I'm Sofia, ___ ___ ___."

"I'm Sofia, by the way."
"By the way" makes the introduction feel relaxed and offhand rather than formal — you're just mentioning it as you go. It's the natural way names get exchanged once the conversation's already warm.
Phrase 4

The big one: "So, what do you do?" Don't recite your job title — give a one-line answer built on the value you bring. The formula is "I help [who] [do what]." In your own words, say what YOU do this way.

"I help [who] [do what]." → e.g. "I help mid-size companies move their products across borders without losing their money."
This beats a job title every time: it's clear, memorable, and about them-style value, so it invites a follow-up question instead of a polite nod. Keep it to one breath. Warm, concrete, a little human — never a list of duties.

🔎 Being genuinely curious

Phrase 5

They've told you what they do. The best networkers now get curious about the story, not the job. Put this warm follow-up in order:

get intohow did youthat?interesting —oh,
"Oh, interesting — how did you get into that?"
People light up when asked about their own path. "How did you get into that?" invites a story, not a fact, and stories are where real conversations — and real connections — happen. Cousins: "What do you enjoy most about it?", "What's the hardest part?"
Phrase 6

They say something that genuinely connects to your world. You want to build a bridge and show it's not a coincidence you're talking. Which reaction connects best?

A. "Okay."
B. "That's funny you say that, because a client mentioned exactly that last week."
C. "Anyway, back to me."
B — "That's funny you say that, because…"
It finds common ground and makes the other person feel the connection is real and mutual. Shared reference points are the glue of networking. A kills the momentum; C is (hopefully) a joke, but not one to risk.

🔗 Swapping details & a graceful exit

Phrase 7

The conversation's good and you'd like to stay in touch. What's a natural, low-pressure way to suggest exchanging contact details?

"Shall we swap details?"
Light and mutual — "swap" makes it an equal exchange, not you asking a favour. Cousins: "Are you on LinkedIn?", "Let's stay in touch — what's the best way to reach you?"
Phrase 8

You want to promise a follow-up so it actually happens — and reassure them it won't be a hard sell. Complete it: "I'll ___ ___ ___ next week — and I promise it won't be a sales email."

"I'll drop you a line next week."
"Drop you a line" is warm and casual for "send you a short message" — far friendlier than "I will contact you." Naming when ("next week") makes it a real promise, not a polite nothing. The follow-up is where networking actually pays off.
Phrase 9

Time to move on — but kindly, so it feels considerate rather than like an escape. What phrase lets you leave a good conversation gracefully?

"I don't want to monopolise you…"
To "monopolise" someone is to keep them all to yourself. Saying you don't want to frames your exit as thoughtful — you're freeing them to meet others — rather than a brush-off. Cousins: "I should let you mingle…", "I've kept you long enough…"
Phrase 10

Land the goodbye so they remember the conversation warmly. Which closing line does that best?

A. "Okay, bye."
B. "This has been the best conversation of the morning — talk soon."
C. "Right, I need to find someone more useful."
B — "This has been the best conversation of the morning — talk soon."
A genuine, specific compliment makes the whole exchange land as memorable and warm, and "talk soon" keeps the door open. A is flat; C is a joke far too easy to take the wrong way.

Now you're in the foyer, coffee in hand. Speak your answers out loud FIRST, then type them. Use the timer — a coffee break is short, and so is the window to make a connection.

You approach a delegate standing alone. They respond — you keep it going. No looking back at the phrases; trust your ear. Remember: open easy → who you are in one line → get curious → exit warm.

The Twist

🐟 The one you actually came to meet

One more round — no script, no safety net. Across the room is the person you most wanted to meet: a keynote speaker, a potential client, someone whose work you genuinely admire. They're just finishing a conversation, and you have about sixty seconds before someone else grabs them. Start the timer and go: approach, open, say who you are and why you'd love to talk — memorably, warmly, and without gushing or freezing — and try to land a real next step (a coffee, a call, an introduction). One rule: no grovelling and no fake confidence — just be a genuine, interesting person who's worth their time.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I opened easily, without it feeling heavy or scripted.
  • I said who I am in one clear, human line — not a job title and a list of duties.
  • I got genuinely curious about them and asked about their story.
  • I found a real point of common ground.
  • I exited warmly and set up a genuine follow-up.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target for the conference — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

We call it "networking" and the word makes everyone's skin crawl — it sounds like collecting business cards from people you'll never think about again. But strip away the jargon and it's just this: being curious about other people, and letting them be curious about you.

The myth is that some people are "natural networkers" born with a gift. They're not. They've simply learned that the pressure isn't on you to be impressive — it's on you to be interested. Ask the good question, listen properly, and the other person walks away thinking you were the fascinating one. That's the whole trick.

And the value almost never lands in the conversation itself. It lands in the follow-up — the short message, a week later, from someone who actually remembered. Be the person who sends it.

Two bigger questions

  1. Is there really such a thing as a "natural" at meeting people, or is it a set of habits anyone can learn? What would change for you if you believed it was learnable?
  2. The most valuable professional relationships often start with the most trivial small talk — bad coffee, a delayed train. Why do big things so often grow from small, throwaway moments? And does that change how seriously you'll take them?
