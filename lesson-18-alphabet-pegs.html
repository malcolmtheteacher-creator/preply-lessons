B, D, E, P, T and V sound the same

Picture a pilot over the sea, a controller on the ground, and a crackling radio. The pilot needs to spell the name of an airfield. She says the letters. The controller hears B, D, E, G, P, T and V, and cannot tell which is which. They all sound like the same short noise, bee, dee, ee, gee, pee, tee, vee, and an error here could be dangerous.

Radio operators had faced this since the early days of wireless. In the Second World War the Allies used a word for each letter: Able, Baker, Charlie, Dog, Easy. It worked for English speakers. But after the war, aviation became international, and a code that only works in English is not enough.

What was the problem with spoken letters on a radio?
Many letters sound alike over a noisy line, so a single letter can be mistaken for another.
Talk about it. Have you ever had to spell your name over the phone? What did you say for the difficult letters?

In the early 1950s the aviation authorities began to design a new alphabet. They asked linguists to help; among them was a Canadian, Jean-Paul Vinay. The new words had to be easy to say and to recognise for speakers of English, French and Spanish, with a clear and different sound for each.

They tested many words. Some were dropped because speakers of one language mispronounced them. In 1956 the final list was adopted, and it is still used today by pilots, ships, police, armies and call-centre workers: Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta, Echo, Foxtrot, and on through Zulu. Two of the spellings, Alfa and Juliett, are unusual on purpose, to show French and other speakers how to say them.

What was special about the words in the final alphabet?
They were chosen to be clear and easy to recognise for speakers of English, French and Spanish.
Talk about it. Why do you think the designers asked a linguist, and not only a pilot, to help?

Look at the list again. It is twenty-six fixed words in a fixed order, which everyone knows, each with a picture or a sound that is easy to see. That is exactly what a memory peg is. If A is Alfa, B is Bravo, C is Charlie, you have twenty-six places in order, and you can hang any list or any set of steps on them.

So: you already know the order. All you need is a picture for each word. Then you have a second, longer set of pegs, to go with the ten body pegs, and with no building.

Why are the code words good pegs?
They are twenty-six fixed words, in a fixed order, that many people already know.
Talk about it. How many of the twenty-six words can you say now? Try the first ten.

A procedure: mending a puncture

Eight steps on eight pegs: A: take the wheel off. B: take out the inner tube. C: find the hole. D: dry the tube. E: put on the patch. F: check the tyre for sharp things. G: put the tube back. H: pump the tyre up. Each step is an action on its peg: an alpha-wolf removes a huge wheel; a crowd shouts 'Bravo!' at an inner tube pulled from the tyre like a snake.
