The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — What managers really noticed

In the 1960s Francis Aguilar, a researcher at Harvard, set out to answer a simple question: how do managers find out what is happening in the world outside their company? In in-depth interviews, he talked to 137 managers from 41 companies, mostly in the chemical industry.

The results surprised him. More than half of the information the managers mentioned was what he called “market tidings” — news about their markets — even though most of them didn't work in marketing. Wider issues in the world around them seemed to be of little importance. And most of their information came not from reports or publications, but from personal contacts — people they knew.

In 1967 he published his findings as Scanning the Business Environment. He introduced the idea of “environmental scanning”: searching the world outside the company systematically, not just by chance.

1. How managers find out what is happening outside their company.

2. “Market tidings” — news about their markets.

3. Personal contacts — people they knew.

Part 2 — From ETPS to PESTLE

The framework later known as PEST is usually traced back to Aguilar's book. As the story is usually told, he grouped the outside world into four areas in a different order: Economic, Technical, Political and Social — ETPS. Later writers rearranged the letters into PEST: Political, Economic, Social, Technological.

Then the letters multiplied. Legal and Environmental factors were added to make PESTLE. Other versions include STEEP and STEEPLE, and military planners use a much longer one, PMESII-PT. Each new letter is an attempt to make sure that nothing important is forgotten.

Today the framework is used far beyond business. Researchers have applied PESTLE to subjects as different as coastal management in India and the biofuel industry in Europe.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What were the four areas, as the story is usually told?

2. What did the L and the E add?

3. Name two other versions of the framework.

1. Economic, Technical, Political and Social (ETPS).

2. Legal and Environmental factors.

3. Any two: PEST, STEEP, STEEPLE, PMESII-PT.

Part 3 — A list is not a forecast

PESTLE has well-known weaknesses. It is much better at explaining what has already happened than at predicting what will happen next. And it is easy to fill in badly: people write long lists of factors under each heading without asking which ones really matter, or how they affect each other.

There is also an irony. Aguilar's own research showed that managers paid most attention to their markets and relied on the people they knew. A tidy six-box chart doesn't change those habits on its own. A team can complete a perfect PESTLE analysis in a planning meeting — and then forget about it.

Used well, the tool is less about the boxes than about the conversation it starts: what is changing out there, what will it mean for us, and what are we going to do about it?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What is PESTLE better at: explaining the past or predicting the future?

2. What mistake do people make when filling it in?

3. What is the irony in Aguilar's research?

1. Explaining the past.

2. They list lots of factors without asking which matter most or how they connect.

3. Managers relied on market news and personal contacts — habits a tidy chart doesn't change on its own.

How the tool works

Six headings for the world outside an organisation. Under each one, list the changes that could affect it — then decide which matter most.

Using it, step by step

  1. Choose the organisation and the time frame: for example, a café in your town over the next three years.
  2. Under each heading, list changes outside the organisation that could affect it — not its own strengths or problems.
  3. Mark the two or three that matter most, and ask how they connect: a new law may come from a social change.
  4. For each important factor, decide what you'll do: prepare, adapt, or keep an eye on it.

Try it yourself

A company that runs a chain of gyms is doing a PESTLE analysis. Which heading does each factor belong under?

1. More people are wearing fitness trackers and following online classes at home.
2. Energy prices have doubled, making the pools very expensive to heat.
3. Hotter summers mean fewer people want to exercise indoors in July.
4. A new law requires every gym to have a trained first-aider on duty.
5. The population is ageing, and older customers want gentler classes.
6. The government plans tax breaks for employers who pay for staff gym memberships.

1. Technological — new technology is changing how people exercise.

2. Economic — costs and prices.

3. Environmental — climate and weather.

4. Legal — a specific legal requirement.

5. Social — a demographic and lifestyle change.

6. Political — a government policy decision.

Where the tool breaks

A list isn't an analysis. Long lists under six headings tell you little until you decide what matters most.

Better at hindsight. It explains the past more easily than it predicts the future.

The boxes overlap. Is a carbon tax political, economic, legal or environmental? Arguing about the box wastes time.

  • Do a quick PESTLE for your own job or school. Which heading has the biggest changes?
  • Which factor is hardest to predict, and why?

Grammar — future continuous and future perfect

PESTLE is about looking ahead. To describe the world at a point in the future, English has two useful tenses: one for actions in progress then, and one for actions finished by then.

Future continuous (will be + -ing) — an action in progress at a time in the future.
“In 2030, more people will be working from home.”  “This time next week, I'll be lying on a beach.”
Future perfect (will have + past participle) — an action completed before a time in the future.
“By 2030, the new rules will have come into force.”  “By Friday, we'll have finished the report.”
by + time is the classic partner of the future perfect: by then, by next year, by the time you arrive.
“By the time the shop opens, prices will have gone up.”
Both can be softened with probably, may, might, could.
“Electric cars will probably have become cheaper.”  “We might be paying more for water.”
Watch out: after by the time / when, use the present, not will: ~~By the time you will arrive~~ → “By the time you arrive, we'll have left.”
