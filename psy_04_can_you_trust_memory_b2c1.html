Can You Trust Your Memory?

If a single word in a question can change what you remember, what is a memory worth?

← All the Psychology Questions

Today: you'll explain why memory is reconstructive, and use modals of speculation (must have, may have, can't have) to reason about what happened.
Warm up — have a go, and don't worry about mistakes. Say whatever comes to mind.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — One word

In 1974, the psychologist Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed students films of traffic accidents and then asked them questions. The key question was how fast the cars had been going. But the verb varied. Some students were asked how fast the cars were going when they 'hit' each other; others when they 'smashed' into each other; and so on.

The results were clear. The average estimate was about 41 miles per hour when the verb was 'smashed', against about 32 miles per hour for 'contacted'. Same film, same cars; the only difference was one word in the question.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What varied between the groups?

2. What was the result?

1. The verb in the question: hit, smashed, contacted and so on.

2. Higher speed estimates for 'smashed' (about 41 mph) than for 'contacted' (about 32 mph).

Discuss:
  • Why might a single word change what someone remembers?

Part 2 — Memories that never existed

A second part of the study was even more disturbing. One week later, the students were asked whether they had seen any broken glass. There had been none. Yet about a third of those who had heard 'smashed' said they had seen some, compared with around one in seven of those who had heard 'hit'.

Loftus called this the misinformation effect: information that arrives after an event can slip into the memory of the event itself. Her later work went further. In the 'lost in the mall' study of 1995, researchers persuaded a quarter of adult participants to 'remember' being lost as a child in a shopping centre, though it had never happened, by repeating the suggestion with a trusted relative's help.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the misinformation effect?

2. What did the 'lost in the mall' study show?

1. Information that arrives after an event can become part of the memory of the event.

2. About a quarter of adults could be led to remember an event that had never happened.

Discuss:
  • If a false memory feels real, what difference does it make that it isn't?

Part 3 — Witnesses and the law

These findings have had a large influence on the law. Eyewitness evidence is among the most persuasive things a jury can hear, yet studies of cases in which innocent people were later cleared by DNA evidence have found that mistaken identification played a part in a high proportion of them. A confident witness is more credible to a jury, but confidence turns out to be only a weak guide to accuracy.

Many legal systems have therefore changed their procedures. Police are now advised to avoid leading questions, to record a witness's confidence immediately and to run line-ups carefully, so that the officer does not unconsciously suggest an answer. The memory is not worthless, but it is delicate, and the way it is collected matters a great deal.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did courts change their procedures?

2. What do the new procedures try to avoid?

1. Because mistaken identifications contributed to wrongful convictions, and confidence is a weak guide to accuracy.

2. Leading questions and unconscious suggestions from the officer.

Discuss:
  • Should a conviction ever depend on a single witness? What other evidence would you require?

Grammar — modals of speculation about the past

When we can't be sure what happened, we use modal verbs + have + past participle to say how certain we are. It is the grammar of detectives, historians and cautious witnesses.

The scale of certainty:
• must have + past participle — almost certain it happened: "She must have heard the question."
• may / might / could have — it's possible: "He might have imagined it."
• can't / couldn't have — almost certain it did NOT happen: "There can't have been any glass; the film didn't show any."
Other useful forms:
• must have been + -ing for an action in progress: "He must have been thinking about something else."
• The negative of must have is can't have (not mustn't have).

How reliable is it?

Five cases. Say how far you would trust the memory, and which expression of certainty you would use. Then reveal.

1. A witness identifies a stranger after seeing him for two seconds in the dark. How reliable is the identification?
Weak evidence. Poor viewing conditions and short exposure reduce accuracy. Confidence at trial is often higher than the original certainty.
2. A person remembers exactly where she was when she heard shocking news. Is the memory accurate?
Flashbulb memories. They feel vivid, but studies show details change over time even when confidence stays high.
3. A therapist asks a patient to imagine a childhood event until it feels real. Could the memory be false?
Possibly. Repeated imagination can create familiarity, which may be mistaken for a real memory.
4. Two eyewitnesses disagree about the colour of a car. What should the police do?
Look for independent evidence. Cameras, objects and records are less likely to be altered than memory.
5. A person tells the same story for thirty years. Does that make it more likely to be true?
Not necessarily. Repeating a story can strengthen the confidence without improving the accuracy.
