Loftus and Palmer: true or false?

In 1974, two psychologists showed students films of traffic accidents and then asked them questions. Here are six claims about the study. Decide which are true.

1. Eyewitness memory is reliable when the witness feels very confident.
False. Confidence is not a reliable guide to accuracy.
2. There was no broken glass in any of the films.
True. The memory of the glass was created by the wording.
3. A single word changed the average speed estimate by several miles an hour.
True. The gap between smashed and hit was around seven miles an hour.
4. A week later, students who had heard smashed were no more likely to report broken glass than the others.
False. About a third of them said they had seen broken glass; far fewer in the hit group.
5. Students saw films of accidents and were asked how fast the cars were going when they hit, smashed or collided.
True. The verb was the only thing that changed.
6. Students who heard hit guessed higher speeds than students who heard smashed.
False. It was the other way round: smashed produced higher guesses.
Talk about it:
  • Why would a single verb change what someone remembers?
  • Where, outside psychology, do questions have this kind of power?

Part 1 — One word

Before you read: What do you think is the effect of putting a particular verb in a question about speed?

In 1974, the psychologist Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed students films of traffic accidents and then asked them questions. The key question was how fast the cars had been going. But the verb varied. Some students were asked how fast the cars were going when they 'hit' each other; others when they 'smashed' into each other; and so on.

The results were clear. The average estimate was about 41 miles per hour when the verb was 'smashed', against about 32 miles per hour for 'contacted'. Same film, same cars; the only difference was one word in the question.

Part 2 — Memories that never existed

Before you read: Some students will later 'remember' broken glass that was never there. What does that tell us about how memory works?

A second part of the study was even more disturbing. One week later, the students were asked whether they had seen any broken glass. There had been none. Yet about a third of those who had heard 'smashed' said they had seen some, compared with around one in seven of those who had heard 'hit'.

Loftus called this the misinformation effect: information that arrives after an event can slip into the memory of the event itself. Her later work went further. In the 'lost in the mall' study of 1995, researchers persuaded a quarter of adult participants to 'remember' being lost as a child in a shopping centre, though it had never happened, by repeating the suggestion with a trusted relative's help.

Part 3 — Witnesses and the law

Before you read: How should courts treat an eyewitness who feels certain?

These findings have had a large influence on the law. Eyewitness evidence is among the most persuasive things a jury can hear, yet studies of cases in which innocent people were later cleared by DNA evidence have found that mistaken identification played a part in a high proportion of them. A confident witness is more credible to a jury, but confidence turns out to be only a weak guide to accuracy.

Many legal systems have therefore changed their procedures. Police are now advised to avoid leading questions, to record a witness's confidence immediately and to run line-ups carefully, so that the officer does not unconsciously suggest an answer. The memory is not worthless, but it is delicate, and the way it is collected matters a great deal.

Speculating about the past

When we cannot be sure what happened, we use modal verbs to say how likely it was. They are essential for weighing up evidence.

must have + -ed — you are almost sure. The question must have influenced her answer.
might / may / could have + -ed — it is possible. He might have changed his memory after the question.
can't / couldn't have + -ed — you are almost sure it did not happen. She can't have seen broken glass, because there was none.

How reliable is it?

Five cases about memory. Choose how far you would trust each one, then explain using must have, might have or can't have. Check the evidence last.

1. A witness identifies a stranger after seeing him for two seconds in the dark. How reliable is the identification?must have
Weak evidence. Poor viewing conditions and short exposure reduce accuracy. Confidence at trial is often higher than the original certainty.
2. A person remembers exactly where she was when she heard shocking news. Is the memory accurate?might have
Flashbulb memories. They feel vivid, but studies show details change over time even when confidence stays high.
3. A therapist asks a patient to imagine a childhood event until it feels real. Could the memory be false?can't have
Possibly. Repeated imagination can create familiarity, which may be mistaken for a real memory.
4. Two eyewitnesses disagree about the colour of a car. What should the police do?might have
Look for independent evidence. Cameras, objects and records are less likely to be altered than memory.
5. A person tells the same story for thirty years. Does that make it more likely to be true?must have
Not necessarily. Repeating a story can strengthen the confidence without improving the accuracy.
