Can You Trust Your Memory?

Loftus and Palmer: true or false?

In 1974, two psychologists showed students films of traffic accidents and then asked them questions. Here are six claims about the study. Decide which are true.

1. Eyewitness memory is reliable when the witness feels very confident.
False. Confidence is not a reliable guide to accuracy.
2. There was no broken glass in any of the films.
True. The memory of the glass was created by the wording.
3. A single word changed the average speed estimate by several miles an hour.
True. The gap between smashed and hit was around seven miles an hour.
4. A week later, students who had heard smashed were no more likely to report broken glass than the others.
False. About a third of them said they had seen broken glass; far fewer in the hit group.
5. Students saw films of accidents and were asked how fast the cars were going when they hit, smashed or collided.
True. The verb was the only thing that changed.
6. Students who heard hit guessed higher speeds than students who heard smashed.
False. It was the other way round: smashed produced higher guesses.
Talk about it:
  • Why would a single verb change what someone remembers?
  • Where, outside psychology, do questions have this kind of power?

Part 1 — One word

Before you read: Students watch a film of a car accident and are asked how fast the cars were going. What effect do you think the verb in the question has, for example hit rather than smashed?

In 1974, the psychologist Elizabeth Loftus and John Palmer showed students films of traffic accidents and then asked them questions. The key question was how fast the cars had been going. But the verb varied. Some students were asked how fast the cars were going when they 'hit' each other; others when they 'smashed' into each other; and so on.

The results were clear. The average estimate was about 41 miles per hour when the verb was 'smashed', against about 34 for 'hit' and about 32 for 'contacted'. Same film, same cars; the only difference was one word in the question.

Words from this part — what does each word in bold mean here? Say your idea, then reveal.

“But the verb varied.”
vary (v) — to change or be different from one case to another. Example: "Prices vary a lot from one supermarket to another."
“The average estimate was about 41 miles per hour when the verb was 'smashed', against about 34 for 'hit' and about 32 for 'contacted'.”
estimate (n) — a guess about a size, amount or number, based on the information you have. Example: "The builder's estimate for the new kitchen was far higher than we expected."

Part 2 — Memories that never existed

Before you read: A week later, the students are asked whether they saw any broken glass. There was none. Who do you think is most likely to say yes, and what would that tell us about memory?

A second part of the study was even more disturbing. One week later, the students were asked whether they had seen any broken glass. There had been none. Yet about a third of those who had heard 'smashed' said they had seen some, compared with around one in seven of those who had heard 'hit'.

Loftus called this the misinformation effect: information that arrives after an event can slip into the memory of the event itself. Her later work went further. In the 'lost in the mall' study of 1995, researchers persuaded a quarter of adult participants to 'remember' being lost as a child in a shopping centre, though it had never happened, by repeating the suggestion with a trusted relative's help.

Words from this part — what does each word in bold mean here? Say your idea, then reveal.

“A second part of the study was even more disturbing.”
disturbing (adj) — making you feel worried or upset. Example: "The report contains some disturbing figures about road safety."
“Loftus called this the misinformation effect: information that arrives after an event can slip into the memory of the event itself.”
misinformation effect (n) — the way details that someone hears after an event can change their memory of the event itself. Example: "Because of the misinformation effect, police try to interview witnesses before they read news reports about the crime."
“Loftus called this the misinformation effect: information that arrives after an event can slip into the memory of the event itself.”
slip into (phrasal verb) — to enter something gradually or without being noticed. Example: "A few errors slipped into the final version of the contract."

Part 3 — Witnesses and the law

Before you read: How should the police and the courts treat an eyewitness who feels certain?

These findings have had a large influence on the law. Eyewitness evidence is among the most persuasive things a jury can hear, yet studies of cases in which innocent people were later cleared by DNA evidence have found that mistaken identification played a part in a high proportion of them. A confident witness is more credible to a jury, but confidence turns out to be only a weak guide to accuracy.

Many legal systems have therefore changed their procedures. Police are now advised to avoid leading questions, to record a witness's confidence immediately and to run line-ups carefully, so that the officer does not unconsciously suggest an answer. The memory is not worthless, but it is delicate, and the way it is collected matters a great deal.

credible (adj) — able to be believed or trusted. Example: "His excuse sounded credible, so the manager accepted it."
“Many legal systems have therefore changed their procedures.”
procedure (n) — an official or accepted way of doing something. Example: "If you lose your card, the procedure is to call the bank straight away."
“Police are now advised to avoid leading questions, to record a witness's confidence immediately and to run line-ups carefully, so that the officer does not unconsciously suggest an answer.”
line-up (n) — a row of people that a witness looks at to try to pick out the person who committed a crime. Example: "The victim picked out her attacker from a police line-up."

Speculating about the past

When we cannot be sure what happened, we use modal verbs to say how likely it was. They are essential for weighing up evidence.

must have + -ed — you are almost sure. The verb must have affected their estimates.
might / may / could have + -ed — it is possible. She might have mixed up two different events.
can't / couldn't have + -ed — you are almost sure it did not happen. He can't have seen the driver's face; it was too dark.
must / might have been + -ing — for an action in progress. She might have been looking the other way.

Build it

Rewrite each one with the modal given. Type it, say it, then reveal a model.

1. Perhaps the witness changed his memory after the question. Use might have. → The witness …
The witness might have changed his memory after the question.
2. It is impossible that she saw broken glass. There was none. Use can't have. → She …
She can't have seen broken glass because there was none.
3. I am sure the word influenced her answer. Use must have. → The word …
The word must have influenced her answer.
4. I am almost certain the driver was looking at his phone. Use must have been + -ing. → The driver …
The driver must have been looking at his phone.

How reliable is it?

Five cases about memory. Choose how far you would trust each one, then explain using the modal shown. Then compare your view with the research or with one idea.

1. A witness identifies a stranger she saw for two seconds in the dark, and says she is completely sure. can't have
Weak evidence. Research on eyewitnesses shows that poor lighting and very short viewing reduce accuracy, and a witness's confidence often grows between the first identification and the trial.
2. A woman remembers exactly where she was when she heard some shocking news many years ago. might have
Flashbulb memories. Studies that recorded people's accounts the day after a shocking event, and again years later, found that many details had changed, although people's confidence stayed high.
3. After a therapist asks him to imagine a childhood event again and again, a man begins to remember it clearly. could have
Imagination inflation. Studies show that repeatedly imagining an event can make people more confident that it really happened, and can help to create false memories.
4. Two witnesses who have never spoken to each other give the same description of a car, and it matches a traffic camera. must have
Idea. This is much stronger. Independent accounts that agree with physical evidence are far harder to explain away.
5. A man has told the same story about his first day at work for thirty years, always in the same way. may have
Idea. Telling a story often can make us more confident without making it more accurate. Each retelling may become part of the memory.
