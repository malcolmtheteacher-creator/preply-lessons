Lesson 47: Understanding Why We Think and Act the Way We Do

🤔 The Irrational Human: Two Systems of Thinking

We like to think we're logical, rational beings. But psychology reveals the uncomfortable truth: most of our thinking is fast, automatic, emotional, and full of errors. Let's explore why.

🧠 System 1 vs System 2 Thinking (Daniel Kahneman)

System 1: Fast, Automatic, Intuitive
  • Operates: Automatically, effortlessly, unconsciously
  • Speed: Instant - milliseconds
  • Examples: Reading words, detecting hostility in voices, 2+2=4, driving on empty road
  • Strengths: Fast, energy-efficient, good for familiar situations
  • Weaknesses: Makes systematic errors (biases), jumps to conclusions, influenced by emotions
System 2: Slow, Effortful, Logical
  • Operates: Consciously, requires attention and effort
  • Speed: Slow - seconds to minutes
  • Examples: 17×24=?, parking in tight space, comparing products, checking logic of argument
  • Strengths: Rational, can handle complexity, can override System 1
  • Weaknesses: Slow, tiring, lazy (often doesn't engage when it should)

🎯 Quick Test: Which System Are You Using?

Problem 1: A bat and ball cost $1.10 together. The bat costs $1.00 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?

System 1 answer (intuitive): $0.10

System 2 answer (logical): $0.05

If ball = $0.10, then bat = $1.10, total = $1.20 ✗
If ball = $0.05, then bat = $1.05, total = $1.10 ✓

Did you get $0.10? That's System 1 jumping in too quickly! Most people get this wrong because System 1 gives a fast answer and System 2 is too lazy to check.

⚠️ The Problem: System 2 is Lazy

System 2 should check System 1's work, but often doesn't because:

  • Thinking is hard work - our brains avoid it
  • System 1 feels confident even when wrong
  • We're often tired, distracted, or in a hurry
  • We trust our intuitions too much

Result: We make predictable errors (biases) in judgment and decision-making.

🔬 Classic Study: The Invisible Gorilla

Researchers asked people to count basketball passes in a video. While counting, a person in a gorilla suit walked through the scene. 50% of people didn't notice the gorilla!

Why? When System 2 is occupied (counting), we become blind to obvious things. We're less aware than we think.

Implications: Don't trust your perception. Eyewitness testimony is unreliable. We miss obvious things when focused on something else.

  • When do you rely on System 1 (intuition) vs System 2 (careful analysis)?
  • Do you consider yourself a rational person? After learning this, do you still think so?
  • How often do you actually check your intuitions before acting on them?

🧩 Cognitive Biases: Our Mental Shortcuts Gone Wrong

Our brains use mental shortcuts (heuristics) to make decisions quickly. Usually helpful, but they lead to systematic errors called cognitive biases. Here are the most important ones to know.

1. Confirmation Bias 🔍

What it is: We seek out information that confirms what we already believe, and ignore information that contradicts it.

Example: You think your team is the best. You remember all their wins and forget their losses. You watch highlight reels, not criticism. You argue with people who disagree.

Real-world impact: Political polarization, conspiracy theories, refusing to change your mind despite evidence

How to fight it: Actively seek out opposing views. Ask "What would prove me wrong?"

2. Availability Heuristic 📰

What it is: We judge probability by how easily examples come to mind, not by actual statistics.

Example: After seeing news about plane crashes, you feel flying is dangerous. In reality, driving is far more dangerous - but car crashes aren't newsworthy.

Real-world impact: Overestimating terrorism risk, underestimating boring but common dangers (heart disease, car accidents)

How to fight it: Look at actual statistics, not vivid examples. The news isn't representative of reality.

3. Anchoring Bias ⚓

What it is: We rely too heavily on the first piece of information we receive (the "anchor").

Example: A shirt originally priced at $100, now on sale for $50. You think it's a great deal! But if it was always $50, you wouldn't care. The $100 anchor made $50 seem cheap.

Real-world impact: Salespeople use this constantly. First offer in negotiation influences outcome. "Original price" manipulates you.

How to fight it: Ignore the first number. Research actual value independently.

4. Sunk Cost Fallacy 💸

What it is: We continue investing in something because we've already invested in it, even when it's clearly not working.

Example: You paid $15 for a movie ticket. 30 minutes in, the movie is terrible. But you stay because you "already paid for it." Rational choice: leave and do something better with your time!

Real-world impact: Staying in bad relationships, continuing failed projects, not changing careers

How to fight it: Past costs are gone. Only consider future costs and benefits.

5. Dunning-Kruger Effect 🎓

What it is: People with low ability overestimate their competence. Experts underestimate theirs.

Example: Someone who just learned guitar thinks they're great. A master guitarist knows how much they still don't know. "The more you learn, the more you realize how much you don't know."

Real-world impact: Incompetent people in power, experts not trusted, overconfidence in early stages of learning

How to fight it: Be humble. Seek feedback. The more confident you feel, the more you should question yourself.

6. Loss Aversion 📉

What it is: We feel losses about twice as strongly as equivalent gains. Losing $100 hurts more than winning $100 feels good.

Example: You're more upset about losing $20 than you are happy about finding $20. You avoid risks that might lose money, even when the expected value is positive.

Real-world impact: People hold losing stocks too long (don't want to "realize" the loss), avoid beneficial risks, negotiators fear losses more than they value gains

How to fight it: Frame decisions in terms of total outcome, not gains/losses from current position.

📚 More Important Biases 👇 Click to explore
  • Hindsight Bias: "I knew it all along!" We think past events were more predictable than they were
  • Optimism Bias: We think bad things happen to others, not us (why people don't save money, don't wear seatbelts)
  • Framing Effect: Same information presented differently changes our choice ("90% survival rate" vs "10% mortality rate")
  • Bandwagon Effect: We believe things because many others do (social proof)
  • Negativity Bias: Bad events affect us more than good events

  • Can you think of a time confirmation bias prevented you from seeing the truth?
  • How do these biases affect politics, relationships, or career decisions?
  • Can we eliminate biases, or just become aware of them?

👥 Social Psychology: How Others Shape Us

We think we're independent thinkers, but we're heavily influenced by social pressure, conformity, and authority. Let's explore some disturbing experiments that reveal how much others control our behavior.

🔬 The Asch Conformity Experiment (1951)

Setup: Show people two cards - one with a line, one with three lines. Ask which line matches. The answer is obvious.

Twist: You're in a group where everyone else (secretly actors) gives the wrong answer.

Results:

  • 75% of people conformed to the wrong answer at least once
  • On average, people conformed 37% of the time
  • Only 25% never conformed

Lesson: Social pressure is incredibly powerful. We'll deny our own eyes to fit in.

🔬 The Milgram Obedience Experiment (1961)

Setup: "Teacher" (real participant) instructed to give electric shocks to "learner" (actor) for wrong answers. Shocks increase from 15V to 450V (labeled "Danger: Severe Shock").

Reality: No real shocks, but teacher doesn't know. Learner screams, begs to stop, then goes silent (simulating unconsciousness).

Results:

  • 65% went to maximum 450V despite screaming and pleas
  • 100% went to at least 300V
  • Many were distressed but continued when authority figure said "you must continue"

Lesson: Ordinary people will do terrible things when ordered by authority. Explains atrocities throughout history.

🔬 The Stanford Prison Experiment (1971)

Setup: College students randomly assigned to be "guards" or "prisoners" in fake prison for 2 weeks.

Results:

  • Guards became sadistic, psychologically torturing prisoners
  • Prisoners became submissive, some had breakdowns
  • Experiment stopped after 6 days because it became too dangerous
  • Normal people became cruel when given power and a role

Lesson: Situations and roles powerfully shape behavior. "Good" people can do evil things in the right (wrong) situation.

⚠️ What These Experiments Teach Us

  • We're not as independent as we think - social pressure is enormous
  • Authority figures can get us to do terrible things
  • Situations matter more than personality - good people can do bad things
  • Roles shape behavior - give someone power, they'll likely abuse it
  • Don't assume you'd resist - these were normal people, not monsters

💡 Other Social Psychology Effects

📚 Click to explore more effects 👇
  • Bystander Effect: The more people present, the less likely anyone helps (diffusion of responsibility)
  • Groupthink: Groups make bad decisions because nobody wants to disagree
  • Fundamental Attribution Error: We blame people's character for their behavior, but blame situations for our own behavior
  • In-group Bias: We favor our group over outsiders, even when groups are arbitrary
  • Social Loafing: People work less hard in groups than alone

  • Have you ever experienced the bystander effect - not helping because others were present?
  • How can we resist social pressure and authority when it's asking us to do wrong?
  • Are these experiments ethical? Should they be allowed?

💰 Behavioral Economics: We're Terrible with Money

Traditional economics assumes humans are rational. Behavioral economics shows we're predictably irrational when it comes to money and decisions. Companies exploit this. Let's learn how.

🎯 The Paradox of Choice

Assumption: More choice is better. More freedom = more happiness.

Reality: Too much choice makes us anxious and less satisfied.

Famous Study: Jam tasting at supermarket

  • 24 flavors available: 60% stopped to taste, 3% bought
  • 6 flavors available: 40% stopped to taste, 30% bought

Lesson: More options = fewer sales. We get overwhelmed and buy nothing.

Why? Decision fatigue, fear of making wrong choice, opportunity cost anxiety

💵 Mental Accounting

What it is: We treat money differently depending on where it came from or what it's for.

Scenario: You win $50 gambling. You're more likely to spend it frivolously than $50 from your salary, even though it's the same money!

Another example: You have $1000 in savings but also $1000 in credit card debt at 20% interest. Logically, you should pay off debt. But we keep separate "mental accounts" - savings feels different from debt.

How companies exploit this: "Rebates" feel like free money to spend. Tax refunds treated as windfalls, not your own money back.

🎁 The Endowment Effect

What it is: We value things more once we own them.

Classic Study: Give people a coffee mug. Ask how much they'd sell it for.

  • Owners: Want $7 to sell
  • Non-owners: Only willing to pay $3 to buy

Same mug, but ownership doubles perceived value!

How companies exploit this: Free trials ("try before you buy"), return policies - once you have it, you don't want to give it up!

🆓 The Power of "Free"

What it is: We overvalue free things, even when they're not the best deal.

Study: Choice between chocolates

  • Option A: Lindt truffle for 15 cents vs Hershey's Kiss for 1 cent → 73% chose Lindt
  • Option B: Lindt truffle for 14 cents vs Hershey's Kiss for FREE → 69% chose Hershey's

People chose worse chocolate just because it was free, even though the price difference was the same!

How companies exploit this: "Free shipping" (built into price), "Buy 2 get 1 free" (you probably don't need 3)

🛡️ Protecting Yourself from Manipulation

  • Recognize tactics: Know when you're being manipulated
  • Pause before buying: Wait 24 hours for big purchases
  • Ignore "original price": Research actual value
  • "Free" isn't free: Calculate real cost
  • Set rules for yourself: "Never buy on first visit" etc.
  • Track spending: Mental accounting disappears when you see real numbers

🎤 Behavioral Economics Discussion (8 minutes)

Discuss:

  • Have you ever been paralyzed by too many choices? What happened?
  • Can you think of times you treated money differently based on mental accounting?
  • Have you kept a free trial subscription you didn't need? (Endowment effect!)
  • What's the most effective marketing trick that works on you?
  • Should companies be allowed to exploit psychological weaknesses, or is it unethical?

🎯 What Motivates Us? (Hint: Not What You Think)

We assume money is the best motivator. Psychology shows this is wrong - especially for creative or complex work. Let's explore what actually drives human behavior.

💡 Intrinsic vs Extrinsic Motivation

Extrinsic Motivation (External Rewards)
  • Money, grades, prizes, recognition, avoiding punishment
  • Works for: Simple, mechanical tasks
  • Doesn't work for: Creative, complex, interesting work
  • Problem: Can actually reduce motivation for interesting tasks!
Intrinsic Motivation (Internal Drive)
  • Enjoyment, curiosity, challenge, mastery, purpose
  • Works for: Creative, complex, meaningful work
  • Result: Better performance, more persistence, deeper satisfaction
  • Key: Autonomy, mastery, purpose (Daniel Pink)

🔬 The Candle Problem (Motivation Study)

Task: Attach a candle to the wall so wax doesn't drip on table. You have: candle, box of tacks, matches.

Solution: Empty the box, tack box to wall, put candle in box. (Requires creative thinking)

Study: Two groups

  • Group A: No reward, just solve it
  • Group B: Money reward for faster solution

Results: Group B (money) took 3.5 minutes LONGER on average!

Lesson: Financial incentives can hurt performance on creative tasks by narrowing focus.

🎯 Self-Determination Theory: Three Needs

For lasting motivation, we need:

1. Autonomy

The feeling of being in control of your own life and choices

Example: Google's "20% time" - employees spend 20% of time on own projects. Gmail came from this!

2. Competence (Mastery)

The feeling of getting better at something, achieving progress

Example: Why video games are addictive - constant feedback, clear progress, achievable challenges

3. Relatedness (Purpose)

The feeling of connection to others, contributing to something bigger

Example: Volunteers work hard for no money because they feel connected to a cause

⚠️ When Rewards Backfire

"Overjustification Effect": Rewarding people for something they already enjoy can make them enjoy it less!

Study: Kids who loved drawing

  • Group A: Draw for fun (no reward)
  • Group B: Get prize for drawing

Later observation: Group B drew LESS when prizes stopped. They now saw drawing as "work for reward" not "fun."

Lesson: Be careful rewarding activities people already enjoy - you might kill their intrinsic motivation!

🎤 Motivation Discussion (6 minutes)

Discuss:

  • When are you most motivated? What drives you - money, mastery, purpose, or something else?
  • Have you experienced the overjustification effect - liking something less after being rewarded?
  • Do you have autonomy, mastery, and purpose in your work/studies?
  • What would motivate you more: 20% raise or freedom to choose your own projects?
  • Why do people play video games for free but complain about paid work?

🔄 Habits & Behavior Change: How to Actually Change

We all want to change - exercise more, eat better, study harder. But willpower fails. Understanding the psychology of habits is the key to lasting change.

🔁 The Habit Loop (Charles Duhigg)

Every habit has three components:

1. Cue (Trigger)

Something that triggers the habit

Example: Feeling stressed

2. Routine (Behavior)

The actual habit behavior

Example: Eating junk food

3. Reward

What you get from the behavior

Example: Temporary comfort, dopamine

How to Change a Habit:

Keep the CUE and REWARD the same. Change the ROUTINE.

Example: Feeling stressed (cue) → Go for walk instead of eating (new routine) → Feel better (same reward)

📈 The Two-Minute Rule (James Clear)

Principle: Make new habits so easy you can't say no. Start with just 2 minutes.

Examples:

  • Want to read more? → "Read one page"
  • Want to exercise? → "Put on running shoes"
  • Want to meditate? → "Breathe deeply for 2 minutes"
  • Want to write? → "Write one sentence"

Why it works: Starting is the hardest part. Once you start, you usually continue. Lower the barrier.

🎯 Implementation Intentions

Instead of: "I will exercise more" (vague, easy to forget)

Say: "I will exercise on Monday/Wednesday/Friday at 7am at the gym" (specific plan)

📊 Research Results

People who use implementation intentions are 2-3x more likely to follow through!

Formula: "When [SITUATION], I will [BEHAVIOR]"

  • "When I feel stressed, I will take 5 deep breaths"
  • "When I finish lunch, I will study for 25 minutes"
  • "When I wake up, I will drink a glass of water"

🏗️ Environment Design > Willpower

Key insight: Your environment shapes your behavior more than your willpower.

Make Good Habits Easy:
  • Want to eat healthy? → Keep only healthy food in house
  • Want to read more? → Put book on pillow
  • Want to exercise? → Sleep in gym clothes
  • Want to practice guitar? → Leave guitar in middle of room
Make Bad Habits Hard:
  • Want to eat less junk food? → Don't buy it / hide it in inconvenient place
  • Want less screen time? → Delete apps / leave phone in other room
  • Want to stop smoking? → Don't carry cigarettes

⚠️ Why Willpower Doesn't Work

  • Willpower is finite: It depletes throughout the day (ego depletion)
  • Stress kills willpower: When stressed, we default to habits
  • Habits are automatic: They happen before willpower kicks in
  • Better strategy: Design environment so willpower isn't needed!

  • Have you tried to change behavior through willpower alone? Did it work?
  • What's one small change you could make to your environment to support a good habit?
  • Do you use implementation intentions? ("When X, I will Y")
  • Why do you think most New Year's resolutions fail by February?
