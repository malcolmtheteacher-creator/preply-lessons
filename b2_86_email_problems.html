Cross-Cultural Email Communication

Communicate effectively across cultures

Learning Objectives

  • Discuss ideas to communicate effectively across cultures
  • Work with advice and suggestions language
  • Understand the function of different sentence structures
  • Practice appropriate responses to workplace situations

Let's Discuss

Do you ever email people from other countries?

For work? For business? For personal reasons?

Do you have problems sending emails to people from different cultures?

Do you sometimes worry about misunderstandings? Is it difficult to be clear in email?

What's important when writing an email?

Think about tone, style, formality, clarity, and how to address the other person.

Advice and Suggestions Language

These sentences are about communicating effectively across cultures. They use different structures to express advice and suggestions:

1. "We have to think about the relationship between the writer and the reader."
2. "We can look at general tendencies."
3. "We can't be too simplistic about it."
4. "It is important to put things in context."
5. "It is better to respond to an email in a considered way."
6. "Companies that have a multicultural workforce need to give their staff some training."
7. "They should only be suggestions."
8. "It is very helpful for employees to have guidelines."

What is the main function of these sentences?

a) Orders and commands

b) Advice and suggestions

c) Describing a process

b) Advice and suggestions — All of these sentences offer advice or suggestions about how to communicate effectively.

Sentence Stress in Advice

In English, we stress important words in sentences. When giving advice, we often stress key words. Listen carefully to which words are stressed:

Example: "It is BETTER to respond to an email in a CONSIDERED way."

The stressed words (BETTER and CONSIDERED) carry the main message. They emphasize what you should do.

Pay attention to stress patterns when listening to advice. The stressed words tell you what is most important in the message.

Tips for Hearing Stress

  • Stressed words are usually longer and louder
  • Stressed words carry the most important information
  • In advice sentences, stress often falls on adjectives and key verbs
  • Listen for the "beat" or rhythm of the sentence

Real-World Scenarios

You receive difficult emails from colleagues. How would you respond? Use advice and suggestions language to discuss these situations.

Scenario A: Last-Minute Cancellation

What happened: Your colleague Karl cancels his help with your afternoon presentation just 2 hours before it starts. His email is very short with no explanation: "Sorry, can't help today."

Discussion questions:

  • How would you feel receiving this email?
  • What advice would you give Karl about email communication?
  • How should you respond to Karl?
  • What should Karl have done differently?

Scenario B: Trip Postponement

What happened: Your colleague Jaime organized everything perfectly for your conference abroad — transport, hotels, meals. But you just learned you must postpone the trip for a month. How do you tell Jaime?

Discussion questions:

  • Is this a problem with email communication, or a general work situation?
  • What's important to include in your email to Jaime?
  • How should you respond to the effort Jaime put in?
  • What advice would you give to someone in this situation?

Scenario C: Mixed Messages

What happened: You receive a friendly email from your colleague Sun in another branch. Sun asks many personal questions about your office AND asks about next week's meeting timetable. The tone is very casual.

Discussion questions:

  • Is this appropriate professional communication?
  • What is the problem with this email?
  • How would you respond?
  • What advice would you give Sun about cross-cultural email?
