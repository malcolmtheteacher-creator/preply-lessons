How to Read a Dictionary Entry

A dictionary entry contains many types of information. Here is an example for the word "argue":

argue /ˈɑːrɡjuː/ verb

Definition: to express disagreement in words; to present reasons or evidence to support a position

Example: "The committee argued about the budget for hours."

Collocation: argue about, argue with, argue for, argue against

Synonym: debate, disagree, discuss

Each Part Explained:

  • Headword — The word itself, in bold and in alphabetical order
  • Phonetic transcription — Shows how to pronounce the word (in slashes: /ˈɑːrɡjuː/)
  • Part of speech — Shows if it is a noun, verb, adjective, etc.
  • Definition — Clear explanation of what the word means
  • Example sentence — Shows how to use the word in context
  • Collocations — Words that often appear with this word
  • Synonyms — Other words with similar meanings

Exercise: Questions About Dictionary Entries

Using the "argue" example above, answer these questions:

Question 1: What part of speech is "argue"?

It is a verb.

Question 2: What are three collocations shown for "argue"?

argue about, argue with, argue for (any three of the four are acceptable)

Question 3: What is a synonym for "argue"?

debate, disagree, or discuss (any of these are correct)

Question 4: How would you pronounce "argue"?

According to the phonetic transcription /ˈɑːrɡjuː/, it is pronounced "AR-gyoo"

Question 5: What is the difference between an example and a collocation?

An example shows the word in a complete sentence. A collocation shows specific words that naturally appear with this word.

Three Strategies for Building Vocabulary

Strategy 1: Learn Word Families

Words often come in families with the same root word but different endings. Dictionary entries often show these related words.

Example:

  • argue (verb) — to express disagreement
  • argument (noun) — a disagreement or a series of reasons
  • arguable (adjective) — can be debated or questioned

Benefit: Learning one word teaches you three or more related words!

Strategy 2: Learn Collocations

Instead of learning individual words, learn them together with their common partners. This helps you use words correctly.

Example: Instead of just learning "argue", learn:

  • argue about — "They argued about money"
  • argue with — "She argued with her boss"
  • argue for — "He argued for longer holidays"

Benefit: You will use the word correctly and naturally in context.

Strategy 3: Learn Register (Formality Level)

Different words are appropriate for different situations. Dictionaries often mark words as formal, informal, slang, or archaic.

Example:

  • informal: "thanks" or "cheers"
  • formal: "I appreciate your assistance"
  • slang: "ta" or "ta mate"

Benefit: You can choose the right words for academic writing, casual conversation, or professional emails.

Speaking Task: Define and Use

Choose 3 new words from this lesson or your recent studies. For each word, you will speak for about 2 minutes total.

For Each Word, Say:

  • 1. The word and how to pronounce it
  • 2. The part of speech
  • 3. The definition in your own words
  • 4. One example sentence
  • 5. A collocation or related word

Example:

"The first word is 'argue', pronounced AR-gyoo. It is a verb. It means to say that you do not agree with someone. For example: "They argued about money." A collocation is "argue with" as in "She argued with her boss about the project." Another word is the noun "argument"."

Tips:

  • Speak clearly and at a natural pace
  • Use complete sentences
  • Check a dictionary or online resource if you are not sure about pronunciation
  • Try to explain the word without reading directly from a dictionary
