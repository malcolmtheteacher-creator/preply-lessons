Priya: How was the new manager's first week, then?

Jonas: Honestly? She seems to know what she's doing. Mind you, it's only been a week — ask me again in a month.

Jonas doesn't say "she's great" or "she's not great." He softens his answer twice — once with seems to, once with mind you. Why hold back like that, do you think? Say your idea out loud before you check.

Check your idea CLICK
A few reasons: he's only had a week's evidence, so a flat "she's great" would overclaim what he actually knows. Softening also keeps him polite — if she turns out to be difficult, he hasn't committed to a strong opinion. And it keeps his options open: he can change his mind later without contradicting himself. Careful speakers do this constantly in everyday conversation, not just in formal writing.
Today: three ways speakers manage what they say — softening opinions (tend to, seem to, arguably, to some extent, in a sense, more or less, it could be argued that, I would say), signalling cautious certainty (apparently, presumably, as far as I know), and steering a conversation (mind you, that said, then again, admittedly, if anything, come to think of it) — so you can give opinions carefully, disagree politely, and think out loud like you mean it.

Softening What You Say

For opinions and generalisations you'd defend, but wouldn't state as flat fact.

Spot the pattern

Three opinions, all a little careful:

  • "People tend to overestimate how much others notice their mistakes."
  • "The new pricing seems to be working — sign-ups are up this month."
  • "It could be argued that the whole plan was rushed."

Before you check: none of these say "this is definitely true." What are they doing instead?

Check the pattern CLICK
Each one softens the claim rather than stating it flatly. Tend to marks a general pattern, not every single case. Seem to marks an impression based on evidence, not a confirmed fact. Could be argued that distances the claim from the speaker personally — it's a case that could be made, not necessarily their own final word.
tend(s) to + verb — a general pattern, not every time
seem(s) to + verb — based on what you've seen or heard
arguably + adjective / claim — a case you could defend
to some extent / in a sense / more or less — how completely true something is
it could be argued that + clause — distances the claim from you personally
I would say (+ opinion) — a personal view, offered gently

From a flat claim to a defensible one

"This is the best pizza in town."
→
"This is arguably the best pizza in town."

The claim is still strong — but "arguably" admits other people might disagree, which makes it easier to defend in conversation.

Sounding Careful, Not Certain

For information you didn't confirm yourself — heard secondhand, worked out logically, or limited to what you personally know.

Spot the pattern

  • "Apparently, the flight's been delayed again — that's what the departures board says, anyway." (reported)
  • "Presumably they'll reschedule the meeting, though nobody's said so officially." (a reasonable guess)
  • "As far as I know, the shop still opens at nine, but don't quote me on it." (limited to your own knowledge)

Say it before you check: what's different about the source of information in each one?

Check the pattern CLICK
Apparently = you heard or read it somewhere, not confirmed firsthand. Presumably = you're reasoning it out from evidence or logic, not reporting anyone's words. As far as I know = you're stating the limit of your own knowledge, which might be out of date. All three protect you from claiming more certainty than you actually have.
apparently + clause — secondhand information
presumably + clause — a reasonable assumption
as far as I know + clause — limited to my own knowledge

Steering the Conversation

For adding a qualifying or contrasting thought — more nuanced than a plain "but."

Spot the pattern

  • "The new café's pricey. Mind you, the coffee's excellent, so maybe it's worth it."
  • "It was a stressful week. That said, we got everything finished on time."
  • "I wasn't keen on the film at first. Then again, I did watch the whole thing without checking my phone once."

What are these doing that a simple "but" wouldn't quite capture? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Each one adds a second, qualifying thought that sits alongside the first rather than flatly contradicting it — the speaker sounds fair, balanced, or like they're genuinely reconsidering as they talk, rather than just switching sides with "but."
mind you — a casual afterthought that qualifies what you just said
that said / having said that — a fairer counterpoint before you move on
then again — reconsidering out loud, seeing it from another angle
admittedly — honestly conceding a point that weakens your own claim
if anything — correcting your point to be stronger or more true, not weaker
come to think of it — a sudden realisation while you're already speaking
