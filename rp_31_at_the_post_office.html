At the Post Office

A birthday parcel that has to reach Canada by Friday, a customs form with boxes you don't understand, and a soggy card on your doormat saying you missed a delivery. One counter, one queue, ten minutes.

The Scene 🎬

Everyone in the queue knows exactly what they want. You have a parcel, a form, and three questions.

Your sister's birthday is Friday, and she lives in Toronto. The present is wrapped, boxed, and — you suspect — heavier than it looks. You have no idea what the options cost, and you'd rather not pay for Tuesday delivery if Thursday is half the price.

There's also the other thing. This morning there was a red-and-white card on your doormat: Sorry we missed you. Something you ordered got driven to your door and driven away again while you were at the gym — and the card doesn't quite say where it is now.

Behind the counter is Priya, who has done this job for eleven years and answered every question you're about to ask roughly forty thousand times. That's good news: nothing you say will surprise her, so you can stop rehearsing and just ask.

  1. Post offices, banks, ticket counters — what's the hardest part of a counter conversation in another language: understanding, or asking again when you don't?
  2. Is "whatever's cheapest" a confession or a perfectly good answer?
YouOne parcel to Canada by Friday, one mystery card from this morning, and a mild fear of official forms.
PriyaEleven years behind this counter. Brisk, kind, and faster than the queue deserves. She will help you — if you ask clearly.
First: read the conversation and notice that You never pretends to understand. Every time something's unclear — a price, a box on the form — there's a plain question within one line. That's the whole skill.

The Conversation 💬

Tuesday, 9:40am. The queue has finally delivered you to the counter.

PriyaMorning. What can I do for you?
YouMorning. I'd like to send this to Canada, whatever's cheapest, really.
PriyaLet's get it on the scales… right. Cheapest is standard international — that's eight forty.
YouHow long would that take to get there, roughly?
PriyaSix to eight working days, no promises.
YouHm. It's a birthday present, and the birthday's Friday.
PriyaThen you want express. Guaranteed three days, and you can follow it online.
YouWhat would tracked cost me on top?
PriyaExpress comes with tracking included — it's nineteen sixty all in.
YouOuch. But fine — her birthday's worth more than eleven pounds. Express it is.
PriyaYou'll need the customs form. Green one, there on the side.
YouRight — what am I supposed to put where it says "detailed description of contents"?
PriyaJust what's in it, plainly. Not "gift" — they hate "gift". What is actually in there?
YouNothing valuable — just clothes and a couple of books. Well, and a small teapot.
Priya"Clothing, books, one teapot." Value, roughly, and sign at the bottom. Lovely. Anything else?
YouYes, actually — I had a card through the door this morning saying you'd tried to deliver.
PriyaLet's see the card… ah, that's this morning's round. It won't be back at the depot yet.
YouCould it still be out with the driver?
PriyaUntil about two, yes. He may well re-try you on his way back through.
YouI'm out all afternoon, that's the trouble. If I miss it again, what happens then?
PriyaThen it comes here, and you collect it with ID any time within eighteen days.
YouCould you redeliver it Thursday instead? I'm home all day Thursday.
PriyaNot me personally — but on the card, that QR code, you can book exactly that. Thursday, no charge.
YouThat's saved me a trip — thanks ever so much. And the teapot's in bubble wrap, before you ask.
PriyaIt always is, love. It always is.

From memory — don't scroll back up.

  1. Why does You end up paying nineteen sixty instead of eight forty?
  2. What does Priya say customs officers hate seeing on the form?
  3. The missed parcel: what are You's two options if the Thursday booking didn't exist?
1. Standard would take six to eight working days with no promises, and the birthday is Friday — so You pays for express: guaranteed three days, tracking included.

2. The word "gift". They want the actual contents, plainly: clothing, books, one teapot.

3. Catch the driver before about two — or let the parcel come back to the post office and collect it there with ID, within eighteen days.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1799, near the Egyptian town of Rosetta, French soldiers digging a fort foundation pulled out a broken slab of grey stone covered in three kinds of writing. It was, in a sense, a parcel — a message sent two thousand years earlier, to whoever could read it.

Nobody could. The same short text sat there in hieroglyphs, in Egyptian script, and in Greek, and it still took the best minds in Europe twenty-three years to make the delivery — until Champollion cracked the code in 1822 and four thousand years of Egyptian writing arrived, all at once, like post held at the depot for forty centuries.

Every parcel is a bet that the other end will understand what you meant by it. Address it clearly. Say what's inside.
