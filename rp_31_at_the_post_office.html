At the Post Office

A birthday parcel that has to reach Canada by Friday, a customs form with boxes you don't understand, and a soggy card on your doormat saying you missed a delivery. One counter, one queue, ten minutes.

The Scene 🎬

Everyone in the queue knows exactly what they want. You have a parcel, a form, and three questions.

Your sister's birthday is Friday, and she lives in Toronto. The present is wrapped, boxed, and — you suspect — heavier than it looks. You have no idea what the options cost, and you'd rather not pay for Tuesday delivery if Thursday is half the price.

There's also the other thing. This morning there was a red-and-white card on your doormat: Sorry we missed you. Something you ordered got driven to your door and driven away again while you were at the gym — and the card doesn't quite say where it is now.

Behind the counter is Priya, who has done this job for eleven years and answered every question you're about to ask roughly forty thousand times. That's good news: nothing you say will surprise her, so you can stop rehearsing and just ask.

  1. Post offices, banks, ticket counters — what's the hardest part of a counter conversation in another language: understanding, or asking again when you don't?
  2. Is "whatever's cheapest" a confession or a perfectly good answer?
YouOne parcel to Canada by Friday, one mystery card from this morning, and a mild fear of official forms.
PriyaEleven years behind this counter. Brisk, kind, and faster than the queue deserves. She will help you — if you ask clearly.
First: read the conversation and notice that You never pretends to understand. Every time something's unclear — a price, a box on the form — there's a plain question within one line. That's the whole skill.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for any counter in the world — post office, ticket office, town hall, bank. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Counter conversations reward the direct and punish the vague — not because staff are unkind, but because there's a queue. State the errand in your first sentence, ask prices and times as plain questions, and never nod at a form you don't understand.

📦 Stating the errand

Phrase 1

The job: your whole errand, plus your one real preference, in a single opening line. Which works best?

A. "Hello. So, I have a somewhat complicated situation involving Canada."
B. "I'd like to send this to Canada, whatever's cheapest, really."
C. "Do you send things to Canada?"
B — "I'd like to send this to Canada, whatever's cheapest, really."
A announces a saga and makes the queue sigh. C asks a question whose answer is obviously yes and wastes a turn. B hands over everything useful at once — the what, the where, and the priority — so the reply can already be an answer. The little "really" softens it from an order into a preference.

Take it anywhere: "I'd like to ___, whatever's cheapest, really." Swap in quickest or simplest as your priority changes.
Phrase 2

The job: asking for a timescale without demanding a promise. Complete it:

"How long would that take to get there, ___?"

"How long would that take to get there, roughly?"
"Roughly" is doing generous work: it tells her an estimate is fine, which gets you an honest answer instead of a careful official one. Note the conditional "would" — you haven't bought anything yet, you're still comparing.

Take it anywhere: "How long would that take, roughly?" Works for deliveries, repairs, applications, queues.
Phrase 3

The job: finding out what the upgrade costs — as a difference, not a total. Complete it:

"What would tracked cost me ___ ___?"

"What would tracked cost me on top?"
On top = in addition to what we've already discussed. This is the comparison-shopper's question: you're not asking the price of tracked post, you're asking the gap between it and the option on the table — which is the only number that matters for the decision.

Take it anywhere: "What would ___ cost me on top?" — insurance, a bigger room, the express option, extra baggage.

📋 The form

Phrase 4

The job: admitting a form has defeated you, without apologising for existing. Put the pieces in order:

where it saysWhat am I supposed"detailed description of contents"?to put
"What am I supposed to put where it says 'detailed description of contents'?"
The honest question, asked plainly — and notice it quotes the form's own words rather than paraphrasing them, so she knows exactly which box you mean. People lose real money nodding at forms. Nobody ever lost anything asking this.

Take it anywhere: "What am I supposed to put where it says ___?" Any form, any country, any language.
Phrase 5

The job: declaring the contents — reassuring first, specific second. Complete it:

"___ valuable — just clothes and a couple of books."

"Nothing valuable — just clothes and a couple of books."
The shape to remember: the reassurance up front ("nothing valuable"), then the plain list. It answers the question behind the question — is this going to be complicated? — before answering the question itself. And "a couple of" keeps it honest without an inventory.

Take it anywhere: "Nothing ___ — just ___ and ___." At customs, at security, at a border: reassure, then list.

🚪 The missed delivery

Phrase 6

The job: raising the second errand with the evidence in your hand. Complete it:

"I had a ___ through the ___ saying you'd tried to deliver."

"I had a card through the door saying you'd tried to deliver."
Through the door — the British way of saying it arrived by letterbox. One sentence carries the whole story: what happened, when-ish, and what you're holding. No blame in it either — "you'd tried" credits the attempt, which keeps her on your side.

Take it anywhere: "I had a ___ through the door saying ___." Letters, cards, notes from the neighbours.
Phrase 7

The job: the hopeful question — is it still rescuable today? Complete it:

"Could it still be ___ with the ___?"

"Could it still be out with the driver?"
Out with the driver = still on the van, still moving, not yet back at the depot. The question costs nothing and regularly saves a day's wait. "Could it still be…" is the polite shape of hope — you're inviting a yes without insisting on one.

Take it anywhere: "Could it still be ___?" — on the shelf, in the stockroom, with the kitchen. Ask before you accept the no.
Phrase 8

The job: finding out the fallback before you need it. Which version?

A. "If I miss it again, what happens then?"
B. "I really hope I don't miss it again."
C. "You're not going to make me come back again, are you?"
A — "If I miss it again, what happens then?"
B is a feeling, not a question — it gets you sympathy and no information. C picks a fight with the one person helping you. A asks for the rule, which is the thing you actually need: now you know about the depot, the ID, and the eighteen days before Thursday goes wrong, not after.

Take it anywhere: "If ___ happens, what happens then?" The fallback question — ask it while everything's still fine.

🙏 Fixing it, and leaving well

Phrase 9

The job: proposing the specific arrangement that suits you. Complete it:

"Could you ___ it Thursday instead? I'm home all day Thursday."

"Could you redeliver it Thursday instead? I'm home all day Thursday."
Don't just report the problem — propose the fix. Naming your day and backing it with a reason ("I'm home all day") turns a complaint into a plan someone can say yes to. Even when the answer is "not me, but here's how", you've moved things forward.

Take it anywhere: "Could you ___ it ___ instead?" — reschedule, redeliver, rebook. Always arrive with a proposal.
Phrase 10

The job: the exit line — gratitude with the reason attached. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "That's saved me a trip — thanks ever so much."
Not just "thanks" — thanks plus what it was for. Naming the favour ("saved me a trip") is what makes gratitude land as real rather than automatic, and "ever so much" is the warm British intensifier that costs nothing and leaves the counter smiling at the next customer.

Take it anywhere: "That's saved me a ___ — thanks ever so much." A trip, a fortune, a headache.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1799, near the Egyptian town of Rosetta, French soldiers digging a fort foundation pulled out a broken slab of grey stone covered in three kinds of writing. It was, in a sense, a parcel — a message sent two thousand years earlier, to whoever could read it.

Nobody could. The same short text sat there in hieroglyphs, in Egyptian script, and in Greek, and it still took the best minds in Europe twenty-three years to make the delivery — until Champollion cracked the code in 1822 and four thousand years of Egyptian writing arrived, all at once, like post held at the depot for forty centuries.

Every parcel is a bet that the other end will understand what you meant by it. Address it clearly. Say what's inside.
