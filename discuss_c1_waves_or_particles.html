Words you'll need

wave (n) — a pattern that spreads out, with repeating high and low parts. Example: "Sound and light both behave like waves in some experiments."
particle (n) — a very small piece of matter or energy at one location. Example: "An electron is detected as a single particle."
interference (n) — the effect of two overlapping waves adding or cancelling each other. Example: "The bands on the screen are a sign of interference."
probability (n) — how likely something is to happen. Example: "Quantum theory gives the probability of each outcome."
measurement (n) — the process of finding out a value, usually with an instrument. Example: "The result depends on how the measurement is made."
superposition (n) — a state that combines several possibilities at once. Example: "The electron is described as a superposition of two paths."
detect (v) — to find or record that something is present. Example: "The screen detects each electron as a dot."
interpretation (n) — a particular way of explaining what a theory means. Example: "Physicists disagree about the correct interpretation."
collapse (v) — to change suddenly from many possibilities to a single outcome. Example: "On one account, the wave function collapses when we measure."
counterintuitive (adj) — opposite to what common sense would expect. Example: "The result is deeply counterintuitive."

The hook: dots that make stripes

In 1801 Thomas Young passed light through two narrow slits and saw bands of light and dark on a screen, the mark of waves overlapping. A century later it became clear that light also arrives in separate lumps, called photons.

Later experiments fired electrons through two slits one at a time. Each electron lands at a single point, like a tiny bullet. Yet after thousands of arrivals, the dots build up the same bands that waves would make.

Say it aloud: Before you read on, say how a single particle could possibly "know" about both slits.

Roughly: nobody agrees on a single picture. The mathematics predicts the pattern with extraordinary accuracy. What is disputed is what the mathematics tells us is physically going on. The next tabs separate what is established from what is interpretation.

Talk it through:

Sort the six observations into "wave-like" and "particle-like". Notice that light and electrons appear in both groups. State the puzzle in one sentence. Hint: ask whether each observation concerns a single event or the pattern formed by many events. Then test your idea on this claim: "A quantum object is sometimes a wave and sometimes a particle."

Particle-like behaviour shows up in single detections, wave-like behaviour in the pattern of many. Many physicists say a quantum object is neither a classical wave nor a classical particle, but is described by a wave-like rule (the wave function) that gives the probabilities of particle-like events. "Sometimes a wave, sometimes a particle" is a loose way of saying this.

Three ideas, in small steps

Idea 1 · Interference is the signature of waves

When two waves overlap they add: crest on crest makes a bigger crest, crest on trough cancels. Two slits make two overlapping waves, so the screen shows alternating bands (interference fringes).

Idea 2 · Quantum objects arrive as single points

A detector registers one electron, or one photon, at one place. Electrons sent through the slits one at a time still build up the bands, so the pattern is not caused by electrons bumping into each other in flight.

Idea 3 · Finding out which slit changes the pattern

If a detector records which slit each electron used, the bands disappear and two plain blobs appear instead. On the standard account, getting "which-path" information is incompatible with the interference pattern. What counts as a "measurement", and why it matters, is where the interpretations part company.

What is really going on?

The mathematics of quantum theory predicts these experiments with astonishing accuracy. What it means is another matter. Three families of interpretation are on the table:

PositionSupporters argue…Critics counter…
A · Collapse (Copenhagen-style)The wave function gives probabilities, and a measurement produces one definite outcome. Asking what happens between measurements is not meaningful."Measurement" is never clearly defined, and the view seems to divide the world into observers and the observed.
B · Many worldsThe wave function never collapses. Every possible outcome happens, in branching realities.It is extravagant, very hard to test, and struggles to explain what probabilities mean.
C · Pilot wave (de Broglie–Bohm)Particles have definite paths, guided by a real wave, which restores determinism.It needs instant influences across distance, and is awkward to reconcile with relativity.

Precision toolkit: saying what is known and what is disputed

Marking established results
· The experiment shows that …
· It is well established that …
· The predictions have been confirmed to …
· Every test so far has …
Marking interpretation
· One interpretation holds that …
· That is a matter of interpretation.
· There is no consensus on whether …
· Whether … remains an open question.
