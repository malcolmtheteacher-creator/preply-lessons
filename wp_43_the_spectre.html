A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XLIII of L

The Spectre

In which a German philosopher in London turns all philosophy upside down—from contemplation to revolution, from ideas to material reality

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: Karl Marx (1818–1883) rejected the idea that philosophy could solve human suffering. Instead, he inverted the entire Western philosophical tradition. Ideas are not primary; they are secondary. Material conditions shape consciousness, not the reverse. Change the economy, and you change minds, morality, and culture. This lesson explores historical materialism, alienation, class struggle, and the revolutionary claim that the point of philosophy is not to understand the world but to change it.

I. Before We Begin

What shapes you more: your thoughts and beliefs, or your material conditions—where you live, how you work, who owns what?

♦ ♦ ♦

A radical question

For thousands of years, philosophers assumed that ideas are primary. The Platonic forms. The Stoic logos. Kant’s categories of thought. Hegel’s World Spirit. But what if all these philosophers had it backwards? What if material conditions—how food is produced, how labour is organized, who owns the factories and the land—determine the ideas people have?

Marx will ask: what if you cannot change minds until you change the economy? What if philosophy has been contemplating a world it should be transforming?

II. The Story — The Materialist Revolution

Karl Marx was born on May 5, 1818, in Trier, Prussia (now Germany), to a family of lawyers. He was trained in philosophy, law, and history. He was brilliant, fierce, ambitious. But unlike the philosophers who came before him, Marx became convinced that philosophy itself was part of the problem. Philosophy contemplates the world. But the world needs to be changed.

In his twenties and thirties, Marx lived in Paris and Brussels, engaged with radical political movements, witnessing industrial capitalism up close. He saw factories. He saw workers. He saw poverty amidst enormous wealth. And he asked: why do people accept this? Why don’t they revolt? And the answer, for Marx, was that their consciousness—their ideas, beliefs, religions, moralities—was shaped by their material conditions. The ruling class maintains power not through force alone, but through ideology.

The Inversion: Base and Superstructure

Marx took Hegel’s dialectical method and inverted it. Hegel said the world unfolds as the self-realization of Spirit moving through history. Marx said: no. The base of society is not ideas or spirit. It is material production. Who controls the means of production (the factories, the machines, the land) determines the structure of society and the ideas people have.

It is not consciousness that determines life, but life that determines consciousness. — Marx and Engels, The German Ideology

This is historical materialism. History is not the progress of ideas or freedom. History is the history of class struggle—the conflict between those who own production and those who must sell their labour. Different economic systems emerge from material conditions: slavery, feudalism, capitalism, and eventually communism. Each system contains contradictions that generate the seeds of its own destruction.

Alienation: The Soul-Sickness of Capitalism

Marx argued that under capitalism, workers are alienated—separated, estranged—from their own humanity. And he identified four dimensions of this alienation:

The Four Forms of Alienation

1. Alienation from their labour: The worker does not own the product of their work. The factory owner does. The worker labours, but the fruit belongs to another.

2. Alienation from the process: The worker does not control how they work. They are cogs in a machine. Their creative potential is deadened. Work becomes drudgery.

3. Alienation from their species-being: Humans are meant to create, to think, to flourish. Capitalism reduces them to survival machines. They work not because they choose to but because they must eat.

4. Alienation from each other: Workers are in competition with each other for jobs, wages, survival. Instead of solidarity, there is atomization. Workers are divided and conquered.

The Coming Revolution

Marx believed capitalism would eventually collapse under its own contradictions. Workers would become conscious of their exploitation. They would organize. They would rise up. And a classless society would emerge, where no one owns the means of production—all share in it collectively. This is communism.

The philosophers have only interpreted the world in various ways; the point is to change it. — Marx, Thesis on Feuerbach

This single sentence captures Marx’s revolution. Philosophy is not enough. Understanding is not enough. Contemplation is not enough. We must act. We must change the material conditions that have enslaved humanity. This is not moral exhortation. This is historical necessity. The revolution is coming whether philosophers will it or not.

An important note: Marx the philosopher and Marxist regimes are not the same thing. Marx died before the Russian Revolution. He could not have foreseen Stalin, Mao, or the horrors committed in his name. When evaluating Marx, we must distinguish between his ideas and how later revolutionaries implemented (or perverted) them.

III. The Ideas — What Marx Offers

Idea 1: Historical Materialism — The Base-Superstructure Model

Material conditions shape ideas, not the reverse.

This inverts thousands of years of Western philosophy. The base of society is economic production: who owns what, how labour is organized, what forces of production exist. The superstructure—law, government, religion, art, philosophy, culture—emerges from and serves the base. Change the base (the economy), and the superstructure will change. But you cannot permanently change the superstructure without changing the base.

Idea 2: Alienation — The Human Cost of Capitalism

Workers are separated from the fruits of their labour and from their own humanity.

This was revolutionary. Marx said capitalism is not just unfair or inefficient. It is inhuman. It destroys what makes us human—the ability to create, to control our own work, to recognize ourselves in what we make. Alienation is not a psychological problem to be solved by individual therapy. It is a structural problem that requires systemic change.

Idea 3: Class Struggle — The Engine of History

History is the history of class conflict, and revolution is historically inevitable.

Marx believed the working class would inevitably become conscious of its exploitation and overthrow capitalism. This is not a moral wish. It is a scientific prediction based on historical analysis. The contradictions of capitalism will force the revolution. Workers will have no choice. Either they transform society or they perish.

Idea 4: The Communist Horizon — A Society Beyond Classes

In a communist society, class divisions dissolve and alienation ends.

This is not a utopian fantasy for Marx but a scientific necessity. When workers own the means of production collectively, when labour is organized democratically, when goods are distributed according to need, alienation disappears. Humans become whole again. Work becomes creative and free.

VI. Your Turn — Grapple with Marx

Marx is intellectually demanding and politically charged. Choose 2–3 of these questions and think deeply.

Ideas or material conditions?

Does Marx have it right? Do ideas shape the world, or does economic reality shape ideas? Can you think of examples?

Alienation today

Do you feel alienated from your work? Separated from what you create? How different is modern work from Marx’s factories?

Philosophy vs. action

Marx said philosophy is not enough; we must change the world. Do you agree? Can understanding without action change anything?

Did the revolution happen?

Marx predicted communism would inevitably arrive. Has it? What went wrong—or did Marx misunderstand history?

Class struggle today

Is history still a history of class struggle? Has capitalism transcended this, or does it persist in new forms?

Marx vs. Marx’s followers

How do we evaluate Marx’s ideas apart from what Marxist regimes did? Can philosophy be separated from politics?

Coming Next: Lesson XLIV — The Leap (Kierkegaard)

In the next lesson, we meet Søren Kierkegaard—Marx’s philosophical opposite. While Marx says material change comes first and consciousness follows, Kierkegaard insists that the individual’s spiritual choice is what matters. He is the father of existentialism, and he takes Jesus seriously in a way no Enlightenment thinker ever did. For Kierkegaard, the truth is subjectivity. The self must choose, even when reason cannot help.
