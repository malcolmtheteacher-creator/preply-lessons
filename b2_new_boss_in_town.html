The "Honeymoon Period"

Experts often talk about a leader's first 90 days — sometimes called the "honeymoon period" — when staff are still curious, hopeful, and willing to give the new person the benefit of the doubt.

What's one advantage AND one risk of this honeymoon period?

Advantages: goodwill from staff, freedom to ask "silly" questions, a clean slate, time to listen before acting.
Risks: trying to change too much too fast, being judged on every small decision, mistaking politeness for genuine support.

Quick Check

1. Choose the best phrase: "She's only been in the role for two weeks, so it's still ___."

"a steep learning curve" — she has a lot to learn quickly.

2. "She took over the company last month and immediately ___ by replacing the old logo."

"ruffled feathers" / "shook things up" — both work, but "ruffled feathers" suggests people were annoyed.

3. Why is "a fresh pair of eyes" useful in an old company?

A new person notices problems and opportunities that long-time employees stopped seeing years ago.

🧐 What Would You Do?

Three classic dilemmas for any new leader. Read each one and decide your move — there are no right answers, only consequences!

Scenario 1

The Loyal Veteran

An employee has worked for the company for 35 years. He still uses a paper calendar, refuses to use email, and tells you he doesn't need any "fancy new systems." But everyone loves him.

How would you handle this?

Useful phrases: "I'd probably start by..." / "It would depend on..." / "I might be tempted to..." / "On the one hand... on the other hand..."

Scenario 2

The Awkward Coffee

On Friday afternoon, a senior manager pulls you aside. "Look, I'll be honest," she says. "Half the team thought I should have got your job. They're watching me to see how I react to you. So... how do you want to play this?"

What do you say?

Useful phrases: "I really appreciate your honesty..." / "Rather than pretending it's not a thing..." / "I'd love to find a way for us to..."

Scenario 3

The Brilliant Idea Nobody Asked For

In your second week, you spot a way to save the company a lot of money — but it would mean changing a process that everyone is very proud of and that's been in place for decades. Saying it feels risky. Saying nothing feels worse.

How do you raise it?

Useful phrases: "Could I run something past you?" / "I noticed something that might be worth a look..." / "I'd really value your perspective on..."

🎤 The First All-Staff Speech

It's the moment everyone's been waiting for. The whole company is in the canteen. They're holding warm coffee and judging your shoes. You have 60 seconds to introduce yourself and set the tone.

The Recipe (use 3 of these 4 ingredients)

The Move

1. Show humility
2. Honour the past
3. Hint at the future
4. Make them smile

An Example Phrase

a. "I'd be lying if I said I wasn't a little nervous standing here..."
b. "What this company has built over the years is genuinely remarkable..."
c. "I'm not here to throw the rulebook out — but I am here to ask a few good questions..."
d. "I promise to learn all your names — though I can't promise to pronounce them correctly on day one!"

Match-up answer

1-a (humility / nervous)  ·  2-b (honour the past / remarkable)  ·  3-c (hint at future / good questions)  ·  4-d (humour / pronouncing names)

Bonus: A Saying to Take Away

"A new broom sweeps clean, but an old broom knows the corners."

What do you think this means? Which kind of leader do you want to be — or is there a way to be both?

The meaning

A new person brings energy and changes things ("sweeps clean"), but experienced people know the hidden details and quirks ("the corners"). The best leaders combine both qualities.
