The Collab

Two channels, one game, and a video to plan. You both played it — now you have to agree on what to say about it, on camera, without faking it.

The Scene 🎬

You run a games channel. Not huge, but growing — and honest, which is why people watch. This week you're doing something new: a collab. Kai, who reviews the same kind of games you do, has jumped on a call to plan a joint video about Ashfall — the enormous open-world RPG that half the internet is calling the game of the year and the other half is quietly refunding.

Here's the catch: you've both finished it, and you don't quite agree. There are things you loved that Kai found dull, and things Kai can't forgive that you're willing to overlook. A lazy collab papers over that and nods along. A good one uses it — because two people disagreeing honestly is far more watchable than two people agreeing politely.

So this call has one job: turn two different verdicts into one honest video. You'll compare takes, push back where you disagree, decide the angle, and split up who covers what — all while keeping the thing that got you subscribers in the first place: you actually mean what you say.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Whose reviews do you actually trust — for games, films, restaurants, anything? What makes you believe them and not the next person?
  2. Think of something everyone loved that you didn't — or the other way round. Could you defend your opinion for two minutes without getting annoyed? Try it.

Who's who

You
The Reviewer 🎙️

Honest, a bit of a softie for a beautiful world. You loved Ashfall more than Kai did — and you're ready to say why, and to admit the bits even you can't defend.

The other voice
Kai, the Co-host 🎮

Sharp, funny, harder to please. Kai thinks the game is overrated and the combat's a joke — and won't pretend otherwise just to keep things comfortable.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the strong opinions, the softeners, and how a disagreement becomes the video.

KaiRight, camera's off — so, properly, off the record: what did you actually make of Ashfall? Because online you were being very diplomatic.
YouHa — you noticed. Okay, honestly? I think it's a masterpiece, flaws and all. The world-building is next-level; I lost about forty hours just wandering around.
KaiSee, that's where we differ. The world's gorgeous, I'll give you that — but forty hours of gorgeous and empty? For me it's a bit overrated.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Kai says that online, you were "being very diplomatic." What does that mean — and why is this off-camera chat different?

2. The two of them clearly disagree about the game. So how does that disagreement become the video — what angle do they land on, and why is it better than "another nine-out-of-ten"?

3. They split the video in two. Who takes which part, and what's the reasoning behind who covers what?

1. "Diplomatic" means you were being careful and polite in public — not saying your full, honest opinion. Off camera, with a fellow reviewer, you can give the real, unfiltered verdict.
2. They stop asking "is it good?" and ask "is the hype real?" — being openly honest that the highs are huge and the combat is poor, and letting viewers decide. It's better because a balanced, honest take is more believable (and more watchable) than a glowing score no one trusts.
3. Kai takes the combat and the story (where Kai has the stronger, harsher opinions), and you take the world and exploration (what you loved). Each covers the part they feel most strongly about — so both sound genuine.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for reviewing anything out loud — giving a verdict, softening the criticism, agreeing and disagreeing, and planning it together. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

Say it like you mean it. A review lives on two things at once: conviction and honesty. "It's good" said flat convinces no one — but pure hype convinces no one either. Real energy for what you love, and the nerve to name what you don't: that mix is the voice people subscribe to. Let your delivery carry the opinion.

🎙️ Getting an honest verdict

Phrase 1

You want your co-host's real, unfiltered opinion — not the careful public version. Which question asks for that?

A. "Did you like the game?"
B. "So, properly — off the record — what did you actually make of it?"
C. "Please give me your review of the game."
B — "…what did you actually make of it?"
"To make of something" = to think of it / your impression of it. "Off the record" and "actually" signal you want the honest take, not the polite one. A is a yes/no dead end; C is stiff and formal.
Phrase 2

Set up your opinion by admitting what you expected going in. Complete the chunk: "I went in with ___ ___, I'll be honest."

"I went in with high hopes, I'll be honest."
"To go in with high hopes" = to start expecting something to be great. It frames your verdict as a personal journey (hope → reality), which is far more engaging than a flat score.

🔥 Giving a strong verdict

Phrase 3

Give a bold, positive verdict that still admits the thing isn't perfect. Complete it: "For me, it's a ___, flaws and all."

"For me, it's a masterpiece, flaws and all."
"Flaws and all" = even with its faults. Pairing a huge compliment ("masterpiece") with an honest admission ("flaws and all") makes praise believable — you clearly aren't just hyping it.
Phrase 4

Praise one part as truly exceptional, using natural reviewer slang. Put the pieces in order:

isthe world-buildingnext-level
"The world-building is next-level."
"Next-level" = so good it's beyond the usual standard. Great for a specific strength: "the voice acting is next-level", "the soundtrack is next-level". Naming what is great is stronger than a vague "it's amazing".

😬 Softening the criticism

Phrase 5

You think the game is praised more than it deserves — but you don't want to sound like a hater. Which is the fair, softened version?

A. "It's garbage and everyone who likes it is wrong."
B. "It's a bit overrated, if I'm honest."
C. "It's fine, I guess."
B — "It's a bit overrated, if I'm honest."
"Overrated" = praised more than it deserves. "A bit" softens it and "if I'm honest" frames it as a personal, fair opinion rather than an attack. A picks a fight; C is so vague it says nothing.
Phrase 6

Criticise the combat specifically, but kindly — name the fault and note it feels dated. Complete it: "The combat feels a bit ___, and honestly it hasn't ___ ___."

"The combat feels a bit clunky, and honestly it hasn't aged well."
"Clunky" = awkward, not smooth. "To age well" = to still feel good after time — so "hasn't aged well" means it already feels old/dated. Specific criticism ("the combat") is fair; a vague "it's bad" isn't.

🤝 Agreeing & disagreeing as equals

Phrase 7

Your co-host makes a point you completely agree with. Agree warmly and strongly. Complete it: "Yeah, I'm totally ___ ___ on that."

"Yeah, I'm totally with you on that."
"I'm with you (on that)" = I agree with you. "Totally" adds energy. Cousins: "A hundred percent", "You're spot on", "Couldn't agree more".
Phrase 8

You disagree — but you respect your co-host and want it to stay friendly, not a fight. Which disagrees warmly?

A. "No, you're just wrong."
B. "See, that's where we differ — I actually loved the ending."
C. "Whatever you say."
B — "See, that's where we differ — I actually loved the ending."
"That's where we differ" names the disagreement as a difference of taste, not a right/wrong — so nobody's attacked. A is combative; C is passive-aggressive and shuts the talk down.

🎬 Planning the video

Phrase 9

Split the work between you. Complete it: "Why don't you take the combat, and ___ ___ the exploration?"

"Why don't you take the combat, and I'll cover the exploration?"
"You take X, I'll cover Y" is the natural way to divide a job between two people. "Why don't you…?" makes it a friendly suggestion, not an order.
Phrase 10

You want to grab viewers in the first few seconds, not lose them to a slow start. Which line plans that?

A. "Let's open with a long, slow introduction about ourselves."
B. "Let's lead with the hot take and hook them in the first ten seconds."
C. "Let's just start talking and see what happens."
B — "Let's lead with the hot take and hook them in the first ten seconds."
A "hot take" = a bold, provocative opinion. To "hook" someone = to grab their attention so they keep watching. "Lead with…" = start with. A loses people; C has no plan.

🔑 Make them yours

This is the language of having an opinion and defending it — useful far beyond one video. Pin three of these to something you actually care about. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"It's a bit overrated, if I'm honest." — a game, film or show YOU think is overrated. Say what and why.

"For me, it's a masterpiece, flaws and all." — something you genuinely love despite its faults.

"Let's lead with the hot take…" — your own hot take right now. Make it bold in one sentence.

The Twist

💥 You genuinely hate what they love

One more round — no script, no safety net. It turns out you two don't just differ on details: you think this game is one of the worst of the year, and your co-host thinks it's a genuine classic. You still have to make ONE video together, tonight, and it has to be honest — no pretending to agree, but no on-camera bust-up either. Start the timer and talk it through: defend your verdict with real reasons, take their best points seriously, and find a format where two people who completely disagree can both be right. One rule: the second it turns personal ("you have no taste") instead of about the game, you've lost the collab — and probably the friendship.

Before you close the tab…

Run back through what you just said. Could you honestly tick each of these?

  • I gave a clear verdict, not a vague "it's good" or "it's bad".
  • I backed my opinion with a specific example.
  • I disagreed with my co-host without it turning into a fight.
  • We landed a real plan — an angle, and who covers what.
  • I kept my honest voice — I didn't just hype it or trash it to be easy.

Any you couldn't tick? That's your target — run the twist once more and win it back.

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Not long ago, a handful of critics told everyone what was good. A film, a game, a restaurant lived or died on a few printed reviews. Now there are millions of critics, and the most trusted one might be a stranger talking to a camera in their bedroom.

That's a strange kind of power, and it runs on one thing: trust. The moment an audience senses you're hyping something you don't believe — for a sponsor, for views, to keep the peace — you lose the only thing you actually have. Which is why the honest "it's brilliant, but here's what's broken" beats the glowing ten-out-of-ten every time.

Two people disagreeing well — really listening, really meaning it — is rarer online than it should be. It's also the most watchable thing there is.

Two bigger questions

  1. When you know a reviewer was paid or given something for free, does it change how much you trust them? Should creators have to tell you every time — and would it even matter?
  2. "Everyone's a critic" now. Has that made us better at spotting what's genuinely good — or just louder, angrier, and quicker to pile on? What's been gained, and what's been lost?
