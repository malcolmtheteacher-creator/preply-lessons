The Collab

You loved it. He didn't. You're recording in twenty minutes and a review where two people politely agree is worth nothing to anyone — so the job is to disagree properly, on air, and still like each other at the end.

The Scene 🎬

The worst possible outcome is that you're both nice about it.

You and Femi make things together — a channel, a podcast, whatever it is. You've both been through the same thing this week, and you're about to talk about it in public for twenty minutes.

The problem is that you disagree, quite sharply, and you're friends. The temptation is to soften: he'll say "yeah, I sort of see that" and you'll say "no, you've got a point", and the result is twenty minutes of nothing that neither of you would listen to.

Doing this well means holding your view under pressure, changing it if he actually convinces you, and making the disagreement the interesting part rather than the awkward part.

  1. Can you disagree with a friend about something you love without it feeling personal? What makes the difference?
  2. Whose opinion do you actually trust — someone who agrees with you, or someone who doesn't?
YouYou loved it, with reservations you haven't fully admitted to yourself. Ready to defend it, and to be moved.
FemiThought it was overrated. Not being contrary — he genuinely didn't get on with it, and he can say why.
First: read the conversation and find the point where one of them actually changes their mind a little. Notice it's not a surrender — it's a concession, and the disagreement carries on afterwards.

2. That it's ambitious and flawed. Once that's shared, the real disagreement becomes visible: whether the flaws are the price of the ambition or the point. Agreeing on the facts is what exposes the actual argument.

3. To push each other on their weakest point — Femi on the pacing, You on the bias. They're asking to be challenged, on air, deliberately.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for disagreeing well about something you both care about. They work on a film, a book, a restaurant, a political argument, a colleague's plan. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. Energetic rather than careful. Good disagreement sounds like enthusiasm, not like a negotiation — quick, warm, slightly amused. If you find yourself getting gentle and slow, you've started protecting his feelings, and that's when the whole thing turns to mush. Interrupt each other a bit. It's allowed.

🎣 Getting a real opinion out

Phrase 1

The job: asking so you get the honest version, not the polite one. Which question?

A. "Did you like it? I loved it."
B. "What did you actually make of it?"
C. "Amazing, wasn't it?"
B — "What did you actually make of it?"
A and C both tell him your verdict first, and once he knows it he'll adjust — most people soften a negative when the other person has just declared enthusiasm. B gives nothing away. To make of something is to form a judgement about it, and actually is the key word: it signals you want the real answer, not the polite one.

Take it anywhere: "What did you actually make of it?" Ask before you reveal your own view. Always.
Phrase 2

The job: declaring a negative view without being aggressive about it. Complete it:

"It's a bit overrated, ___ ___ ___ ___."

"It's a bit overrated, if I'm honest."
Overrated is a very useful word — it criticises the reputation rather than the thing, which is milder and often more accurate. "A bit" softens it and "if I'm honest" flags that you're choosing candour over politeness, which invites the same back. Together they let you say something quite dismissive in a way nobody could take offence at.

Take it anywhere: "It's a bit ___, if I'm honest." — overrated, disappointing, hard work.
Phrase 3

The job: welcoming the disagreement so he keeps going. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "Good. That's better than us both nodding for twenty minutes. Go on — what didn't work for you?"
The instinctive response to disagreement is to defend, and defending closes it down. Actively welcoming it — and then immediately asking for more — tells him it's safe to be blunt, which is the only way you'll get anything worth hearing. "Go on" is doing a lot in two words.

Take it anywhere: "Good — go on. What didn't work for you?"

⚔️ Holding your ground

Phrase 4

The job: marking the exact point of disagreement, warmly. Put the pieces in order:

I think the slow startSee, that's whereis the point.we differ —
"See, that's where we differ — I think the slow start is the point."
"That's where we differ" is precise and completely unaggressive: it locates the disagreement rather than attacking the person, and it implies you agree about everything up to that point. Starting with "See," makes it sound like a discovery you're sharing rather than a rebuttal you'd prepared.

Take it anywhere: "That's where we differ — I think ___." Works in any argument, including at work.
Phrase 5

The job: admitting your expectations were not neutral. Complete it:

"I went in with ___ ___, I'll be honest."

"I went in with high hopes, I'll be honest."
To go in with an expectation is to start with it already formed. Declaring your bias makes everything you say afterwards more credible rather than less — you've shown you can see your own thumb on the scale. It also pre-empts the accusation, which is exactly the one Femi makes anyway.

Take it anywhere: "I went in with high hopes." / "I went in wanting to hate it, I'll be honest."
Phrase 6

The job: stating a strong positive verdict while admitting the faults. Complete it:

"For me it's a masterpiece, ___ ___ ___."

"For me it's a masterpiece, flaws and all."
Flaws and all means including its faults, not despite them — a lovely construction that lets you praise something wholeheartedly without pretending it's perfect. "For me" marks it as your judgement rather than a fact, which is what makes a strong opinion easy to disagree with rather than a challenge.

Take it anywhere: "For me it's ___, flaws and all."

🤝 Conceding without collapsing

Phrase 7

The job: admitting he's landed a hit — and carrying on anyway. Which version?

A. "Yeah, okay, you're probably right, I don't know."
B. "That's fair, actually. I'll give you that. But it doesn't make the point wrong."
C. "That's not really relevant though, is it."
B — "That's fair, actually. I'll give you that. But it doesn't make the point wrong."
A is a collapse — you've conceded the whole argument because he scored once, and now there's nothing to discuss. C refuses a fair hit and you'll look like you can't take one. B does the grown-up thing: concedes the specific point completely, then holds the general position. To give someone that is to grant them a point in an argument. It's the sentence that lets a disagreement continue honestly.

Take it anywhere: "I'll give you that — but it doesn't make the point wrong."
Phrase 8

The job: agreeing enthusiastically when you genuinely do — because selective agreement is what makes disagreement credible. Complete it:

"Yeah, I'm ___ ___ ___ on that, actually."

"Yeah, I'm totally with you on that, actually."
Agreeing loudly at the right moment is a tactic, not a weakness — it proves you're responding to what he's saying rather than defending a position. "Actually" signals mild surprise at yourself, which makes it sound spontaneous. If you never agree, nobody believes your disagreements either.

Take it anywhere: "I'm totally with you on that." Cousins: "A hundred per cent." / "You're spot on there."

🎯 Finding the real argument

Phrase 9

The job: working out what you're actually disagreeing about, once the noise clears. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "So where does that leave us? Because if you're both saying it's ambitious and flawed…"
This is the move that turns a scrappy disagreement into a real one. Once you've established what you agree on, the actual dispute becomes visible and small — here, whether the flaws are the price or the point. Most arguments are much narrower than they feel, and finding the narrow bit is what makes them worth listening to.

Take it anywhere: "So where does that leave us?" Use it whenever an argument is going in circles.
Phrase 10

The job: inviting them to attack your weakest point. Complete it:

"Don't let me off the ___ thing — push me on it."

"Don't let me off the pacing thing — push me on it."
To let someone off is to spare them something they deserved. Asking to be pressed on your own weak spot is what separates two people thinking out loud from two people performing agreement — and it's generous, because it gives the other person permission to be sharp without being rude. Say it before you start, and mean it.

Take it anywhere: "Push me on it." / "Don't let me off that."

🔑 Make them yours

These work in any argument you'd like to be a good one — about a film, a plan at work, or politics with someone you like. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"What did you actually make of it?" — think of something you have strong views on. Ask before you tell.

"I'll give you that — but it doesn't make the point wrong." — where have you collapsed instead of conceding one point?

"Don't let me off ___ — push me on it." — what's your weak spot in an argument you keep having?

⚡ The twist

Ten minutes into recording, Femi says something you hadn't thought of — and it's right. Not a small point: it undoes the central thing you've been arguing for twenty minutes, on air, in front of everyone who listens.

You can bluster through it or you can change your mind live. What do you say?

Before you close the tab…
  • Can you ask for someone's opinion without revealing your own first?
  • Can you welcome disagreement rather than defending against it?
  • Can you concede one point and hold the argument?
  • Can you agree enthusiastically when you actually agree?
  • Can you ask someone to attack your weakest point?

The Echo 🕰️

At a party, a banker and a young lawyer argue about whether life imprisonment is crueller than execution. It's the kind of argument that should end when the coffee arrives. Instead they bet two million roubles on it, and the lawyer agrees to be locked in a lodge for fifteen years to prove his side.

He does it. And over fifteen years of solitude and reading he arrives somewhere neither of them expected — a place that makes the wager, and the money, and the original disagreement look like the least interesting thing about the whole business.

That's what a good argument does. You go in to win and you come out somewhere else, and the somewhere else is the bit worth recording.
