Six Moves for Crisis Communication

Click each move. These are the building blocks of internal crisis communication that keeps trust intact.

1. Lead With the Situation: State What Happened Clearly +

Why it matters: Silence kills trust faster than bad news. The second people sense you're hiding something, they assume it's worse than it is. Lead with facts.

Weak: "We've had some challenges with the project timeline."
Clear: "The project is 3 weeks behind schedule. We're also 22% over the original budget."

The benefit: You've told them what they're going to find out anyway. Now you control the narrative, not the rumor mill.

2. Own the Impact: Don't Soften What It Means +

Why it matters: People respect leaders who don't minimize damage. The impact is real. Acknowledge it.

"This means we won't hit the client deadline. That's a problem — they're counting on us. It also means we'll need more budget from the company. I own that."

The benefit: You're not pretending it's small. That signals you understand the seriousness. And that signals competence, not incompetence.

3. The Accountability Statement: Take Ownership +

Why it matters: People need to know who's responsible and what they're doing. If it's you, say so. If it's a decision you made, own it. People respect that.

"I take responsibility for not catching this earlier. We had early signals we missed. That's on me. And here's what I'm doing now to fix it."

The benefit: You've signalled that you're in control. You're not blaming circumstances or other people. You're solving this.

4. The Plan: What Happens Next, Specifically +

Why it matters: After bad news, people need to know there's a plan. It doesn't have to be perfect. But it has to exist. And it has to have timelines.

"Here's what we're doing: By Wednesday, we'll have a revised timeline. By Friday, we'll present options to the client. By next Monday, we'll have made the trade-off decisions."

The benefit: You've given them a path forward. They can stop worrying about the unknown and start helping you execute the plan.

5. The Ask Up: What You Need From Leadership +

Why it matters: When communicating to your CEO or leadership, you need to ask for what you need to fix this. Be specific.

"I need three things: approval to reallocate budget from Q4, two additional engineers for the next three weeks, and a decision on what we tell the client by 2pm Friday."

The benefit: You're not asking them to solve it — you're asking them to enable you to solve it. That's leadership.

6. The Honest Uncertainty: Say What You Don't Know Yet +

Why it matters: Don't make up answers to look like you have it figured out. Say what you don't know. And say when you'll know it.

"I don't yet know if we'll need additional budget beyond the 22% overrun. But I will by Wednesday. I also don't know if this affects any other projects — I'm checking today."

The benefit: You've signalled honesty. You're not spinning. And you've given people a timeline for clarity.

Your Crisis Communication

Pick one scenario. Deliver a full crisis communication. You have 2 minutes to prepare. Then deliver the message. Record yourself.

A. Project Failure

The situation: 3 weeks late, 20% over budget, key deliverable missed.

Your challenge: Communicate this to your leadership team. State the facts, own the impact, say what you're doing, ask for what you need.

B. Team Conflict That Became Public

The situation: Two senior team members had a public argument in front of a client. The client saw tension. Team morale is shaken.

Your challenge: Communicate to your team. Acknowledge what happened, show it's being addressed, signal that it's not normal, give them confidence.

C. Data Error

The situation: Your team's analysis contained a significant error. It was presented to the board last week. You just found it today.

Your challenge: Communicate to leadership. Explain the error, the impact, what you're doing to fix it, and how this won't happen again.

Reflection & Language Bank

The Core of Crisis Communication

Trust is rebuilt through honesty, speed, and action. When something goes wrong, people don't expect perfection. They expect leaders to be straight with them, move fast, and fix it.

What kills trust: Spinning, delaying, or appearing to minimize the problem. The moment someone thinks you're hiding something, the crisis gets bigger.
