The STEAL Framework for Crisis Communication

When a crisis hits, follow the STEAL framework to manage your response and protect stakeholder trust.

S - Stop (Pause & Assess)

In the first moments, don't react. Gather facts.

What exactly happened? Who is affected? What's the scope and severity?

Assemble your crisis team immediately. Define roles: decision-maker, communications lead, spokesperson, legal advisor, operations lead.

Don't comment publicly until you have accurate information. Missteps early damage credibility far more than delayed response.

T - Think (Plan Your Response)

Develop a coordinated response strategy.

Separate audiences: Internal (employees), media, customers, investors, regulators. Each needs targeted communication.

Control the narrative: What's our story? What's the key message? Lead with facts and accountability, not excuses.

Timing matters: Silence creates a vacuum that media and social platforms fill with speculation. Communicate quickly (holding statement) even when full details aren't available.

E - Empathize (Address Stakeholder Impact)

Show that you understand the real impact on those affected.

Acknowledge the concern and inconvenience caused. Don't minimize or dismiss.

Demonstrate care for affected parties (customers, employees, community). This builds trust and shows your values.

Avoid corporate platitudes ("we deeply regret"). Use genuine, human language that shows you understand the real impact.

A - Act (Take Immediate, Visible Steps)

Demonstrate that you're doing something concrete.

Share specific actions you're taking: investigations underway, immediate fixes implemented, support provided to those affected.

Assign clear responsibility: "Our VP of Operations is personally overseeing the response."

Provide timelines: "We'll have full details by Friday. We're briefing media daily on progress."

L - Learn (Commit to Prevention & Change)

Demonstrate commitment to ensuring this doesn't happen again.

Announce a review process: "We're conducting a comprehensive review of our processes."

Share what you've learned: "We identified three systemic gaps. Here's how we're closing them."

Communicate changes and improvements as they're implemented. This shows accountability and builds confidence.

Key Principles for Crisis Communication

1. Speed trumps perfection. Get something accurate out quickly (holding statement) rather than waiting for comprehensive details.

2. Transparency builds trust. Candor about what you don't yet know is better than silence or vague reassurances.

3. One voice, one message. Designate a single spokesperson and ensure all communications align. Contradictions destroy credibility.

4. Address emotions first, facts second. People need to know you understand the impact before they'll listen to how you're fixing it.

5. Own it immediately. Don't blame external factors or other parties initially. Take responsibility, then provide context.

6. Resist the urge to spin. Media and social platforms quickly detect and amplify perceived evasion or manipulation. It makes everything worse.

Language for High-Stakes Moments

The Holding Statement (First 2-4 hours)

Characteristics: Acknowledges the situation, shows you're engaged, commits to more information soon. 2-3 sentences max.

Example:

"We're aware of the reported issue affecting some of our customers today. Our technical team is investigating immediately. We'll provide a full update within four hours."

The Internal Communication (To employees first)

Purpose: Employees hear the truth from you first, not news or social media. This prevents rumors and maintains internal trust.

Key elements:

  • What happened (facts)
  • Impact on customers or operations
  • What we're doing about it
  • What we expect employees to do (e.g., direct customer inquiries to communications team)
  • How we'll keep them updated

Tone: Calm, factual, accountable. Not panicked, not dismissive.

The Press Statement (When you have fuller information)

Structure:

  • Lead paragraph: What happened, when, impact
  • Company response: Actions taken, people involved, immediate fixes
  • Customer/stakeholder impact: Concrete support being provided
  • Next steps and timeline: When will we have full details?
  • Contact information: Who journalists can reach for follow-up questions

Key rule: Never speculate. Stick to what you know. "We're still investigating X" is better than guessing.

Handling Difficult Questions Under Pressure

Q: "Is this a safety issue?"

A (if true): "Customer safety is our absolute priority. We have no evidence at this time of safety risk. However, we're conducting a full investigation and will share any findings immediately if they emerge."

Q: "Who's responsible for this failure?"

A: "We're focused first on resolving the issue and supporting customers. A full investigation will identify exactly what happened and why. When we have answers, we'll be transparent about findings and any changes needed."

Q: "Is there a cover-up?"

A: "Absolutely not. We're providing information as fast as we can verify it. We understand the urgency and are being as transparent as possible. Here's what we know so far..."

Q: "What about your customers' data?"

A: "Data security is paramount. We have no evidence of unauthorized data access. As a precaution, we're implementing [specific steps]. We're working with [security firm] to verify our systems. We'll update everyone by [specific time]."

Language to Avoid Under Pressure

Don't say: "No comment" (looks evasive)

Don't say: "We deeply apologize" without action (sounds hollow)

Don't say: "This is unprecedented" (invites speculation)

Don't say: "We had no way of knowing" (sounds like blame avoidance)

Don't say: Anything you aren't certain is true

Don't say: Anything off the record (it will leak)

When You Don't Have an Answer

Use bridging language:

"That's an excellent question. I don't have that specific detail yet, but it's exactly what we're investigating. I'll make sure you get that answer by [specific time]. What I can tell you right now is..."

Then pivot to what you do know and what you're doing about it.

Case Study: Product Recall Crisis

Time remaining: 10:00

Scenario

At 7:30 AM on a Monday, your company learns that a customer reported an injury caused by a defective batch of your product. The injury was minor (treated at urgent care), but the customer has posted on social media with photos. Within two hours, local news outlets have picked up the story.

What you know at this point:

  • The defect is real: quality control missed a batch from your supplier
  • You estimate 2,000-5,000 units in the field, but full inventory is still being verified
  • Legal advises against admitting liability until full investigation is complete
  • Your CEO is in a board meeting in another country; unreachable for 30 minutes
  • Social media is starting to fill with complaints and speculation

Your job: You are the VP of Communications. It's 8:30 AM, and the local news wants a comment by 10 AM for the midday broadcast.

Immediate Actions (First 30 minutes)

1. Convene the Crisis Team

Call emergency meeting: CEO (when available), Legal, Operations/Quality, Customer Service, PR, Senior Leadership. Define roles and decision-making authority now.

2. Establish Facts

Operations: Full scope of defect? How many units? Any other reported incidents?

Quality: Root cause analysis underway? Supplier involvement?

Customer Service: Are we hearing from other customers? What are they saying?

3. Develop Holding Statement (for immediate use)

Your team decides this is the immediate response (while full investigation is underway):

"We're aware of a report regarding one of our products and are taking it very seriously. Customer safety is our highest priority. We're investigating immediately to understand what happened and are already in contact with the affected customer. We'll provide a comprehensive update by [specific time]."

The 10 AM Press Statement (You have more facts now)

Decision point: Do you proactively announce a recall, or wait for more investigation?

Given the public knowledge and social media spread, a proactive recall announcement actually reduces reputational damage. It shows you're in control and taking swift action.

Your press statement:

"This morning, we became aware of a defect in one batch of our [product] that was sold between [dates]. One customer was injured as a result. Customer safety is non-negotiable, so we're immediately recalling affected units from this batch. We've informed regulatory authorities and are setting up a hotline for customers. If you purchased [product] during this period, please call [number] or visit [website]. We'll replace your product free of charge or provide a full refund. We're investigating how this defect reached customers and will share detailed findings by [date]. We apologize for this failure and any concern it's caused."

Internal Communication (To Employees, Same Day)

Email from CEO to All Staff (within 2 hours of public statement):

"A serious defect in one batch of our [product] caused an injury. We've announced a recall and are supporting the affected customer. This is exactly what we don't stand for. Our quality standards clearly failed here. I want you to know: we're doing everything right now to prevent this from happening again. Every team is reviewing their processes this week. We'll all meet Thursday to share what we've learned and commit to the changes. Thank you for your trust in our mission. We're going to come out of this stronger."

The Critical Next 72 Hours

Hour 48 Update: Share root cause findings and all preventive measures being implemented.

Daily customer hotline updates: How many calls? How are we responding? What are customers saying?

Social media monitoring: Respond to customer concerns directly and empathetically.

Supplier accountability: Share what happened with supplier and what changes they're making.

By Day 7: Comprehensive report released to media and customers showing exactly what happened and how it's being fixed. This closes the crisis narrative and allows return to normal operations.

Your Response: Media Interview at 10 AM

Use the timer to prepare a 2-minute opening statement for the local news. Your goals:

  • Lead with the recall announcement (proactive, not reactive)
  • Emphasize customer safety as the driver of decision
  • Acknowledge the failure without defensiveness
  • Provide clear next steps for customers
  • Show that leadership is fully engaged and accountable

Prepare your statement. Focus on calm, clear delivery. Media will press on "why did this happen?" and "how can we trust you again?" Have answers ready for both.

Handling Tough Questions

Q: "Why didn't your quality control catch this?"

A: "That's the question we're asking ourselves. Our quality process clearly broke down. We're investigating the exact point of failure right now, and I'll share those findings publicly within 48 hours. Then we'll implement changes to ensure it can't happen again."

Q: "Should people stop using your product?"

A: "We've identified the specific batch at risk. If your product was purchased after [date], please check our website or call our hotline. If it's from an affected batch, we'll replace it or refund it immediately. If it's not, it's safe to use. We're being as specific as possible so customers know exactly what to do."

Q: "Is this an isolated incident or a pattern?"

A: "We have no reports of similar defects from any other batches. We're reviewing quality data from the past two years as part of our investigation. We take any defect as a signal that something in our system needs fixing."

Critical Decisions in Crisis

Speed vs. perfection: You can't know everything in hour one. Move with what you know. Update as you learn more.

Transparency vs. legal liability: Work closely with legal, but don't let fear of lawsuits prevent candor. Evasion costs more in reputation.

Blame vs. accountability: Don't blame the supplier, your employees, or external factors first. Own it. Find and fix root cause. Hold accountable parties privately.

Silence vs. constant updates: Daily updates (even "we're still investigating") beat silence. Radio silence creates vacuum for rumors.
