The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — Fifty studies on one desk

In the early 1960s, a young American psychologist named Bruce Tuckman was working for the US Navy, studying how small groups behave. The Navy had a practical reason to care: crews, teams and committees must work together quickly, often with strangers.

Tuckman did something unglamorous. Instead of running a new experiment, he collected around fifty existing studies of small groups — therapy groups, training groups, work teams — and read them side by side, looking for a pattern.

There was one. The groups in these very different studies seemed to travel through the same sequence. In 1965 he published a short paper describing four stages, and gave them names that rhymed: forming, storming, norming, performing. That rhyme is probably why, sixty years later, managers still remember them.

1. The US Navy.

2. He collected and compared about fifty existing studies of small groups.

3. Because the names rhyme — they are easy to remember.

Part 2 — The stage nobody wants

Forming is the polite beginning. People are careful, friendly and a little anxious. They ask what the group is for and watch to see who is in charge. Very little real work happens, although it often feels pleasant.

Storming is the difficult part. As people relax, differences appear: about the goal, the method, and who decides. People clash, become rivals, or go quiet and complain to each other privately. Many teams try to skip this stage — and skipping it is exactly why some teams stay polite and useless for years.

Norming is the agreement. The group works out how it will operate: who does what, how decisions are made, what is acceptable. Often nobody writes it down.

Performing is the reward. The team can disagree without damage and work without constant supervision. Tuckman's point is that you cannot arrive here without passing through the storm.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What happens in the forming stage?

2. Why is storming useful, even though it is unpleasant?

3. What is agreed in the norming stage?

1. People are polite and anxious; they ask what the group is for and who leads; little real work is done.

2. The disagreements are real, and a team that avoids them never reaches the later stages.

3. How the group works: roles, decisions and acceptable behaviour.

Discuss:
  • Have you seen a group stuck at the polite stage? What did that cost them?

Part 3 — And then it ends

In 1977, Tuckman returned to the model with a colleague, Mary Ann Jensen, and added a fifth stage: adjourning — the end. Projects finish, classes graduate, teams disband. People often feel the loss, and organisations often ignore it entirely.

The model has plenty of critics. Real teams do not march neatly through five steps: a new member or a new boss can push a performing team straight back into storming. Some teams never storm; some storm forever. And the evidence behind the stages is thinner than the model's fame suggests.

Its real value is different: it takes something uncomfortable and makes it normal. A team in its first argument does not think “we have failed”; it thinks “we are in stage two.” Sometimes the whole usefulness of a thinking tool is that it gives people permission.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What is the fifth stage, and when was it added?

2. Give one criticism of the model.

3. What does the model do for a team having its first argument?

1. Adjourning — the end of the team — added in 1977 with Mary Ann Jensen.

2. Any one: teams don't move neatly through the stages; a new member can send a team back; some never storm; the evidence is thin.

3. It tells them the argument is normal — stage two, not failure.

Discuss:
  • Is it useful to be told that a difficult feeling is “normal”? When is that unhelpful?

How the tool works

Five stages, in order. The arrows go round because a change — a new member, a new goal, a new boss — can send a team backwards.

Using it, step by step

  1. Name the stage your group is in — out loud, together if you can.
  2. Forming: be clear about the purpose, the roles and who decides.
  3. Storming: raise the disagreement rather than hiding it; argue about the work, not the person.
  4. Norming and performing: write down what you agreed, and protect it when someone new arrives — then, at the end, mark the ending properly.

Try it yourself

Which stage is each team in? Choose, then check.

1. Two designers stop speaking after a row about who owns the project, and others take sides.
2. Week one. Everyone is friendly, nobody disagrees, and most of the talk is about where to get coffee.
3. The team can now finish work without the manager, and disagreements are settled in ten minutes.
4. After a difficult month, the group agrees who leads meetings and that everyone answers messages within a day.
5. A new manager arrives at a settled team and changes the goals. Old arguments come back and people start complaining privately.
6. The project ends on Friday. Some people feel oddly sad; nobody has said anything about it.

1. Storming — open conflict about roles and power.

2. Forming — polite, cautious, no real work yet.

3. Performing — productive, and conflict no longer damages the team.

4. Norming — rules and roles are being agreed.

5. Storming — a change pushed the team back into stage two.

6. Adjourning — the team is ending, and the loss is not being handled.

Where the tool breaks

Teams don't move in a straight line. They loop, jump and go backwards.

The evidence is thin. It came from reading other people's studies, not from testing the stages.

It can excuse bad behaviour. “We're just storming” is sometimes the truth — and sometimes a cover for rudeness or bullying.

Talk it through:
  • Which stage is the hardest to lead a group through?
  • Should a manager cause the storm deliberately, to get it over with?

Grammar — present perfect with for and since

Stages take time, and teams talk about how long: we've worked together for two years. This is the tense English uses for something that started in the past and is still true.

Form: have/has + past participle.
“We have worked together since March.”  “She has been in the team for a year.”
for + a length of time: for three weeks, for ages, for two years.
since + a point in time: since Monday, since 2019, since I joined.
Present perfect continuous (have been + -ing) stresses the activity, especially a repeated or ongoing one.
“They have been arguing about the budget all week.”
Not the past simple. “I have lived here for six years” (still here) vs “I lived there for six years” (not any more).
Watch out: ~~I am here since Monday~~ → “I have been here since Monday.” This is one of the most common B2 errors.
