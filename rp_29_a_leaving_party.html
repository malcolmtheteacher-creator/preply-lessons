A Leaving Party

Eight years, a cardboard box of desk junk, and one speech standing between your colleague and the door. Time to send them off properly.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

It's a Friday evening and the office has quietly turned into a party. Someone has pushed the desks back, there's warm Prosecco in plastic cups and a supermarket cake with the wrong name piped on it. On the corner of a desk sits a cardboard box of eight years' worth of desk junk — a stress ball, a chipped mug, a wilting plant nobody remembered to water.

Because Sam is leaving. After eight years, a new job in another city, and today is the last day. Everyone keeps saying "we must do speeches" and nobody has done one — and you can see Sam eyeing the exit, hoping to slip away before anyone makes a fuss.

So it falls to you. A glass to tap, a room to hush, and roughly ninety seconds to do the hardest easy thing in the language: say what someone has meant to you, out loud, without turning it into a funeral or a joke. Thanks, a warm goodbye, and a promise you actually intend to keep.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of someone who left a job, a class or a group you were part of. Did anyone say anything worth remembering when they went — or did they just quietly disappear one Friday?
  2. "We must keep in touch." How many of the people who've said that to you have you actually seen since? Why do we make a promise we half-know we won't keep?

Who's who

You
The Colleague 🥂

Fond, a little nervous, secretly gutted. You've worked beside Sam for years and you're not letting them leave with a mumbled "bye". You've got a glass and something to say.

The other voice
Sam · The Leaver 📦

Touched but squirming, dreading any fuss, one eye on the door. Will pretend to hate the attention and secretly treasure every word of it.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how the speaker steadies a wobbly moment: a bit of warmth, a bit of humour, and one promise pinned down so it can't float away.

SamThere you are! I was starting to think you'd sneaked off before the speeches — which, honestly, I'd have completely understood.
YouAnd miss your big send-off? Not a chance. Eight years, Sam — you can't just slip out the back door with a slice of supermarket cake and a wave.
SamPlease don't make a fuss. You know I can't stand a fuss. If someone makes me cry into the Prosecco, I'll never forgive them.
YouNo promises. Actually — hold that thought. (taps glass) Sorry everyone, sorry — could I grab you all just for a moment? I won't keep you long, I promise, but I couldn't let Sam leave without saying a few words.
SamOh no. Here we go.
YouWhen Sam started here, the coffee machine was broken, the wifi was a rumour, and I'd been in the job precisely four days and had absolutely no idea what I was doing. Sam took one look at me, said "right, you look terrified, let's get a coffee" — and has basically not stopped rescuing me since.
SamThat's… actually completely true.
YouSo I'll be honest with you all — I genuinely don't know what this place is going to do without you. It's a real loss for us and an enormous gain for them, and it simply won't be the same around here come Monday morning. But we're not going to be selfish about it, because you've earned this, every last bit of it.
SamYou're going to set me off, you absolute…
YouNearly done. But here's the deal: don't you dare be a stranger. And I don't mean the thing everyone says at these parties and never actually does — I mean it properly. Coffee, first month, you and me. Let's not leave it too long.
SamI'd genuinely love that. You'd better hold me to it.
YouOh, I will. So — if you'd all raise your glasses. To Sam: the best colleague any of us could ask for, a better friend, and the only human being alive who ever understood how that printer worked. To Sam!
Sam(laughing) Thank you — really. Can I just say one thing back? I have loved working with every one of you, and leaving is far and away the hardest part of the whole decision. So this isn't goodbye. It's — you know. "See you soon." Now will somebody please top up my glass before I actually cry.
YouThat's the spirit. Come here. Go and be brilliant, alright? And answer your phone when I call.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Before the speech even begins, the speaker slips in "I won't keep you long, I promise." What is that little line quietly doing for the room — and why say it first?

2. The speaker makes the classic party promise to keep in touch — but adds two things most people leave out. What are they, and how do they change how the promise lands?

3. Sam ends with "So this isn't goodbye. It's… 'See you soon.'" What is Sam doing to the idea of leaving here — and why is it kinder than just saying "goodbye"?

1. It reassures a roomful of people who are secretly dreading a long, cringey speech — it manages their expectations before a single sentence of tribute lands, so everyone relaxes and actually listens. The promise of brevity buys the speaker the room's attention.
2. First, they call out the empty version — "the thing everyone says and never does" — which signals they know it's usually hollow. Second, they make it concrete: coffee, first month, you and me, let's not leave it too long. A vague "we must catch up" becomes a real, near-dated plan you could hold someone to.
3. Sam reframes a final goodbye as a temporary one — softening the finality of leaving into something that leaves the door open. "See you soon" turns an ending into a pause, which is warmer and far less painful for everyone in the room than a flat, final "goodbye".

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that carry a send-off from awkward silence to warm applause — at this party and every leaving do you'll ever stand up at. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🥂 Calling for a toast

Phrase 1

The room is loud and everyone's mid-chat. You need to gather them for the toast — without banging a glass like a tyrant or barking an order. Which line hushes the room and keeps you likeable?

A. "Everyone be quiet, please!"
B. "Right. Listen up, all of you."
C. "Sorry to interrupt — could I grab you all just for a moment?"
C — "Sorry to interrupt — could I grab you all just for a moment?"
The little "sorry to interrupt" and "just for a moment" ask for attention rather than demand it — so people give it gladly. "Grab you" is warm and casual, exactly the register a party wants.
Phrase 2

Half the room is silently dreading a ten-minute speech. Reassure them in one breath before you begin — there's a four-word chunk that promises you'll be brief: "I ___ ___ ___ ___, but I couldn't let her go without saying a few words."

"I won't keep you long…"
It manages the room's expectations before you've said a thing — everyone relaxes and listens. Close cousins: "I'll be brief, I promise…" and "Just a few words…".

🙏 Giving thanks

Phrase 3

You want to say the place simply won't function the same without them — but as a heartfelt compliment, not a worried complaint. Which one lands as warmth rather than panic?

A. "We'll manage without you, I suppose."
B. "Honestly, I don't know what we're going to do without you."
B — "Honestly, I don't know what we're going to do without you."
"Honestly…" marks a sincere, human moment, and the phrase turns "you were useful" into "you were irreplaceable" — the highest compliment you can pay a colleague. A alone sounds almost like a shrug.
Phrase 4

You want to launch into a fond story everyone in the room remembers. There's a two-word chunk that opens the memory door: "I'll ___ ___ the day you talked me down off the ledge before that presentation…"

"I'll never forget…"
The classic runway for a shared memory — it tells the room "here comes something that mattered". Relatives: "I'll always remember…" and "None of us will forget the time…".

👋 The warm goodbye

Phrase 5

You want to admit the place will feel emptier without them — bittersweet, but affectionate. Put the pieces in order:

the sameit justaround herewon't bewithout you
"It just won't be the same around here without you."
Gentle, honest, and instantly recognisable — it names the loss without wallowing in it. "It just…" adds a resigned, affectionate shrug that keeps it from tipping into a funeral.
Phrase 6

You want to admit that losing them hurts you — but frame it generously, as a genuine win for wherever they're going next. There's a neat four-word saying that does both at once. Put it together:

their gainour lossis
"Our loss is their gain."
A single balanced phrase that mourns and celebrates in the same breath: you feel the loss, but you're big enough to be happy for them. Often stretched for warmth: "It's a real loss for us — and an enormous gain for them."

🤝 Keeping in touch

Phrase 7

As they head off, you want to tell them — casually but meaning it — to stay in contact and come back and visit. English has a warm little three-word idiom for exactly this. What is it?

"Don't be a stranger."
An affectionate command to stay in touch and keep showing up. It's warmer and less formal than "please stay in contact", and carries a real invitation: the door's still open.
Phrase 8

"We should keep in touch" can sound like polite noise that both of you will forget by Monday. Which version makes it clear you actually mean it?

A. "We should keep in touch sometime."
B. "Let's actually keep in touch — and I mean properly, not just the thing people say."
B — "Let's actually keep in touch — and I mean properly, not just the thing people say."
By naming the empty version out loud, you promise you're not doing it. "Actually" and "properly" do the heavy lifting — they upgrade a reflex into a real intention.
Phrase 9

A promise floats away unless you pin it down. You want to turn a vague "let's meet up" into something real and near-dated — there's a five-word chunk that means "let's not let ages go by": "Let's grab a coffee soon, and let's ___ ___ ___ ___ ___ this time."

"…not leave it too long."
It quietly sets a deadline without naming a date, so the plan can't drift for a year. Useful anywhere a promise needs teeth: "Lovely to see you — let's not leave it so long next time."
Phrase 10

The speech is done and it's time to bring it home — get everyone to lift their drinks and name the guest of honour. Put the toast together:

raise your glassesso if you'd allto Sam!
"So if you'd all raise your glasses… to Sam!"
The traditional signal that a toast has arrived — "so if you'd all…" gathers the room into one action, and landing on the name turns a speech into a shared salute. Swap in any name and you're ready for any leaving do.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole send-off out loud using all ten, in order — hush the room → keep it short → the thanks → the bittersweet goodbye → the real promise → raise the glass. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

🌧️ The send-off nobody wanted

One more round — no script, no safety net. This isn't a happy departure. Sam didn't choose to leave; the role was quietly made redundant, and everyone in the room knows it, including Sam — who is putting on a brave face and pretending it's all fine. Start the timer and give the toast anyway. The challenge: be warm and completely honest, honour eight real years, and leave Sam their dignity — without pretending nothing's wrong, without a word of pity, and without letting the room curdle into an awkward silence. What do you actually say? And what do you carefully not say?

The Echo 🕰️

In April 1916, Ernest Shackleton stood on a freezing beach on Elephant Island and said goodbye to twenty-two of his men. He was about to sail 800 miles across the most violent ocean on Earth in an open boat barely bigger than a rowing boat, to fetch help. The unspoken promise folded into that goodbye was the biggest anyone could make: I will come back for you.

Most farewell promises — "we'll stay in touch", "I'll visit", "let's not leave it too long" — quietly evaporate the moment the door closes. Shackleton's didn't. After several attempts were beaten back by the ice, in August 1916 he broke through and rescued every single man. Not one of them had died.

So the next time you tell a leaving colleague "don't be a stranger", remember what a kept promise is worth. It's the ones we actually honour that turn a goodbye into a "see you soon".

Two bigger questions

  1. Shackleton's goodbye came with a promise he moved heaven and earth to keep. Most of ours — to call, to visit, to keep in touch — quietly fade. Why do we make them anyway? Are they worthless, or does a promise do something even when it's broken?
  2. At a leaving party we celebrate someone precisely as they walk out of our daily lives. Shackleton's men were left behind, not leaving by choice. Which is harder, do you think — being the one who goes, or being the one who stays behind and waves?
