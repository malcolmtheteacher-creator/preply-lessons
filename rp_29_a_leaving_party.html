A Leaving Party

Eight years, a cardboard box of desk junk, and one speech standing between your colleague and the door. Time to send them off properly.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

It's a Friday evening and the office has quietly turned into a party. Someone has pushed the desks back, there's warm Prosecco in plastic cups and a supermarket cake with the wrong name piped on it. On the corner of a desk sits a cardboard box of eight years' worth of desk junk — a stress ball, a chipped mug, a wilting plant nobody remembered to water.

Because Sam is leaving. After eight years, a new job in another city, and today is the last day. Everyone keeps saying "we must do speeches" and nobody has done one — and you can see Sam eyeing the exit, hoping to slip away before anyone makes a fuss.

So it falls to you. A glass to tap, a room to hush, and roughly ninety seconds to do the hardest easy thing in the language: say what someone has meant to you, out loud, without turning it into a funeral or a joke. Thanks, a warm goodbye, and a promise you actually intend to keep.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of someone who left a job, a class or a group you were part of. Did anyone say anything worth remembering when they went — or did they just quietly disappear one Friday?
  2. "We must keep in touch." How many of the people who've said that to you have you actually seen since? Why do we make a promise we half-know we won't keep?

Who's who

You
The Colleague 🥂

Fond, a little nervous, secretly gutted. You've worked beside Sam for years and you're not letting them leave with a mumbled "bye". You've got a glass and something to say.

The other voice
Sam · The Leaver 📦

Touched but squirming, dreading any fuss, one eye on the door. Will pretend to hate the attention and secretly treasure every word of it.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each, then swap. Spot as many phrases from your toolkit as you can. Listen for how the speaker steadies a wobbly moment: a bit of warmth, a bit of humour, and one promise pinned down so it can't float away.

SamThere you are! I was starting to think you'd sneaked off before the speeches — which, honestly, I'd have completely understood.
YouAnd miss your big send-off? Not a chance. Eight years, Sam — you can't just slip out the back door with a slice of supermarket cake and a wave.

SamThat's… actually completely true.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Before the speech even begins, the speaker slips in "I won't keep you long, I promise." What is that little line quietly doing for the room — and why say it first?

2. The speaker makes the classic party promise to keep in touch — but adds two things most people leave out. What are they, and how do they change how the promise lands?

3. Sam ends with "So this isn't goodbye. It's… 'See you soon.'" What is Sam doing to the idea of leaving here — and why is it kinder than just saying "goodbye"?

1. It reassures a roomful of people who are secretly dreading a long, cringey speech — it manages their expectations before a single sentence of tribute lands, so everyone relaxes and actually listens. The promise of brevity buys the speaker the room's attention.
2. First, they call out the empty version — "the thing everyone says and never does" — which signals they know it's usually hollow. Second, they make it concrete: coffee, first month, you and me, let's not leave it too long. A vague "we must catch up" becomes a real, near-dated plan you could hold someone to.
3. Sam reframes a final goodbye as a temporary one — softening the finality of leaving into something that leaves the door open. "See you soon" turns an ending into a pause, which is warmer and far less painful for everyone in the room than a flat, final "goodbye".

The Echo 🕰️

In April 1916, Ernest Shackleton stood on a freezing beach on Elephant Island and said goodbye to twenty-two of his men. He was about to sail 800 miles across the most violent ocean on Earth in an open boat barely bigger than a rowing boat, to fetch help. The unspoken promise folded into that goodbye was the biggest anyone could make: I will come back for you.

Most farewell promises — "we'll stay in touch", "I'll visit", "let's not leave it too long" — quietly evaporate the moment the door closes. Shackleton's didn't. After several attempts were beaten back by the ice, in August 1916 he broke through and rescued every single man. Not one of them had died.

So the next time you tell a leaving colleague "don't be a stranger", remember what a kept promise is worth. It's the ones we actually honour that turn a goodbye into a "see you soon".

Two bigger questions

  1. Shackleton's goodbye came with a promise he moved heaven and earth to keep. Most of ours — to call, to visit, to keep in touch — quietly fade. Why do we make them anyway? Are they worthless, or does a promise do something even when it's broken?
  2. At a leaving party we celebrate someone precisely as they walk out of our daily lives. Shackleton's men were left behind, not leaving by choice. Which is harder, do you think — being the one who goes, or being the one who stays behind and waves?
