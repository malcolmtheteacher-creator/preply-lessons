The ideas, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory first, then take the discussion question.

Part 1 — The urinal

In 1917 an artist who called himself R. Mutt sent a work to an exhibition in New York. The organisers had said that they would accept any piece from any artist who paid a small fee. What arrived was a white porcelain urinal, turned on its back and signed with a false name. It was titled Fountain.

The committee was embarrassed. The urinal was not shown, and the original has been lost. But the real author was Marcel Duchamp, who had bought it from a plumbing supplier. He called such objects ready-mades. He had not made it; he had chosen it, named it and placed it in a new setting.

It was a deliberate joke, but also a serious question. If an artist chooses an ordinary object and calls it art, has something changed? Or is it still a urinal? The question has never gone away. In 2004, a group of art professionals voted it the most influential artwork of the twentieth century.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Fountain, and who really made it?

2. What question did it raise?

1. A white porcelain urinal, signed R. Mutt and submitted to an exhibition in 1917; Marcel Duchamp chose it but did not make it.

2. Whether an ordinary object becomes art when an artist chooses it and presents it as art.

Discuss:
  • Before you read on, is Fountain art? What would you need to hear to change your mind?

Part 2 — Three theories, and a problem

Philosophers have offered several answers to the question of what art is. The oldest, from Plato's time, is imitation: art copies the world. Another, associated with Immanuel Kant, links art to beauty and a special kind of aesthetic pleasure. A third says that art expresses feeling, passing an emotion from artist to audience.

Each theory fails against the twentieth century. Duchamp's urinal is not beautiful or imitative. In 1952 the composer John Cage presented 4'33", in which the performer sits at a piano and plays nothing for four minutes and thirty-three seconds, so that the sounds of the room become the music. And in 1964 Andy Warhol showed wooden boxes that looked exactly like the cardboard boxes of Brillo soap pads.

Seeing the Brillo Boxes, the philosopher Arthur Danto asked: if they look identical to the real ones, what makes one of them art? His answer was that it is a theory, a way of seeing the object within a history of art. George Dickie later developed this into the institutional theory: something is art if the art world, its artists, curators and critics, treat it as art.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name two older theories of art.

2. What was Danto's question about the Brillo Boxes?

1. Imitation (art copies the world) and beauty or aesthetic pleasure; also expression of feeling.

2. If they look identical to ordinary boxes, what makes one of them art? His answer: it is how the work is understood within the art world.

Discuss:
  • The institutional theory says art is whatever the art world accepts. Is that a clever answer or a way of avoiding the question?

Part 3 — The banana, and the machine

In 2019, at an art fair in Miami, the Italian artist Maurizio Cattelan showed a work called Comedian: a real banana attached to a wall with a piece of silver tape. It was offered for $120,000, and it sold. A few days later another artist walked up, took the banana off the wall and ate it in front of the cameras. The gallery replaced the fruit, because what the buyer owned was not the banana but the certificate and the idea.

Then in 2018 an auction house sold a portrait made by an artificial intelligence trained on thousands of old paintings, for $432,500. Who was the artist: the programmers, the machine, or the people whose paintings had been used to train it?

These cases make some old questions urgent. Is skill part of art? Is intention? Is price? Is a work with a good idea and no craft better or worse than one with fine craft and no idea? The answers may say less about the objects than about what we value in human beings.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the buyer of Comedian actually own?

2. What question did the AI portrait raise?

1. Not the banana itself, which could be replaced, but the certificate and the idea.

2. Who the artist was: the programmers, the machine or the artists whose work trained it.

Discuss:
  • If an idea is excellent but the skill is minimal, is the work good? Does it matter who had the idea?

Grammar — passive reporting structures

When we talk about what people think or say, rather than what we know, English has a family of passive structures. They give a formal, detached tone, and they are everywhere in reviews, reports and academic writing.

Three structures:
• It + passive + that clause — "It is said that he bought the object in a shop." "It was widely believed that the original had been thrown away."
• Subject + passive + to-infinitive — "He is said to have bought it in a shop." (Use to have + participle for earlier time.) "The piece is thought to be worth a fortune."
• is regarded / seen / viewed / considered as + noun — "It is widely regarded as a turning point."
Feel the difference:
• It is said that… keeps the report as a whole clause and is the easiest to build.
• He is said to… puts the person or thing first and makes the sentence shorter.
• regarded as gives a judgement of status, and as is needed before the noun.

Art or not?

Five cases. Say whether you would call it art and which theory you are using, then reveal what each case tests.

1. A urinal, signed with a false name, submitted to an exhibition.
Institutional theory. It is art if the art world treats it as art. Nobody thinks it is beautiful, so the case defeats the beauty theory.
2. A pianist sits for four minutes and thirty-three seconds without playing a note.
Intention and framing. The sounds were always there; the frame turns listening into the work. A test for whether art must be made or can be noticed.
3. A child's finger painting, hung in a gallery with a famous artist's label.
Context. Does the label change what we see? If it does, then art is partly about expectations, not just the object.
4. A perfect forgery of a famous painter, sold for a fortune and then exposed.
Authenticity. In the 1940s Han van Meegeren's forged Vermeers fooled experts and a leading Nazi. When exposed, the price collapsed though the image was unchanged, which links this case to the ship of Theseus: history matters.
5. A portrait generated by an AI and sold at auction.
Authorship. Who is the artist? If no human intended the meaning, is it art, or only a clever tool?
