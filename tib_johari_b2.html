The story, in three parts

Part 1 — The age of the group

In the years after the Second World War, American psychologists became fascinated by groups. How do strangers become a team? Why do some groups work well while others fall apart?

In 1946, a psychologist named Kurt Lewin ran a workshop in Connecticut. In the evenings, his researchers discussed what they had seen during the day. One night, some of the participants asked to listen — and were surprised by what the researchers had noticed about them. Hearing how others saw them turned out to be one of the most powerful parts of the whole workshop. A year later, a training centre for this kind of group learning opened in Maine, and similar summer workshops soon appeared across the country.

One of them was held in California by the University of California, Los Angeles. Two psychologists who worked there, Joseph Luft and Harrington Ingham, were trying to explain to participants what happened when people gave each other honest feedback. They needed a simple picture.

Part 2 — Four panes of glass

In 1955, Luft and Ingham presented their picture: a window with four panes. It was built from two simple questions. Do I know this about myself? Do other people know it?

The open pane holds what everyone knows — your job, your sense of humour. The hidden pane holds what you know but keep private — your fears, your façade. The blind pane holds what others see but you don't — the way you interrupt, or look bored when you are only thinking. And the unknown pane holds what nobody knows yet — a talent never tested, a reaction you have never had the chance to show.

They needed a name, so they combined their own first names: Joe and Hari, from Harry. The result, “Johari”, sounds almost like a word from another language, and many people still assume it is. Luft later wrote that he and Ingham were surprised by how quickly people, both experts and ordinary readers, began using the model and changing it.

Part 3 — Opening the window

The window's main message is that the open pane can grow. It grows in two directions. When you disclose something about yourself, the hidden pane gets smaller. When you ask for feedback — and really listen — the blind pane gets smaller. As people trust each other more, the open pane becomes larger, and teams usually work better.

Today, the window is used in nursing schools, teacher training, leadership courses and counselling. A popular exercise gives people a list of 56 adjectives — kind, nervous, clever, patient… Each person picks five or six that describe themselves, and their colleagues pick five or six for them. Words chosen by both go in the open pane. Words chosen only by colleagues go in the blind pane. It can be a moment of real insight.

The model has critics. It is a way of thinking, not a scientific test. Disclosing too much can make people vulnerable, and in many cultures, private life is meant to stay private. Honest feedback can hurt as well as help. Still, the idea has travelled far — decades later, the American politician Donald Rumsfeld became famous for talking about “unknown unknowns”, which sounds a lot like Joe and Harry's fourth pane.

How the tool works

Two questions build four panes. Across the bottom: do I know this about myself? Up the side: do others know it? The aim is to make the open pane larger — by disclosing, and by asking for feedback.

Using it, step by step

  1. Choose a group you trust — a team, a class, a few friends.
  2. Each person picks five or six words that describe themselves. The others pick words for them, too.
  3. Sort the words: chosen by both → open; only by others → blind; only by yourself → hidden (at least from this group).
  4. Talk about the surprises. Then decide one thing to share and one piece of feedback to ask for.

Where the tool breaks

Not a scientific test. It's a picture that helps people talk, not a measurement.

Sharing has limits. In many cultures and workplaces, keeping private things private is healthy, not a problem to fix.

Feedback depends on the giver. A “blind spot” may be real — or just one colleague's opinion.

Talk it through:
  • Should managers try to make their team's open pane bigger? Where is the line?
  • Once someone tells you about your blind spot, which pane does it move into?

Grammar — seem, appear, come across as

The Johari Window is about impressions — how we seem to others. English has a set of verbs just for this, and each has its own pattern.

seem / appear + adjective — the impression something gives. Appear is a little more formal.
“The new neighbours seem friendly.”  “The results appear accurate.”
seem / appear + to + verb (also to have + past participle for the past).
“The shop seems to be closed.”  “He appears to have lost his keys.”
look + adjective · look like + noun · look as if / as though + clause — impressions from what we see.
“That cake looks delicious.”  “It looks like a castle.”  “The sky looks as if it's going to snow.”
come across as + adjective/noun — the impression a person gives to others (often different from reality).
“On the phone, my uncle comes across as rather cold, but he's very warm in person.”
Watch out: look like + noun, but look + adjective. We don't say “She looks like tired.”
