Asking Good Questions

B1 Intermediate Academic English

⏱️ Time: 5:00

Open vs Closed Questions

Good questions are essential for learning. The type of question you ask determines the quality of response you receive.

Closed Questions

Yes/no questions or questions with limited answers. Quick but limited information.

Example: "Is climate change caused by humans?" "Did you understand the lecture?"

Open Questions

Questions that require explanation and detail. Provide deeper understanding.

Example: "How does climate change affect different regions?" "What was unclear about the lecture?"

Why Open Questions Matter

  • They encourage critical thinking and analysis
  • They show genuine interest in the topic
  • They lead to deeper conversations
  • They demonstrate active engagement
  • They help you learn more complete information

Follow-Up Questions and Socratic Questioning

One question is rarely enough. Follow-up questions deepen understanding and show you're listening carefully.

Types of Follow-Up Questions

  • Clarification: "Could you explain what you mean by...?" "Can you give me an example?"
  • Depth: "Why is that important?" "How does that connect to...?"
  • Alternative perspectives: "Has anyone considered...?" "What would happen if...?"
  • Evidence: "What evidence supports that?" "How do you know that?"

Socratic Questioning in Seminars

The Socratic method uses questions to lead someone to discover ideas themselves, rather than telling them the answer. This is common in university seminars.

  • Questions build on each other
  • They encourage students to justify their beliefs
  • They reveal gaps in reasoning
  • They promote deeper understanding

Classifying Questions

Identify whether each question is closed or open, and whether it's a good question for a seminar.

Question 1

"Is globalization good?"

Closed question. Better version: "What are the positive and negative effects of globalization on developing nations?" This open question requires explanation and analysis.

Question 2

"How do renewable energy sources compare to fossil fuels in terms of cost-effectiveness and environmental impact?"

Open question. Excellent for a seminar. It requires analysis, comparison, and consideration of multiple factors. The answer will likely spark discussion.

Question 3

"When was the Industrial Revolution?" followed by "What were the social consequences?"

First is closed (factual date). Second is open and represents good follow-up questioning. This shows progression from basic facts to deeper analysis. The follow-up question is much more valuable.

Question 4

"Could you explain how these two theories complement each other?" "What evidence supports this connection?" "What objections might critics raise?"

Excellent series of Socratic questions. They build on each other: explanation → evidence → critical evaluation. This encourages deep thinking and explores multiple perspectives. Perfect for academic discussion.
