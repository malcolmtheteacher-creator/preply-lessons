The story, in four parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A continent up for grabs

For most of the nineteenth century, Europeans knew the coasts of Africa but little of the vast interior. They traded along the shore — in gold, ivory and, for centuries, in human beings — yet the great rivers, forests and grasslands inland remained, to them, a blank space on the map. Then, in a single generation, that changed with astonishing speed. From around 1881 to 1914, in a rush historians later called "the Scramble for Africa," a handful of European powers seized almost the entire continent. By 1914, of all the lands of Africa, only two — Ethiopia and Liberia — remained independent.

Several things made it possible at once. New medicines such as quinine let Europeans survive deep inland, where disease had once killed them. The railway and the steamship could carry soldiers and goods across enormous distances. And one invention above all tilted the balance: the machine gun, which could cut down a charge in seconds and gave small European forces a terrible advantage over far larger armies. What they wanted was not only land but also wealth — the raw materials their factories craved, new markets for their goods, strategic ports, and the simple prestige of painting more of the map in their nation's colour than a rival did.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Roughly what years does "the Scramble for Africa" cover, and which two countries stayed independent?

2. Name two of the inventions or advances that made the rapid conquest possible.

1. Roughly 1881 to 1914; Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent.

2. Any two of: quinine (against disease), the railway, the steamship, and the machine gun.

Discuss:
  • The powers wanted both wealth and prestige — money and the pride of beating a rival. Which do you think drove them more, and does it matter?

Part 2 — Lines drawn in a room in Berlin

As rival flags spread across Africa, the European powers grew nervous that their competition might lead to war among themselves. So in the winter of 1884 to 1885 they met around a table in Berlin, the German capital, at a conference hosted by the German chancellor, Otto von Bismarck. There, representatives of Britain, France, Germany, Portugal, Italy and others agreed on rules — not for the African peoples, but for one another: how a power could claim a region, and how it should notify its rivals so that the carve-up stayed orderly and peaceful between Europeans.

One detail tells the whole story. Among all the maps and the careful talk in Berlin, not a single African was present. The borders that would shape the lives of millions were drawn by strangers who had often never seen the land they were dividing. Lines were ruled straight across deserts and rivers with no regard for the peoples who lived there — splitting a single community between two empires, or forcing rival groups together inside one. The same conference handed an enormous region around the Congo River not to a nation at all, but to one man: King Leopold II of Belgium, who would rule it as his private possession.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the European powers meet in Berlin, and who was notably absent?

2. What was unusual about who received control of the Congo region?

1. They met (in 1884–85) to agree rules so their competition for Africa would not lead to war between them; no Africans were present.

2. It went not to a country but to one man — King Leopold II of Belgium — who ruled it as his private possession.

Discuss:
  • Borders were drawn by people who had never seen the land. How might that single fact still shape life on the ground today?

Part 3 — The human cost

Behind the tidy lines on the map lay a heavy human price. The European powers came to exploit Africa's wealth — its rubber, ivory, gold and diamonds — and the demand for labour fell on the people of the continent. Nowhere was the cruelty plainer than in the Congo Free State, the vast land Leopold ruled in person. There, men, women and children were forced to gather wild rubber to fill the king's quotas. Those who failed faced savage punishment, and the suffering was on a scale that horrified even other Europeans once the truth began to reach the wider world. Leopold, who never set foot in the Congo, grew immensely rich from it.

Yet it would be wrong to picture the peoples of Africa only as victims. Across the continent, many societies fought back hard. There was resistance everywhere — in long wars, in raids, in the quiet refusal to obey. Some kingdoms held the newcomers off for years. The Africans whose lands were being signed away were neither asked nor consulted; a local ruler faced with a treaty he barely understood could either sign it or face the guns. Many chose to fight rather than submit, and their resistance is as much a part of this story as the conquest itself.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened in the Congo Free State under King Leopold II?

2. Why is it important not to see Africans only as victims in this story?

1. People were forced to gather rubber for the king's profit and were brutally punished if they failed; the suffering was enormous, and Leopold grew rich from a land he never visited.

2. Because many African societies actively resisted — in wars, raids and refusal — rather than passively accepting conquest.

Discuss:
  • "Either sign or face the guns." Is a treaty signed under that kind of pressure really an agreement at all?

Part 4 — Adwa, and the lines that remain

One resistance stands above the rest. In 1896, Italy invaded Ethiopia, expecting an easy conquest. Instead, the Ethiopian emperor Menelik II raised a huge, well-armed army and met the invaders at the Battle of Adwa. There the Ethiopians did not merely resist — they defeated a European power decisively, in open battle. Italy was forced to recognise Ethiopia's independence, and Adwa became a symbol across Africa and beyond: proof that the partition was neither natural nor inevitable, and that a European empire could be beaten. Ethiopia kept its freedom, and together with Liberia it remained one of the only two African lands never colonised in this period.

By 1914 the map of Africa was almost entirely coloured in European shades. The Scramble was over, but its legacy was only beginning. When African nations finally won their independence in the decades after the Second World War, most inherited the very borders the Europeans had drawn — those straight lines through deserts and across peoples. Many of the national boundaries on the map of Africa today come directly from those European pencils. The men in Berlin have long since died, but the lines they ruled across a continent they did not know still shape the lives of hundreds of millions of people.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal. (One question asks you to order events.)

1. What made the Battle of Adwa (1896) so significant?

2. Put these four moments in the order they happened: (a) African nations win independence after WWII; (b) the Berlin Conference; (c) the Battle of Adwa; (d) by 1914 the map is almost fully colonised.

1. Ethiopia decisively defeated an invading European power (Italy) in open battle, forcing Italy to recognise its independence — proof that a European empire could be beaten.

2. b (Berlin Conference, 1884–85) → c (Adwa, 1896) → d (map almost fully colonised by 1914) → a (independence after WWII).

Discuss:
  • A continent today still lives partly inside borders chosen by strangers over a century ago. Should those lines ever be redrawn — or is it now far too late?

Grammar — Balancing Ideas: both…and / either…or / neither…nor

This story is full of pairs — two powers claiming one land, two choices forced on a ruler, two countries that stayed free. English has a neat set of paired words for joining two ideas: both … and, either … or, neither … nor, and not only … but also. The key rule is simple but easy to break: the two parts you join must match in form.

What each pair does:
• both … and — adds two things together: "Africa was claimed by both Britain and France."
• either … or — offers two options, one or the other: "A local ruler could either sign the treaty or face the guns."
• neither … nor — joins two negatives, so the verb stays positive: "The peoples of the continent were neither asked nor consulted."
• not only … but also — adds a second, often stronger point: "The powers wanted not only land but also resources."
The one rule that ties them together — parallel structure: whatever comes after the first word, the same kind of word must come after the second. Two nouns, or two verbs, or two adjectives — not a mix.
• Balanced: "neither asked nor consulted" (verb + verb). • Not balanced: "neither asked nor a consultation."
And a small agreement point: with neither … nor, the verb usually agrees with the nearer subject: "Neither the soldiers nor the general was ready" / "Neither the general nor the soldiers were ready."

1. Choose the right pair

Fill each gap with both…and, either…or or neither…nor so the meaning fits.

1. The continent was carved up by Britain France — the two largest empires of all.

2. The Africans whose lands were signed away were asked consulted.

3. A ruler facing a treaty could sign it face the guns.

1. both … and (two things added together)

2. neither … nor (two negatives — they did not ask and did not consult)

3. either … or (two options, one or the other)

2. Make it parallel

Each sentence is broken — the two halves don't match in form. Rewrite the part after the second word so the structures are parallel.

1. The powers wanted not only land but also (fix: "to get rich").

2. Leopold neither visited the Congo nor (fix: "a care for its people").

3. The new borders both split communities and (fix: "the forcing together of rivals").

1. e.g. wealth / riches — a noun to match the noun "land" (not the phrase "to get rich").

2. e.g. cared for its people — a verb to match "visited" (not the noun phrase "a care…").

3. e.g. forced rivals together — a verb to match "split" (not "the forcing together…").

3. Build the balance yourself

Finish each sentence with your own idea, using the paired words shown. Keep the two halves matched in form, then read each one aloud.

1. The Berlin Conference was both and .

2. At Adwa, the Ethiopians not only but also .

3. Many of today's African borders are neither nor .

Answers will vary — check your own. The test is parallel form, e.g.:

1. "…both powerful and shameful." (adjective + adjective)

2. "…not only resisted but also defeated an empire." (verb + verb)

3. "…neither natural nor fair." (adjective + adjective)

Hold on to these pairs — you'll reach for them in the Speak tab, where balancing two ideas in one breath makes your argument sound sharp and complete.
