# The House Voice — read this before writing a single line

This governs every role play, every series, every level. It exists because the
lessons drifted into a register that reads as American business-book English —
short, punchy, every line landing like a punchline — and that is not what this
site sounds like, and not what people sound like.

Two separate voices live in a lesson. Get both right.

---

## 1. The dialogue — people talking

**The problem, with numbers.** Gilmore (2004) counted discourse features in
seven coursebook dialogues against matched recordings of real conversation.
Per ~1,283 words:

| | Coursebook | Real speech |
|---|---|---|
| Pauses | 4 | 61 |
| Hesitations (er, um) | 8 | 54 |
| Repetition | 1 | 24 |
| Backchannels (mm, yeah, right) | 4 | 30 |
| False starts | 0 | 6.5 |
| Latching / talking over | 1 | 9 |

Scripted dialogue is roughly seven to fifteen times tidier than talk. Our
dialogues have been at the coursebook end. Move them.

**Caveat, honestly.** Nobody has proven that messier models teach better —
the evidence says real speech is messier, not that learners need it. But a
model dialogue is also the only picture a student gets of what the situation
sounds like, and if that picture is a quiz show, they will be surprised by
the real thing. Aim for talk that has been tidied a little, not talk that
was never messy.

### Required in every dialogue

- **Discourse markers**: well, anyway, look, right, I mean, actually, so, oh.
- **Vague language**: sort of, kind of, a bit, about, or something, that kind
  of thing, whatever. Precision is formal and directive; vagueness is how
  equals speak to each other.
- **At least three false starts or self-corrections** mid-sentence —
  "is this — are you still up?", "I've sort of — I've looked at it nine times".
- **Tag questions**: isn't it, don't you, is it, though, yeah.
- **Backchannels**: mm, yeah, right, oh, God.
- **At least one turn where someone loses the thread and picks it back up.**
- **At least one place where a speaker is interrupted, talked over, or has
  their sentence finished for them.**
- **Hedges**: I think, probably, I suppose, to be honest, honestly, a bit.

### Banned

- **The epigram.** No line written to be quotable. If a turn would look good
  on a poster, cut it.
- **Every line landing.** Real people say things that go nowhere. Let some
  turns be dull, redundant, or half-abandoned.
- **The antithesis tic**: "X isn't Y. X is Z." Once per lesson at most; ideally never.
- **Verbless fragments used for emphasis**: "Beautiful, quiet demolition."
  "Two halves, and both matter."
- **Americanisms.** This is British English: half ten not ten thirty, ring
  not call, sorted, a fortnight, made up for you, fobbed off, brilliant,
  gone off, queue, flat not apartment.

### The test
Read the dialogue aloud. If it sounds like two well-written characters in a
drama, it has failed. It should sound like two people, one of whom is a bit
tired.

## 2. The teaching prose — the reveals, the tips, the scene

This is where the epigrams bred. The reveals had turned into a TED talk:
aphorism, brisk explanation, punchy closer, repeat.

- **Never be cleverer than the phrase you are teaching.** A B2 student
  reading "beautiful, quiet demolition" as the *explanation* of a phrase has
  been handed something harder than the phrase.
- **Plain sentences, with the joins left in.** Use "which", "because", "so
  that", "rather than", "although". Do NOT chop explanation into short
  sentences — stripping the connectives measurably *hurts* comprehension
  (Carrell 1987). The fix for dense prose is plainer words, not shorter ones.
- **Hedge like a person**: rather, a bit, on the whole, perhaps, roughly,
  more or less. Confidence in every sentence is what makes prose sound
  like a brochure.
- **One idea per reveal.** Say what the phrase does and why it works.
  Then stop. Don't reach for the closing flourish — that flourish is the
  entire problem.

### Level-pitching the explanations
- **B2/C1**: reasonably rich prose is fine. Long, clause-heavy sentences do
  not measurably hurt comprehension at high proficiency.
- **A2/B1**: they do. Keep explanation sentences short-ish (10–13 words is
  the researched safe band) but KEEP the connectives. Plainer vocabulary,
  same joins.

## 3. What isn't changing

The things students actually respond to stay exactly as they are: the
Useful Phrases as the heart of the lesson, guess-first teaching, the two
rounds with the roles swapped, the twist, the four scaffolding blocks.
This document is about how it sounds, not what it is.

And every hard rule in `ROLEPLAY_SPEC.md` still binds — the 4th wall, empty
inputs, never giving the answer away above the exercise, exercises that
actually test, nothing untaught in an answer key.
