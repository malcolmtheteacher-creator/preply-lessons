Explaining a Declined Loan Application

B2 Level · Retail Banking · Customer Conversations

Retail Banking · Dewey 332.1

First Thoughts

A customer you have known for six years has applied for a personal loan of £15,000. The application has been declined by the credit system. The reasons are real — recent missed payments and a high debt-to-income ratio — but this is someone who trusts you and who came in expecting a yes.

You cannot change the decision. What you can do is how you deliver it, what you explain, and what you offer next.

Take a moment with each question below. Share as much detail as you like — specific examples, what you would actually say, what tripped you up last time.

1. How do you feel when you have to tell a long-standing customer “no” for something they were counting on?

2. What is the difference between a bad outcome and a bad conversation? Which one do customers remember?

3. Should you explain the full reason for a decline, or just the decision? Where is the line between transparency and over-sharing?

4. Think of a time someone refused you something in a professional setting. What did they do well — or badly?

Quick opener — pick whichever you prefer:

  • Picture the customer sitting opposite you. What is the very first sentence you will say when they sit down?
  • Imagine a colleague rushes through the bad news in thirty seconds. Why is that a mistake, even if technically efficient?

to decline (an application)To formally refuse a loan, card, or product request. Softer than ‘reject’ in banking English.
creditworthinessHow likely a customer is to repay a loan, based on their financial history.
a credit scoreA number that summarises how reliably a person has repaid debt in the past.
debt-to-income ratioHow much of a person’s monthly income goes on existing debt payments. High ratios mean lenders worry.
to reapplyTo submit a new application — often after waiting or improving something in between.
an affordability checkA review of whether a customer can realistically repay the loan based on income, expenses, and other debts.
a soft searchA credit check that does not leave a mark on your credit file. Useful for exploring options.
to signpostTo guide someone towards another service or source of help — for example, a debt advice charity.

Key Language

Useful chunks of language for this conversation. Read each phrase aloud and think about when you would actually reach for it.

Opening gently

  • “Thank you for coming in today — I wanted to talk to you in person rather than over the phone.”
  • “I have some news that is not what either of us was hoping for, and I want to walk you through it properly.”
  • “Before I get into the detail, let me say: this is not a judgement on you, it is a decision from our system based on specific criteria.”

Delivering the decision

  • “I am sorry to tell you that on this occasion we have not been able to approve the application.”
  • “The application has been declined — let me explain what has led to that.”
  • “Unfortunately, the numbers do not quite meet our current lending criteria for this amount.”

Explaining without lecturing

  • “There are two main factors here, and I want to share both of them with you.”
  • “The system has looked at your debt-to-income ratio and at your payment history over the last twelve months.”
  • “None of this is permanent — it is a snapshot of where things are right now.”

Offering a way forward

  • “Here is what I would suggest we look at together.”
  • “A smaller amount — say £5,000 rather than £15,000 — might change the picture significantly.”
  • “If you would like, I can signpost you to a free debt advice service — they are excellent and completely independent of us.”

The Scenario

The situation: Your branch. Mrs. Taylor, a customer of six years, has applied for a £15,000 personal loan to consolidate credit card debt. The application has been declined — reason codes: three missed payments in the last six months, and debt-to-income ratio above the bank’s threshold. She is waiting at your desk. She is polite but visibly anxious.

Your task: Deliver the decision clearly, explain the key reasons in language a non-banker will understand, and offer two practical next steps. She should leave feeling that you respected her time and treated her fairly, even though she cannot have what she came in for.
