2. Encounter

This tab explains the assessment task. This is not new content — it's the brief that tells you what you're creating.

The Assessment Task: Your Worldview Map

What is it? A visual or written representation of your own worldview — what you believe, why you believe it, and how you'd explain it to someone who knows nothing about you or your beliefs.

This is 70% of your output for Unit 1. Your thoughts, your synthesis, your voice.

What Your Worldview Map Must Include

Your map must address all six of these elements:

1. The God Question

Where do you sit on the God spectrum? Are you closer to theist, atheist, agnostic, or somewhere else? You don't have to commit to one label — but you need to explain your position on whether God/gods exist.

2. Smart's Dimensions

Which of Smart's 7 dimensions matter to you? Which ones (narrative, doctrinal, ethical, ritual, experiential, institutional, material) shape how you live? Pick at least two and explain why.

3. Origins

What do you believe about where we came from? Do you believe in creation? Evolution? A mix? Why? (You don't need to pick a religion's answer — this is your thinking.)

4. Death & Afterlife

What do you believe happens after death? Do you believe in an afterlife? Reincarnation? Nothing? What gives your life meaning in light of that?

5. Right & Wrong

What makes something right or wrong? Is morality absolute or relative? Where does it come from for you? (God? Society? Your own reason? A mix?)

6. You in the Data

Where do you fit in the British census? Are you religious, non-religious, spiritual? What percentage of the British population shares your position? (Use facts from the unit.)

• • •

Success Criteria

You'll assess yourself against this. Make sure you understand it before you start writing.

Your Worldview Map succeeds when:

It clearly states your position on the God spectrum (not vague, not evasive)
It identifies at least two of Smart's dimensions and explains why they matter to you
It includes a genuine belief about origins (not copied from a religion, but your own thinking)
It addresses death/afterlife and what that means for how you live
It explains where you think right and wrong come from
It locates you in the British census data (e.g., "I'm in the 52% who are Christian" or "I'm in the growing 26% non-religious")
It uses evidence and ideas from Unit 1 to support your thinking
It's 200+ words and shows genuine reflection (not rushed or tokenistic)

Model Example

Here's an anonymised example of what a good Worldview Map looks like. Use it as a model, not a template.

Student A's Worldview Map (Adapted Example)

I'd say I'm agnostic-leaning-atheist on the God spectrum. I don't see evidence for God, but I'm not certain God doesn't exist — and I'm okay with that uncertainty.

The ethical dimension is most important to me. How I treat people, whether I'm honest, whether I help when I can — that's what defines me, not belief in God. The narrative dimension also matters: I find meaning in stories, history, and understanding human experience. I don't need those stories to be about God to find them valuable.

I believe we evolved — the evidence is overwhelming — but I also think there's something amazing about that. We don't need creation myths to feel wonder at existence. On death: I think this life is all we have, and that actually makes it more precious. It means every choice matters, every relationship matters, because it's not a rehearsal for an afterlife. Right and wrong come from empathy, consequences, and treating others how I'd want to be treated. It's not handed down by God — it's something humans have figured out through living together.

According to the British Social Attitudes survey data we studied, about 26% of the UK population is non-religious and growing. I'm part of that group, though I know many non-religious people have different reasons from mine. Some are atheist-certain; some, like me, are agnostic. The point is we share a life without religious practice, even if our worldviews aren't identical.

Why this works: It's specific. It names the God position. It picks dimensions and explains why they matter to this student. It connects beliefs about origins, death, morality, and locates the student in real data. It's clearly this student's thinking, not a copy of humanism or any religion.
