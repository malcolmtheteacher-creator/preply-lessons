The time-machine principle

Section B always pairs an old text with a new one doing a SIMILAR JOB — advising, persuading, instructing, reporting. That pairing is a controlled experiment: the job is held steady, so every difference you find is change, isolated and measurable. And the question always contains a trap: candidates who write "the old text is complicated and boring, the new one is friendly" have described their feelings, not the language. Your task is to name the changes like a scientist — and to notice the continuities, which weaker candidates never do.

The old text — read it aloud, slowly

From an advice book for young people, written in the 1790s (the great age of "conduct literature" — bestselling manuals on how to behave):

It is a truth which cannot too frequently be impressed upon the youthful mind, that the happiness of any person's life dependeth far less upon great occurrences of fortune, whether prosperous or adverse, than upon the diligent improvement of those small and unfrequent opportunities which Providence is pleased daily to bestow. The young person desirous of improvement will therefore attend, First, to the government of the temper; Secondly, to the choice of companions, by whose society the character is insensibly formed; and Thirdly, to the employment of those hours of leisure, which, like the small streams that feed a great river, do severally appear of little consequence, yet together determine the whole course of the life.

Excavation — one find per layer

a) Sentence architecture: how many sentences is that first paragraph — and what is stacked inside them?

Two sentences, both enormous — clause upon subordinate clause ("which cannot…", "whether…", "which Providence…"). The term: heavy subordination, building periodic sentences that delay their point. Long, architected sentences were the prestige style of the age: complexity itself performed education and authority.

b) Lexis: find three words a modern advice text would never reach for — and one word history later "corrected".

Latinate formality: "occurrences", "diligent", "insensibly", "consequence". And "unfrequent" — standard in the 1790s, replaced since by "infrequent": morphology caught mid-change, one word carrying a whole paragraph about standardisation still settling.

c) Grammar fossils: one verb ending that has died.

"dependeth" — the old third-person -eth inflection, archaic even then but surviving in solemn registers. Term: archaism.

d) The authority machine: where does this writer's right to advise come from?

"It is a truth which cannot…" — truth announced, never argued; "Providence is pleased to bestow" — religion underwriting the advice; the reader addressed as a category ("the young person"), never as an individual. Authority is assumed from above.

e) The beautiful bit: find the simile and translate its message.

Leisure hours are "like the small streams that feed a great river" — nature imagery giving moral advice gravitas. Message: small habits determine the whole life. Keep this simile in hand — the new text is about to say the same thing in a very different voice.

The new text

From the transcript of a popular online talk about habits, delivered live and streamed (key: (.) pause, bold = stress, [ ] = audience):

okay so quick show of hands (.) who here has ever deleted a fitness app out of pure **shame** [laughter] yeah (.) me too (.) three times (1) so here's the thing the science is actually really clear on this (.) you don't need a life overhaul you need **tiny** wins (.) two minutes a day (.) that's it (.) I started with two press-ups (.) two! (.) my brother literally laughed at me (.) fast forward eighteen months and I'm doing triathlons (.) so ask yourself (.) what's YOUR two press-ups (.) what's the smallest thing you could start **today** (.) because small stuff (.) done daily (.) is the whole game

Same job, new century — find the replacements

a) The 1790s wrote "It is a truth which cannot too frequently be impressed…". What is this speaker's version of announcing authority?

"the science is actually really clear on this" — vague, uncited, and completely effective: science has replaced Providence as the era's unquestioned authority. Note also confession as credentials: "who has deleted a fitness app out of shame… me too" — the modern adviser earns trust from BELOW, by being flawed first.

b) Sentence architecture now?

Breath-length units chained by pauses: "two minutes a day (.) that's it". Minor sentences, parallel fragments ("small stuff (.) done daily"). The periodic cathedral has been demolished; rhythm and compression do the persuading now.

c) Where the old text said "the young person", this one says…?

"you… yourself… YOUR two press-ups" — direct, stressed, individual address, plus interaction ("show of hands", [laughter]). The audience stopped being a category and became a room full of individuals who answer back.

d) Modern lexis the 1790s could not have produced — three items.

"fitness app", "fast forward" (a technology metaphor from tape machines), "the whole game" (colloquial idiom), "okay so" as a discourse opener. Technology and informality flooding the lexicon.

e) And the continuity — what has NOT changed since the streams-and-river simile?

The advice itself is identical: small daily things determine the whole life. Both use imagery, both use the rule of numbered structure (First/Secondly/Thirdly → "two press-ups… two!… two minutes"), both are professionally persuasive. The genre survived intact; only its manners changed. Change PLUS continuity = the top-level Section B answer.

Naming the changes — the Section B toolkit

1 · Informalisation
The long historical drift of public language toward conversation — chatty registers in adverts, politics, advice. The attached name: Fairclough. The single most useful concept in Section B.
2 · Sentence slimming
From heavily subordinated periodic sentences to short, parallel, breath-shaped units. Quote sentence LENGTH itself as evidence.
3 · Lexical change
Latinate formality giving ground to colloquial idiom; technology coining and lending words ("fast forward", "app"); borrowings and Americanisms spreading through media.
4 · Archaism + standardisation
Dead inflections ("dependeth"), spellings and forms since standardised ("unfrequent") — the language visibly under construction in older texts.
5 · The authority migration
From assumed authority above (scripture, rank, "it is a truth") to earned authority alongside (personal confession, "the science", statistics). A change in SOCIETY read through language.
6 · Continuity
What survives: the rule of three, imagery for abstractions, direct persuasion, the advice genre itself. Naming continuity separates Level 5 from Level 3.

Diagnose the change

1. An 1800 recipe says "take a quantity of flour sufficient thereto"; a modern one says "chuck in enough flour".

2. A 1750 letter closes "Your most obedient humble Servant"; an email closes "Best".

Exam voice: the change paragraph

Model, comparing our two texts:

"The deepest change is in where authority lives. The 1790s adviser announces from above — 'It is a truth which cannot too frequently be impressed' — in heavily subordinated sentences whose Latinate lexis ('diligent', 'insensibly') and religious warrant ('Providence') assume the reader's deference; even its morphology, the archaic 'dependeth' and the then-standard 'unfrequent', shows a language still settling. The modern speaker earns authority from below: confession ('who here has deleted a fitness app out of pure shame… me too') and the era's new scripture, 'the science is actually really clear', delivered in breath-length minor sentences — 'two minutes a day (.) that's it' — to an audience that answers back in [laughter]. This is informalisation in Fairclough's sense, yet the genre's bones are untouched: numbered structure, imagery for the abstract, and precisely the same doctrine — small daily acts, the old text's 'small streams that feed a great river', decide the whole life. Two centuries changed the manners of advice, not its heart."

Your turn

Write the SENTENCE-ARCHITECTURE paragraph: subordinated cathedral vs breath-length units, with quotes from both, a toolkit name, and one continuity.

"Where the 1790s text builds a single periodic sentence across six clauses — 'which, like the small streams…, do severally appear of little consequence, yet together determine the whole course of the life' — the modern talk persuades in fragments timed to breath: 'two minutes a day (.) that's it'. The slimming of the sentence is informalisation made visible in syntax; yet both architectures serve the same rhetorical instinct, holding the key idea back for the final position, proof that persuasive timing outlives any century's grammar."

A glottal stop replacing medial /t/, and the /n/ for /ŋ/ ending (g-dropping) — widespread accent features, often carrying covert prestige informally.
