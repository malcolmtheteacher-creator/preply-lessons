Part 1 — Meehl's question

Before you read: A formula and an expert both try to predict who will do well at university. Who wins?

In 1954, a psychologist called Paul Meehl asked a simple question. Who is better at predicting, a clinician or a formula? For example: Who will do well at university? Who will stay out of trouble after prison? Who will get better after treatment?

A clinician uses experience and judgement. A formula uses a few facts, such as age, test scores and past history, and adds them in a fixed way. Most people thought the clinician would win.

The formula used four simple numbers: age, past problems, test score and job.
formula (n) — a rule or list of steps that gives a result, often with numbers.

Part 2 — The surprise

Before you read: Why might a formula be better than an expert, even when the expert has years of experience?

Meehl looked at 20 studies. In most of them, the formula was as good as the clinician, or better. Later, in 2000, researchers looked at 136 studies. In nearly every study, the formula was equal to or better than the expert. The expert was clearly better in only a small number, about 6%.

This did not mean that experts know nothing. It meant that people are not very consistent. One day an expert gives one answer; on another day, with the same facts, she gives a different one. A formula gives the same answer every time.

Part 3 — When can experts be trusted?

Before you read: When do you think an expert's feeling can be trusted?

Two scientists, Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein, disagreed about experts for many years. Then they wrote a paper together in 2009. They said: expert intuition is good when two things are true. First, the world is regular: there are patterns, like in chess or in the work of a fire-fighter. Second, the person gets quick, clear feedback after each decision.

In many areas of psychology, the world is not so regular, and feedback is slow. For example, a clinician may never learn if her prediction about a client was right. In those cases, a simple tool, used together with the clinician's judgement, can help. A well-known example from medicine is the Apgar score, a quick list of five checks that nurses use for new babies.

New words in this part — say what you think each one means, then reveal.

Feedback helps people learn.

feedback (n) — answer (a). Information about how well you did, which helps you improve.

Predictions with will, going to and might

Use will for opinions and decisions, going to for predictions from evidence, and might for possibilities.

Forms:
• will + verb — a firm prediction or opinion: I think the formula will win.
• be going to + verb — a prediction from what you see now: Look at the scores. He's going to fail.
• might / may / could + verb — a possible result: The client might get better.
Also useful:
• probably / definitely / perhaps: She will probably improve. (probably goes after will)
• won't = will not: It won't work.
• I think … will and I don't think … will (not: I think … won't).
