When people argue, are they usually disagreeing about the facts, or just using words in different ways?

Two friends argue for an hour about whether a hot dog is a sandwich. They agree on everything about the bread and the sausage, yet neither will give up. Is this a real disagreement, or a trap made of words? A philosopher from Vienna believed that many of our biggest puzzles are traps like this.

Meet Wittgenstein

Read one part at a time. After each part, close your eyes and answer the question before you check.

I

From riches to logic

Ludwig Wittgenstein was born in Vienna in 1889 into one of the richest families in Europe. Famous musicians played in their house. He first studied engineering, in Berlin and then in Manchester, where he became fascinated by the logic behind mathematics. In 1911 he went to Cambridge to study with the philosopher Bertrand Russell. Russell soon believed that his difficult, intense young student was a genius.

Why did Wittgenstein go to Cambridge?

To study logic and philosophy with Bertrand Russell, after becoming fascinated by the logic behind mathematics.
II

A book from the war

When the First World War began, Wittgenstein joined the Austrian army as an ordinary soldier. In his notebooks, between battles, he worked on his first book, the Tractatus. It ends with a famous line: "Whereof one cannot speak, thereof one must be silent." He believed he had now solved the problems of philosophy. So he gave away his fortune to his family and became a primary school teacher in poor Austrian villages.

What did Wittgenstein do after finishing his first book?

He thought he had solved philosophy's problems, so he gave away his fortune and taught in village primary schools.
III

Changing his mind

Later he decided that his first book was wrong in important ways. He returned to Cambridge in 1929 and became a professor in 1939. In the Second World War he even worked as a porter in a London hospital. His new ideas, published after his death as Philosophical Investigations, said that words get their meaning from how we use them. He died in Cambridge in 1951. His last words were: "Tell them I've had a wonderful life."

How were Wittgenstein's later ideas different from his first book?

He decided the first book was wrong in important ways. Later he said words get their meaning from how people use them in everyday life.
Talk: Wittgenstein publicly changed his mind about his own famous book. When an expert changes their mind, is it a sign of strength or of weakness?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. The rules were confusing, so the manager sent an email to clarify them.
clarify (v) — to make something easier to understand by explaining it in more detail. Example: "Could you clarify what you mean by 'soon'? Today or next week?"
2. His answer was ambiguous: "Maybe" could mean yes or no.
ambiguous (adj) — having more than one possible meaning, so it is not clear. Example: "The sign 'Fine for parking' is ambiguous: is parking allowed, or will you have to pay?"
3. The word 'cool' can mean cold or great; the context tells you which.
context (n) — the situation or the words around something that help to explain its meaning. Example: "Taken out of context, the joke sounded rude."
4. Everyone has a different definition of success: for some it's money, for others it's free time.
definition (n) — a statement that explains exactly what a word or idea means. Example: "Dictionaries often give several definitions for one word."
5. It wasn't an argument, just a misunderstanding: she said 'Tuesday' and he heard 'Thursday'.
misunderstanding (n) — a failure to get the right meaning, often causing a small problem between people. Example: "There was a misunderstanding about the date, so half the guests came on Friday."
6. He interpreted her silence as anger, but she was just tired.
interpret (v) — to decide what something means when it is not completely clear. Example: "How do you interpret the ending of the film? Is it happy or sad?"
7. With the same eyes and smile, the boy bears a strong resemblance to his grandfather.
resemblance (n) — a similarity between two or more people or things. Example: "There is a strong resemblance between the two sisters; people often confuse them."

The idea: Meaning is use

1
Take a wordChoose a word that seems simple, like "game".

4

See it in action

Here is the idea in action. Think of the word "game". Chess has rules and a winner. Football has teams and a ball. A child throwing a ball against a wall has no winner and no team. Patience, the card game, is played alone. Is there one thing they all share? Not really. Instead there is a network of similarities, which Wittgenstein called "family resemblances". So to know what "game" means, you don't need a perfect definition. You need to know how people use the word.

Wittgenstein believed that words get their meaning from how we use them. For example, the word "game" covers chess, football and a child playing alone, but they don't all share one feature. Instead of one perfect definition, there are family resemblances between them. So when people argue, it helps to ask how each person is using the key word.

Thought experiment: The one-meaning law

Imagine a new law: from tomorrow, every word has one exact, official definition, and using it in any other way is not allowed. Arguments about meaning would end, but poetry, jokes and new slang would become very difficult.

Connect

Compare

Socrates and Wittgenstein: Socrates asked questions like "What is courage?" and looked for one definition that covers every case. Wittgenstein said many words have no single definition, only family resemblances. Who is right?
Socrates believed every important idea has a core that we can define. Wittgenstein thought this search can mislead us: for many words, it is enough to see how they are used and how the cases resemble each other.
Augustine and Wittgenstein: Philosophical Investigations begins with Augustine's memory of learning words as a child, while adults pointed at things. Why might Wittgenstein think that picture of language is too simple?
Augustine's picture says each word names an object. But many words, like "hello", "maybe" or "game", don't name a thing; they do a job in a conversation. So meaning is use, not just pointing.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From William James

According to William James, how should we judge whether an idea is true?

By its practical results: by the difference it makes in real life, and whether it works.
From Søren Kierkegaard

What did Kierkegaard mean by 'the leap'?

Big personal choices, like faith or love, can't be proved first. When reasons run out, you have to choose and commit, and that is how you become yourself.
From Immanuel Kant

What is Kant's test for right and wrong?

Before you act, ask: could I want everyone to do this? If the action would stop working if everyone did it, it's wrong. And never treat people only as tools.
From René Descartes

What was the one thing Descartes could not doubt?

That he was thinking, and so that he existed: "I think, therefore I am."

Final review

From memory, out loud. Then check.

1. What did Wittgenstein mean by "meaning is use"?

That a word gets its meaning from how people use it in real life, not from one fixed definition.

2. What did Wittgenstein do after writing his first book, the Tractatus?

He gave away his fortune and became a primary school teacher in Austrian villages.

3. What are "family resemblances"?

Overlapping similarities between the things one word covers, like games, which don't all share one single feature.

4. What does 'ambiguous' mean? Give an example.

Having more than one possible meaning. For example: the answer "Maybe" can mean yes or no.
