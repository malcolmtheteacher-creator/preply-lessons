The Cure in the Ancient Book

Malaria was defeating modern medicine — until a Chinese scientist found the answer in a 1,600-year-old text, and tested it on herself first.

Today: you'll follow the discovery that has saved millions of lives a year — and you'll master the grammar of the past that is gone: malaria used to kill millions; healers would prepare the herb in cold water.
Before we start. Talk these through out loud.
Discuss:
  • What did people in your country use to do for illness before modern medicine — and has any of it survived into use today?
  • Should traditional knowledge be tested by modern science, protected from it, or both?
  • What is the oldest piece of written advice you have ever actually used?

The vocabulary of this story

Ten items. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the context

1. The disease travels in the bite of a mosquito, brings burning fever in waves, and has killed more humans than perhaps any other single cause: malaria.
malaria (n) — from Italian mal aria, "bad air" (the same wrong theory as London's cholera). Still infects hundreds of millions a year; this story is why far fewer of them die.
2. Old medical writing is full of remedies — preparations of plants, minerals and instructions, some useless, some poisonous, and a few hiding treasure.
a remedy (n) — a treatment, especially traditional or home-made: a herbal / folk / home remedy. Her team screened more than two thousand of them.
3. To get the medicine out of the plant, the laboratory had to extract its active substance — and the method of extraction turned out to be everything.
to extract (v) — noun an extract: "a plant extract". The story's hinge is one sentence in an old book about how to extract — cold, not hot.

B · Which word means…?

Three words: an ingredient · a parasite · resistant. Say the right one, then reveal.

1. Which word means an organism that lives inside another and harms it?
a parasite (n) — malaria is caused not by a virus or bacterium but by a parasite injected by the mosquito. Adjective: parasitic.
2. Which word means no longer affected by a drug that once worked?
resistant (adj) — drug-resistant malaria; resistance. The 1960s crisis: the parasite had learned to survive chloroquine, the standard cure, and soldiers and children were dying again.
3. Which word means one of the substances something is made from?
an ingredient (n) — the active ingredient = the component that actually works. Hers came from a roadside herb: sweet wormwood, qinghao.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Before giving the extract to patients, she and two colleagues took it themselves — volunteering as the first test subjects. To volunteer means…
  (a) to be selected   (b) to offer yourself freely   (c) to be paid
(b) — to volunteer (v) — to volunteer for something; a volunteer. An echo of Marshall's drink in this series' first story — she called it her responsibility as the project's head.
2. Her work was carried out in secret and published anonymously; for decades she was almost unknown. Her contribution went unrecognised. This means…
  (a) unpaid   (b) not acknowledged or honoured   (c) unproven
(b) — unrecognised (adj) — the shared fate of half this series. Her recognition arrived four decades late, and then all at once.

D · Word building & collocation

1. You know tradition. Build the set: ___ medicine (adjective) · a ___ healer · knowledge handed ___ from generation to generation.
Traditional medicine · a traditional healer · handed down. Also: to draw on tradition — what her project was created to do, systematically.
2. One verb completes all three: to ___ through old texts (read searching) · to ___ a candidate list (test one by one) · to ___ samples for activity. The laboratory's daily verb.
to screen — screen texts, candidates, samples. A screening programme. Sample number 191 is the number to remember.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick check (answer from memory first), then one question to discuss.

Part 1 — Project 523

In the late 1960s, malaria staged a comeback that frightened generals as much as doctors. The parasite had evolved resistance to chloroquine, the drug that had controlled it for a generation, and in the jungles of Vietnam the disease was disabling more soldiers than combat — on both sides. North Vietnam asked China for help, and on 23 May 1967 Beijing quietly launched a secret military-scale programme, named for its date: Project 523. Hundreds of scientists across dozens of institutes were set one task — find a new antimalarial — during the very years when the Cultural Revolution was closing universities and sending scientists to the countryside. Research continued under the project's protection, unpublished and anonymous by design.

One branch of the project took a road Western pharmacology would not have taken: back through China's own medical past. In 1969, Tu Youyou — a 39-year-old researcher at the Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine in Beijing, trained in both modern pharmacology and classical medical texts — was appointed to lead the search of the tradition. Her team combed ancient recipes, provincial folk knowledge and dynastic pharmacopoeias, and assembled a list of more than two thousand candidate remedies, which they narrowed and began, one by one, to screen against the malaria parasite in mice.

The personal price was of its era. Her husband had been sent away to a labour camp; sending herself fully into the project meant placing her two young daughters in care — one with her parents, one in a nursery — and she has spoken of her younger daughter failing to recognise her when they were finally reunited years later. She would say afterwards, simply, that the work needed doing and it was hers to do.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was Project 523 created, and what was unusual about its circumstances?

2. What was Tu Youyou's specific assignment, and what did it cost her family?

1. Chloroquine-resistant malaria was devastating soldiers in Vietnam; China launched a secret programme in 1967 to find a new drug — running through the Cultural Revolution, when open science had largely stopped, so the work was protected, anonymous and unpublished.

2. To lead the systematic search of traditional Chinese medical texts and folk remedies — 2,000+ candidates screened. Her husband was in a labour camp; she placed her daughters in care for years, and the younger one did not recognise her on reunion.

Discuss:
  • The project survived because it was military and secret while open science was being dismantled. What does it mean that the era's best research needed protection from its own country?

Part 2 — The sentence in the old book

One candidate kept flickering: qinghao, sweet wormwood — a roadside herb that Chinese texts had recommended against fevers for well over a thousand years. Extracts of it sometimes suppressed the parasite dramatically, and sometimes did nothing at all. The inconsistency was maddening, and by the standards of drug screening it should have disqualified the plant. Tu went back — not to the laboratory, but to the library.

She found the answer in a slim volume by Ge Hong, a physician and alchemist of the fourth century: A Handbook of Prescriptions for Emergencies, written around 340 CE. For fevers, it advised: take a handful of qinghao, soak it in cold water, wring it out, drink the juice. Cold water. Every standard laboratory extraction used heat — and heat, Tu realised, might be destroying the very substance they were hunting. Her team rebuilt the process at low temperature, using ether as the solvent, and in October 1971, extract number 191 achieved what nothing else in the project had: one hundred per cent suppression of the malaria parasite in infected mice, and then in monkeys. A 1,600-year-old preparation note had corrected the twentieth century's laboratory technique.

Then came the step that places her beside Marshall in this series. Animal safety data was thin, the era's clinics were chaotic, and the malaria season in the south would not wait a year. Before any patient would be asked to swallow the extract, Tu and two colleagues volunteered to take it themselves, under observation in hospital, in escalating doses. She was the project's head, she argued; the risk was properly hers. There was no harm. Trials on malaria patients in Hainan followed within months: fevers that used to burn for days collapsed in hours, and parasites vanished from the blood. The active compound was isolated and named qinghaosu — artemisinin — and, in the project's anonymous fashion, announced to the world in a 1977 paper with no authors' names at all.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the problem with qinghao, and how did the old book solve it?

2. What did Tu do before patient trials, and why?

1. Its extracts worked inconsistently. Ge Hong's 4th-century handbook prescribed soaking the herb in COLD water — revealing that heat in standard lab extraction was destroying the active compound. A low-temperature ether extraction (sample 191) then achieved 100% suppression in mice and monkeys.

2. She and two colleagues took the extract themselves in hospital, in rising doses — as project head she claimed the risk. Unharmed, they proceeded to patient trials in Hainan, where the extract cleared fevers and parasites; the compound was named artemisinin and published anonymously in 1977.

Discuss:
  • The decisive insight was reading the source more carefully than anyone had. In your field, what "obviously outdated" sources might reward a second reading?

Part 3 — Three noes, one Nobel

The drug conquered the world slowly, and mostly without her name. Through the 1980s and 1990s artemisinin and its derivatives proved themselves the fastest-acting antimalarials known; combined with partner drugs — the artemisinin combination therapies, ACTs — they became, from the 2000s, the World Health Organization's standard treatment worldwide. The global death toll from malaria fell by roughly half in the two decades that followed, a decline in which ACTs, alongside bed nets, carry a great share of the credit; the lives saved are counted in millions, most of them African children. Meanwhile the discoverer worked on in Beijing, obscure even at home — famously a "three noes" professor: no doctorate, no study abroad, no membership of the national academies, which never elected her.

Recognition arrived through detective work. In the 2000s, researchers tracing the anonymous Project 523 record — interviewing veterans, reading the unpublished archives — established what the namelessness had hidden: the cold-extraction insight, sample 191 and the first self-test led back to Tu Youyou's group. The Lasker Prize, American medicine's most prestigious award, followed in 2011, and in 2015, at the age of eighty-four, she received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine — the first Chinese scientist honoured for work done in China, and the first female Chinese laureate. In Stockholm she gave her lecture seated, and framed the discovery her own way: as "a gift from traditional Chinese medicine to the world" — while insisting, in the same breath, that the gift had required modern science to unwrap it. Neither tradition alone nor the laboratory alone had cured anything; the cure lived in the collision.

The story remains open at both ends. At one end, the parasite is doing what parasites do: artemisinin-resistant strains have emerged in Southeast Asia and are being watched anxiously in Africa — the chloroquine story beginning again, with the world's stockpile of miracles one drug lighter. At the other end sits the question her career poses to every research system: the cure for humanity's oldest killer was found by an unfashionable researcher, in an unfashionable institute, reading unfashionable books, under a programme no committee would approve today. How many Ge Hong sentences — in Ayurvedic texts, in monastery recipe books, in grandmother's instructions nobody wrote down — are still waiting for someone trained to read both ways?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the drug achieve globally, and what was its discoverer's status meanwhile?

2. How was she finally identified, and how did she frame the discovery in Stockholm?

1. As ACTs, artemisinin became the global standard treatment; malaria deaths roughly halved over two decades, with millions of lives saved. Tu remained obscure — the "three noes" professor: no doctorate, no foreign training, no academy membership.

2. Researchers tracing the anonymous Project 523 archives established her group's role, leading to the 2011 Lasker Prize and the 2015 Nobel at 84. She called artemisinin "a gift from traditional Chinese medicine to the world" — a gift, she stressed, that modern science had to unwrap: the cure lived in the collision of the two.

Discuss:
  • "Trained to read both ways." What would it take to produce more scientists like that — and does your own education system even try?

Grammar — used to & would

This story is framed by a vanished world: what malaria used to do, what healers would prepare, what science used to ignore. English has two beautiful tools for the past that is no longer true — with one trap between them.

used to + verb — past states and habits, now finished:
• "Malaria used to kill over a million people a year." (state/repeated event — no longer true)
• "Doctors used to prescribe chloroquine for everything."
Negative and question with did: "It didn't use to be treatable." · "Did people use to trust the old remedies?" (note: use, no d).
Don't confuse with be/get used to + -ing = be accustomed to: "She was used to working alone."
would + verb — repeated past actions, in storytelling colour:
• "Every fever season, healers would gather the herb and would soak it overnight."
The trap: would works only for repeated actions — never for states.
✓ "She would read the old texts late into the night."
✗ "The lab would be small." ✗ "She would have two daughters." → for states, only used to (or past simple).

The elegant pattern: open with used to to set the vanished world, then paint its habits with would: "Malaria used to rule whole regions. Families would sleep away from the rivers; every village would keep its own fever remedy…"

1. used to or would — or only one possible?

Complete each sentence; where both work, say so — where only used to works, know why.

1. Before ACTs, a malaria fever (burn) for days, and many children (not / survive) it.

2. The institute (be) small and unfashionable — a state, so choose carefully.

3. During the project, Tu (work) through the night and (test) each new extract at dawn.

1. used to burn / would burn · didn't use to survive (or: wouldn't survive)

2. used to be (state — "would be" impossible)

3. would work / used to work · would test (repeated actions — both fine, "would" more literary)

Going deeper

A note on the record: Project 523's archives were secret and its publications anonymous, so parts of the credit were reconstructed decades later and remain debated — team members' contributions included. The self-test, sample 191 and the cold-extraction insight are documented in the project record and her Nobel lecture.

What the story actually teaches. First, the collision thesis — hers, not this lesson's. Tradition alone had carried qinghao for 1,600 years without curing malaria at scale; laboratories alone had boiled the cure away. The discovery required one person fluent in both languages, refusing to let either side dismiss the other. Every field guards similar borders — between old practice and new theory, between craft knowledge and formal method — and staffs them, mostly, with nobody.

Second, screening plus scholarship. The project's power was not mystical respect for tradition; it was treating 2,000 folk remedies as a candidate library and testing them with full modern rigour — while also, crucially, reading the sources carefully enough to extract their method, not just their ingredient. Respect without testing is superstition; testing without reading is what boiled the extracts. The pairing is the method.

Third, anonymity's double edge. The namelessness of Project 523 protected the work in a dangerous era — and then cost its author forty years of recognition, while the "three noes" gates (doctorate, foreign training, academy) kept her outside the very establishment her drug was saving lives for. The series ends where it began: with Marshall drinking his flask and Tu drinking hers — two scientists whose systems demanded proof of them that the systems themselves refused to go and get. The question all ten stories leave is one question: what does your era's version of the establishment currently refuse to test — and who, right now, is quietly testing it anyway?

Talk it through:
  • Map a border in your own field where craft knowledge and formal method don't speak. Who could stand on it — and would they be rewarded or punished?
  • "Respect without testing is superstition; testing without reading boiled the cure." Apply both halves to how your country treats its traditional knowledge.
  • Artemisinin resistance is now spreading, as chloroquine resistance once did. Should the next Project 523 already exist — and who should run it, for a disease of the world's poorest?
  • Series finale: across all ten stories, name the single most expensive institutional habit — filtering, boiling, gatekeeping, forgetting — and the cheapest fix any of the stories demonstrated.
