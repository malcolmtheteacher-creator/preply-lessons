The Rise of "Dazi" Culture

In China, a new word has emerged: "dazi" (literally "partner"). It describes people you do specific activities with - but who aren't really your friends.

Wei, 28
Software developer, Shanghai

"I have a gym dazi, a hotpot dazi, and a hiking dazi. They're great for those activities. But if I had a real problem? I wouldn't call any of them. We keep things light - that's the whole point."

Min-Ji, 31
Marketing manager, Seoul

"After university, all my close friends moved away or got married. Now I'm too busy to make new real friends. So I use apps to find people for specific things - dinner companions, museum buddies. It's efficient, but sometimes I wonder if I'm just avoiding the hard work of real friendship."

Why Is This Happening?

Experts point to several factors:

  • Work pressure - Long hours leave no energy for maintaining friendships
  • Geographic mobility - People move cities for jobs, leaving old friends behind
  • Social media - We have more "connections" but fewer real conversations
  • Pandemic effects - Years of isolation changed our social habits
  • Self-protection - Deep friendships require vulnerability, which feels risky
Li Xiaofang
Social club founder, Kunming

"People today are more self-aware. They want relationships that make sense for them. I don't think dazi connections are a lesser form of friendship - they're just a reflection of modern realities. People often say they're introverted, but I've seen them stay until the end of our events, opening up, laughing, sharing."

Is this a crisis? Or are we just finding new ways to connect that fit modern life?

Language Focus A

Click each expression to reveal its meaning, form, and an example. Then try to use it yourself!

"to keep things light"

Click to reveal
MEANING

To avoid serious, deep, or emotional topics; to keep conversations and relationships casual and superficial

FORM

Subject + keep(s) things light (with someone / in a relationship)

REGISTER

Informal, conversational. Common in discussions about relationships and social dynamics.

"We've been colleagues for two years, but we keep things light - just work chat and lunch sometimes."
"He prefers to keep things light on first dates - nothing too personal."
NOW YOU TRY:

Think of a relationship in your life where you keep things light. Why do you do that? Tell your teacher.

"to confide in someone"

Click to reveal
MEANING

To tell someone your private thoughts, feelings, or secrets because you trust them

FORM

Subject + confide(s) in + person
Past: confided | Can also use: confide something to someone

REGISTER

Neutral to slightly formal. Often used when discussing trust and emotional intimacy.

"She's the only person I confide in about my problems at work."
"I've never confided in my parents about my relationship difficulties."
NOW YOU TRY:

Who do you confide in most? Or do you find it difficult to confide in anyone? Explain to your teacher.

"to drift apart"

Click to reveal
MEANING

To gradually become less close to someone over time, often without any specific conflict or reason

FORM

Subject + drift(s) apart (from someone)
Past: drifted apart | Progressive: have been drifting apart

REGISTER

Neutral. Very common when discussing how friendships naturally end.

"We were best friends in university, but after graduation we just drifted apart."
"I've noticed we've been drifting apart since she got that new job."
NOW YOU TRY:

Have you ever drifted apart from someone you were once close to? What happened?

Language Focus B

Two more expressions to complete your toolkit for discussing modern friendship.

"to put yourself out there"

Click to reveal
MEANING

To take social risks; to make yourself vulnerable by trying to meet new people or share your true self

FORM

Subject + put(s) yourself/themselves out there
Often used with: need to, willing to, scared to, hard to

REGISTER

Informal. Common in advice about dating and making friends. Often implies courage.

"If you want to make new friends, you have to put yourself out there - join clubs, start conversations."
"She's naturally shy, so it's hard for her to put herself out there."
NOW YOU TRY:

Are you good at putting yourself out there, or do you find it difficult? Give an example.

"to be there for someone"

Click to reveal
MEANING

To support someone emotionally, especially during difficult times; to be available and reliable when they need you

FORM

Subject + be there for + person
Often used with: always, when you need me, through thick and thin

REGISTER

Informal but meaningful. Shows emotional commitment. Very common in discussions about friendship quality.

"A true friend is someone who will be there for you when things go wrong, not just when life is easy."
"I know I can count on her - she's always been there for me."
NOW YOU TRY:

Think of someone who has been there for you during a difficult time. What did they do?

Your 5 Expressions

  2. to confide in someone - share secrets/feelings with trust
  3. to drift apart - gradually become less close
  4. to put yourself out there - take social risks
  5. to be there for someone - support emotionally
