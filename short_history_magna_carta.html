Magna Carta

A failed peace treaty between a broke king and his angry barons, which became the most famous document in the English-speaking world.

Today: you'll tell the story of a peace deal that collapsed in ten weeks and outlived everyone who sealed it — and you'll master the legal modals (shall, must, may, be entitled to, be required to), the words that turn a sentence into a rule binding a king.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A king who lost everything

John was the youngest son of Henry II, born into a family so quarrelsome that its own chroniclers compared it to a nest of vipers. As the last-born, he inherited nothing at first, and the nickname stuck to him for life: John Lackland. Yet by 1199, when his brother Richard the Lionheart died of a crossbow wound in France, every obstacle had conveniently disappeared, and John took the throne of England. He inherited not a country but a sprawling patchwork: England, plus Normandy, Anjou, Maine and lands stretching towards the Pyrenees, held together by nothing more solid than feudal loyalty and the sword. Under the feudal system land was not owned outright but held from a lord in exchange for service, and the greatest lords of all — the barons — held theirs from the king. In theory this bound the kingdom together. In practice a king who failed to lead, failed to win, or failed to respect his barons' customs was a king with a problem. John would manage, over sixteen bruising years, to do all three.

The collapse came quickly. Between 1202 and 1204, Philip Augustus of France stripped John of Normandy and most of his continental inheritance. It was a catastrophe of a particular kind: the barons of England held lands on both sides of the Channel, and many now lost estates their families had held since the Norman conquest. Worse, John did not accept the loss. He spent the next decade on one obsessive project: assembling enough money to take it all back. And money, in medieval England, meant squeezing the barons. He levied scutage, the payment a lord made instead of turning up for war, more often and more heavily than any king before him. He sold justice, sold offices and sold widows in marriage. He seized the wardship of noble children and drained their estates. He demanded crushing fines and took hostages from families he distrusted. Each, in isolation, was something a king was entitled to do. Together, pushed to their limits, they felt like something new: the arbitrary use of royal power, restrained by nothing but the king's appetite.

Then John picked a fight with Rome. When the monks of Canterbury and the king backed different candidates for archbishop, Pope Innocent III cut through the dispute by appointing his own man, Stephen Langton — a distinguished scholar who would prove one of the key figures at Runnymede. John refused to receive him. In 1208 the Pope placed England under an interdict: church doors were shut, and for years the ordinary business of Christian life — funerals in consecrated ground, the sacraments, the bells — was suspended. In 1209 John was excommunicated. He responded by seizing Church property and growing rich on it, until in 1213, facing invasion, he submitted spectacularly, surrendering England to the Pope and receiving it back as a papal fief — a clever move that would matter enormously later. In 1214 the money finally bought a campaign to recover Normandy — and John's allies were smashed at Bouvines. He came home defeated, broke, and detested. The grievances of a generation now had nothing left to fear.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the loss of Normandy matter so much to the English barons themselves?

2. How did John's quarrel with Innocent III end, and why was that ending useful to him?

1. Many baronial families held estates on both sides of the Channel and lost their continental lands — and John's determination to win them back meant years of scutage, fines and taxation at home.

2. After the interdict of 1208 and his excommunication in 1209, he submitted in 1213, surrendering England to the Pope and taking it back as a papal fief — giving the Pope a reason to defend him later.

Discuss:
  • Almost everything John did to raise money was legal under feudal custom. Is a ruler who pushes lawful powers to their absolute limit behaving better than one who simply breaks the law, or worse?

Part 2 — The meadow at Runnymede

In the spring of 1215 the northern barons renounced their allegiance. This was no peasant rising: it was a rebellion of the men closest to the throne, and its decisive moment came in May, when London opened its gates to them. With his capital gone and his army unreliable, John had to negotiate. Archbishop Langton — the man he had fought so hard to keep out — helped broker the talks. In June 1215 the two sides met in a meadow at Runnymede, on the Thames between the royal castle at Windsor and the rebel camp at Staines: neutral ground, chosen because neither side trusted the other indoors. There John agreed to a charter of liberties. He did not sign it; medieval kings authenticated documents with a great seal in wax, and that is what was done. Nobody there called it Magna Carta. The name — the Great Charter — came later, to distinguish the big document from the smaller Charter of the Forest issued alongside a version of it. Copies were written out by hand and sent across the kingdom to be read aloud.

Read today, most of the charter is startlingly narrow. Its sixty-odd clauses list the specific grievances of a feudal elite, set down with the precision of men who had been cheated. No scutage shall be levied without the common counsel of the realm. A widow shall not be forced to remarry. An heir shall pay a fixed and reasonable sum to inherit, not whatever the king demands. One clause states that no scutage or aid shall be levied except by the common counsel of the realm — a narrow, practical rule about consent to taxation later read as the ancestor of "no taxation without representation". There are clauses on debts to Jewish moneylenders, on standard measures for wine and cloth, on the removal of foreign mercenaries, and — famously — on the pulling down of fish weirs from the Thames and the Medway. The first clause guarantees the freedom of the English Church; another confirms the ancient liberties of the city of London. This is not a philosophy of human rights. It is a peace deal drafted by aggrieved landowners about wardship, inheritance, forests and money.

And yet, buried in that list, two clauses would outlive everything around them. One declares that no free man shall be seized, imprisoned, stripped of his rights or possessions, outlawed or exiled, nor will the king proceed against him by force, except by the lawful judgement of his equals or by the law of the land. The other, shorter still, promises that to no one will the king sell, deny or delay right or justice. But the limit must be faced honestly: free man — liber homo in the Latin — excluded the great majority of the English population, unfree villeins bound to their lord's land and outside the protection described. The charter also carried a remarkable enforcement clause: twenty-five barons were empowered to seize the king's castles if he broke his word. No monarch could live with that. Within ten weeks John had appealed to his new protector in Rome, and Innocent III annulled the charter, denouncing it as shameful and extorted by force. Civil war resumed, a French prince was invited in, and in October 1216 John died of dysentery with his kingdom torn apart. The charter he had sealed was already, on paper, dead.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What do most of the charter's clauses deal with — and who were they written for?

2. Why did the charter fail almost at once?

1. The feudal grievances of a landholding elite: scutage and consent to taxation, inheritance payments, wardship and widows, the liberties of the Church and of London — even fish weirs. A peace deal for barons, not universal rights.

2. It let twenty-five barons seize royal castles if John broke it — unacceptable to any king. He appealed to Innocent III, who annulled it in about ten weeks as extorted by force; civil war restarted and John died in 1216.

Discuss:
  • The most famous clause protects the "free man" — and leaves out most people living in England at the time. Does a document that protects only the powerful deserve celebrating for the principle it happened to state?

Part 3 — A failed treaty that would not die

What saved Magna Carta was John's death. His heir, Henry III, was a boy of nine, and the men governing for him needed to win a civil war they were losing. So they did something shrewd: they reissued the charter in the child king's name, stripped of the clause that had made it intolerable, turning the rebels' demands into the government's own programme. It came again in 1217, and once more in 1225 in the definitive version that actually entered English law. In 1297 Edward I confirmed that text on the statute roll, where a small part of it remains today. A statute of Edward III in 1354 restated the great clause in words that would echo down the centuries, replacing "the law of the land" with a new phrase: due process of law. Slowly, by repetition and reissue, a failed treaty became furniture — an ordinary, half-forgotten part of the legal landscape. Of the copies written out in 1215, four survive today: two in the British Library and one each at Lincoln and Salisbury cathedrals.

Its second life began four centuries after Runnymede, and it was largely an act of creative reinterpretation. In the early seventeenth century, English lawyers looking for weapons against the Stuart kings' claim to rule by divine right reached back and found one. The greatest of them, Sir Edward Coke, argued that Magna Carta was not a grant of favours by a king at all but a declaration of ancient English liberties that had always existed and that no monarch could touch. He treated its clauses as binding precedent, arguing that arbitrary imprisonment without cause shown was unlawful — an argument that fed directly into the Petition of Right of 1628. Historians have pointed out ever since that this reading is generous to the point of invention: Coke read a thirteenth-century feudal bargain as though it were a constitution, and the liberties he found there were substantially his own. But myths do work in the world. Coke's Magna Carta was the one that crossed the Atlantic in the charters of the American colonies, and the one invoked against tyranny by people who had never read a word of the original Latin.

From there the echoes multiply. The English Bill of Rights of 1689 grew from the same seventeenth-century struggle. American colonists protesting against taxation reached for the charter's language, and the principle later surfaced in the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution, which enshrine the guarantee that no person shall be deprived of life, liberty or property without due process of law. In 1948 the drafters of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights placed themselves in the same tradition, and Eleanor Roosevelt described the Declaration as a Magna Carta for all mankind. In England, meanwhile, almost all of it has quietly been repealed; only three clauses of the 1225 text still stand, including the one about the free man. That is the honest shape of the story. Magna Carta did not create democracy, did not mention it, and was annulled within weeks. Its power lies in something stranger: the idea, written down in desperation by men pursuing their own interests, that the ruler is beneath the law and may not simply do as he pleases.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did a charter annulled in 1215 end up in English law?

2. What did Edward Coke do with Magna Carta, and why do historians treat his version cautiously?

1. After John's death it was reissued for the boy king Henry III, without the enforcement clause, to win the civil war — then again in 1217 and definitively in 1225. Edward I confirmed it in 1297; a statute of 1354 restated its great clause as "due process of law".

2. He reinterpreted it as a declaration of ancient liberties no king could remove and used it as precedent against the Stuarts, feeding the Petition of Right of 1628. Historians note the reading was largely invented centuries later.

Discuss:
  • Magna Carta matters for what people later decided it meant, not for what it said. Does that make its influence less real — or is that how all founding documents work?

Grammar — legal modals

Magna Carta is not a story told in the past simple. It is a set of rules — and rules have their own grammar. No free man shall be seized… A widow shall not be required to remarry… No scutage may be levied without the common counsel of the realm. These legal modals still run through constitutions, contracts and terms of service today.

How they are formed:
• shall + bare infinitive — a binding rule, not a prediction: "Justice shall not be sold." Very common in the passive: shall be + past participle.
• must + bare infinitive — strong necessity: "The king must follow the law of the land."
• may + bare infinitive — permission; with not, prohibition: "No fine may be levied without consent."
• be entitled to + noun or to-infinitive — a right you hold: "Every free man is entitled to a fair trial" / "…is entitled to be judged by his equals."
• be required to + to-infinitive — a duty imposed from outside: "Barons were required to pay scutage."
• No one / No free man + shall — the negative goes into the subject: "No one shall be imprisoned", never "Everyone shall not be imprisoned".
When we choose each one:
• shall states the rule in the voice of the document: formal, old, and kept by lawyers for exactly that reason.
• must states an obligation plainly, and works in speech: "The state must prove its case."
• may = permitted, may not = forbidden: "The king may levy scutage with consent" but "may not levy it without consent."
• be entitled to puts the right-holder first, be required to the duty-bearer: "An accused person is entitled to due process" ↔ "The Crown is required to follow it."
• Register matters: these belong to charters and contracts. In conversation you'd say "he had to", "he wasn't allowed to", "he had the right to".

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Magna Carta says nothing about democracy, equality or ordinary people, yet it is invoked in defence of all three. Why do societies need an ancient document to point at, rather than writing a better one now?
  2. The barons at Runnymede were defending their own privileges, not liberty. Does it matter why a principle gets written down, if it protects others later?
  3. Only three clauses still stand in English law, and Britain has no written constitution. Is a country better protected by a document that enshrines its rights, or by centuries of accumulated precedent?
