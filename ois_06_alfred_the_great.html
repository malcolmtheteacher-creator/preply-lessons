I. Three Lessons Ago...

Let's walk back through Roman Britain one more time before we meet the king who would save Saxon England.

Quick Recall: Roman Britain (Lesson 3)

Without looking back, answer these:

Hadrian's Wall: Built around 122 AD to mark the edge of Roman Britain. A stone and turf fortification stretching 73 miles across northern England, from the Solway Firth to the River Tyne. It divided Roman-controlled Britain from the unconquered Picts beyond.

St Alban: A Roman soldier who became the first Christian martyr in Britain (around 250 AD). He sheltered a Christian priest, was discovered, and was executed on a hillside near Verulamium (modern St Albans). His shrine became one of the most important pilgrimage sites in medieval Britain.

Roman Departure: Around 410 AD, the Roman legions withdrew from Britain to defend Rome itself against barbarian invasions. The Romanised Britons, no longer protected by soldiers, had to defend themselves against raids from Angles, Saxons, and other Germanic peoples.

The Bridge: From Chaos to the Warrior King

After the Romans left in 410 AD, Britain fell into the Dark Ages. Germanic peoples — Angles, Saxons, Jutes — invaded from what is now Germany and Denmark, gradually pushing the Britons westward. For centuries, there was no unified kingdom. Just scattered kingdoms, constant warfare, and raids from across the sea.

But in the 870s, something new appeared on the horizon: not more invaders, but a different kind of enemy. The Danes. Vikings who would reshape English history and finally provoke a king brilliant enough, desperate enough, and stubborn enough to stop them.

That king was Alfred the Great. He is the only English monarch ever given that title.

II. Alfred's Crisis — The Burning Years

When Alfred Became King

Alfred was born in 849 AD. He was never expected to be king. He was the youngest of four brothers, and when he was a boy, his father took him to Rome to receive the Pope's blessing. But life had other plans.

One by one his brothers died, and at last, when Alfred was twenty-two years old, the crown fell to him. But it was no easy crown to wear, for at this very time the Danes were ravaging and destroying the kingdom of Wessex. They were fierce, wild people who delighted in fighting and bloodshed. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XV
The historian's note: Alfred became King of Wessex in 871 AD, not 849. He was actually 22 when he inherited. The "Great Heathen Army" had already invaded England in 865 AD, and by 871, most English kingdoms had fallen to Danish control. Alfred inherited a kingdom under siege.

The Great Heathen Army

Marshall paints a vivid picture of what Alfred faced:

The Danes were a fierce and wild people. They delighted in fighting and bloodshed. They sailed across the sea in their dragon ships, and whenever they could find a coast town, they would attack it, kill the people, and carry off their gold and treasures. They were heathen, and they cared nothing for the Christians or their faith. Nothing stopped them. They burned the churches, killed the monks and priests, and scattered the holy books to the winds. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XV

For nine years, Alfred fought the Danes with mixed success. He won some battles. He lost others. The Danes remained relentless.

Athelney: Alfred in the Marshes

In 878 AD, things reached their worst. A Danish surprise attack forced Alfred to flee his kingdom entirely. Marshall tells the story with dramatic power:

At last things became so desperate that Alfred could hold out no longer. He had to fly, and taking his wife and a few faithful followers with him, he hid in the dense swamps of Athelney in Somerset. There, in the middle of the marshes, he built himself a small camp, and from there, with his few faithful friends, he prepared to fight the Danes. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XV

It was here, in hiding, that perhaps the most famous story of Alfred emerges. Marshall tells it with humour and warmth:

The Burning of the Cakes

One day, Alfred was in the cottage of a poor cowherd, waiting for news from his men. He was dressed as a wandering minstrel, and the goodwife of the cottage, not knowing who he was, gave him bread and cakes to eat, and bade him stay and watch them while she was busy about her work. But Alfred, thinking of his troubles and his losses, forgot about the bread. He sat lost in thought, thinking of the Danes, and of how he could save his kingdom. The cakes, and not the Danish ships, were burning. Alfred was a great king, but he had proved a poor cook; and the good wife was very angry. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XVI

What happens next captures Alfred's character perfectly:

She scolded him well, little thinking that she was scolding her King. The peasant woman cried angrily: 'The lazy loon, the idle good-for-naught, to sit by the fire, and see the cakes burn, and never stir a finger!' — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XVI

The cowherd was named Denewulf. When he realized he had just scolded the King of Wessex, he was terrified. But Alfred was not angry. Later, when he returned to his throne, Alfred made Denewulf a bishop. Why? Because Alfred understood something crucial: wisdom and goodness matter more than rank.

The historian's note: The burning cakes story is likely a legend, added much later by hagiographers (writers who wanted to make Alfred seem saintly). However, it reveals something true about Alfred: he was humble enough to live among common people while in hiding, and wise enough to recognize good character wherever he found it. Denewulf did become Bishop of Winchester, though we don't know the real story of how.

III. Alfred's Triumph — The King Who Saved England

The Battle of Edington (878 AD)

From his marsh fortress, Alfred did not despair. He sent messengers to his thegns (nobles), and slowly, an army gathered. In May 878, just months after fleeing into Athelney, Alfred met the Danish forces at Edington in Wiltshire. What followed changed English history.

At last Alfred deemed himself strong enough to face the Danes in battle. So he gathered his army together and met the Danes at Edington. And in a great battle, Alfred defeated them utterly. The Danes, who had seemed so invincible, fled before him. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XV

This was more than just a victory. It was a turning point. The Vikings realized Alfred was not another weak English king they could break at will.

The Treaty of Wedmore (878 AD)

After Edington, Alfred and the Danish leader Guthrum negotiated peace. Marshall describes what happened:

After his great victory at Edington, the Danes agreed to make peace with Alfred. The treaty was called the Treaty of Wedmore, and by it the Danes agreed to stay in the eastern part of England, in what was called the Danelaw, and not to trouble Wessex any more. They also agreed that the Danish king should become a Christian. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XV
The historian's note: The Danelaw was a real division of England. The Danes kept the east and north; Alfred controlled the south and west. This peace held because Alfred then did something no other English king had done: he built fortifications (burhs) and a navy to defend his coast. The Danes learned that attacking Wessex was no longer easy.

Alfred's Navy

Alfred did not simply rule in peace. He built a defense system that would make Wessex untouchable:

Alfred understood that the danger from the sea would never be over as long as the Danes could sail freely. So he built the first English navy. He had ships made, faster and stronger than the Danish ships, with oars both above and below. These ships, he hoped, would protect his coasts from future raids. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XVII

This was revolutionary. Before Alfred, English kings had no ships. Now Wessex had a fleet.

Alfred's Schools and Books

But Alfred's true genius lay deeper. He understood that warriors alone could not save a kingdom. A kingdom needed educated people — priests, administrators, judges. He made a decision that would echo for centuries:

Alfred set about to make his kingdom learned. He brought scholars to his court from all over Christendom. He himself studied hard, and he made schools where young men could learn to read and write. He wanted his people to know about the great deeds of the past, so he made them write down their histories. And he made laws, good laws, to bring order to his kingdom. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XVII

Alfred translated Latin books into Anglo-Saxon English so that his people could read them. He created a law code that balanced justice with mercy. He founded monasteries as centers of learning. He even commissioned the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, a year-by-year record of English history that gives us much of what we know about his time.

The historian's note: Alfred's "English Renaissance" (the 880s and 890s) really did happen. He brought scholars from Wales and the Continent. He himself mastered Latin, Greek, and Anglo-Saxon. His law code was remarkable for the time: it opened with the Ten Commandments and the Golden Rule, establishing that English law should have Christian principles at its heart. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle survives in four manuscript copies and is one of the most valuable sources we have for English medieval history.

Why Only Alfred Is "the Great"

Alfred lived 849-899 AD. He ruled Wessex for 28 years. He repelled the Vikings, unified English law, created a navy, and made learning a pillar of his kingdom. In all of English history, from the Norman Conquest onward, only one king earned the title "the Great" as part of his official name.

That king was Alfred.

What Made Alfred Great?

Military genius: He defeated an enemy everyone thought was unbeatable.

Strategic vision: He saw that winning battles wasn't enough. He built fortifications, a navy, a legal system, and a culture of learning to make Wessex strong.

Humility: A king who could be scolded by a peasant woman about burnt cakes and take it humbly.

Belief in knowledge: He understood that a kingdom of educated people was stronger than one of warriors alone.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2-3 of these questions to reflect on.

Why the burning cakes?

The burnt cakes story is probably a legend. But what does it tell us about Alfred's character that Marshall wanted to include it? What kind of king deliberately hides among common people?

What made Alfred unstoppable?

Many kings fought the Vikings. Why did Alfred succeed where others failed? Was it his military skill, his vision, his stubbornness, his luck, or something else?

The power of books

Alfred spent as much energy building schools and translating books as he did building ships and fortifications. Was learning really as important as military power in saving his kingdom?

Guthrum's choice

After Edington, the Danish king Guthrum agreed to become Christian and keep to the Danelaw. Why would a fierce warrior accept these terms? What did Alfred offer that changed his mind?

Only "the Great"

Alfred is the only English king to be called "the Great." What standards should a ruler meet to earn that title? Would any modern leader qualify?

The Danelaw

Alfred didn't defeat all the Danes or expel them from Britain. He just pushed them back to the eastern half of England. Was this peace a victory or a compromise? What would you have done?
