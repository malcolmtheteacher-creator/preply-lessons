SHORT STORIES, LONG SHADOWS · B1/B2 · 50 min

The Open Window

Saki's story of a girl of fifteen, a nervous visitor, and a perfectly told lie.

Today: you'll tell the story of the most convincing liar in English literature — and you'll take control of the three tenses every good story needs: past simple, past continuous, past perfect.
Warm up — before we read. Talk these through out loud. There are no wrong answers.
Discuss:
  • What makes a story believable? Think of details, confidence, timing — what actually convinces you?
  • Have you ever believed something completely and then found out it wasn't true? How did you feel about the person who told you?
  • Is there a difference between lying and storytelling? Where is the line?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four short activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. The ground looked like ordinary grass, but underneath it was soft and wet, and a man who stepped on it could sink to his waist. The whole field was treacherous.
treacherous (adj) — dangerous, especially when it looks completely safe. Roads, ice and people can all be treacherous.
2. It was almost eight o'clock. The sun had gone, the garden was grey, and you could only just see the shapes of the trees. In that twilight everything looked strange.
twilight (n) — the half-light after sunset (or before sunrise), when it is neither day nor night.
3. When she described the accident, he went cold and shuddered — his shoulders moved once, quickly, and he could not stop it.
to shudder (v) — to shake for a moment because of fear, cold or horror. "I shudder to think about it."

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: niece · cure · speciality. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means the daughter of your brother or sister?
a niece (n) — your brother's or sister's daughter. (Their son is your nephew.)
2. Which word means something that makes a sick person well again?
a cure (n) / to cure (v) — "He came to the country for a rest cure." · "The doctor cured him."
3. Which word means the thing a person or a place is especially good at, and known for?
a speciality (n) — "Fish is the speciality of this restaurant." · "Making people believe her was her speciality."

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Her story was completely convincing: every date, every name, every small detail fitted. Convincing means…
  (a) long and complicated   (b) so believable that you accept it as true   (c) funny
(b) — convincing (adj) = it makes you believe. Verb: to convince someone. Opposite: unconvincing.
2. Nobody gave her time to prepare. Somebody asked a question, and she invented the whole answer on the spot. On the spot means…
  (a) carefully, over several days   (b) immediately, with no preparation   (c) in a dark place
(b) — on the spot = right there and then, with no time to think. "They offered him the job on the spot."

D · Word building & word partners

1. One phrasal verb fits all three: to ___ up an excuse · to ___ up a name for the band · to ___ up the whole story. What is it, and what is the single-word formal verb with the same meaning?
make up = invent something that is not true. Formal single word: to invent / to fabricate. Noun from it: a made-up story.
2. He didn't say goodbye. He grabbed his hat and dashed off. Is he moving slowly or quickly? Now complete: to dash ___ of a room · to dash ___ the road.
to dash off = to leave very fast and suddenly. Also: dash out of a room · dash across the road. (A gentler word for the same idea: to rush.)

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A visitor with a letter of introduction

Framton Nuttel had come to the countryside on doctor's orders. His nerves were bad, and he had been told to rest completely, avoid excitement, and take gentle walks. His sister thought this was a terrible plan. He would sit alone in a rented house and speak to nobody for a month, she said, and come home worse than he went. So she wrote letters of introduction to every family she had known when she lived there, and made him promise to deliver them.

This was the fourth house. A very composed young lady of fifteen came into the drawing room, told him that her aunt would be down shortly, and said that he would have to put up with her company until then. Framton tried to be charming, and privately doubted that formal visits to complete strangers were doing his nerves any good at all.

The girl let the silence sit for a while. Then she asked whether he knew many people in the area. Hardly anybody, he admitted: his sister had stayed here four years ago, and these letters were all he had. So he knew essentially nothing about her aunt? Only her name and address, he said. The girl nodded slowly, and something in the room changed. Her aunt's great tragedy, she said, had happened just three years ago — after his sister's time. Framton was surprised. Tragedy seemed out of place in such a peaceful house. And then the girl looked towards the large window standing wide open on to the lawn, and asked whether he had wondered why they kept it like that on an afternoon in October.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why has Framton come to the country, and why is he making these visits?

2. What two things does the girl establish before she starts her story?

1. He is under doctor's orders to rest his nerves. His sister gave him letters of introduction so that he would not spend the whole month alone and silent.

2. That he knows almost nobody in the area, and that he knows nothing whatever about her aunt beyond a name and an address — in other words, that there is nobody who can contradict anything she is about to say.

Discuss:
  • Look at the two questions the girl asks before she begins. What is she really finding out — and what does that tell you about her?

Part 2 — The window that is never closed

The girl's explanation came quietly, in the voice people use for things they would rather not discuss. Three years ago, she said, on exactly this date, her aunt's husband and her two young brothers walked out through that window to spend the day shooting. They crossed the fields towards the marsh, and the ground there is not what it looks like. None of the three ever came home.

That, she said, was why the window stayed open until it was completely dark, every evening, from spring to winter. Her poor aunt still believed they would walk back across the lawn one day, with the little brown dog running ahead, exactly as they had left. The youngest brother used to sing a silly song to annoy his sister, and her aunt sometimes talked about it. The girl's voice wavered at that point, and Framton felt a wave of relief when the aunt herself came bustling into the room, full of apologies for being late.

She talked cheerfully about the open window: her husband and brothers would be home from shooting at any moment, straight across the lawn, and they always made a dreadful mess of her carpets. Framton listened with horror. He tried to change the subject to his own health, explaining what the doctors had told him about rest and quiet and the avoidance of anything exciting. The aunt was clearly bored, her eyes moving constantly past him to the window and the darkening garden.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. According to the girl, what happened three years ago, and why is the window left open?

2. What does the aunt say when she finally comes downstairs — and why does that frighten Framton?

1. Her aunt's husband and two young brothers went out shooting through that window and never returned; the ground near the marsh was dangerous. The window is left open every evening because the aunt still believes they will come back.

2. She chats happily about her husband and brothers coming home at any moment and walking mud across her carpets. Framton believes they are dead, so he thinks the poor woman has lost her mind — and he feels the whole situation is horribly wrong.

Discuss:
  • The girl adds small, useless details: the little brown dog, the silly song. Why do details like those make a story feel true?

Part 3 — Three figures on the lawn

Framton was still describing his diet when the aunt suddenly brightened and said, "Here they are at last! Just in time for tea — and look at the state of them."

Framton turned to the girl to share a look of sympathy for the poor confused woman. But the girl was staring out through the open window with an expression of blank horror on her face. Framton spun round in his chair and looked in the same direction.

In the deep twilight, three figures were walking across the lawn towards the window. All of them carried guns under their arms, and one had a white coat hung over his shoulders. A tired brown dog kept close at their heels. Silently they came nearer, and then a young voice began to sing that silly song out of the darkness.

Framton grabbed his stick and his hat. He was out of the front door, down the drive and into the road in a matter of seconds; a man on a bicycle had to ride into a hedge to avoid a collision. "Here we are, my dear," said the husband, climbing in through the window. "Fairly muddy, but most of it's dry. Who was that who dashed off as we came up?" "A most extraordinary man, a Mr Nuttel," said his wife. "He could only talk about his illnesses, and he ran off without a word of goodbye when you arrived. One would think he had seen a ghost." "I expect it was the dog," said the niece calmly. "He told me he was terrified of dogs. He was once chased into a graveyard by a pack of wild dogs somewhere in India, and had to spend the night in a freshly dug grave with them snarling above him. Enough to make anyone lose their nerve." Making up stories at a moment's notice was her speciality.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Framton see through the window, and what does he do?

2. How does the girl explain his sudden departure to the others?

1. Three men walking across the lawn in the twilight with guns and a dog, one singing the song from her story. Believing he is seeing ghosts, he seizes his hat and stick and runs from the house without a word.

2. She invents a second story instantly: that he had a terror of dogs after being chased into a graveyard in India and spending a night in an open grave. Nobody questions it.

Discuss:
  • She lies twice in fifteen minutes and nobody suspects her once. What exactly is she good at — and is it a talent, a weapon, or both?

Grammar — narrative tenses: past simple, past continuous, past perfect

The girl's lie works because her grammar is perfect. A believable story needs three tenses doing three different jobs — and once you can control them, you can tell any story in English.

The three jobs:
• Past simple — the events, in order. The spine of the story: "I opened the door, walked in and sat down."
• Past continuous — the background: something already in progress when an event happened: "I was cooking dinner when the lights went out."
• Past perfect — a step further back: something that happened before the moment you are describing: "By the time I arrived, the train had left."
How they work together: "She was waiting at the station because her brother had promised to meet her there. In the end she took a taxi."
Background (was waiting) → earlier event (had promised) → next event in the chain (took).

Two words that change everything: when and while. "While I was reading, the phone rang." (long action interrupted) · "When the phone rang, I answered it." (one event, then the next).

1. Choose the tense

Put the verb in brackets into the past simple, past continuous or past perfect.

1. Framton (come) to the country because his doctor had ordered him to rest.

2. The girl (wait) in the drawing room when the visitor arrived.

3. She explained that the three men (leave) through that window three years earlier.

1. came (a completed event — the spine of the story)

2. was waiting (already in progress when he arrived)

3. had left (further back than the moment she is describing)

2. when or while — and the right tense

Write two or three words in each gap.

1. Framton was talking about his health, the aunt watched the garden.

2. The aunt came into the room the girl finished her story.

3. Framton (still / describe) his diet when the three figures appeared on the lawn.

1. While (a long action forming the background)

2. just as / when (one moment, immediately after another)

3. was still describing (in progress, interrupted by the event)

3. Retell the trick — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the correct form, then read the whole paragraph aloud.

Framton (sit) in a strange drawing room, wishing he were somewhere else, when a girl of fifteen (decide) to entertain herself. She first checked that he (never / meet) her aunt. Then she told him that three men (disappear) near the marsh three years before, and that the window (stay) open ever since. By the time the men walked calmly across the lawn, Framton (already / believe) every word.

was sitting · decided · had never met · had disappeared · had stayed (or: had been staying) · had already believed

Notice how the past perfect carries everything that happened earlier, so the past simple can keep moving forward. That is the whole engine of storytelling in English.

Going deeper

Saki was the pen name of H. H. Munro, who published this in 1914. His stories are short, sharp and slightly cruel, and they almost always end with one sentence that rearranges everything you have just read. He wrote dozens of them, usually featuring clever young people and foolish adults.

The heart of it. Notice how carefully the girl works. She asks two questions first, and only when she knows the visitor cannot check anything does she begin. She gives dates. She gives a specific date. She adds details nobody would invent — a brown dog, a silly song — and she lets her voice fail at exactly the right moment. Then reality obligingly walks across the lawn and proves her right. The final sentence is where the story turns: we learn that this was not a one-off. Making things up instantly is simply what she does, and she has already started her next story before the front door has stopped swinging.

Talk it through:
  • Rank these in order of how much they make the story believable: the exact date · the dog · the song · the pause in her voice. Defend your order.
  • Is she cruel? He is a nervous man who did nothing to her. Does the fact that she is bored, or clever, or fifteen, change your answer?
  • Nobody in the room ever finds out. Does a lie that is never discovered do any harm?
  • The story is about how easily a confident voice is believed. Where do you see the same thing happening outside books?

This is the heart of the lesson — do all of it out loud. The timer is there to keep you going: fill the time with detail rather than racing through it.

Task A · Tell the whole story (4 minutes)

Tell The Open Window in your own words, as if to a friend who has never heard it. Move through the arc: the nervous visitor and the letters → the girl's two questions → the tragedy and the open window → the aunt's cheerful chatter → three figures in the twilight → the run for the door → and the last explanation.

Use at least three past perfect verbs (had left, had never met, had promised) and at least five key words (treacherous, twilight, shudder, niece, cure, speciality, convincing, on the spot, make up, dash off).

Task B · Was she clever or cruel? (4 minutes)

Take a side and argue it properly. The girl frightened a sick stranger for her own entertainment — but she also produced a small work of art in about four minutes. Build your case with three reasons, answer the strongest objection to your own view, and say what, if anything, should happen to her.

Then swap and argue the opposite side for one minute. It's harder, and it's where the good language comes out.

Task C · Believing things (4 minutes)

The whole story turns on how easy it is to believe a confident voice. Talk about believing and not believing.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Invent a completely false story about yourself, on the spot, and tell it convincingly for one minute. Your partner then hunts for the weak point.
  • Something a lot of people believe that you think is not true. Why does it spread?
  • How do you decide whether something you see online actually happened?
  • A famous hoax, prank or urban legend from your country — explain how it fooled people.
  • Describe the perfect excuse: what makes one believable, and what gives one away?
  • Is a "harmless" lie ever genuinely harmless? Give a case for and a case against.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The last sentence tells us she does this all the time. How does that single fact change the whole story you have just read?
  2. The adults in this story are polite, dull and easy to fool. Is Saki laughing at them, at Framton, or at us?
  3. Storytelling is the same skill as lying, pointed in a different direction. Do you agree — and where exactly is the line between them?
