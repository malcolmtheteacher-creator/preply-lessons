Now — the words for today's story

Ten words unlock the story. Work through the four activities — say your answer out loud before you reveal it.

A · What do they mean here? Work it out from the sentence

1. She was fifteen, alone with a strange adult, entirely in charge of the conversation, and not remotely flustered by any of it. She was thoroughly self-possessed.
self-possessed (adj) — calm, controlled and confident, especially in a situation that would unsettle most people. Near neighbours: composed, unflappable, imperturbable.
2. The turf looked solid enough to walk on, but three men had gone into it and had never come out again. The ground beyond the garden was treacherous.
treacherous (adj) — dangerous precisely because it appears safe. Used of ground, ice, roads, currents — and of people who betray trust. Noun: treachery.
3. He did not excuse himself, or reach for his coat, or say goodnight. He seized his hat and stick and bolted, and a cyclist coming down the road had to swerve into the hedge.
to bolt (v) — to run away suddenly and uncontrollably, like a frightened animal. Compare the calmer to dash off and the formal to flee.

B · Which word means…?

Three words from the story: plausible · pretext · fabricate. Say the right word, then reveal.

1. Which word means seeming reasonable and likely to be true, though possibly not?
plausible (adj) — believable on the surface. Note the useful edge to it: "a plausible explanation" often implies and yet…. Opposite: implausible.
2. Which word means a false reason given to hide the real one?
a pretext (n) — "He called on a pretext, but he had come to see the house." Collocation: on the pretext of doing something.
3. Which word means to invent something false and present it as fact?
to fabricate (v) — stronger and more formal than make up: to fabricate evidence, an alibi, a story. Noun: a fabrication.

C · Choose the meaning — a / b / c

1. Her voice failed at exactly the right moment. She was feigning distress, and doing it expertly. To feign something is to…
  (a) exaggerate a real feeling   (b) pretend to have a feeling you do not have   (c) suppress a feeling entirely
(b) — to feign (v) = to pretend: feign surprise, feign ignorance, feign illness. Note the silent g: /feɪn/.
2. Everything she said was calculated to unnerve him, and by the end he was gripping the arms of his chair. To unnerve someone is to…
  (a) make them lose their composure   (b) bore them   (c) make them angry
(a) — to unnerve (v) = to destroy someone's confidence or calm. Adjective: unnerving. Related: to lose your nerve.

D · Word building & collocation

1. Complete the phrase, which appears in the story's final line: she could invent an entire history at short ___. What does the whole phrase mean, and what is its opposite — booking something weeks ahead?
at short notice = with very little warning or preparation time. Opposite: well in advance. Also: a moment's notice, give notice.
2. You know romance as love. Saki uses it in an older sense — invented, imaginative storytelling. Given that, what does a romancer do, and what does the verb to romance mean in "he was romancing again"?
romance (n, older sense) — imaginative invention, a tall tale. A romancer invents such stories; to romance = to spin fiction as though it were fact. The story's last line calls invention her speciality.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The interrogation disguised as small talk

Framton Nuttel had been dispatched to the country by his doctor, who prescribed absolute rest, an absence of mental excitement, and nothing in the way of violent physical exercise. His sister, who had lived in the district years earlier, took a dim view of the plan. He would bury himself in a rented house, speak to nobody for a month, and return with his nerves in a worse condition than before. She therefore armed him with letters of introduction to every family she could remember, and extracted a promise that he would deliver them.

He was on his fourth such visit when a niece of fifteen came into the drawing room, announced that her aunt would be down presently, and observed that in the meantime he would have to make do with her. Framton produced the correct noises. Privately he was beginning to doubt whether a programme of formal calls on total strangers constituted a rest cure at all.

The girl allowed the silence to stretch until it had done its work, and then began, with an air of mild curiosity, to establish two facts. Did he know many people in the neighbourhood? Hardly a soul, he admitted; his sister had stayed here four years ago, and the letters were the sum of his knowledge. So he knew practically nothing about her aunt? Only a name and an address. She received this with a small nod, and Framton, had he been paying attention, might have noticed that the conversation had just changed hands. Her aunt's great tragedy, she said, had taken place three years ago — since his sister's time, then. Tragedy seemed an unlikely word for so restful a house. And with that, she directed his eye to the long French window standing open on to the lawn, and wondered aloud whether he had asked himself why it was left that way on an afternoon in October.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What has Framton been told to do, and why is he making these visits at all?

2. What two facts does the niece establish before she begins, and why does each one matter to her?

1. Complete rest, no excitement, no strenuous exercise — a nerve cure. His sister insisted on the letters of introduction so that he would not spend the month in total isolation.

2. That he knows almost nobody locally, and that he knows nothing whatever about her aunt. Together they guarantee that nothing she invents can be checked or contradicted.

Discuss:
  • The two questions look like ordinary politeness and function as reconnaissance. What does that tell you about her — and about how the most effective deceptions begin?

Part 2 — A tragedy, supplied with detail

Three years ago to this very day, she said, her aunt's husband and her two young brothers had gone out through that window for a day's shooting. They had crossed towards the marsh, where a stretch of ground gives way without warning under a man's weight, and not one of the three had come back. Her voice had dropped into the flat, careful register people use for facts they would rather not handle.

That, she explained, was why the window was left open until dusk every evening of the year. Her poor aunt persisted in the belief that they would walk back across the lawn one day exactly as they had gone, with the little brown spaniel trotting at their heels. The youngest of them used to sing a foolish song to irritate his sister; her aunt sometimes said she could still hear it. At this point the girl broke off, and Framton felt a distinct relief when the aunt herself came bustling in, apologising volubly for the delay.

She spoke about the open window as though it were the most ordinary thing in the house. Her husband and brothers would be in from shooting at any moment now, straight across the lawn, and they invariably ruined her carpets. Framton listened with a growing sense of horror, and attempted — as a drowning man reaches for anything — to change the subject to his own condition, expounding at some length on what the doctors had ordered and forbidden. His hostess was manifestly bored. Her eyes travelled repeatedly past him to the window and the darkening lawn beyond it, and Framton reflected on the terrible coincidence of his having chosen this anniversary for his call.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. According to the niece, what happened three years ago, and why does the window stay open?

2. How does the aunt's arrival deepen Framton's horror rather than relieving it?

1. Her aunt's husband and two young brothers went out shooting through that window and were lost in treacherous ground near the marsh. The window is left open each evening because the aunt still believes they will return.

2. Because the aunt chatters cheerfully about the men coming home at any moment and muddying her carpets — which Framton can only interpret as the delusion of a woman unhinged by grief.

Discuss:
  • The spaniel and the foolish song are details no inventor would need. Why do redundant details make a false account more convincing than a tidy one?

Part 3 — Three figures, and a second story

Framton was mid-sentence on the subject of diet when his hostess broke in with evident satisfaction: here they were at last, and only just in time for tea, and look at the state of them. He turned to the niece intending to convey sympathy for a woman visibly unwell, and found her staring out through the window with an expression of dawning horror. He swung round in his seat and looked in the same direction.

In the deepening dusk three figures were crossing the lawn towards the window. Each carried a gun beneath his arm; one had a white coat slung across his shoulders; a tired brown spaniel kept close at their heels. Noiselessly they approached the house, and then a young voice began, out of the dark, to sing that foolish song.

Framton seized his hat and stick and bolted. He was through the hall, out of the front door and into the gravel drive without a word of farewell, and a cyclist on the road beyond had to run into the hedge to avoid a collision. "Here we are, my dear," said the man with the white coat, coming in through the window. "Fairly muddy, but most of it's dry. Who was that who bolted out as we came up?" "A most extraordinary man," said his wife, "a Mr Nuttel. Could talk about nothing but his ailments, and dashed off without a word of goodbye or apology when you arrived. One would think he had seen a ghost." "I expect it was the spaniel," said the niece composedly. "He told me he had a horror of dogs. He was once hunted into a cemetery somewhere on the banks of the Ganges by a pack of stray dogs, and had to spend the night in a newly dug grave with the creatures snarling and grinning just above him. Enough to make anyone lose their nerve." Romance at short notice was her speciality.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Framton see, and what does he do about it?

2. What does the niece produce the moment an explanation is required — and what does the final sentence tell us?

1. Three men crossing the lawn in the dusk with guns and a spaniel, one of them singing the song from her story. Convinced he is watching the dead return, he bolts from the house without a word.

2. A second, entirely different fiction — a terror of dogs acquired in a graveyard by the Ganges — delivered instantly and accepted without question. The final line tells us this is not an isolated performance: invention at a moment's notice is simply what she does.

Discuss:
  • She improvises two complete fictions inside twenty minutes and is doubted by nobody. Is her real talent invention, or something colder — an accurate reading of what each listener is prepared to believe?

Grammar — narrative tenses, aspect & dramatic inversion

The niece's lie is a piece of engineering, and tense is the engineering. At this level the interesting work is in aspect — the difference between what happened, what was happening, and what had been happening — and in the inversions English keeps for suspense.

The four narrative tools:
• Past simple — the events in sequence: "She asked two questions and began."
• Past continuous — the frame around an event: "He was explaining his diet when they appeared."
• Past perfect — a completed action anterior to the narrative moment: "They had gone out through that window."
• Past perfect continuous — duration up to that earlier moment, often with a visible result: "He had been travelling for six hours, and it showed."
Inversion for dramatic effect (C1). When a negative or restrictive adverbial is fronted, subject and auxiliary swap places — the structure of a question, the function of a spotlight:
• "Little did he suspect that the story was an invention."
• "Not once did she hesitate." · "At no point had anyone questioned her."
• "No sooner had he sat down than she began." · "Hardly had she finished when her aunt came in."
Note the pairs: no sooner… than, hardly/scarcely… when. In a simple tense you need did: "Little did he know." Reserve these for moments that deserve the weight — overused, they sound like a bad thriller.

1. Aspect — choose the form the meaning requires

Put the verb in brackets into the past simple, past continuous, past perfect or past perfect continuous.

1. By the time the aunt appeared, the girl (already / construct) an entire family tragedy.

2. Framton (describe) his symptoms at some length when the three figures crossed the lawn.

3. He (deliver) letters of introduction all week, and he was thoroughly sick of it.

1. had already constructed (completed before that moment)

2. was describing (the frame interrupted by the event)

3. had been delivering (duration up to that point, with a present result — his exhaustion)

2. Inversion — rewrite for effect

Rewrite each sentence beginning with the words given. Write only the words that follow.

1. He did not suspect for a moment that she was inventing it. → Little that she was inventing it.

2. She had hardly finished speaking when her aunt came into the room. → Hardly when her aunt came into the room.

3. Nobody questioned her version at any point. → At no point her version.

1. did he suspect   2. had she finished speaking   3. did anyone question

The auxiliary jumps in front of the subject — exactly as in a question, but the sentence stays a statement.

3. Reconstruct the deception — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the form the meaning requires, then read the whole paragraph aloud.

Framton (sit) uncomfortably in a stranger's drawing room when a girl of fifteen (decide) to entertain herself at his expense. She first established that he (never / meet) her aunt and that he (know) nobody in the district. No sooner (she / satisfy) herself on both points than the tragedy began. Little (he / imagine) that the men he would shortly see on the lawn (shoot) all afternoon, and that by the time he reached the road he (become) the subject of a second story altogether.

was sitting · decided · had never met · knew · had she satisfied · did he imagine · had been shooting · had become

Note the two inversions carrying the suspense, and "had been shooting" — duration up to the moment of the reveal, which is precisely what makes the reveal land.

Going deeper

Saki — the pen name of H. H. Munro — published this in 1914, months before he went to the front, where he was killed in 1916. His comic method is consistent: a self-important adult, a composed and merciless young person, and a final sentence that reorganises everything preceding it.

The craft. Look at how the deception is assembled. Reconnaissance first: two questions that eliminate any possibility of verification. Then a date — not "some years ago" but three years ago to this very day, which converts coincidence into significance. Then the details a liar would never bother to invent: a spaniel, a foolish song, a habit of leaving a window open. Then the calculated failure of the voice. Every element is chosen for the listener in front of her — a nervous man predisposed to find the world alarming. And then the story does something better than any twist: it lets reality walk across the lawn and corroborate the lie. Saki gives us the reveal not through explanation but through Framton's flight and one composed sentence from the girl. The final line — invention at short notice as her speciality — retrospectively converts a single prank into a settled habit, and invites us to reread everything she has said, including her opening apology for her aunt's delay.

Talk it through:
  • Rank the devices by how much work each does: the verified ignorance, the exact date, the spaniel, the faltering voice. Defend your ranking.
  • Saki gives the girl no motive whatsoever — no grudge, no gain, only boredom. Is motiveless mischief more disturbing than malice, or less?
  • The comedy depends on our siding with the deceiver against the victim. What does Saki do to make that feel comfortable, and should it?
  • Consider the second lie, about the dogs and the grave. Structurally, why does the story need it — what would be lost if it ended with Framton's flight?

Task C · Credibility and its machinery (4 minutes)

The story is a manual for making things believed. Talk about how that works outside fiction.

Stuck for an idea? Pick one of these and run with it:

  • Construct a plausible fiction about yourself on the spot and deliver it for ninety seconds. Your partner then identifies the weakest joint.
  • Something widely believed that you consider false — analyse why it spreads rather than simply refuting it.
  • What actually makes a source credible online? Give three tests you use, and one that has failed you.
  • A famous hoax or misattributed story from your country. What did it exploit in the people who believed it?
  • Anticipation is the deceiver's ally: Framton was already nervous. Where else do people believe things because they were primed to?
  • Is there any professional context where inventing convincingly is a virtue rather than a fault? Make the case.
