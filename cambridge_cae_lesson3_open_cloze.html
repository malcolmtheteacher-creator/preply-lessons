Reading & Use of English — Part 2

C1 Advanced (CAE)

Part 2 at a Glance

This is the part that separates good English users from great ones. There are no options to choose from — you have to find the right word yourself.

FormatA text with 8 gaps. You write ONE word in each gap.
TimeAbout 10-12 minutes (of your 90-minute paper)
Marks8 marks — 1 per correct answer. Spelling must be correct.
What it testsGrammar and structure: prepositions, articles, pronouns, linking words, auxiliary verbs, quantifiers
The key difference from Part 1: In Part 1, you choose from four options. In Part 2, there are no options at all. You must think of the word yourself. The good news? The answers are almost always small, common words — the, of, which, been, having, despite. You already know every word that could be an answer.

A Quick Example

The museum, (1) was built in 1920, attracts over a million visitors a year.

which — This is a relative clause. The gap needs a relative pronoun that refers to "the museum" (a thing). "Which" is the only word that works here. Notice the commas — this is a non-defining relative clause, so "that" is NOT acceptable.

How confident do you feel about English grammar words? Things like prepositions (in, on, at, for, by), relative pronouns (which, who, whose), and linking words (however, although, despite)? Which group do you find trickiest?

Almost Every Answer Falls Into One of These 6 Groups

When you look at a gap, ask yourself: which category is being tested? This narrows your options dramatically.

1. Prepositions

The most common answer type. Look at the word before or after the gap.

  • interested in
  • depend on
  • result of
  • responsible for
  • accused of

2. Linking Words

Connectors that join ideas. Look at the relationship between the clauses.

  • although / though / even though
  • however / nevertheless
  • despite / in spite of
  • whereas / while
  • unless / provided

3. Relative Pronouns

Words that introduce relative clauses. Look for a noun before the gap.

  • who (people)
  • which (things)
  • whose (possession)
  • where (places)
  • whom (formal, after preposition)

4. Auxiliary & Modal Verbs

Verbs that build tenses, passives, and conditionals.

  • been (present perfect passive)
  • being (continuous passive)
  • have / has / had
  • would / could / might
  • do / does / did

5. Pronouns & Determiners

Reference words — what or who is the gap pointing to?

  • it / this / that / those
  • such (such a / such as)
  • each / every / both
  • few / little / much / many
  • own (their own)

6. Parts of Fixed Expressions

One piece of a set phrase. You need to recognise the pattern.

  • as well as
  • not only... but also
  • in order to
  • no matter what
  • as long as

Identify the Category

For each gap, decide which of the 6 categories is being tested, then find the word.

1. "The report, ___ was published yesterday, highlights several concerns."

which — Category 3 (Relative Pronoun). Non-defining relative clause referring to "the report" (a thing).

2. "She succeeded ___ spite of the difficulties."

in — Category 6 (Fixed Expression). "In spite of" is a set phrase meaning "despite."

3. "The building had ___ demolished before anyone could object."

been — Category 4 (Auxiliary Verb). Past perfect passive: had + been + past participle.

4. "He is not used ___ working under pressure."

to — Category 1 (Preposition). "Used to + -ing" means accustomed to. The "to" here is a preposition, not part of an infinitive.

Your 4-Step Approach

Step 1: Read the Whole Text First

Just like Part 1 — understand the topic, tone, and overall meaning before you touch any gaps.

Step 2: Look at What Surrounds the Gap

The word before and the word after the gap are your biggest clues. If you see a past participle after the gap ("___ been done"), you know you need an auxiliary. If you see a noun before and a verb after ("the man ___ discovered"), you need a relative pronoun.

Key question: What word MUST go with the word after/before the gap? "Pay attention ___" → to. "Keen ___" → on. The surrounding words dictate the answer.

Step 3: Ask "Which Category?"

Run through the 6 categories in your head. Is this a preposition gap? A linking word? A relative pronoun? Once you identify the category, there are only a handful of possible answers.

Step 4: Read the Completed Sentence Aloud (in Your Head)

Does it sound natural? Does the grammar work? If something feels awkward, it is probably wrong. Trust your instinct — but verify with grammar.

Common Traps

Trap 1: Writing Two Words

The answer is ALWAYS one word. If you want to write "in order" — only one of those is the answer. Read the gap carefully to see which word is already there.

Trap 2: Confusing "that" and "which"

After a comma → which (non-defining). No comma → either works, but which is safer. "That" CANNOT follow a comma in a relative clause.

Trap 3: "Despite" vs "Although"

Despite + noun / -ing ("despite being tired"). Although + subject + verb ("although she was tired"). Getting the grammar wrong means 0 marks even if you identified the right meaning.

Trap 4: Spelling

Spelling must be perfect. "Althought" = 0 marks. "Wich" = 0 marks. Double-check every answer.

Which of these traps have you fallen into before? Can you think of a sentence where you would be unsure between "despite" and "although"?

Practice Text 1: The Psychology of Colour

Read the text and type ONE word in each gap. Then check your answers.

Time: 10:00

Colour plays a far greater role in our daily lives than most people realise. Research has shown that (1) can influence everything from our mood to our purchasing decisions. Restaurants, for example, often make use (2) warm colours such as red and orange, (3) are believed to stimulate appetite.

(4) there is broad agreement about the emotional impact of certain colours, individual responses can vary considerably. A colour that one person finds calming (5) feel oppressive to another. This is partly (6) to cultural associations — in many Western countries, white is linked with purity, (7) in some East Asian cultures it is associated with mourning.

Marketers have long (8) aware of the power of colour. Major brands invest heavily in choosing the right shade for their logos, knowing that consumers often make snap judgements based on colour alone.

Answer Explanations

1. it — Pronoun referring forward to the clause "everything from our mood..." This is a common structure: "Research has shown that it can influence..."

2. of — Fixed expression: "make use of." Category 6.

3. which — Non-defining relative clause (after comma) referring to "red and orange" (things). Category 3.

4. Although — Linking word introducing a concession. Followed by subject + verb ("there is"). "Despite" would need a noun/gerund. Category 2.

5. may — Modal verb expressing possibility. "Might" and "could" are also acceptable.

6. due — Fixed expression: "due to" meaning "because of." Category 6.

7. whereas — Linking word showing contrast between two different cultural views. "While" is also acceptable.

8. been — Present perfect: "have long been aware." The auxiliary "been" completes the tense. Category 4.

Practice Text 2: Urban Green Spaces

Same format — type ONE word in each gap.

Time: 10:00

In recent years, city planners have increasingly recognised the importance of green spaces in urban environments. Parks and gardens do not (1) provide residents with a place to relax, but also help to reduce air pollution and lower temperatures in built-up areas.

(2) , creating green spaces in crowded cities is far from straightforward. Land is expensive, and local authorities often struggle to justify the cost, (3) when there is pressure to build more housing. (4) these challenges, some cities have found creative solutions.

Singapore, (5) instance, has pioneered the concept of vertical gardens, in (6) plants are grown on the sides of buildings. This approach makes it possible to increase greenery (7) taking up valuable ground-level space.

Experts believe that (8) innovations will become increasingly common as cities continue to grow and the need for sustainable solutions becomes ever more urgent.

Answer Explanations

1. only — Part of the fixed structure "not only... but also." Category 6.

2. However — Sentence-initial linking word introducing a contrast. Followed by a comma. "Nevertheless" also acceptable.

3. particularly — Adverb meaning "especially." "Especially" also acceptable.

4. Despite — Preposition meaning "in spite of," followed by a noun phrase ("these challenges").

5. for — Fixed expression: "for instance" meaning "for example."

6. which — Relative pronoun after a preposition: "in which." This is a formal structure common in C1 texts.

7. without — Preposition meaning "not + -ing." "Without taking up" = managing to avoid using.

8. such — Determiner: "such innovations" = "innovations like these."

Looking at both practice texts: Which category of word did you find hardest — prepositions, linking words, or something else? Make a note of it — that is what to practise before next time.
