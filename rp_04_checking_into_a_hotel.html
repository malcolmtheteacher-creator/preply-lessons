Checking into a Hotel

A grand lobby, a long journey behind you, and a room that isn't quite what the photos promised. Time to charm, request, and — when it goes wrong — rise above it.

The Scene 🎬

It's late. You've been travelling since dawn, your bag has a mind of its own, and the revolving doors of the Grand Meridian finally spit you out into a lobby that smells faintly of lilies and money. Three nights of this were supposed to be the reward at the end of a hard week.

You booked online, you paid in advance, and in theory everything is sorted. In practice, a smooth check-in is never really about the booking — it's about the small things you ask for on top, and the way you ask for them. A higher floor. A quieter room. A late checkout for that awkward evening train. None of it is guaranteed; all of it is possible, if you're pleasant about it.

Behind the marble desk: a receptionist at the end of a long shift, professionally warm, quietly deciding — as they always do — which guests are worth going the extra mile for.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. What's the best — or the worst — hotel you've ever stayed in? What exactly made it so? Was it the room, or the way you were treated?
  2. When something's wrong with a hotel room at midnight, some people ring down furious and some say nothing at all. Which are you — and which one actually gets a better result?

Who's who

You
The Guest 🧳

Tired, travelled, and quietly hopeful. You want your room, a few small favours, and — when it turns out the heating's dead — a solution, without turning into the guest everyone dreads.

The other voice
The Receptionist 🛎️

Unfailingly polite, endlessly patient, and holding more power than the uniform suggests — the free upgrade, the late checkout, the room with the better view are all quietly in their gift.

First: read the conversation in the next tab. Don't just follow what the guest asks for — watch how they turn a cold, broken room into a warmer welcome than they started with. That's the whole game.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for the softeners, the understatement, and the moment a complaint somehow leaves everyone happier than before.

ReceptionistGood evening, and welcome to the Grand Meridian. Have you stayed with us before?
YouFirst time, I'm afraid — though I've heard nothing but good things. I've got a reservation under the name of Okafor, checking in for three nights.
ReceptionistOkafor… here we are. A double room, three nights, breakfast included. Could I just take a card for any incidentals, and see some ID?
YouOf course — there you go. And while you've got my details up: I don't suppose there's any chance of a room on a higher floor, is there? I'm a terribly light sleeper.
ReceptionistLet me have a look… You're in luck, actually — I can move you up to the eighth. It's much quieter up there, well away from the road.
YouThat would be wonderful, thank you. One other small thing, if it's not too much trouble — would it be at all possible to have a late checkout on our last day? Our train isn't until the evening.
ReceptionistI can pencil you in for two o'clock — I'll note it on the booking now, so there's no awkwardness later on.
YouYou're a lifesaver. I think that's everything — oh, and is breakfast still served until ten?
ReceptionistHalf past ten at weekends. Here's your key card, room 812 — the lift is just behind you. Do let us know if there's anything at all you need.
YouSorry to trouble you again — I'm afraid there seems to be a bit of a problem with 812. The heating doesn't appear to be working, and it's rather cold up there.
ReceptionistOh, I am sorry — that really shouldn't be happening. Let me get someone up to look at it straight away.
YouI'd really appreciate that. I don't mean to be difficult — it's just that after a long day we were rather hoping to thaw out.
ReceptionistNo, you're quite right to flag it. Tell you what — rather than leave you waiting on an engineer, why don't I move you next door to 814? Same view, and I know for a fact the heating's fine in there.
YouThat's incredibly good of you — honestly, that would solve everything.
ReceptionistIt's the least we can do. And I'll take a little off the rate for the inconvenience — you shouldn't be checking in to a cold room. New key coming right up; my apologies again for the frosty welcome.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Before any problem appears, the guest slips in two extra requests on top of the basic booking. What are they — and what little phrase does the guest use each time to make them easy to grant?

2. When the room turns out to be cold, the guest says "I don't mean to be difficult." Why say that at all — and what does it do to the mood of the complaint?

3. The receptionist doesn't wait for an engineer. What do they do instead, and what extra gesture turns a broken room into a guest who wants to stay longer?

1. A room on a higher floor (softened with "I don't suppose there's any chance of… is there?"), and a late checkout (softened with "if it's not too much trouble… would it be at all possible to…?"). Both are framed as favours the guest half-expects to be refused — which makes them painless to say yes to.
2. "I don't mean to be difficult" heads off the fear that the guest is about to become a nightmare. It signals the complaint is reasonable, not a tantrum — so the receptionist stays on the guest's side instead of getting defensive.
3. Rather than leave the guest waiting, the receptionist simply moves them to the identical room next door, where the heating works. Then they knock a little off the rate for the trouble — an apology backed by action, which is why the guest ends up wanting a fourth night.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that get you a better room — and rescue a bad one — without ever raising your voice. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🛎️ Checking in

Phrase 1

You reach the desk and need to say who you are and that you've booked. Which version sounds like a seasoned traveller rather than a nervous one?

A. "Hello. Booking. Okafor."
B. "I've got a reservation under the name of Okafor, checking in for three nights."
C. "I think I might have a room here, possibly, under Okafor?"
B — "I've got a reservation under the name of Okafor, checking in for three nights."
"A reservation under the name of…" is the standard, confident hotel formula, and adding "checking in for three nights" gives the receptionist everything they need in one clean sentence. A is blunt; C sounds unsure of your own booking.
Phrase 2

You want to slip in an extra request — but only while the receptionist already has your booking on screen, so it feels like no trouble. Complete the chunk: "And while you've got my ___ up…"

"And while you've got my details up…"
It frames the extra request as convenient timing rather than a fresh demand — you're catching a wave that's already there. Cousins: "While I've got you…", "While we're at it…"

🌟 Asking for a little more

Phrase 3

You'd love a higher, quieter floor — but you want to sound easy-going, not fussy, so you phrase it to half-expect a no. Put the pieces in order:

is there?any chance ofa higher floor,I don't supposethere's
"I don't suppose there's any chance of a higher floor, is there?"
Three cushions in one line: "I don't suppose", "any chance of", and the little "is there?" tag. It makes refusing painless — and saying yes feel generous.
Phrase 4

Before asking your second favour, you want to signal "feel completely free to say no". What short phrase buys that goodwill in advance?

"…if it's not too much trouble."
The classic pressure-release valve — it hands the other person an easy exit, which paradoxically makes them more likely to help. Relatives: "I hate to be a pain, but…", "Only if it's no bother…"
Phrase 5

Now the actual request — a late checkout. You want the most tentative, polite way to ask whether something can be arranged: "___ it ___ at all ___ to have a late checkout?"

"Would it be at all possible to have a late checkout?"
"Would it be at all possible to…" is one of the most useful request frames in English — endlessly polite and it fits anything: "…to store our bags?", "…to get a quieter table?", "…to move the meeting?"

🌧️ When something's wrong

Phrase 6

The room is cold and the heating's dead — but you open the complaint gently, understating it so it doesn't sound like an accusation. What do you say?

"There seems to be a bit of a problem with the room."
"Seems to be" softens the blame — you're describing, not accusing — and "a bit of a problem" downplays it so nobody gets defensive. British understatement is your friend when you want help, not a fight.
Phrase 7

You want to name the exact fault without stating it as a flat, blunt fact. There's a two-word verb chunk that hedges it perfectly: "The heating ___ ___ to be working."

"The heating doesn't seem to be working."
"Doesn't seem to" (or "doesn't appear to") leaves a polite sliver of doubt — maybe it's you, maybe it's the switch — so you report the problem without pointing a finger. Use it anywhere: "The key doesn't seem to work", "The shower doesn't appear to be draining."
Phrase 8

You're back at the desk to report the fault. Which opener keeps the receptionist on your side?

A. "Right, the room's freezing and I'm not happy."
B. "Sorry to trouble you again, but I'm afraid there's a problem with the room."
C. "This is unacceptable for a hotel of this standard."
B — "Sorry to trouble you again, but I'm afraid there's a problem with the room."
"Sorry to trouble you" + "I'm afraid" acknowledges you're interrupting and cushions the bad news — so they help gladly. A and C start a war you don't need; you'll get more, faster, by staying warm.

🤝 Pressing gently, then a proper goodbye

Phrase 9

You need them to take the problem seriously, but you don't want to come across as a diva. What phrase lets you press your case while insisting you're a reasonable person?

"I don't mean to be difficult, but…"
It pre-empts the very fear it names — you're flagging that what follows is fair, not a tantrum. Close cousins: "I don't want to make a fuss, but…", "It's not a big thing, only…"
Phrase 10

They've sorted it beautifully — moved you and knocked money off. Which closing line will the receptionist still feel good about at the end of their shift?

A. "Fine. Thanks."
B. "I trust that concludes the matter satisfactorily."
C. "You've handled it so graciously — I've half a mind to book a fourth night."
C — "You've handled it so graciously — I've half a mind to book a fourth night."
It praises the person ("so graciously") and pays the ultimate compliment — you'd happily stay longer. A is flat; B sounds like a complaints letter. "I've half a mind to…" is a lovely, warm way to say you're tempted.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole check-in out loud using all ten, in order — arrive and announce yourself → slip in two favours → discover the cold room → report it gently → press your case → warm goodbye. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

🌊 The room they can't give you

One more round — no script, no safety net. It's nearly midnight, you're checking in exhausted, and there's a real problem: the room you booked and paid for has flooded, the hotel is completely full, and there is genuinely no other room. Start the timer and talk your way through it — stay warm, but stand your ground. What can they offer instead? A room at their sister hotel and a taxi to get you there? A partial refund and an upgrade tomorrow? Their very best suite for tonight because it happens to be empty? You've got a right to be looked after — so aim high, but keep the room on your side. One rule: the second you turn rude or threatening, the goodwill drains away and you'll be sleeping in the lobby.

The Echo 🕰️

In 1912, the grandest hotel in the world didn't stand on any street — it floated. The Titanic had first-class staterooms, a swimming pool, a Turkish bath and a sweeping grand staircase, and the White Star Line sold exactly what every good hotel sells: comfort, service, and the reassuring sense that someone had thought of everything.

Guests boarded believing nothing could go wrong. Then came a problem with the accommodation that no receptionist, however charming, could talk their way out of.

A cold room you can fix with a smile and a phone call. The real test of "everything's been taken care of" only arrives when it hasn't.
