The Necklace

Guy de Maupassant (1884) — a woman borrows a beautiful necklace for one night, and pays for ten years

Original version available: read this lesson at B2/C1 level →

Read the story in three parts

This is a famous short story, told here in simple English. Read each part, then stop and talk about the questions. The end is a big surprise — read slowly and do not jump ahead.

Part 1 — The party and the necklace

Mathilde was a pretty woman, but she was not rich. She always wanted a finer life. She wanted beautiful clothes and a big house. But she had a small house and a simple husband.

One day her husband came home very happy. He had two tickets to a big, important party.

But Mathilde was sad. "I have no beautiful jewels to wear," she said. "I cannot go to the party like this."

So she went to her rich friend, Madame Forestier. She borrowed a lovely diamond necklace. It was the most beautiful thing in the box. Mathilde was very, very happy.

Stop and talk:
  • What does Mathilde want? Is she happy with her life?
  • Why is she sad about the party?
  • What does she borrow from her friend?

Part 2 — The party and the loss

At the party, Mathilde was very happy. She was beautiful. Everyone looked at her. She danced all night and she forgot about her small house.

But when she got home, she looked in the mirror. The necklace was gone! She had lost it!

Mathilde and her husband were afraid. They could not tell Madame Forestier. So they bought a new diamond necklace. It cost a lot of money — too much money. They borrowed money from many people.

For ten years they worked very hard. They lived like poor people. They paid back the money, little by little. Mathilde became tired and old.

Stop and talk:
  • How does Mathilde feel at the party? Why?
  • What happens to the necklace?
  • Why do they not tell Madame Forestier the truth?

Part 3 — The truth

One day, ten years later, Mathilde met Madame Forestier in the street. Her friend did not know her — Mathilde looked so old and tired now.

"Do you remember the necklace I borrowed?" said Mathilde. "Well, I lost it. We bought a new one. We worked for ten years to pay for it."

Madame Forestier took her hands. She was very sad.

"Oh, my poor Mathilde! But my necklace was not real. It was cheap glass! It was worth almost nothing!"

The end — stop and talk:
  • What is the big surprise at the end?
  • Was the necklace real? How much was it worth?
  • How do you feel about Mathilde now? Sad? Why?

Past simple and "If" sentences

This story happened in the past. So we use the past simple to tell it.

Past simple = a finished action in the past. We often add -ed: want → wanted, work → worked. But some verbs are different: go → went, buy → bought, lose → lost.

1. Past simple — finish the verb

1. Mathilde a finer life. (want)

2. She a necklace from her friend. (borrow)

3. She the necklace at the party. (lose)

4. They a new necklace. (buy)

5. They hard for ten years. (work)

1. wanted   2. borrowed   3. lost   4. bought   5. worked

2. "If" sentences — If + would

We can talk about a different life with "If". We use this pattern: If + past simple, ... would + verb.

Example: If she had more money, she would be happy. (She does not have more money, so this is only an idea.)

1. If Mathilde more money, she happy. (have / be)

2. If they the truth, they for ten years. (tell / not work)

1. had … would be

2. told … would not work

How the writer surprises you

A good short story is more than just words. The writer uses tricks to make you feel something. Here are two simple ones from this story.

1. The surprise ending (the "twist")

For the whole story, we think the necklace was real and very expensive. Then, at the end, the friend says it was only cheap glass. This big surprise at the end is called a twist. It makes us think about the whole story again.

2. The necklace was fake all along

The diamonds looked beautiful and real. But they were not real. Mathilde and her husband never knew. They lost ten years of their life for a thing that was worth almost nothing. The story shows us that the most important things are not always what they look like.
