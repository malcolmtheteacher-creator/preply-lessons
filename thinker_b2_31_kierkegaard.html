When you face a big life choice, should you trust careful reasoning, or take a leap?

You've been offered a job in another country. You write a list of pros and cons, and it comes out exactly equal. Your friends give you opposite advice. At some point, no more thinking will help: you just have to choose. A Danish writer in the 1840s said this moment is where you really become yourself.

Today: You'll meet Kierkegaard and his idea of the leap, so you can talk about making difficult choices and explain why he thought choosing is how we become ourselves.

Meet Kierkegaard

Read one part at a time. After each part, close your eyes and answer the question before you check.

I

The broken engagement

Søren Kierkegaard was born in Copenhagen in 1813 and lived there almost all his life. His father was a rich and deeply religious man, and Søren grew up serious and often sad. In 1840 he got engaged to a young woman, Regine Olsen. About a year later, he broke off the engagement. He never fully explained why, and he kept thinking and writing about that choice for the rest of his life.

What happened between Kierkegaard and Regine Olsen?

They got engaged in 1840, but about a year later he broke off the engagement and never fully explained why.
II

Books with false names

In 1843 he published Either/Or and Fear and Trembling, under made-up names such as 'Johannes de Silentio', which means John of Silence. Each false author shows a different way of living. Either/Or sets a life of pleasure next to a life of duty and commitment, and asks you to choose. Kierkegaard wanted readers to stop watching from a distance and decide for themselves how they would live.

What did each of Kierkegaard's made-up authors show?

A different way of living, such as a life of pleasure or a life of duty, so readers had to decide for themselves.
III

The leap

Kierkegaard attacked Hegel's huge system, which tried to explain all of history. Where, he asked, is the single person in all this? The truth that matters most is personal: it is what you are ready to live for. Big choices, like faith or love, can't be proved first; they need a 'leap'. He called anxiety 'the dizziness of freedom'. He died in Copenhagen in 1855, aged 42.

Why did Kierkegaard say big choices need a 'leap'?

Because things like faith or love can't be proved first. At some point reasons run out, and you have to choose and commit.
Talk: Kierkegaard broke off his engagement and thought about it for the rest of his life. Is it ever right to break a serious promise like an engagement? What makes it right or wrong?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. Standing at the edge of a big decision, he felt a strange anxiety, a mix of fear and excitement.
anxiety (n) — a feeling of worry and nervousness about what might happen. Example: "She felt a lot of anxiety before her driving test."
2. Getting married is a serious commitment: you promise to stay together through good times and bad.
commitment (n) — a promise to give your time, energy or loyalty to something or someone. Example: "Learning an instrument well takes years of commitment."
3. She faced a real dilemma: take the dream job abroad, or stay near her ageing parents.
dilemma (n) — a situation where you must choose between two options, and both are difficult. Example: "I'm in a dilemma: stay late to finish the report, or go to my friend's birthday party."
4. When the plane door opened, she hesitated for a second, then jumped with her parachute.
hesitate (v) — to pause before you do or say something because you are unsure. Example: "He hesitated for a moment before answering the question."
5. He stopped trying to impress people and started living an authentic life, doing what he truly believed in.
authentic (adj) — real and true to who you are, not copied or pretended. Example: "The restaurant serves authentic Greek food."
6. Many older people say they regret the chances they didn't take more than the mistakes they made.
regret (v) — to feel sorry or sad about something you did, or did not do. Example: "I regret not learning to swim when I was a child."
7. Moving to a new country without knowing anyone was a real leap into the unknown.
leap (n) — a big jump, or a sudden, bold step into something new and uncertain. Example: "Starting her own business was a huge leap."

See it in action

Here is the leap in action. Lena loves music but is studying law because it feels safe. She writes lists, reads articles and asks everyone for advice. Every list gives a different answer. Kierkegaard would say more thinking will not settle this, because the real question is not "Which life is best for everyone?" but "Which life am I ready to live for?" One morning Lena applies to music school. She can't prove it's the right choice. But by committing to it, she stops watching her life and starts living it.

Think it through

Thought experiment: The sealed envelope

You must make a big choice: move abroad for an exciting job, or stay where you are. A stranger offers you a sealed envelope. Inside is a perfect description of how both lives would turn out. Open it, and you'll choose with certainty. Burn it, and you'll choose without knowing, like everyone else.

Now give two reasons for your choice. Then argue for the other side for thirty seconds. Which side was harder to defend?

Connect

Compare

Hegel and Kierkegaard: Hegel built a huge system to explain all of history. Kierkegaard asked: where is the single person in that system? Is it more important to understand the big picture of history, or to decide how you will live your own life?
Hegel looks at humanity from above, as a story moving towards freedom. Kierkegaard looks from inside one life, where you must choose without seeing the whole story. You could say Hegel explains history, while Kierkegaard asks what you will do with your own freedom.
Socrates and Kierkegaard: Kierkegaard admired Socrates and wrote his university thesis about him. Both men annoyed their cities with uncomfortable questions. What else do they have in common?
Both believed that the most important truth can't simply be handed to you: you have to find it yourself. Both talked to ordinary people, both were mocked in public (Socrates in a comic play, Kierkegaard in a satirical newspaper), and both cared more about how a person lives than about building a system.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From Karl Marx

What did Marx say drives history?

Class struggle: the conflict between those who own things like factories and land, and those who must sell their labour for a wage.
From G. W. F. Hegel

How did Hegel think history moves forward?

Through conflict: one idea meets its opposite, they clash, and out of the conflict comes something new that keeps the best of both. Step by step, humanity becomes more free.
From Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz

What did Leibniz mean by 'the best of all possible worlds'?

That God looked at every possible world and chose the best one. Even with its evil, a world with free people is better than any other.
From Thomas Aquinas

How did Aquinas build every argument in the Summa?

He asked a question, listed the strongest objections against his own view, gave his answer with reasons, and then replied to each objection.

Final review

From memory, out loud. Then check.

1. What did Kierkegaard mean by 'the leap'?

Big personal choices, like faith or love, can't be proved first. When reasons run out, you have to choose and commit, and that is how you become yourself.

2. Why did Kierkegaard criticise Hegel's system?

Because it tried to explain all of history but forgot the single person, who has to choose how to live.

3. What did Kierkegaard call 'the dizziness of freedom'?

Anxiety: the feeling we have when we face a big choice and nothing forces us.

4. What does 'dilemma' mean? Give an example.

A situation where you must choose between two difficult options. For example, taking a dream job abroad or staying near your family.
