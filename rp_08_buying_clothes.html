Buying Clothes

The right jumper, the wrong size, and a jacket from last week that just isn't you. Time to sort it all out — charmingly.

The Scene 🎬

There's a wedding in three weeks and, as of this morning, absolutely nothing in your wardrobe rises to the occasion. So here you are, on a busy Saturday, in the kind of shop where the assistant appears at your elbow the second you touch anything.

You've spotted a jacket you actually like — but the only one on the rail looks a size too small, and you'd want to try it on before you commit to anything. And there's a second job in your bag: a jumper you bought here last week that, in the cold light of day, simply isn't you. You'd like to swap it, ideally without a scene.

Behind the till, and now beside you, and now holding three hangers you didn't ask for: an assistant who is friendly, helpful, and just a touch too keen to make a sale.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Are you someone who tries everything on and agonises, or someone who grabs their size and marches to the till? When did that approach last let you down?
  2. Think of one item in your wardrobe you've never worn. Why did you buy it — and why is it still there instead of back at the shop?

Who's who

You
The Shopper 🧥

Fussy about fit, allergic to hard-sell, quietly determined to leave with the right thing — and to hand back the wrong one without any awkwardness.

The other voice
The Shop Assistant 🛍️

Warm, attentive, working on commission. Genuinely wants to help — but would also love to sell you two more things while you're here.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. Twice the assistant tries to sell the shopper more than they came for. What are the two attempts — and how does the shopper turn each one down without a hint of rudeness?

2. Why did the shopper reject the first jacket, and what exactly was wrong with the fit? Which single word did they use to describe the problem?

3. The shopper wanted to hand back the jumper. What did they NOT ask for — and what did they ask for instead? Why might that be the smoother move?

1. First, the assistant pushes the matching shirt ("the full look") — the shopper deflects with warmth and humour: "I'll resist, I think. Just the jacket for today." Earlier, the 20%-off nudge gets a friendly but non-committal "Oh, good to know." Neither refusal stings, because both stay warm.
2. The first jacket (a medium) was too small — it was pulling across the shoulders. "Pulling" (or "a bit of a squeeze") tells you it was tight in one specific place, not baggy or long.
3. They did NOT ask for a refund — they asked to exchange the jumper for something else. Offering to swap rather than demanding money back sounds easier and less confrontational, and it gave the assistant room to find an even neater solution (knocking it off the total).

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for finding your size, surviving the fitting room, and handing things back — warm, unpushy, and quietly in control. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🚪 Holding off the hard-sell

Phrase 1

The assistant swoops the moment you walk in. You'd like to look in peace — but you don't want to be short with them. Which reply keeps them happy AND keeps them away?

A. "No."
B. "I'm fine, leave me alone."
C. "I'm just having a browse for now, thanks — I'll give you a shout if I get stuck."
C — "I'm just having a browse for now, thanks — I'll give you a shout if I get stuck."
"Just having a browse" signals you're not ready to buy without closing the door; "I'll give you a shout" hands them a friendly exit. You get your space and they get a smile.
Phrase 2

The assistant is now steering you towards a matching shirt to complete "the full look". You're not biting — but you want to decline lightly, almost playfully. Complete the get-out: "I'll ___, I think — just the jacket for today."

"I'll resist, I think — just the jacket for today."
"I'll resist" turns the refusal into a little joke about your own willpower, so it lands as charming rather than blunt. Cousins: "I'll be good, I think…" and "I'd better not, tempting as it is."

👕 Sizes & the fitting room

Phrase 3

The only one on the rail looks too small. You want to ask — very lightly — whether a bigger one exists, without assuming they have it. Put the pieces in order:

you've got thisI don't supposethe next size up?in
"I don't suppose you've got this in the next size up?"
"I don't suppose…" politely expects a no, so a no costs nobody anything — and "the next size up" is the natural way to ask for one size larger (its partner is "the next size down").
Phrase 4

You never trust the number on the label. You want to try the jacket on first — asked as a gentle request, not a demand. What do you say?

"Is there any chance I could try it on before I decide?"
"Is there any chance I could…?" is the soft, roundabout way to ask permission for almost anything. Note the phrasal verb: to try on a piece of clothing — and you try it on in the fitting room.
Phrase 5

Out of the fitting room, you can feel exactly what's wrong: the smaller jacket is too tight in one specific spot — right where your arms meet your back. Complete the chunk: "It was pulling ___ the shoulders."

"It was pulling across the shoulders."
"Pulling across the shoulders" pins the problem to one place — far more useful than "it doesn't fit". Build your kit: "a bit tight around the waist", "too loose on the hips", "a touch long in the sleeve".

🪞 Deciding out loud

Phrase 6

The larger one sits properly and you've made up your mind — but you want to say so casually, leaving yourself a sliver of room, not gushing. Which sounds most like a real, confident decision?

A. "I will purchase this item."
B. "I think this is the one, actually."
C. "It is acceptable."
B — "I think this is the one, actually."
"This is the one" is warm, human shorthand for this is what I'll buy; "I think… actually" softens it into a decision you've just happily arrived at. A is stiff; C sounds like you don't really want it.
Phrase 7

Different shop, different day: nothing's quite right and you want to leave empty-handed — but graciously, so you'd be welcomed back. What's your polite exit line?

"I think I'll leave it for now, thanks — I might have a think and come back."
"I'll leave it for now" declines without slamming the door; "have a think and come back" keeps it friendly and open. Nobody's offended, and you're free to walk.

🔁 Taking something back

Phrase 8

Now the trickier job: the jumper from last week. You want to hand it back gently, giving a reason that's honest but blames nobody — not the shop, not the jumper. How do you open?

"I bought this last week, but I'm afraid it's just not really me."
"It's not really me" is the perfect no-fault reason — nothing's wrong with it, it simply doesn't suit you, so nobody has to be defensive. "I'm afraid" softens the whole thing before it lands.
Phrase 9

You'd rather swap it than get your money back — and asking for the swap makes you sound easier to deal with. Reorder the request:

would it berather than a refund,to exchange it?possible
"Rather than a refund, would it be possible to exchange it?"
Leading with "rather than a refund" shows you're not out to be difficult; "would it be possible to…?" makes the request feel like a small favour, which people grant far more readily than demands. Note: you exchange something for something else.
Phrase 10

You want the return to go smoothly, so you head off the obvious questions before they're even asked — proving you've done everything by the book. What do you volunteer?

"I've got the receipt here, and the tags are all still on."
Offering the receipt and pointing out the tags are on ("I've not even worn it") answers the two things they'd have to check — so the assistant can just say yes. Smoothing the path is half the battle.
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole trip out loud using all ten, in order — hold off the hard-sell → ask for a bigger size → try it on → name the fit problem → decide → then hand back last week's jumper and ask to swap it. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

👗 The wrong size, the last one, the wedding tomorrow

One more round — no script, no safety net. The wedding is tomorrow, you've finally found the perfect outfit… and it's the very last one in the shop, in a size that's almost right but not quite — a shade tight where it matters. There's no time to order another and no other branch nearby. Start the timer and talk it through with the assistant: could it be let out or altered in time? Is there an identical piece being held for someone else? A near-enough alternative you hadn't spotted? Would they do a deal if you take it as-is and get it adjusted yourself? Explore every angle — and stay the kind of customer they'll bend over backwards for. One rule: the second you turn demanding or blame them, the goodwill vanishes — and so does your outfit.

The Echo 🕰️

Five hundred years ago, under the Tudors, you couldn't just buy whatever caught your eye. Laws decided who was allowed to wear what: purple silk and certain furs were reserved for royalty, deep reds and fine velvets for the nobility, and everyone else made do with wool and plain cloth. Your outfit wasn't a choice — it was a label announcing exactly where you stood.

Henry VIII padded his shoulders to fill a doorway; the Elizabethans starched their ruffs into great white cartwheels. What you wore could win you favour at court — or, if you dressed above your station, land you in real trouble.

Today, you can try the jacket on, decide it isn't you, and hand it straight back. A Tudor couldn't return their whole identity to the shop — the law had sewn it on for life.

Two bigger questions

  1. The Tudors read your rank straight off your clothes. We claim to be free of all that — but do the brands, logos and prices we wear still quietly announce who we are? How honestly can anyone "just wear what they like"?
  2. You can return a jumper because it "isn't you". What does it say about us that we now expect to try on identities and hand back the ones that don't fit — something no Tudor could ever have dreamed of?
