Return to the Moon

The Artemis Missions and the New Space Race

C1 - Speculation & Prediction

Fifty Years of Silence

December 14, 1972. Gene Cernan climbs back into the Apollo 17 lunar module, the last human to stand on the Moon. He was 38 years old. He died in 2017, still waiting for someone to follow him. Why did it take so long?

For five decades, no human has left low Earth orbit. The Moon — just three days away — has sat empty. The International Space Station orbits at 400km. The Moon is 384,000km away. We stopped going.

But now, suddenly, everyone wants to go back. NASA's Artemis program. China's lunar program. Private companies. The question isn't whether humans will return to the Moon — it's who will get there first, and what happens when they do.

What Changed?

600M+ tonnes of water ice confirmed at the lunar south pole NASA LCROSS mission, 2009
2028 China's target date for landing astronauts on the Moon
$3,000/kg SpaceX's launch cost vs $54,000/kg for the Space Shuttle

Three things changed. First, we discovered water — hundreds of millions of tonnes of ice in permanently shadowed craters. Water means drinking water, breathable oxygen, and rocket fuel, all extractable on the Moon itself. A lunar base becomes possible.

Second, China emerged as a serious space power. In 2019, they landed on the far side of the Moon — something no other country has done. They're not part of the US-led Artemis program. They're building their own lunar base with Russia.

Third, private companies like SpaceX have cut launch costs dramatically, making ambitious missions financially viable for the first time since Apollo.

The People Behind It

Zhang Yuhua is a deputy chief designer of China's Chang'e lunar probes. When the first Yutu rover broke down on the Moon in 2014, she took it personally. In an interview with Xinhua, she described wanting to go to the Moon herself to fix it: "I thought it was like a silver swan standing on the desolate moon, more beautiful than anything else." She went on to help develop Yutu-2, which successfully landed on the far side and is still operating today.

Victor Glover is the pilot of Artemis II — the first crewed mission to the Moon since 1972, scheduled for early 2026. A former Navy test pilot with combat experience, he became the first Black astronaut to complete a long-term stay on the International Space Station. When asked about the risks of deep space travel, he's been direct about the reality: the training helps, but the danger is real. For Glover, representation matters — he wants young people to see themselves in space exploration, not just imagine it.

The Uncertainty

Nobody knows exactly how this will unfold. Will Artemis meet its deadlines, or face more delays? Will China get there first? Will the Moon become a place of international cooperation, or competition? Will private companies transform space travel, or will the costs prove too high?

When we talk about the future — especially uncertain, high-stakes futures like this — we need precise language for expressing how likely or unlikely we think different outcomes are.

That's what we'll practise today: the language of speculation and prediction.

1. is bound to / is certain to

+
Meaning

Expresses very high certainty - you believe something will definitely happen, almost 100% sure. Stronger than "will probably."

Form

Subject + is/are bound to + infinitive

Subject + is/are certain to + infinitive

Note: "bound to" is slightly more informal/conversational than "certain to"

Examples
"The Artemis program is bound to face more delays - space exploration always does."
"With China accelerating its program, the geopolitical tensions are certain to increase."
Register

"Bound to" works in both formal and informal contexts. "Certain to" sounds slightly more formal/written. Both are common in news analysis and expert commentary.

2. is (un)likely to

+
Meaning

Expresses probability (likely = probably will happen, 60-80%) or improbability (unlikely = probably won't happen, 20-40%). More measured than "bound to."

Form

Subject + is/are likely to + infinitive

Subject + is/are unlikely to + infinitive

Can be modified: "highly likely," "extremely unlikely," "increasingly likely"

Examples
"Private companies are likely to play an increasingly important role in lunar development."
"A crewed landing before 2026 is highly unlikely given the current technical challenges."
Register

Neutral to formal. Very common in academic writing, journalism, and professional analysis. Safe choice when you want to sound measured and evidence-based.

NOW YOU TRY

Make a prediction: "International cooperation on lunar resources is (un)likely to..." - and explain your reasoning.

3. may well / might well

+
Meaning

Stronger than simple "may/might" - adds emphasis to indicate you think something is quite possible, perhaps more possible than people realise. "Well" strengthens the possibility.

Form

Subject + may well + infinitive (slightly more certain)

Subject + might well + infinitive (slightly less certain)

"Well" always comes immediately after may/might

Examples
"China may well reach the Moon before NASA lands its next astronauts."
"The first person on Mars might well be someone who hasn't been born yet."
Register

Slightly formal but natural in educated speech. Often used when presenting a possibility that contradicts expectations or common assumptions.

NOW YOU TRY

Language Focus: Speculation Modals (Part B)

4. could conceivably

+
Meaning

Expresses theoretical possibility - something is possible to imagine or believe, even if unlikely. Suggests "if you think about it, this is within the realm of possibility." More remote than "may well."

Form

Subject + could conceivably + infinitive

Similar structures: "could potentially," "could theoretically," "could possibly"

Examples
"A permanent lunar base could conceivably become self-sustaining within 50 years."
"Lunar water mining could conceivably make deep space missions economically viable."
Register

Formal/academic. Common in scientific speculation, policy analysis, and futures thinking. Signals that you're exploring possibilities rather than making predictions.

NOW YOU TRY

Think of something ambitious but not impossible about lunar development. Start with: "If everything goes well, we could conceivably..."

5. it remains to be seen whether/if

+
Meaning

Expresses genuine uncertainty about an outcome - we simply don't know yet and won't know until the future. Often used when there are good arguments on both sides, or when experts disagree.

Form

It remains to be seen whether + clause

It remains to be seen if + clause

Can add: "only time will tell," "the jury is still out on"

Examples
"It remains to be seen whether the Artemis Accords will hold up under real commercial pressure."
"Whether lunar resources will spark cooperation or conflict remains to be seen."
Register

Formal, journalistic, analytical. Very useful for concluding arguments or acknowledging complexity. Shows intellectual honesty - you're admitting uncertainty rather than overstating your case.

The Next Flag on the Moon

The Artemis Accords allow countries to establish "safety zones" around lunar operations. Is conflict over lunar territory bound to happen eventually, or is cooperation likely to prevail? What role might private companies play?

2

Who Should Go?

NASA has committed to landing the first woman and first person of colour on the Moon. Is this symbolic goal likely to matter in the long run, or could it conceivably distract from scientific priorities? What's your view?

3

The Price Tag

At $93+ billion, Artemis is one of the most expensive government programs ever. Is this investment bound to pay off, or might the money well be better spent solving problems on Earth? Where do you stand?

4

China's Parallel Path

China and Russia are building their own lunar base, excluded from Artemis. Could this conceivably lead to a new Cold War in space? Or is competition likely to accelerate progress for everyone?

5

Living on the Moon

NASA envisions permanent habitation - people living and working on the Moon for months at a time. Is this bound to happen within your lifetime? What challenges may well prove harder than expected?

6

Your Country's Role

Over 40 countries have signed the Artemis Accords. Is your country involved? Should it be? What opportunities could it conceivably offer, and what might the risks be? It remains to be seen whether smaller nations will truly benefit...

Language to Use

Try to naturally include these in your discussion:

  • "I think [X] is bound to..." / "...is certain to..."
  • "It's (highly) likely/unlikely that..."
  • "[This] may well / might well..."
  • "We could conceivably see..."
  • "It remains to be seen whether..."
