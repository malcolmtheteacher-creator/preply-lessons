Warm-up: Give and Receive

Two sides. Two timers. Two completely different skills.

Part 1: Give Feedback (45 seconds)

A colleague delivered a piece of work that was late and below standard. Give them feedback on what went wrong and how to improve.

Part 2: Receive Feedback (45 seconds)

Your manager says to you: "Your presentation last week wasn't your best. I think you rushed it." Respond without defending. Show you heard it.

What you just did: Giving feedback and receiving it are separate muscles. Most people are okay at one and terrible at the other. Professional communication means doing both equally well. Feedback isn't pleasant — but it's the only thing that moves people forward.

6 Feedback Moves

Click each card to see the move and real examples.

1. The SBI Frame

Situation-Behaviour-Impact. The only structure that consistently lands.

Situation: When did this happen? What was the context?

Behaviour: What specifically did the person do?

Impact: What was the result? How did it affect others?

Example: "In the Tuesday meeting, when you interrupted three times before the client finished speaking, they visibly pulled back. The deal felt less likely after that."

Why this works: It's not personal. It's observable. It's specific to one moment.

2. Specific Over Vague

Vague feedback is useless. Specific feedback is actionable.

Vague: "The presentation needed more evidence."

Specific: "The second slide had three claims with no supporting data. I didn't believe the numbers until you said them verbally in the Q&A. Put evidence on the slide itself."

Vague: "You didn't seem engaged in the meeting."

Specific: "You didn't take notes, you didn't ask questions, and your camera was off. When you're camera-off in a video call, people think you're not interested."

The point: Specific is kinder. It tells people exactly what to change.

3. The Developmental Angle

Feedback is not about the past. It's about the future. Frame it as growth, not failure.

Past-focused (defensive): "You were disorganised and missed deadlines."

Future-focused (developmental): "What would make your next project stronger is a project plan shared with the team upfront. It would keep everyone aligned and catch timeline issues early."

Why it works: People can't change the past. But they can change what comes next. Make the feedback about building something better, not about fixing something broken.

4. Receiving Without Defending

The hardest skill. When someone gives you feedback, your instinct is to explain why they're wrong. Don't.

Defending: "I didn't have time to polish it because you gave me the deadline last minute."

Receiving: "I hear you. It wasn't my best work. What specifically would have made it better?"

Why it works: Defending closes the conversation. Receiving opens it. You're showing them you actually want to improve.

5. The Clarifying Question

When feedback is vague, don't accept it. Ask clarifying questions.

Vague feedback: "It wasn't clear."

Your response: "When you say it wasn't clear, which part lost you? Was it the structure, the language, or the logic?"

Why it works: You're turning unhelpful feedback into useful feedback. You're also showing them you take it seriously.

6. Asking For Feedback

Don't wait for feedback. Ask for it. But ask specifically.

Weak ask: "Any thoughts on my work?"

Strong ask: "I'd value your honest view on [specific thing] — specifically, did the argument land? And what would make it stronger?"

Why it works: Specific questions get specific answers. Vague questions get vague feedback.

The Full Cycle: Give and Receive

Two rounds. You give feedback, then you receive it. Both on timed tasks.

Round 1: You Give Feedback

The scenario: A team member sent you a detailed project proposal. It was thorough and well-researched. But it missed the core business case — why this matters to the company. There are also three typos.

Give them feedback. 90 seconds. Use the SBI frame. Be specific. Be developmental.

1:30

Round 2: You Receive Feedback

The feedback you get: "Your report was too detailed. I didn't need all that background. And it took me too long to find the key points."

Receive it. Don't defend. Ask clarifying questions. Show you heard it. 90 seconds.

1:30
What just happened: Feedback is a skill that gets better with practice. The more you give specific, developmental feedback, the more comfortable you become. The more you receive without defending, the more people trust you with honest input. Both directions move teams forward.

Feedback Language Bank

SBI Frame (Giving)

"When [situation], you [specific behaviour], and the impact was [result]."

"In [context], I noticed [specific action]. It meant that [consequence]."

Developmental Framing

"What would make this stronger next time is..."

"For your next project, I'd suggest..."

"The skill to build here is..."

Receiving Feedback

"Thank you for that. Can I ask what specifically led to that view?"

"I appreciate you saying that. What would have made it better?"

"I hear you. Let me think about that."

Clarifying Questions

"When you say [vague feedback], what specifically do you mean?"

"Which part didn't land for you?"

"Can you give me a concrete example?"

Asking For Feedback

"I'd value your honest view on [specific thing]. What could improve?"

"I'm working on [skill]. Your perspective would help."

"What's one thing I could do differently next time?"

Following Up

"I've been thinking about what you said. Here's how I'm addressing it."

"I want to show you what I changed based on your feedback."

"That feedback landed. Here's what I'm doing with it."
