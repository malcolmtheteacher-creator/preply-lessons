A hundred thousand digits

In 2006, in a public hall in Japan, a retired engineer named Akira Haraguchi sat down to recite the number pi, the ratio of a circle's circumference to its diameter, which goes on forever and never repeats. He kept going for hours, with short breaks, far into the night.

By the time he stopped, he had reportedly said a hundred thousand digits, over about sixteen hours. The record was widely reported. It was not officially certified by the record-keeping organisation, so we have to say 'reported'. But nobody doubts that he knew a very large number of digits.

What did Haraguchi reportedly do?
He recited pi from memory, to a hundred thousand digits, over about sixteen hours.
Talk about it. What is the longest number you have ever tried to remember? How did you do it?

How did he do it? Japanese is helpful here. In Japanese there is a long tradition of making numbers into words by sound. Each digit can be read in several ways, so a string of digits can be turned into syllables, and the syllables into words and stories. According to what he said afterwards, Haraguchi turned the digits into stories made of such words, and he learned them in small groups until they were automatic.

That is just what you are learning. The number becomes words, and the words become a story. The details are different, but the idea is the same.

What is the idea behind his method?
He turned digits into words and stories, learned in small groups, rather than trying to hold the bare digits.
Talk about it. Which of the four systems you know most resembles his method?

But notice what Haraguchi's feat does not tell us. It does not tell us that everyone should learn a hundred thousand digits. Most of us need a phone number, a PIN, a year, a code of ten digits on a parcel. For those jobs the best system is the one that is quickest for the job in front of you.

You now have five. In this room you put them side by side and learn to choose. A good memory athlete, like a good carpenter, has more than one tool and knows which one to pick up.

What is the lesson of this room?
Choose the number system that suits the job; do not use the biggest tool for a small task.
Talk about it. Which of your own numbers (phone, PIN, birthday) would you be happy to keep in your head for good?

How to choose

  1. Ask how long the number is. Up to four digits: shape, rhyme or Major. Longer: Dominic or PAO.
  2. Ask how long you need to keep it. Till tonight: any quick system. For good: build a scene you will return to.
  3. Ask what you have already built. Use the list you know best. A half-built list is slower than a simple one.
  4. Mix them if it helps. Years in Major; a long code in PAO; an ID number by shape. Choosing is part of the skill.

long number, big tool.

Hybrids

Many people mix the Major System for single words with PAO for long strings, so that a number of any length can be handled. There is no rule. What matters is that you can decode your own pictures without hesitation.

Say it aloud. Choose the system you would use for your own phone number, and explain why, in one minute.
