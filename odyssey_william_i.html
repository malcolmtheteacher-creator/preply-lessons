The words for today's story

1. William's claim rested on a promise he said Edward had made.

2. The Normans built a castle in every important town.

a survey (n) — a careful examination and record.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The duke and the crown

William, Duke of Normandy, was born around 1028, the illegitimate son of Duke Robert. He won his duchy by fighting. In 1066 he claimed that Edward the Confessor had promised him the English crown and that Harold had sworn to support him.

After victory at Hastings, he advanced on London. He was crowned at Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day 1066. According to a Norman writer, shouts inside the abbey alarmed the guards outside, who set fire to nearby houses.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was William's claim?

2. What happened at his coronation?

1. That Edward had promised him the crown and Harold had sworn to support him.

2. Shouts alarmed Norman guards, who set nearby houses on fire.

Discuss:
  • What does the coronation chaos tell us about the conquest?

Part 2 — Castles and the north

The Normans held England with castles — first wooden mottes, later stone towers such as the White Tower in London. English rebellions were crushed. In the winter of 1069–70, after a northern rising, William's army carried out the "Harrying of the North", destroying crops and animals. A chronicler wrote of widespread famine; the scale is debated, but the destruction was severe.

By 1086 almost all English great landowners had been replaced by Normans.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the Normans control England?

2. What was the Harrying of the North?

1. By building castles and crushing rebellions.

2. The destruction of northern crops, animals and villages after a rebellion in 1069–70.

Discuss:
  • Can a ruler justify destroying a region to keep control?

Part 3 — Domesday

At Christmas 1085, William ordered a survey of England. Royal officers recorded who held each piece of land, how many ploughs, mills and animals there were, and what it was worth. The result, the Domesday Book, survives today in the National Archives.

William died in 1087 after an injury in France. He was buried at Caen.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the Domesday survey record?

2. Where is it today?

1. Who held each piece of land, its resources and value.

2. In the National Archives at Kew.

Discuss:
  • Why would a king want such detail?

Grammar — The Passive Voice

Historians often care more about what was done than who did it — or they simply do not know who did it. That is the job of the passive.

How it works:
• Form: be + past participle: "The king was crowned at Westminster."
• Add by only when the doer matters: "The castle was built by Norman masons."
• Reporting passives hedge a legend: "He is said to have…", "It was believed that…"

1. Make it passive

Complete each sentence about William the Conqueror. Use the word in brackets.

1. The White Tower (build) in London.

2. The north (destroy) in 1069–70.

3. Every piece of land (record) in Domesday.

1. was built

2. was destroyed

3. was recorded

2. Choose the right passive form

1. English landowners (replace) by Normans.

2. The Domesday Book (keep) in the National Archives today.

3. William (bury) at Caen in 1087.

1. were replaced

2. is kept

3. was buried

Going deeper

William's conquest was brutal, but his government was efficient. Domesday is one of the most remarkable documents of medieval Europe — a picture of a whole country in a single year. The Norman Conquest also brought thousands of French words into English.

Worth noticing: "Domesday" comes from "doomsday", the day of judgment — because its records could not be argued with.
Learning from the past — for today: What gets measured gets managed. William wanted facts before he made decisions. Before changing anything — a budget, a business, a habit — make your own "Domesday": know what you have.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Was William a builder or a destroyer?
  • Which French words in English come from 1066?
  • What would your "Domesday book" show?
