Seven digits

In the late 1970s, at a university in the United States, two psychologists, William Chase and Anders Ericsson, were looking for a volunteer for a very dull experiment. They wanted to know how far a person could stretch the memory for digits. They found a student, known in their reports as S.F., who was a keen runner and an ordinary undergraduate.

In the first session he could repeat back about seven digits, which is what most people can do. The psychologists asked him to come back, a few hours a week, to practise.

How many digits could S.F. remember at the start?
About seven, which is typical.
Talk about it. Would you volunteer for an experiment that asked for a few hours a week for over a year? Why or why not?

S.F. practised for many weeks. He found that he could do better if he turned the digits into something meaningful. As a runner, he knew a lot about running times, so he made groups of digits into times: 3 4 9 2 was 3 minutes 49.2 seconds, which was almost a world-record mile. He also used dates and ages. He built the groups into larger groups, in a structure.

Slowly the number rose: ten digits, twenty, thirty, forty. After about two hundred and thirty hours of practice, spread over around a year and a half, he could repeat back about seventy-nine digits, from a single hearing. Nobody had thought it possible for an ordinary person.

How did S.F. get better?
He made the digits meaningful (running times, dates) and built them into larger groups, with a lot of regular practice.
Talk about it. What knowledge of your own could you use to make digits meaningful?

The experiment taught the psychologists something about practice. S.F.'s memory itself had not changed: when they tested him on letters, he went back to about six or seven. What had grown was a skill, a set of methods tuned to digits. Skill grows with practice, in small regular steps.

The same is true for you. Everything you have learned in the first twenty-eight rooms is a skill. It will fade if you do not use it, and it will grow if you do. This lesson builds a plan for keeping it alive.

What had grown in S.F.?
A skill (methods for digits), not his general memory: with letters he was back to about seven.
Talk about it. Which of your skills got better because you practised a little every day?

A practice system that lasts

A little, often, on purpose. That is the whole secret. The rest is organisation.

A daily session of twenty minutes

MinutesWhatWhy
0 to 3Warm-up: walk a palace in your headWakes the routes
3 to 8Review: test yourself on yesterday's itemsKeeps the old ones alive
8 to 16New work: one small set (ten items, one chapter, five cards)Adds a little each day
16 to 20Log: write what you did, the time, the mistakesShows progress

A review calendar

Tag each new set with the date you learned it. Review it after one day, three days, a week, two weeks and a month, as in the forgetting curve (lesson 1). Mark each review in the log.

Each review slows the forgetting.

A weekly test

Once a week, run a ten-minute test (see lesson 28): some words, some faces, a few cards or numbers. Record the score. A weekly number is the best way to see progress.

Rules for a routine that lasts

  • Same time and place. A habit grows when it has a cue.
  • Small and regular. Twenty minutes a day beats three hours once a week.
  • Make it hard enough. If it is easy, raise the difficulty. If you fail a lot, lower it.
  • Start with the end in mind. Decide what you want the skill for: exams, talks, names, competition.
  • Sleep and rest. Memories are consolidated while you sleep.
  • Teach someone. Explaining a method shows what you have not understood.
