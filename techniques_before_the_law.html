Before the Law

Franz Kafka (1915) — a man waits his whole life at an open door that was meant only for him

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Read in three parts

Kafka's parable is one of the shortest stories in this series and one of the strangest. This is a graded retelling in three short movements. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. Everything depends on the last sentence. Do not skip ahead.

Part 1 — The doorkeeper

Before the Law there stands a doorkeeper. To this doorkeeper there comes a man from the country, who asks to be admitted to the Law. But the doorkeeper says that he cannot grant him entry now. The man thinks it over, and then asks whether he will be allowed to enter later.

"It is possible," says the doorkeeper, "but not now."

Since the gate to the Law stands open, as it always does, the man bends down to peer through it into the interior. When the doorkeeper sees this, he laughs and says: "If you are so drawn to it, try going in despite my forbidding it. But take note: I am powerful. And I am only the lowest doorkeeper. From hall to hall there are other doorkeepers, each more powerful than the last. The mere sight of the third is more than even I can bear." The man from the country has not expected such difficulties; the Law, he thinks, should be accessible to everyone, at all times. But he decides he had better wait until he is given permission to enter.

Pause and discuss:
  • The gate is open the whole time. The doorkeeper never physically stops the man. Why does the man not simply walk through?
  • The doorkeeper says entry is "possible, but not now." How much power does that one phrase give him?
  • The man believes the Law should be open "to everyone, at all times." Is that belief his strength or his trap?

Part 2 — The years of waiting

The doorkeeper gives him a stool and lets him sit down at the side of the door. There the man sits for days, and then for years. He makes many attempts to be admitted, and he wearies the doorkeeper with his requests. The doorkeeper often holds little interrogations with him, asking about his home and many other things — but they are the indifferent questions that great men ask, and at the end the doorkeeper always tells him again that he cannot let him in yet.

The man, who has equipped himself with many things for his journey, gives everything away, however valuable, in the hope of bribing the doorkeeper. The doorkeeper accepts it all, but each time he says: "I take this only so that you will not feel you have left something untried." Over the long years the man watches the doorkeeper almost without pause. He forgets the other doorkeepers, and this first one comes to seem the single obstacle standing between him and the Law. He curses his bad luck — loudly at first, and then, as he grows old, only muttering to himself.

Pause and discuss:
  • The doorkeeper takes the bribes but lets nothing change. Why does he accept gifts he has no intention of being moved by?
  • The man gives away everything and fixes his whole attention on one doorkeeper. What has the waiting done to his sense of the world?

Part 3 — The last question

Now the man's life is drawing to its close. His eyesight is failing, and he no longer knows whether the world around him is really growing darker or whether his eyes are only deceiving him. But in the darkness he can now make out a radiance that streams, inextinguishably, from the door of the Law. He has not much longer to live. Before he dies, all that he has experienced over the years gathers in his mind into one question — a question he has never yet put to the doorkeeper. He beckons him over, for he can no longer raise his stiffening body.

"Everyone strives to reach the Law," says the man. "How does it come about, then, that in all these years no one but me has ever asked to be let in?"

The doorkeeper sees that the man is at his end, and to reach his failing hearing he bellows into his ear:

"No one else could ever be admitted here, because this entrance was meant only for you. I am now going to shut it."

The final discussion — the ending lands:
  • Read the doorkeeper's last words again. The door was the man's own, and his alone. How does that single fact reorganise the entire story?
  • If the door was always his to enter, was the doorkeeper ever really an obstacle — or only a permission the man waited for that he never actually needed?
  • The radiance only becomes visible as the man goes blind and dies. Why does Kafka let him see the light only when it is too late to reach it?
  • Your turn: Describe a "door meant only for you" that you waited for permission to walk through — permission that, looking back, was never anyone's to give.

The grammar this story teaches itself

This is a story about waiting — a whole life of it — and about who is allowed to do what. So it teaches two things at once: the present perfect and its duration partners (for / since / how long), and the modals of permission and possibility (can / could / may / be allowed to).

1. Present perfect & duration — how long the waiting has lasted

Use the present perfect (have + past participle) for something that started in the past and continues now, with for (a period) and since (a starting point). For an action still in progress, the present perfect continuous (have been + -ing) stresses the duration.

1. The man (wait) at the door many years.

2. He (sit) on that stool the day he arrived.

3. "How long you (try) to enter?" — "All my life."

4. The gate (stand) open the whole time; it was never locked.

1. has waited / has been waiting … for

2. has been sitting … since

3. have … been trying

4. has stood / has been standing

2. Modals of permission & possibility — who is allowed in

can / can't = permission or ability now. be allowed to = formal permission. may / might / could = possibility. Notice how the doorkeeper keeps everything possible but never permitted.

1. The doorkeeper says the man (not / enter) now. (no permission)

2. " I enter later?" the man asks. (asking permission)

3. "It is possible — you be admitted one day," the keeper replies. (possibility)

4. In truth the man was always (allow) to walk through; he simply never did.

1. cannot / can't enter

2. May / Can / Could

3. may / might / could

4. allowed to (was always allowed to walk through)

3. Present perfect vs past simple — the finished life

While the man is alive we use the present perfect ("he has waited for years"). Once the life is over and sealed off, we switch to the past simple ("he waited his whole life and never entered"). Choose the right form.

1. (while he is still at the door) "I (give) you everything I own, and still you refuse me."

2. (after his death, telling the tale) The man (sit) there until he died, and the door (close).

3. (now, drawing the lesson) No one else (ever / come) to that door, because it was his alone.

1. have given (present perfect — his life is still going)

2. sat … closed (past simple — a finished sequence)

3. has ever come (present perfect — true up to now, drawing the moral)

What Kafka does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are why a parable of a few hundred words has haunted readers for a century.

1. The open door — the visible trap

Kafka makes the gate stand open the entire time. He withholds the obvious solution in plain sight: nothing physical stops the man. The barrier is permission, not a wall — and Kafka exposes how a barrier made of nothing can hold a person for a lifetime.

2. Compression of a whole life — time as a single image

An entire existence passes in a paragraph: the stool, the years, the bribes, the fading sight. Kafka compresses a life into a few sentences so that the waiting feels both endless and instant — the way a wasted life looks in hindsight.

3. The final reversal — the door was his

The last line reframes everything: the entrance existed only for this man, and now it is closing. The story inverts from a tale of an inaccessible institution into a tale of a man who refused his own open door. The tragedy was never the Law's; it was his.

4. The parable form — meaning left open

Kafka refuses to tell us what the Law is, what lies inside, or whether the doorkeeper lied. The blankness invites every reader to fill it — with God, the state, justice, death, or their own deferred life. The story works precisely because it explains nothing.

Three speaking tasks

Do all three. Each one builds on the last. The timer is for your benefit — it forces you to fill the time rather than escape it. Use the present perfect, duration phrases and permission modals from the previous tabs.

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · The Man's Complaint (4 minutes)

Speak as the man from the country, late in life, explaining to a stranger how long you have been waiting and what you have done. "I have sat here for forty years… I have given away everything I owned… I have asked a thousand times and I still haven't been let in."

Build the whole speech on the present perfect and duration phrases (for, since, all this time). Use at least one permission modal as you describe what the doorkeeper will and won't allow.

Task B · The Door You Waited At (4 minutes)

Describe a real "door" you waited at for permission that, in hindsight, you could simply have walked through — a job you didn't apply for, a question you never asked, a chance you waited to be offered. Tell how long you waited, what you told yourself, and when you realised the door had been open.

Contrast present perfect ("I have always assumed I needed permission…") with past simple for the moment of realisation. Use at least three effect verbs from Tab 3.

Task C · Whose Fault Was It? (4 minutes)

For two minutes, argue that the man is the author of his own tragedy: the door was open, no one stopped him, and a braver person would have walked in. For two more, defend him: the doorkeeper's authority was real, the warnings were frightening, and most of us obey invisible barriers every day without question.

Use cleft sentences ("What really traps him is…") and modals of obligation and possibility ("He could have walked in", "He should never have waited").

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The doorkeeper never lies and never uses force. Is he cruel, or is he just doing his job? Could the man have blamed anyone but himself?
  2. What are the "doorkeepers" in modern life — the permissions we wait for that no one actually has the right to withhold?
  3. The man only sees the radiance as he goes blind. Is it crueller to glimpse the light too late, or never to have seen it at all?
  4. Compare this with "The Verger": one man is thrown out of an institution and flourishes; the other waits his whole life to be let into one and dies. What do the two stories together say about institutions and permission?
