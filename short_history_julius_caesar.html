The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The rise of an ambitious man

He was born Gaius Julius Caesar around 100 BC, into an old but no longer wealthy Roman family. Rome in his youth was a republic, not a kingdom: power was meant to be shared, held for short terms, and above all never handed to one man for life. Yet the republic was cracking. Rich generals bought armies loyal to themselves, and the streets ran with the violence of rival mobs.

Caesar was clever, charming and dangerously ambitious. A gifted orator, he won crowds with his speeches and won friends by spending money he did not have, sinking deep into debt to fund public games and grand gestures. To rise faster, he formed a private alliance with the two most powerful men in Rome — the immensely rich Crassus and the great general Pompey. Together, unofficially, they carved up the state between them.

Caesar's share was command in Gaul, roughly modern France. Over eight years of hard campaigning he conquered it for Rome, becoming spectacularly rich and, more importantly, the beloved commander of a battle-hardened army. But his rivals in the Senate had grown afraid of him. As his command ended, they ordered him to give up his army and return to Rome alone — as a private citizen, with no protection from his many enemies.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What kind of government did Rome have when Caesar was young, and what was it meant to prevent?

2. How did Caesar's command in Gaul make him so powerful?

1. It was a republic, in which power was shared and held only for short terms — it was meant to stop any single man taking total power for life.

2. He conquered Gaul over eight years, which made him enormously rich and, above all, gave him a loyal, experienced army devoted to him personally.

Discuss:
  • Caesar rose by borrowing huge sums and making deals with powerful men. How much of great success do you think is talent, and how much is knowing the right people?

Part 2 — Crossing the Rubicon

By law, no general could bring an army out of his province into Italy itself. The northern boundary was marked by a small river called the Rubicon. To cross it under arms was treason — a declaration of war on his own country. Caesar knew that to obey the Senate meant ruin, and perhaps death; to disobey meant civil war. For a long moment he hesitated on the bank.

Then, in January of 49 BC, he made his choice. Leading a single legion across the water, he is said to have declared that the die was cast — the dice were thrown, and there could be no going back. That small stream has echoed down the centuries: to this day, "crossing the Rubicon" means passing a point of no return, taking a step you can never undo.

What followed was a civil war that spread across the whole Roman world, from Italy to Greece, Egypt and Africa. Caesar pursued his old ally Pompey, now his greatest rival, and defeated him. Everywhere he proved himself a brilliant and ruthless commander. Within a few years he stood without equal. The Senate, cowed and defeated, named him dictator — first for ten years, and then, in an astonishing break with every Roman tradition, dictator for life.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was crossing the Rubicon such a serious act?

2. What did "crossing the Rubicon" lead to, and what title did Caesar finally hold?

1. The Rubicon marked the boundary of Italy; bringing an army across it was treason — effectively a declaration of civil war against his own state.

2. It led to a civil war he won, defeating his rival Pompey; he was eventually made dictator for life — a title no Roman had held before.

Discuss:
  • "The die is cast" — a decision from which there is no return. When, if ever, is it right to take an irreversible risk like Caesar's?

Part 3 — The Ides of March

As dictator, Caesar used his power to reform Rome. He reorganised the calendar — giving us, in time, the month of July, named after him — settled his veteran soldiers on land, relieved debt, and extended Roman citizenship. To many ordinary people he was a hero. But to a proud circle of senators, one man ruling for life was exactly what the republic had been built to prevent. They feared he meant to become a king in all but name.

A conspiracy formed among some sixty senators, led by Cassius and, most painfully for Caesar, by Brutus, a man he had trusted and favoured like a son. Roman writers tell of warnings ignored: a soothsayer who told him to beware the middle of March — the Ides — and troubling omens in the days before. Caesar, dismissive of the danger, went to the Senate as planned on 15 March, 44 BC.

There, the conspirators surrounded him and killed him. It is said that when he saw Brutus among them, he ceased to resist. He died at the foot of a statue of Pompey, the very rival he had defeated. But the killing did not save the republic the senators hoped to restore. Instead it plunged Rome into fresh civil wars, out of which Caesar's adopted heir, Octavian, emerged as Augustus — Rome's first emperor. The republic was gone for good; the age of emperors had begun, and Caesar's name would be carried by rulers for two thousand years.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did some senators turn against Caesar, and who led the conspiracy?

2. What did the assassination actually lead to, instead of saving the republic?

1. They feared that one man ruling for life would destroy the republic and make him a king; the conspiracy was led by Cassius and Brutus, whom Caesar had trusted.

2. Instead of restoring the republic, it caused more civil wars; Caesar's heir Octavian eventually became Augustus, Rome's first emperor, ending the republic for good.

Discuss:
  • The men who killed Caesar believed they were saving Rome's freedom, yet they ended it. Can a violent act ever truly protect a free society?

Grammar — participle clauses

Caesar's story moves fast: one action leads straight into the next. English has an elegant tool for exactly this — the participle clause, which lets you fold two actions into one flowing sentence without repeating the subject. "He crossed the river. He marched on Rome." becomes: "Having crossed the river, he marched on Rome."

The two main types:
• -ing (present) participle — for an action by the same subject, happening at the same time or just before: "Leading a single legion across the water, he declared war on Rome." "Fearing his power, the Senate turned against him."
• having + past participle (perfect) — to show one action was clearly finished before the next: "Having conquered Gaul, he returned to Italy a rich man." "Having defeated Pompey, Caesar stood without a rival."
The passive form — for when something is done TO the subject:
• past participle alone, or being / having been + past participle: "Warned of the danger, Caesar ignored it." (= Because he had been warned…) "Named dictator for life, he held power no Roman had held before." "Surrounded by the conspirators, he ceased to resist."
The golden rule: the participle and the main clause must share the same subject. "Having crossed the Rubicon, he marched on Rome." ✓ (He crossed; he marched.) Avoid the "dangling" participle: Having crossed the Rubicon, the war began ✗ — the war did not cross the river.

1. Join the two sentences with a participle clause

Rewrite each pair as one sentence, starting with a participle. Say your version aloud, then reveal a model answer.

1. Caesar conquered Gaul. Then he returned to Italy. → Gaul, Caesar returned to Italy.

2. The Senate feared his power. So it ordered him to give up his army. → his power, the Senate ordered him to give up his army.

3. Caesar was warned of the danger. But he still went to the Senate. → of the danger, Caesar still went to the Senate.

1. Having conquered (perfect participle — the conquest finished before he returned)

2. Fearing (-ing participle — the fear caused the order)

3. Warned (passive past participle — he was warned by others)

2. Choose the correct participle form

Put the verb in brackets into the right participle form (-ing, having + past participle, or a plain past participle).

1. (lead) his legion across the Rubicon, Caesar began a civil war.

2. (defeat) his rival Pompey, Caesar returned to Rome in triumph.

3. (name) dictator for life, Caesar alarmed the old senators.

1. Leading (-ing — same subject, at that moment)

2. Having defeated (perfect — the defeat came before the return)

3. Named (passive — he was named by the Senate)

3. Spot and fix the dangling participle

Two of these sentences break the golden rule — the participle and the main subject don't match. Find the two that are wrong and say how you'd fix them; one is already correct.

1. Having crossed the Rubicon, the civil war began. → problem or OK?

2. Warned by the soothsayer, Caesar went on anyway. → problem or OK?

3. Surrounded by the conspirators, resistance seemed pointless to Caesar. → problem or OK?

1. Problem — the war didn't cross the river. Fix: "Having crossed the Rubicon, Caesar began a civil war."

2. OK — Caesar was warned and Caesar went on; same subject.

3. Problem — resistance wasn't surrounded, Caesar was. Fix: "Surrounded by the conspirators, Caesar saw that resistance was pointless."

Task B · One man, total power (4 minutes)

Rome handed one man near-total power because it was efficient in a crisis — and then could not take it back. Is it ever safe to give one leader that much power, even temporarily? Build an argument, using other examples you know from history or the present day, and decide where you'd draw the line.

Task C · A point of no return (4 minutes)

"Crossing the Rubicon" means passing a point from which there is no going back. Tell a story — real or imagined — built around such a moment. Lean on the grammar: what someone did having decided to act, what they were doing leading up to the choice, how they felt warned of the risk but going on anyway.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. Caesar was loved by ordinary Romans and feared by the elite. Was he a champion of the people, or simply a man who wanted power for himself?
  2. The senators killed him to protect the republic, and destroyed it instead. What does that tell us about violence as a way to defend freedom?
  3. Two thousand years on, his name still means "ruler" — Kaiser, Tsar. Why do you think some figures cast such a long shadow over history?
