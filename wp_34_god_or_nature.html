A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XXXIV of L

God or Nature

Baruch Spinoza: Deus sive Natura, determinism, and the intellectual love of God

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We meet a man excommunicated from his own Jewish community. Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677) lived in Amsterdam, grinding lenses and rethinking God. He said: God and Nature are the same thing. Everything that exists follows necessarily from God's nature. There is no free will, no accidents, no miracles. Everything is determined. But paradoxically, this leads to freedom: understanding necessity is the path to blessedness. Spinoza rewrites the very concept of divinity, and in doing so, plants a seed that will grow into modern atheism.

What is God to you?

A person? A force? Nature? Something else? Something nothing?

♦ ♦ ♦

A paradox to carry

Spinoza's God does not love, does not care, does not will anything. And yet he offers a path to blessedness through intellectual love of God. How can you love something that cannot love back? This is the deepest question Spinoza leaves us.

II. The Story — The Excommunicated Philosopher

Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677) was born in Amsterdam to a Portuguese Jewish family. He became a lens-grinder, and in the evenings, he thought. What he thought terrified his community so much that they excommunicated him at age 24.

Deus sive Natura

Spinoza's central insight: God and Nature are one and the same thing.

God, or Nature, is the single infinite substance of which all things are modifications. God is immanent, not transcendent. God acts internally in nature, not externally upon it. — Baruch Spinoza, Ethics, Part I

This was heresy. The Jewish and Christian traditions taught that God is separate from nature, transcendent, personal, able to will and act. Spinoza said: no. God is nature. God's essence is the very laws of nature. There is no gap between the divine and the natural.

Why this mattered: Spinoza's move collapsed the boundary between God and nature. If God is nature, then God does not have a mind separate from the physical world. God does not think about you, love you, or judge you. God simply is the totality of all things, unfolding according to necessary laws. For the religious establishment, this was atheism in disguise.

Everything Follows Necessarily

If God is Nature, then everything that happens follows necessarily from God's nature. There is no free will. There are no miracles. Nothing is contingent. Everything is determined.

In the infinite intellect of God, all things which can be conceived by human intelligence exist from eternity. Whatsoever follows, follows necessarily from God's nature. — Baruch Spinoza, Ethics, Part II, Proposition 6

But here is the paradox: Spinoza thinks determinism leads to freedom. How? Through understanding. If you understand that everything follows necessarily, if you see through false hopes and desires and understand what is truly good for you, then you are free. Freedom is understanding necessity.

The Intellectual Love of God

And here is where Spinoza's thought becomes mystical again. The highest human achievement is what he calls the "intellectual love of God"—understanding the necessity of all things and, in that understanding, achieving a kind of union with God (Nature).

♦ ♦ ♦

The Question He Left Us

Spinoza removed God's will, God's love, God's personal presence. But he kept God. He kept the word "love." He kept blessedness. His is a God that cannot love us back. Yet understanding this God becomes the highest human experience. Is this wisdom, or is it the last rationalist dream before God disappears from philosophy entirely?

III. The Ideas — The God Behind God

Idea 1: Substance Monism

There is only one substance: God/Nature.

Everything else is a modification of this single substance. The many appears to be many, but it is really one thing expressing itself in infinite ways. This is monism: the view that ultimately all things are one.

Idea 2: Determinism

Everything that happens follows necessarily from the nature of God.

There is no randomness, no free will, no divine choice. Everything was always going to happen this way, because it follows from eternal laws. This terrified people. But Spinoza insisted it was liberating: once you stop fighting against necessity, you are free.

Idea 3: The Intellectual Love of God

Blessedness comes through understanding God/Nature intellectually.

The highest human achievement is not moral virtue (as Augustine taught), not prudence (as Aristotle taught), but intellectual understanding of the necessity that governs all things. In this understanding, the self dissolves into the infinite, and we achieve a kind of eternal blessedness.

VI. Your Turn

Is Spinoza's God still God?

If God and Nature are the same, if God does not will or love or judge, is this still religion? Or has Spinoza disguised atheism as God?

Determinism and freedom

Spinoza says understanding necessity is freedom. Does this make sense? Can you be free if everything you do is determined?

Plotinus and Spinoza

Plotinus taught that everything flows from the One. Spinoza teaches that everything flows from God/Nature. Is this the same thing with different names?

The intellectual love of God

Spinoza's God cannot love you. But you can love it intellectually. Does this satisfy the human hunger for love? Or does it leave something essential unfulfilled?

Excommunication

Spinoza's own community cast him out. Why do you think his ideas were so threatening?

God: present or absent?

Does Spinoza's system leave room for a God who is present to us? Or does it eliminate divine presence entirely?

VII. Final Retrieval

1. Deus sive Natura

What did Spinoza mean by this phrase?

God, or Nature. God and Nature are one and the same thing. There is no separation between the divine and the natural. God is immanent in nature, not transcendent above it.

2. Determinism and Freedom

How does Spinoza connect necessity and freedom?

Understanding necessity is freedom. Everything follows necessarily from God's nature. But when you understand this necessity, when you see clearly how all things follow from eternal laws, you achieve freedom. Freedom is not the absence of causation, but understanding causation.
