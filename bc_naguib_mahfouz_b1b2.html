MINDS WORTH MEETING · B1–B2 · 50 min

Naguib Mahfouz

One small street in Cairo, and the ordinary people who live on it, can hold the whole world inside it.

You talk first — before you read anything

This is a talking lesson, not a reading one. So before a single fact, the floor is yours.

Start here · 2 minutes

Look at the title of this lesson: a writer from "one small street in Cairo". What is the name of the street or area where you grew up? Tell me one thing that happened there. Can a small place hold a big story? Just talk.

Now meet him — one beat at a time

A few short facts. After each one, you do the talking.

Naguib Mahfouz was born in 1911 in Gamaliya, an old district of Cairo. He grew up in narrow streets, near old houses, small shops and busy cafes. These streets stayed in his heart all his life, and later they filled his books.

Over to you:
  • He wrote about the place where he grew up. Why do you think childhood places stay so strong in our memory?

For many years he worked in an office for the government. He was not rich. He wrote his stories early in the morning or late at night, in his free time. Writing was his real life, even when his job paid the bills.

Over to you:
  • Mahfouz wrote in his free time for years. Do you have something you love to do in your free time? Could it ever become more than a hobby?

In 1988 he won the Nobel Prize in Literature — the first writer in Arabic ever to win it, and Egypt was very proud of him. His most famous work is the "Cairo Trilogy": three books that follow one family across three generations, from grandparents to grandchildren.

Over to you:
  • Would you like to read a book about three generations of one family? Whose story in your own family would make the best book — and why?

The whole world in one street — you do the thinking

Mahfouz had one big, simple idea. I will give it to you in small pieces. After each piece, you talk — I will mostly listen.

Most people think you need a big subject to write a big book — a king, a war, a great hero. Mahfouz did the opposite. He looked at one small street in Cairo, and at the ordinary families who lived there: a father, a mother, their children, the shopkeeper, the neighbours.

Over to you:
  • Do you agree that the lives of "ordinary" people can be just as interesting as the lives of kings and heroes? Tell me about an ordinary person near you with an interesting story.

He believed that everything important about life was already there, in that one small street. Love, money, fights, dreams, growing old, change — you could find all of it between a few simple houses. You did not need to look anywhere else.

Over to you:
  • Think of the street where you grew up. What "big things" in life — love, money, change — actually happened there?

His books also show a country that is changing. The older people love the old ways and traditions. The younger people want new, modern things. This quiet battle between tradition and change happens inside one family, in one house — but it is really the story of the whole of Egypt.

Look closely at one ordinary street, Mahfouz seemed to say, and you will see the whole of Egypt — and the whole of human life — living inside it.

Over to you:
  • In your family, is there a quiet battle between "old ways" and "new ways"? Who wants the old, who wants the new — and where do you stand?

Did you understand the idea?

What kind of subject did Mahfouz choose for his books?
What did Mahfouz believe about a small street?
What "quiet battle" appears in his books?
Let's talk about it:
  • Do you agree that one small street can hold the whole story of life? Why or why not?
  • Is it better to keep old traditions, or to welcome new and modern things?
Your turn to talk · 2 minutes

Imagine you are Mahfouz. Choose one street you know well and describe it as if it were the setting for a book. Who lives there? What happens between the houses? Make me see it.

Grammar focus — relative clauses

To tell stories like Mahfouz, you need to describe people and places. We use small words — who, which, that, where — to add more information. "A man who sold bread." "A street where everyone knew each other." These are called relative clauses, and they make your speaking richer and more natural.

1. What they are, and how they work

A relative clause adds extra information to a noun (a person, a thing or a place). We join the two ideas with a small word instead of starting a new sentence.

noun + who / which / that / where + extra information
a man + who + sold bread → a man who sold bread
WordUse it for…Example
whopeopleA writer who loved his city.
whichthingsA book which tells the truth.
thatpeople or thingsA house that is very old.
whereplacesA street where children play.

2. Two things to remember

Tip 1: For people you can use who or that. For things you can use which or that. ("a man who/that…", "a book which/that…")

Tip 2: Use where only for places, NOT "which": say "a city where I lived" — not "a city which I lived".

The relative clause goes right after the noun it describes: "the woman who lives next door is very kind."

Practice A — choose who, which/that, or where

1. Mahfouz was a writer ____ grew up in Cairo.

2. He wrote books ____ describe ordinary families.

3. Gamaliya is the old district ____ he was born.

4. I have a friend ____ reads a book every week.

5. This is the cafe ____ we always meet.

1. who (or that) — a person

2. which (or that) — things

3. where — a place

4. who (or that) — a person

5. where — a place

Practice B — join the two sentences into one

1. I know a man. He sells fruit on my street. → I know a man ____ ____ fruit on my street.

2. This is the house. My grandmother lived here. → This is the house ____ my grandmother ____.

3. She wrote a story. It made me cry. → She wrote a story ____ ____ me cry.

4. That is the school. I learned English there. → That is the school ____ I ____ English.

1. I know a man who sells fruit on my street.

2. This is the house where my grandmother lived.

3. She wrote a story which/that made me cry.

4. That is the school where I learned English.

Now say it · 2 minutes

Describe a real place or person from your own life — your favourite cafe, your old school, a neighbour you remember. Use who, which and where as you go: "It's a place where…", "There was a man who…", "It had a window which…". Keep talking until you have used all three.

Your turn to talk

Three speaking tasks, all about your own world. Take your time — the timer is just there to help you keep going, not to rush you. Try to use relative clauses (who, which, that, where) and the phrases from the earlier tabs.

A · A street you know well (5 minutes)

Describe a street or neighbourhood you know well — maybe where you live now, or where you grew up.

Tell me about the people on it: "There is a man who…", "It's a place where…". What does it look like? What happens there every day?

B · A place that shaped you (4 minutes)

Think of one place that was important when you were young — a house, a school, a shop, a park.

Why was it special? Use sentences like "It's the place where…" and "It's a house that…". What do you remember most?

C · A person from your world (4 minutes)

Tell me about a person who was important in your childhood — a family member, a neighbour, a teacher, a friend.

Describe them: "She is the kind of person who…", "He is someone that…". What did they teach you, or how did they help you?

To finish — what do you think?

  1. Do you agree with Mahfouz that one small street can hold the whole story of life?
  2. Is your own neighbourhood changing? Do you like the change, or miss the old days?
  3. Which is more interesting to you: a story about famous, powerful people, or about ordinary people?
  4. In your family, who keeps the old traditions, and who wants new things?
  5. If you wrote a book, what place from your life would you write about — and why?
  6. Why do you think people all over the world still read books about one small Egyptian street?
