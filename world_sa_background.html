The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. India, Pakistan and Bangladesh lie on the Indian subcontinent.
subcontinent (n) — a large part of a continent that has its own character and geography. Example: “India, Pakistan and Bangladesh lie on the Indian subcontinent.”
2. Indonesia is an archipelago of more than seventeen thousand islands.
archipelago (n) — a group of many islands. Example: “Indonesia is an archipelago of more than seventeen thousand islands.”
3. The monsoon brings the rain that farmers depend on.
monsoon (n) — a season of very heavy rain, or the wind that brings it. Example: “The monsoon brings the rain that farmers depend on.”
4. The colonial period left new borders, languages and railways.
colonial (adj) — connected with a country ruling another country. Example: “The colonial period left new borders, languages and railways.”
5. India is a multilingual country with many official languages.
multilingual (adj) — using or speaking several languages. Example: “India is a multilingual country with many official languages.”
6. Ancient trade routes carried spices to Europe.
trade route (n) — a path or sea lane along which goods travel between places. Example: “Ancient trade routes carried spices to Europe.”
7. Buddhists are the majority in Thailand, but other religions are practised too.
majority (n) — more than half of a group. Example: “Buddhists are the majority in Thailand, but other religions are practised too.”
8. The diversity of the region makes any single label misleading.
diversity (n) — the fact of including many different kinds of people or things. Example: “The diversity of the region makes any single label misleading.”

The stereotype: “South and Southeast Asia is just one exotic place of spices, temples and crowds.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A huge, varied region

South Asia includes India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Bhutan and the Maldives. Southeast Asia includes Indonesia, Thailand, Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, Singapore, Myanmar, Cambodia, Laos and Brunei, and in 2025 Timor-Leste became the eleventh member of the regional group ASEAN. Together about 2.6 billion people live there — roughly one in three people on Earth. India alone has over 1.4 billion, and Indonesia, an archipelago, has about 280 million.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. About what share of the world's people live in these two regions?

2. What kind of country is Indonesia, geographically?

1. Roughly one in three.

2. An archipelago — many islands.

Discuss:
  • How many of these countries could you name before today?

Part 2 — Spices, seas and rulers

For thousands of years traders carried pepper, cloves, nutmeg and cinnamon across the Indian Ocean. Indian ideas, including Hinduism and Buddhism, travelled east and shaped the temples of Cambodia and Indonesia; Islam later spread along the same routes, and Indonesia is now the world's largest Muslim-majority country. European powers then ruled much of the region: Britain in India, France in Vietnam, Spain and later the USA in the Philippines. Thailand is often said to be the only country in the region that was never colonised.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which spices crossed the Indian Ocean?

2. Which religion is the majority in Indonesia?

1. Pepper, cloves, nutmeg and cinnamon.

2. Islam.

Discuss:
  • How might a country's history be visible in its food, buildings or language?

Part 3 — Hundreds of cultures

India's constitution recognises 22 official languages, and Indonesia has more than 700 languages. The region includes Hindus, Muslims, Buddhists, Christians, Sikhs and many followers of local faiths: the Philippines is mostly Catholic, Thailand mostly Buddhist, and Bali has its own form of Hinduism. The monsoon ties many of these places together, bringing the rain that grows rice from Kerala to Vietnam, but life under it is very different from a mountain village in Nepal to a skyscraper in Singapore.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many official languages does India's constitution recognise?

2. What does the monsoon bring?

1. Twenty-two.

2. The rain that grows rice and crops.

Discuss:
  • Which is the hardest part of describing a whole region in one sentence?

Grammar — Relative Clauses — Who, Which, Whose, Where

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
A relative clause gives extra information about a noun.
• who for people: “a trader who carried spices across the ocean”
• which / that for things: “a monsoon which brings heavy rain”
• whose for belonging: “a country whose people speak hundreds of languages”
• where for places: “an archipelago where thousands of islands are home to different cultures”

1. Choose the right form

Complete each sentence about this topic.

1. Indonesia is an archipelago has more than seventeen thousand islands.

2. India is a country constitution recognises 22 official languages.

3. Bali is an island people practise their own form of Hinduism.

1. which (or that)

2. whose

3. where

2. Now try these

1. Pepper was carried by traders crossed the Indian Ocean.

2. The monsoon is a season rice farmers depend on.

3. Singapore is a city skyscrapers and temples stand side by side.

1. who (or that)

2. which (or that / when)

3. where

Going deeper

The idea of “exotic Asia” grew partly from travellers' tales and colonial photographs that picked out temples, spices and crowds. Today people in the region more often describe themselves by country, language, faith or city — a Tamil speaker in Chennai, a Javanese farmer, a Filipino in Manila. Real connections exist, such as trade, rice and shared festivals, but they connect many cultures rather than make them one.

Worth noticing: Relative clauses: who (people), which / that (things), whose (belonging), where (places), when (times).
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: Name the country, city or language. “In India…”, “in Thailand…” or “in Bali…” is much more useful than “in Asia…”. If you are not sure, ask: “How does your family celebrate this?” People usually enjoy explaining their own traditions.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Which labels are used for the region where you live, and do you like them?
  • What do spices and trade routes tell us about how cultures connect?
  • How can one country contain many cultures at once?
