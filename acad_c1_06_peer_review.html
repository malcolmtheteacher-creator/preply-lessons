Social Media Engagement and Psychological Wellbeing in Adolescents: A Mixed-Methods Study
Chen, M., Patel, R., & Okafor, J. (2024)

Background: Research on social media use and adolescent mental health shows conflicting results. Some studies report increased anxiety and depression; others show positive community effects.

Methods: This mixed-methods study examined 847 adolescents aged 13–18 across three urban and two rural schools. Phase 1 (quantitative): participants completed validated scales measuring depression (PHQ-9), anxiety (GAD-7), self-esteem (Rosenberg), and daily social media engagement tracked via smartphone logs (n=847). Phase 2 (qualitative): 42 participants participated in semi-structured interviews exploring their experience of social media relationships.

Results: Quantitative analysis showed a weak negative correlation between daily social media time and depression scores (r = –0.18, p = 0.02), and no significant correlation with anxiety. However, qualitative coding revealed three distinct user types: "Active Sharers" (27%, high wellbeing, frequent meaningful interactions); "Passive Scrollers" (45%, lower wellbeing, report feeling inadequate); and "Strategic Users" (28%, moderate engagement, high wellbeing). The relationship between platform use and wellbeing was mediated by engagement style and perceived authenticity of interactions.

Conclusions: Social media's effect on adolescent wellbeing is not determined by screen time alone, but by the nature and quality of engagement. Future research should examine interventions targeting engagement quality rather than time restriction.

A good peer reviewer evaluates papers systematically. Here are the six core dimensions:

1Research Question ▼

What to ask: Is the research question clear, specific, and significant? Is there a real gap in the literature being addressed?

Red flags: Vague questions ("Does social media affect people?"), questions that replicate existing work without new angle, questions too broad to answer in one study.

Strong: "How does the type of social media engagement (active vs. passive) mediate the relationship between screen time and depression in adolescents?"

Weak: "Does social media affect mental health?"
2Methodology ▼

What to ask: Is the design appropriate for the research question? Are methods clearly described so that other researchers could replicate them? Are there limitations in the design that weaken the conclusions?

Red flags: Vague method descriptions, inappropriate design (e.g., correlational design claiming causation), failure to acknowledge design constraints.

Good methodology: Mixed methods (quantitative + qualitative phases) allows both breadth and depth, and the qualitative phase explains the quantitative findings.

Potential concern: Were the qualitative sample (n=42) selected randomly or purposively? If purposive, selection bias could be introduced.
3Sample and Data ▼

What to ask: Is the sample size adequate? Is the sample representative of the target population? Are there obvious selection biases? Are measurement instruments validated?

Red flags: Tiny sample, convenience sampling, instruments with unknown validity, lack of demographic detail.

Strength: N=847 is substantial; three urban and two rural schools increases geographical diversity.

Question: What was the response rate? Were non-responders systematically different? Are the schools representative of the national school population?
4Analysis and Results ▼

What to ask: Are the statistical tests appropriate? Are results clearly presented? Do the statistics actually support the claims made? Are effect sizes reported, not just p-values?

Red flags: Cherry-picked significant results, misuse of statistics, results presented in ways that obscure effect sizes, no correction for multiple comparisons.

Concern: A correlation of r = –0.18 is weak, even if p = 0.02. The authors correctly acknowledge this, but it should be emphasized more clearly as a very small effect.

Strength: Qualitative coding is transparent and allows verification.
5Discussion and Conclusions ▼

What to ask: Do the conclusions follow logically from the findings? Are limitations discussed honestly? Are implications overstated? Is the work situated in the existing literature?

Red flags: Conclusions that go beyond the data, ignored limitations, overselling of implications, cherry-picked citations.

Good: The authors conclude that engagement style matters more than screen time—this follows from their mixed-methods findings.

Check: Is the generalisability limited to urban/rural schools in developed countries? Do the authors address this?
6Language of Critique ▼

What to ask: How do you give critical feedback fairly and specifically? How do you raise concerns without being dismissive?

NOT: "This paper is poorly designed."

YES: "The selection procedure for the qualitative sample is not clearly described. On page 6, the authors state that 42 participants were 'selected from those willing to participate,' but this raises potential selection bias. The authors should clarify whether the sample was random or purposive, and discuss the implications for generalisability."

Teacher Implementation Fidelity and Student Outcomes in Literacy Intervention Programs
Kowalski, S., & Hughes, D. (2024)
This quasi-experimental study (n=234 Grade 3 students, 12 schools) examined whether implementation fidelity—the degree to which teachers follow intervention protocols—predicts reading outcomes. Schools were matched and assigned to the Structured Phonics Program (SPP) or control (standard instruction). Implementation fidelity was measured through classroom observation (adapted from Dane & Schneider, 1998), and student reading outcomes via standardized reading assessment (DIBELS Next). Results showed that students in high-fidelity SPP classrooms outperformed controls (mean difference = 0.72 SD, p < 0.01), while low-fidelity classrooms showed no significant advantage. Qualitative interviews (n=18 teachers) revealed that time constraints, insufficient initial training, and inadequate feedback mechanisms were barriers to fidelity. We conclude that intervention effectiveness depends critically on implementation quality, and that programmes require robust teacher support systems.

Option B: Organisational Psychology

Remote Work Arrangements and Team Cohesion: A Longitudinal Study of Tech Sector Employees
Martinez, L., Suzuki, K., & van der Berg, A. (2024)
Following three technology companies' shift to hybrid work (2020–2023), we measured team cohesion, engagement, and collaboration outcomes across 1,847 employees. Data collection occurred at baseline (pre-transition), 6 months, 12 months, and 24 months post-transition, using validated scales (Team Diagnostic Survey; Utrecht Work Engagement Scale) and objective performance metrics (project completion time, cross-team collaboration frequency). Results indicated a temporary dip in cohesion at 6 months (M = 3.42, SD = 0.91, vs. baseline M = 4.01), which recovered by 12 months and exceeded baseline by 24 months (M = 4.18). However, this varied significantly by team type: teams with structured asynchronous communication protocols showed sustained cohesion, while teams lacking clear protocols experienced persistent decline. Engagement remained stable throughout. We argue that hybrid work is compatible with team cohesion when communication structures are deliberately designed.
