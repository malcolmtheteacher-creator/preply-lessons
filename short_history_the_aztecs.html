Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. From a single city, one ruler came to command dozens of distant lands, each paying him taxes and sending him soldiers; together they held a hegemony wider than anything the region had seen.
a hegemony (n) — the dominance or controlling influence of one state or people over others.
2. On the altar at the top of the temple, a priest raised his knife; the crowd believed that without this propitiation, the harvest would fail and the gods would turn away.
propitiation (n) — the act of appeasing a god or spirit to win its favour or avert its anger.
3. They carved his image in stone and burned incense before it, for he was the deity who, they believed, made the rain fall and the maize grow.
a deity (n) — a god or goddess; a divine being who is worshipped.
4. Every conquered town had to send goods to the capital each year — baskets of cotton, feathers, gold and grain — and a town that refused to pay its tribute could expect an army at its gates.
tribute (n) — payment in goods or money that a weaker state is forced to give to a stronger one as a sign of submission.
5. When a comet split the night sky and a temple caught fire on its own, the priests grew pale: surely this was an omen, a warning of some disaster still to come.
an omen (n) — a sign believed to predict something in the future, whether good or, more often, bad.
6. The island city had no land road, so the builders drove a long raised path of earth and stone straight across the water — a causeway wide enough for ten men to march side by side.
a causeway (n) — a raised road or path built across water or wet ground.
7. The general's subjugation of the valley was swift: within two years its towns had fallen, its people bent to a foreign ruler, and its wealth flowed back to his own city.
subjugation (n) — the act of bringing a people or land under complete control by force.
8. With good rains, fair rulers and busy markets, the city began to flourish: its population doubled, its temples rose higher, and traders came from far away to sell there.
to flourish (v) — to grow strongly and successfully; to thrive.
9. An ancient prophecy said that one day a pale, bearded god would return from the east across the sea — and when strange ships appeared, some wondered if the moment had finally come.
a prophecy (n) — a statement that says what will happen in the future, often claimed to come from a god.
10. In the fields around the city, farmers planted row upon row of maize, and from its golden cobs they ground the flour for the flat bread that fed almost everyone.
maize (n) — corn; the tall cereal plant with large yellow grains, a staple food across the Americas.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The city on the lake

The people we call the Aztecs called themselves the Mexica. According to their own tradition, they had wandered for generations out of a northern homeland they remembered as Aztlan, a poor and unwanted tribe pushed from place to place. Their god Huitzilopochtli, the deity of the sun and of war, had given them a sign to watch for: they were to settle where they saw an eagle perched on a cactus. Around the year 1325, on a marshy island in the middle of Lake Texcoco, in the valley that is now Mexico City, they finally saw it. There they began to build, and they called the place Tenochtitlan.

It was a strange spot for a capital — wet, crowded and ringed by stronger neighbours — but the Mexica turned its weaknesses into strengths. They drove long causeways across the water to link the island to the shore, dug canals for canoes to slip between the houses, and floated whole gardens of rich soil, the chinampas, on the lake to grow maize, beans and flowers. Fresh water arrived by an aqueduct from the hills. The city flourished, and over the next two hundred years it grew into one of the largest and best-ordered cities on Earth, home to perhaps two hundred thousand people — far bigger than London or Paris at the time.

At its heart rose the Templo Mayor, a great twin pyramid where the most important rituals were performed. The Mexica believed the sun itself was locked in a constant struggle to rise each day, and that it could only win if it were fed with human blood. It was essential, the priests taught, that the gods be fed, lest the sun fail to rise and the world end in darkness.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What sign told the Mexica where to build their city, and where did they build it?

2. How did the Mexica turn the disadvantages of their island into strengths?

1. An eagle perched on a cactus, promised by their god Huitzilopochtli — they saw it on an island in Lake Texcoco (now Mexico City) around 1325 and built Tenochtitlan there.

2. They built causeways and canals, floated farming gardens (chinampas) on the lake, and piped in fresh water by aqueduct, turning the marsh into a great, well-fed city.

Discuss:
  • The Mexica chose the worst land in the valley and made it the best city. When does a hard starting point actually become an advantage?

Part 2 — The empire of tribute

For its first century the city was no empire at all, but a junior partner serving more powerful lords. That changed in 1428, when the Mexica and two neighbouring cities formed what historians call the Triple Alliance, threw off their old overlords, and turned outward in conquest. Under a line of ambitious rulers, their armies pushed in every direction, until the alliance controlled much of what is now central Mexico — hundreds of towns and millions of people.

It was not, however, an empire of roads and governors like Rome. The Mexica rarely settled conquered lands or replaced their rulers. Instead they demanded tribute. The emperor insisted that every city pay its share — cotton cloth, cocoa, gold, feathers, jade, maize and more — carried to Tenochtitlan on the backs of porters and recorded by scribes in painted books. A city that refused, or rebelled, could expect the army to return; and defeat in war meant more than tribute, for prisoners were marched back to the capital to be sacrificed.

This was the engine of Mexica power, and also its weakness. The subject peoples paid because they were afraid, not because they were loyal. Many of them hated their masters and the endless demand for victims. The priests required that sacrifices be made on a vast scale to keep the gods fed and the empire favoured — but every captive killed left another grieving, resentful town beyond the lake, waiting for the day the balance might tip.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the Mexica empire control the lands it conquered — and how was this different from Rome?

2. Why was the tribute system also a weakness?

1. It mostly left local rulers in place and demanded tribute (goods and sacrificial prisoners) rather than settling the land or installing governors as Rome did.

2. Subject towns obeyed out of fear, not loyalty; they resented the tribute and the demand for sacrificial victims, so many were ready to turn against the Mexica given the chance.

Discuss:
  • An empire held together by fear can be huge yet fragile. Why is fear such an unreliable glue for holding people together?

Part 3 — The strangers from the east

In 1519, ships unlike any the Mexica had seen appeared off the eastern coast. They carried a few hundred Spanish soldiers under Hernán Cortés, with horses, steel and guns the people of Mexico had never met. The emperor, Moctezuma II, received unsettling reports and, it is said, weighed strange omens that had troubled his priests for years. Whatever he believed, he hesitated — and rather than crush the small band at once, he let them march inland toward his capital.

It was a fatal hesitation. As Cortés advanced, the peoples who hated Mexica rule flocked to him, above all the Tlaxcalans, who had never been conquered. Tens of thousands of native warriors now marched against Tenochtitlan alongside the Spanish. Cortés entered the city as a guest, then seized Moctezuma as a prisoner inside his own palace. When the Mexica finally rose in fury, they drove the Spanish out across the causeways in a single bloody night — but Moctezuma was dead, and the strangers would be back.

Then came an enemy no army could fight. Smallpox, brought across the ocean by the Spanish, swept through a people who had never met it, killing without mercy and emptying whole districts of the city. In 1521 Cortés returned with his native allies and laid siege to Tenochtitlan, cutting its causeways and its water. For some eighty days the starving, plague-struck city held out, until on 13 August 1521 it finally fell. The great capital on the lake was torn down, and on its ruins the Spanish built a new city of their own. The Mexica empire had crumbled — but its language, its foods and its blood run through Mexico to this day, a legacy that conquest never quite annihilated.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was Cortés, with only a few hundred men, able to threaten so large an empire?

2. What two things, besides Spanish soldiers, helped bring Tenochtitlan down?

1. The peoples who hated Mexica rule — above all the Tlaxcalans — joined him in their tens of thousands, and Moctezuma hesitated instead of destroying the small band at once.

2. A smallpox epidemic that devastated the Mexica, and the long siege of 1521 that cut the city's causeways and water until it fell on 13 August.

Discuss:
  • A few hundred Spaniards did not topple the Mexica alone — disease and angry neighbours did much of the work. Does that change how you'd tell the story of "the conquest"?

The grammar this story runs on: the subjunctive (demanded that he be…)

This is a story of priests, emperors and commands — of what must be done. English keeps a special verb form for exactly that: the subjunctive. After certain verbs and adjectives of demanding, requiring and urging, the verb in the that-clause drops to its plain base form — no -s, no to, and be instead of is/are/was. It sounds formal and a little old, which is why it fits ritual and command so well.

The pattern in one line:
demand / insist / require / request / recommend / suggest / order + that + subject + BASE verb
or: It is essential / vital / important / necessary + that + subject + BASE verb.
The base form never changes for he/she/it, and "to be" appears as plain be.
See it move from this story:
• "The priests demanded that a sacrifice be made at dawn." (not "is made")
• "It was essential that the gods be fed, lest the sun fail to rise." (not "were fed")
• "The emperor insisted that every city pay tribute." (not "pays")
• "The priests required that sacrifices be made on a vast scale."
Notice: third person, but no -s; and be where you might expect is/was.

1. Put the verb in its base form

After these verbs and adjectives, the that-clause takes the plain base form. Watch the third person — no -s.

1. The priests demanded that a captive (be) brought to the altar.

2. The emperor insisted that every town (pay) its tribute in full.

3. It was vital that the sun god (be) honoured each day.

4. Tradition required that the eagle (mark) the place for the city.

1. be (base form of "to be", not "is")

2. pay (no -s, even though "town" is third person)

3. be ("be honoured", not "is/was honoured")

4. mark (base form, no -s)

2. Demand / require / essential — finish the sentence

Use the subjunctive (base form) in the that-clause. Remember: no to, no -s, and be for "to be".

1. The law of the empire required that a rebellious city (send) hostages to the capital.

2. It was essential that the army (return) before the rains came.

3. The high priest demanded that the temple (be) cleansed with fire.

4. Cortés insisted that Moctezuma (remain) at his side.

1. send (base form after "required that")

2. return ("the army return", no -s)

3. be ("be cleansed", not "is cleansed")

4. remain (base form after "insisted that")

3. Spot and fix — or confirm

Some of these already use the subjunctive correctly; others slip into the ordinary present. Write the correct base form in each gap to make the rule consistent.

1. The priests demanded that the offering (be) made at dawn, and that no one (speak) during the ritual.

2. It is essential that a leader (listen) to omens, the elders said, and vital that he (be) brave.

3. The alliance required that each member (give) soldiers and that the spoils (be) shared.

1. be · speak ("be made"; "no one speak", no -s)

2. listen · be ("a leader listen", no -s; "he be brave", not "is brave")

3. give · be ("each member give", no -s; "the spoils be shared")

Notice the constant: after demand/insist/require/essential/vital + that, the verb is always the bare base form — and "to be" is just be.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The Mexica believed the world itself depended on sacrifice — that it was essential the gods be fed. How does a belief that total shape the way a whole society behaves?
  2. The Spanish wrote much of the history we have, and they had reasons to paint the Mexica as cruel. How should we read the story of a people whose own voice was mostly silenced?
  3. Mexico City stands today on the ruins of Tenochtitlan, and the eagle on the cactus is on its flag. Why do you think a fallen empire can still become a nation's proudest symbol?
