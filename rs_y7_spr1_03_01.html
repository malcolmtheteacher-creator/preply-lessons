Key Concepts

Sacred: Worthy of religious respect or devotion; holy
Scripture: Sacred or holy writing; the texts believers regard as divinely inspired
Revelation: God or divine being making themselves known; the process by which God communicates truth to humans
Divine Inspiration: The belief that the Holy Spirit or God guided the authors of sacred texts
Oral Tradition: Stories, teachings, and knowledge passed down by word of mouth before being written down
Canon: The official list of sacred texts accepted by a religious community as authoritative
Authority: The power to influence belief and behaviour; having the right to be obeyed or trusted

Why Sacred Texts Matter to Believers

Source of Authority

Sacred texts are the highest authority. When a believer wants to know what to do or believe, they turn to their sacred text.

Guidance for Life

Sacred texts offer moral laws, wisdom, and practical guidance for living ethically and spiritually.

Connection to God

Reading sacred texts is an encounter with the divine. Believers experience God's presence through the words.

Identity

Sacred texts define what a believer is. They shape how they see themselves and their community.

Community

Believers gather to read and discuss sacred texts together. This strengthens their shared faith.

Living Tradition

Sacred texts connect today's believers to thousands of years of religious history and ancestors.

How Sacred Texts Are Used

Recited in Worship

Sacred texts are read aloud in prayers and services. Muslims recite the Qur'an in salah (daily prayer). Christians read the Bible in church services.

Memorised

Believers commit texts to memory. Muslim hafiz memorise the entire Qur'an. Jewish students study Torah extensively.

Studied with Commentary

Scholars write explanations and interpretations. These are studied to understand deeper meaning.

Used in Rites of Passage

Sacred texts are central to births, coming-of-age, marriages, and death ceremonies.

Carried and Displayed

Sacred texts are treated with physical respect: placed on high shelves, wrapped in cloth, never placed on the floor.

Consulted for Guidance

When facing decisions, believers look to sacred texts for wisdom and direction.

The Question of Authority: How Should We Read?

Not all believers approach their sacred texts in the same way. There's a spectrum of interpretation:

Literal / Fundamentalist Approach: Every word is exactly as it was meant. The text should be read word-for-word, exactly as written. No flexibility for modern context.

vs.

Interpreted / Liberal Approach: The text contains divine truth, but was written by human beings in a specific historical context. We interpret meaning for our time.

Most believers fall somewhere in the middle: they see the text as sacred and authoritative, but also recognize that interpretation and context matter.

Overview of the Main Sacred Texts

The Bible (Christianity)

What it is: 66 books divided into Old Testament (39 books) and New Testament (27 books). Written over ~1,500 years by multiple authors.

Key content: History of Israel, the Torah (God's law), poetry and wisdom, the life and teachings of Jesus (Gospels), letters to early churches, prophecy.

Belief: Divinely inspired by the Holy Spirit, but written by human authors. Most Christians don't believe every word is literal.

The Qur'an (Islam)

What it is: 114 chapters (surahs) arranged roughly from longest to shortest. Revealed to Prophet Muhammad over 23 years (610-632 CE).

Key content: Stories of past prophets, guidance for living, laws, spiritual teachings, the final revelation from Allah.

Belief: The literal, unchanged, perfect word of Allah. Unlike the Bible, Muslims believe only the Arabic Qur'an is truly the Qur'an. Translations are interpretations.

The Torah, Tanakh, and Talmud (Judaism)

What it is: The Torah (5 books) is the core. The Tanakh includes the Torah plus Prophets and Writings. The Talmud is centuries of rabbinic commentary and interpretation.

Key content: Creation, Abraham's covenant, the Law (613 commandments), history of Israel, wisdom, prophecy, and extensive rabbinic discussion of how to live.

Belief: The Torah was given directly by God to Moses. The Talmud represents ongoing divine guidance through scholarly discussion.

The Guru Granth Sahib (Sikhism)

What it is: 1,430 pages compiled by the 5th Guru. Contains hymns and writings from the 10 Gurus plus other saints and poets.

Key content: Devotional poetry, spiritual teachings, praise of God (Ik Onkar), social justice, guidance for living.

Belief: Treated with extraordinary reverence. It is placed on a raised platform, dressed in cloth, and fanned ceremonially. It is the living Guru of Sikhism.

Did you know? The Bible is the world's best-selling book of all time, with over 5 billion copies printed. The Qur'an is memorised in full by millions of people worldwide—no other religious text comes close to this level of memorisation.

Quote 1: "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth."

Discussion Questions

Pick at least two of these questions and write 3-4 sentences for each. There are no right answers—these are questions to help you think deeply.

Question 1: Can a text be sacred if you don't believe in God?

Some Humanists value sacred texts like the Bible or Qur'an for their wisdom and literature, even if they don't believe God wrote them. Does that make the text "sacred" to them? Why or why not?

Question 2: Is a sacred text more like a rulebook, a love letter, or a history book?

Think about how believers use their sacred texts. Are they looking for rules to follow? A personal message of love? Historical facts? Or something else entirely?

Question 3: Should sacred texts be updated for modern life, or kept exactly as they are?

Some texts contain teachings about slavery, women's roles, or other issues that seem outdated. Should they be revised? Or would that destroy something essential?

Question 4: What's the difference between reading a sacred text and studying it?

You might read the Bible for comfort, or study it to understand theology. Is one more "faithful" than the other? Can they both be valuable?

The Big Question

"Sacred texts should be taken literally, word for word."

Decide: Do you agree or disagree with this statement? Then explore both sides.

Arguments FOR (Why someone might agree):

  • If a text is truly from God, wouldn't every word matter? Changing or interpreting it undermines its authority.
  • If believers are allowed to interpret however they want, the text loses meaning. There would be no shared truth.
  • Many believers report that the "literal" message speaks to their life. Why change it?

Arguments AGAINST (Why someone might disagree):

  • Sacred texts were written by humans in ancient times. Some parts were cultural, not universal. We need interpretation.
  • Different parts of the Bible are different genres: poetry isn't meant to be literal like a law code is.
  • The text itself contains contradictions. You can't take everything literally—you have to interpret.
  • The best traditions have always involved interpretation. Rabbis argue about Torah meaning. Scholars debate Bible passages. This is faithful, not unfaithful.
Your turn: Write 3-4 sentences explaining which side you find more convincing, and why. You can disagree with both, too—what's your view?
