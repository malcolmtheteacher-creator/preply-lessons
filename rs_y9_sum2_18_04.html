The Art of Evaluation: Building Arguments

YEAR 9 · UNIT 18: GCSE BRIDGE · LESSON 4

What You'll Learn

Master AO2 evaluation skills: PEEL paragraphs, weighing arguments, counter-reasoning, and 6-mark/12-mark response structures.

Recall: What Is Evaluation?

Quick recall (5 minutes)
5:00

Evaluation is the hardest part of GCSE RS, but also the most rewarding. Let's build the skills now.

What is AO2 evaluation?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: AO2 is showing understanding (explaining why beliefs matter) and making judgments about ideas — weighing different viewpoints and supporting your own reasoning.

What's the difference between "opinion" and "evaluation"?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Opinion: "I think X is right." Evaluation: "X is right because [evidence]. However, Y argues [counter-argument]. On balance, [reasoned judgment]."

What makes an evaluation strong?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Evidence, examples, acknowledgment of different perspectives, logical reasoning, and a clear judgment supported by your argument.

Why do GCSE examiners value evaluation?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Evaluation shows sophisticated thinking. It's not just knowing facts — it's thinking critically like a philosopher, weighing ideas, and making reasoned judgments.

AO1 Knowledge vs AO2 Evaluation

AO1 (Knowledge): Learning facts and definitions. "What do Christians believe about sin?"

AO2 (Understanding): Explaining why beliefs matter and how they connect to practice. "Why do Christians believe Jesus died to save humanity from sin?"

AO2 (Evaluation): Making reasoned judgments about ideas. "Is the idea that one person can die for everyone's sins morally fair? What are arguments for and against?"

Mark distribution: AO1 ≈ 40% of marks. AO2 understanding ≈ 50%. AO2 evaluation (6-mark and 12-mark questions) ≈ 50% on thematic papers.

The PEEL Paragraph Structure

A PEEL paragraph is the foundation of effective GCSE evaluation:

  1. Point: State your main idea clearly
  2. Evidence: Give a specific example, quote, or fact to support it
  3. Explain: Interpret the evidence — why does it matter? What does it show?
  4. Link: Connect back to the question

Example PEEL paragraph:

Point: Christian beliefs about resurrection give believers hope in eternal life.

Evidence: The Nicene Creed states belief in "the resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come."

Explain: This means Christians believe death is not the end. Jesus' resurrection is proof that God has power over death, so believers trust they too will be resurrected to a better existence.

Link: This makes Christian belief in resurrection not just a doctrine, but a source of comfort and meaning in facing death.

Building Evaluation: The "Some... However... Therefore" Structure

On 6-mark and 12-mark questions, examiners want you to consider multiple perspectives. Use this structure:

  1. Some argue that [viewpoint 1] because [evidence/reason]. This is often a religious perspective or one side of the debate.
  2. However, [viewpoint 2] argues [counter-argument] because [evidence/reason]. This might be an alternative religious view, secular perspective, or opposite position.
  3. Therefore, [your reasoned judgment]. Based on both arguments, what's your evaluation? You don't have to pick one side — you can say both have merit, or one is stronger for certain reasons.

Example (6-mark question: "Evaluate whether sacred places are real or imagined"):

Some argue that sacred places are truly holy because believers experience genuine spiritual presence there. For example, pilgrims at Lourdes report healing and peace, suggesting sacred places have real power beyond imagination.

However, others argue that sacredness is a human construct because the same place means nothing to someone outside that religion. A church is just a building to a non-Christian; the sacred meaning is added by belief and culture, not inherent in the place itself.

Therefore, sacred places are likely real in their effects, even if people disagree about why. Whether a place is "truly" sacred depends on your worldview, but the comfort, inspiration, and community it creates are genuinely real and important in people's lives.

Avoiding Common Evaluation Mistakes

  • ❌ Stating opinions without evidence: "I think religion is a waste of time." ✅ Instead: "Some argue religion diverts resources from practical social help, while others believe faith communities provide support secular systems can't."
  • ❌ Only presenting one side: "Christians believe in God because the Bible says so." ✅ Instead: "Some find the Bible convincing evidence, but others argue it's circular reasoning — using the Bible to prove the Bible."
  • ❌ Forcing a conclusion: "Therefore, I believe X." ✅ Instead: "Therefore, the evidence suggests X, though Y remains a reasonable counter-argument."
  • ❌ Ignoring context: Evaluation must tie to the question. Don't write general essays about religion; focus on the specific claim you're evaluating.
  • ❌ Under-explaining: "Sacred places exist." ✅ Instead: "Sacred places exist because believers create meaning through ritual and belief, demonstrated by annual pilgrimages that maintain sacred significance."

Worked Example: 6-Mark Evaluation (Low Mark Level: 2–3 marks)

Question: "Evaluate whether it is right that Christians forgive their enemies."

Weak answer (2/6):

"Jesus told Christians to forgive people. I think forgiveness is good because it helps people feel better. Also, forgiveness is a Christian value. Therefore, Christians should forgive."

What's wrong: No specific evidence, no counter-argument, minimal explanation, weak reasoning.

Better answer (5–6/6):

"Jesus taught forgiveness in Matthew 18:21–22, where he tells Peter to forgive "seventy-seven times" — meaning unlimited, unconditional forgiveness. This reflects God's forgiving nature. Christians believe unforgiveness damages the soul and prevents reconciliation, which Jesus valued. However, critics argue that unconditional forgiveness enables abusers and wrongdoers to escape justice. Victims of serious harm may struggle to forgive without accountability or repentance. On balance, Christian forgiveness is valuable for personal healing and reconciliation, but should ideally involve genuine repentance from the wrongdoer to be truly Christian and effective."

What's right: Specific scripture, clear reasoning, acknowledges counter-argument, nuanced judgment.

Worked Example: 12-Mark Extended Evaluation

Question: "Evaluate whether religious belief is necessary for a moral life."

Strong answer structure (10–12 marks):

  1. Introduction (1–2 marks): Clarify what you're evaluating. "This requires considering whether morality is grounded in faith or reason, and whether non-believers can be ethical."
  2. Argument 1 — Religious perspective (2–3 marks): "Many argue religious belief is necessary for morality because God provides absolute moral standards. Without God, they claim, morality is subjective and arbitrary. The Bible gives clear moral guidance (Ten Commandments, Golden Rule)."
  3. Counter-argument 1 (2–3 marks): "However, secular philosophers argue humans develop morality through reason, empathy, and social contract. Many atheists and non-believers live highly ethical lives. Morality seems grounded in reducing suffering and promoting wellbeing, not divine command."
  4. Argument 2 — Religious community (2–3 marks): "Religious communities do provide powerful motivation for moral behavior through faith, accountability, and shared values. Religious traditions offer centuries of ethical wisdom."
  5. Counter-argument 2 (2–3 marks): "Yet religious belief has motivated terrible acts (crusades, terrorism). Religion isn't necessary — secular frameworks (human rights, law) also drive moral behavior."
  6. Conclusion (2 marks): "Therefore, religious belief can strengthen moral conviction and provide framework, but is not strictly necessary. Moral behavior flows from conscience, reason, and community — whether grounded in faith or not."

Review: Evaluation Checklist

What are the four parts of a PEEL paragraph?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Point (state your idea), Evidence (specific example), Explain (why it matters), Link (back to the question).

What's the "Some... However... Therefore" structure?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Some argue [viewpoint 1 with evidence]. However, [alternative viewpoint with counter-evidence]. Therefore, [your reasoned judgment].

What should a strong 6-mark evaluation include?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: A clear statement of judgment, specific evidence (scripture/examples), explanation of why the evidence matters, acknowledgment of alternative views, and a reasoned conclusion.

What's the difference between opinion and evaluation?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: Opinion: personal preference without reasoning. Evaluation: judgment supported by evidence, alternative perspectives, and logical reasoning.

Why do counter-arguments strengthen evaluation?

🔒Reveal answer

Answer: They show you understand the issue's complexity. Acknowledging counter-arguments makes your own judgment more persuasive because you've considered alternatives.
