The first championship

London, 1991. A small group of people sit at tables in a hall and wait for a signal. Nobody has done this before. There is no rule book yet worth the name. A writer on mind and learning, Tony Buzan, and a chess master and organiser, Raymond Keene, have invited a few people who claim to have unusual memories to compete in the first World Memory Championship.

The winner is a young Englishman who has trained in secret, using the methods of Roman orators and a system of his own: Dominic O'Brien, whom you met in lesson 8. He will win it eight times. But what is important is not who wins. It is that the old art has become a sport, with scores and a clock.

What happened in London in 1991?
The first World Memory Championship was held, and Dominic O'Brien won it.
Talk about it. Do you think memory should be a sport? What do you think it measures?

Since then the championship has grown into an international event, with national contests and young competitors from many countries. The events have settled into a standard programme of ten. Some use words, some use digits, some use cards, faces or images, and a few last an hour while others last seconds.

What strikes a visitor is the quiet. Competitors wear ear defenders, or headphones, to block noise. They sit still. When the clock starts, they are not memorising in the old sense of repeating. They are building and placing pictures, at the speed of a person turning pages.

What is the atmosphere of a championship like?
Very quiet: competitors wear ear defenders and sit still, building and placing pictures.
Talk about it. Would you like to watch a memory championship? To compete in one?

You do not need to compete to learn from them. The ten events are a catalogue of kinds of memory, and each is a practical question. How do you keep a list of words? A sequence of faces? A run of binary digits? A set of dates? Each event was designed to be hard, and each has a method.

In this lesson you will go through all ten, match each to the tool from this course, and set up a small event of your own as a personal test.

How events are scored

  • A correct answer gets points; for many events, a mistake in a section ends or reduces its score.
  • In speed events, the time counts and so does accuracy.
  • In long events, the total number of correct items counts.

The exact rules change from year to year, so check the current rules if you want to compete. The point for us is the training idea: every event is a skill that is trained, not a gift.

Binary digits in threes

The binary row 1 1 0 1 0 0 1 1 1 has nine digits. Group it into three groups of three, and give each group a picture (for example 110, 100, 111). Write your pictures.
Groups: 110, 100, 111. Pictures: 110 = a tin (one-one-oh); 100 = a hundred-pound note; 111 = three candles. Three pictures for nine digits.
