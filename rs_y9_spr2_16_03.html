Encounter: Religious Attitudes to Divorce

Christianity

Roman Catholic: Marriage is indissoluble. Jesus taught, "What God has joined together, let no one separate" (Mark 10:9). Divorce is not permitted. Annulment is possible — a declaration that the marriage was not valid from the beginning (e.g., due to lack of consent, impediments).

Church of England: Since 2002, permits divorce and remarriage. The grounds for divorce have broadened to include "irretrievable breakdown" of marriage.

Protestant churches: Generally view divorce as a last resort when the marriage has truly broken down. Remarriage is permitted and often supported pastorally.

Islam

Divorce (Talaq): Islam permits divorce, but it is framed as "the most disliked of permissible things" in Islamic tradition (hadith).

  • Who can initiate: Both husband and wife have the right to divorce. The wife can seek khul' (returning her mahr to gain her release) or use legal grounds.
  • Iddah (waiting period): After divorce, the wife has a waiting period (typically three months) to determine if she is pregnant and to allow for reconciliation.
  • Reconciliation: Islamic teaching strongly encourages reconciliation attempts during this period.

Judaism

Get (Divorce Document): Both civil and religious divorce are required in Jewish law. The get is a legal document given by the husband to the wife, formally dissolving the marriage.

Historical imbalance: In Orthodox tradition, only the husband can initiate divorce (grant the get). This has sometimes left wives "chained" (agunot) if the husband refuses. The Reform and Conservative movements have introduced joint procedures to address this.

Humanism

Divorce is sometimes the healthiest outcome when a marriage breaks down. Humanists believe:

  • There is no moral shame in divorce.
  • The wellbeing of both partners (and children) is the priority.
  • Laws should make divorce accessible and fair.
  • Children deserve support from both parents.

Explore: Case Studies

For each scenario, discuss what each tradition might advise. Remember: there are multiple views within traditions.

Case 1: Incompatibility

A couple married five years ago. They love each other but have realised they want very different lives. One wants to travel and pursue a career; the other wants to settle and start a family. They are not unhappy — just incompatible.

Reflect: Would each tradition encourage them to stay together or allow them to separate?

Catholic: Encourage reconciliation; consider it a test of commitment. Divorce not permitted unless grounds for annulment exist.

Islamic: Reconciliation strongly encouraged, but if marriage cannot work, talaq permitted. The waiting period (iddah) allows final reflection.

Reform Jewish: Both could seek divorce; mutual wellbeing is important.

Humanist: If incompatibility is genuine, divorce is acceptable. Better to separate than suffer.

Case 2: Abuse

One partner is being emotionally and physically abused. They have two young children. The abused partner wants to leave for safety.

Reflect: How would each tradition prioritise safety?

Catholic: Modern Catholic teaching acknowledges abuse as grounds for separation (though not remarriage). Safety comes before marriage vows.

Islamic: Islamic teaching forbids harm. Divorce is permitted to protect safety. Tradition says "no harm and no reciprocal harm" (la darar wa la dirar).

Reform Jewish: Divorce permitted; safety and wellbeing paramount.

Humanist: Divorce is not just acceptable — it is the ethical choice. Children's safety is paramount.
