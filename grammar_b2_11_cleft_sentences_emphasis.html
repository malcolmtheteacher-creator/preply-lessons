Warm-up

Out loud, before anything else — don't worry about getting it perfect, just talk.

SPEAK

Someone blames you for something you didn't do. How do you correct them, out loud, right now? Say the exact sentence you'd use.

Listen in on this argument

Jess: You said you'd lock the door. That's why we got robbed!

Tom: Hang on — it was you who said you'd do it. I never agreed to anything.

Jess: Wait, what? I'm sure it was your turn.

Tom: What actually happened is we both assumed the other one would sort it.

Look at what Tom does in two lines. He says almost the same thing twice, but each time the sentence is built completely differently. Can you spot the two different shapes? Say them before you check.

Check your answer CLICK
1. "It was you who said you'd do it" — it + was + who, putting the person in the spotlight to correct a wrong assumption.
2. "What actually happened is we both assumed..." — what + happened + is, putting the whole situation in the spotlight to explain.

Same facts, said twice — but the stress lands somewhere different each time. That's not an accident. It's a choice.
Today: three ways to put the stress exactly where you want it — it was X who/that..., what I need/want is..., and all I want is... — so you can correct a wrong assumption, say exactly what's bothering you, and make your point land in everyday arguments and conversations.

No, It Was Ana Who Called

The everyday tool for correcting someone's wrong idea about who did what — or what caused what.

Spot the pattern

  • Plain: Ana called, not Sam. → It was Ana who called, not Sam.
  • Plain: The traffic didn't make us late — the roadworks did. → It was the roadworks that made us late.
  • Plain: I didn't leave the light on. → It wasn't me who left the light on.

Same information both times. What's been added, and what job is it doing? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
It + is/was + [the one fact you're pointing at] + that/who + [the rest]. Nothing new is added to the meaning — the extra words just build a frame around the one word or phrase you want stressed. Perfect for correcting someone's wrong assumption about who or what.
It + is / was + [FOCUS] + that / who + [the rest of the sentence]
  who → a person   |   that → a thing, a reason, a time
One trap: which is never used for a person here. Not "It was Ana which called" — say "It was Ana who called" (or, less formally, "that").

Quick check

1. Everyone at the office assumes Priya paid for the team lunch. Actually it was Marcus. Correct them:

"It for lunch, not Priya."

2. Your friend blames the traffic for you being late. Actually it was the roadworks on Elm Street. Correct her:

"It the delay, not the traffic."

What I Actually Need

The structure for saying exactly what you mean when you're tired of going round in circles.

Spot the pattern

  • Plain: I need a bit of peace and quiet. → What I need is a bit of peace and quiet.
  • Plain: It really annoys me when people cancel last minute. → What really annoys me is when people cancel last minute.
  • Plain: The delivery never turned up — that's what happened. → What happened is the delivery never turned up.

Where does the sentence start now, and why might that help in an argument? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
What + [subject + verb] + is/was + [the one thing you're pointing at]. Starting with "what" makes your listener wait for the point — by the time you say "is", they're paying full attention. Perfect for arguments and clarifying: "What I'm saying is...", "What annoys me is...".
What + [subject + verb] + is / was + [FOCUS]
One trap: don't add an extra "to" before a plain noun. Not "What I need is to a holiday" — just "What I need is a holiday." (Only add "to" when the focus is an action: "What I need is to relax.")

All I Want Is...

The structure for making your point sound small, simple, and impossible to argue with.

Spot the pattern

  • Plain: I only want an apology, nothing else. → All I want is an apology.
  • Plain: You only need to ask — it's that simple. → All you have to do is ask.
  • Plain: We only suggested a different restaurant, and she got upset. → All we did was suggest a different restaurant.

What does "all" seem to promise about how big or small the thing is? Say it, then check.

Check the pattern CLICK
All + [subject + verb] + is/was + [FOCUS]. "All" signals "the only thing" — it makes a request sound minimal ("all I want is...") or makes something you did sound smaller than someone's reaction to it ("all we did was...").
All + [subject + verb] + is / was + [FOCUS]
  before a verb, "to" is optional: All you have to do is ask (or "is to ask") — but never add "to" before a plain noun.

Quick check

1. Your friend keeps overcomplicating the apology conversation. Tell them the only thing you want, right now:

"All I a proper apology."

2. Your colleague thinks fixing the printer is complicated. Tell them it's simple:

"All you is press the reset button."

Now say it

  2. "All you have to do to make me happy is..."
  3. "All we did was..." (describe something small that got blown out of proportion)
