The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The outsider from Corsica

He was born Napoleone di Buonaparte in 1769, on the Mediterranean island of Corsica, which France had bought and taken over only the year before. His family were minor nobility, but far from rich, and on the mainland he was treated as a provincial outsider. At military school in France his classmates mocked his accent and his strange island name; he answered by working harder than anyone, burying himself in books on history, mathematics and war.

Then, in 1789, the French Revolution swept away the old world of kings and privilege. For an ambitious young officer of no great birth, it was the chance of a lifetime: suddenly, talent counted more than a family name. Napoleon rose with astonishing speed. By his mid-twenties he had already made his name crushing a rebellion and driving the British from the port of Toulon, and the new government had promoted him to general.

Sent to fight in Italy, he transformed a ragged, half-starved French army into a force that won victory after victory. By the time he returned to Paris, he had become the most famous soldier in France — and he had understood something his rivals had not: in a country exhausted by years of revolution and chaos, the people were ready to follow a strong hand.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why was Napoleon treated as an outsider when he arrived in mainland France?

2. Why was the French Revolution such an opportunity for a young man like him?

1. He was born on Corsica, which France had only just taken over; classmates mocked his accent and his island name, and his family were minor, not wealthy, nobility.

2. The Revolution swept away the old system of birth and privilege, so talent and ability could carry someone far — and he had exactly that.

Discuss:
  • Napoleon turned being an outsider into a driving force. Why do outsiders sometimes end up more ambitious than those born at the centre of power?

Part 2 — From general to Emperor

In 1799, with France still unstable, Napoleon and a small circle of allies seized power in a swift coup, and he made himself First Consul — in practice, the ruler of France. He proved to be far more than a soldier. He reorganised the courts and drew up the Napoleonic Code, a clear new system of law that swept away centuries of tangled custom; versions of it still shape legal systems around the world today. He founded schools and banks and rebuilt the shattered finances of the state.

But he wanted more than to govern. In 1804, in a ceremony of extraordinary grandeur in Notre-Dame cathedral, he was crowned Emperor of the French. In a gesture that shocked observers, he took the crown from the Pope's hands and placed it on his own head — a message the whole of Europe understood: his power came from no one but himself.

For a decade he seemed unstoppable. Battle after battle went his way, and by 1810 he had built an empire that stretched across much of Europe, ringed by states ruled by his own relatives. Yet these were years of near-constant war, and each victory was paid for in enormous loss of life on every side. His great rival, Britain, he could not defeat at sea; unable to invade, he tried instead to ruin its trade with a vast blockade of the continent's ports. It was a decision that would slowly turn much of Europe against him.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name two lasting things Napoleon did as ruler that were not about war.

2. What did he do at his coronation in 1804, and what did it signal?

1. Any two of: the Napoleonic Code (a clear new legal system), reformed courts, new schools and banks, and rebuilt state finances.

2. He took the crown from the Pope's hands and crowned himself — signalling that his authority came from himself alone, not from the Church.

Discuss:
  • Napoleon is remembered both as a reformer who modernised France and as a warlord whose ambition cost countless lives. Can both of those things be true at once?

Part 3 — Russia, Waterloo, and exile

In 1812 Napoleon made the greatest mistake of his career. Frustrated that Russia had broken with his blockade, he invaded it with an enormous army — the largest Europe had ever seen. The Russians retreated before him, refusing to give him the single crushing battle he wanted, and burning their own fields and towns as they went. He reached Moscow, only to find much of it in flames and empty of the surrender he expected. With winter closing in, he had no choice but to turn back. The long retreat through the snow, harried and freezing, destroyed his army; of the hundreds of thousands who had marched east, only a fraction came home.

Sensing weakness, the powers of Europe united against him. In 1814, after they had entered Paris, Napoleon was forced to give up his throne and was sent into exile on the small island of Elba. It might have ended there. But less than a year later he escaped, landed in France, and marched on Paris. The soldiers sent to arrest him rallied to his side instead, and for a hundred days he ruled again.

The final reckoning came at Waterloo, in June 1815. There, a British-led army under the Duke of Wellington, joined at the crucial hour by a Prussian force, broke Napoleon's last army for good. This time there was no return. He was exiled again — far away this time, to the remote Atlantic island of Saint Helena, where he died in 1821, aged fifty-one. And yet the legend outlived the man: the poor outsider from Corsica who had crowned himself Emperor became one of the most studied, admired and argued-over figures in all of history.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the invasion of Russia in 1812 end in disaster?

2. What happened after his first exile, and how did it finally end?

1. The Russians retreated and burned their own land rather than fight; Napoleon reached a burning, empty Moscow, then had to retreat through the winter, which destroyed most of his army.

2. He was exiled to Elba, escaped, and briefly ruled again — until he was defeated at Waterloo in 1815, then exiled to distant Saint Helena, where he died in 1821.

Discuss:
  • The invasion of Russia undid everything Napoleon had built. Why do successful, powerful people so often overreach in exactly this way?

Grammar — the past perfect & sequencing

Napoleon's story is all about order: what had already happened before the next thing began. When you are telling a story in the past and you need to step further back — to an event that happened before your main past moment — English uses the past perfect: had + past participle.

How it works:
• Past simple is your main storyline: "He reached Moscow."
• Past perfect (had + past participle) steps back to something earlier: "The Russians had burned the city before he arrived."
Put them together and the sequence is crystal clear: "By the time he reached Moscow, the Russians had already burned much of it."
Sequencing signals to look out for:
• by 1804 / by the time… — often pairs with the past perfect: "By 1804 he had crowned himself Emperor."
• already, just, never (before), until then — sit inside the past perfect: "He had already made his name…"
• after / before / once / when — link two moments in time: "After they had entered Paris, he gave up his throne."
• Time words like first, then, next, soon, later, finally keep your main storyline moving in order.

1. Past simple or past perfect?

Put the verb in brackets into the past simple or the past perfect, so the order of events is clear.

1. By the time he was twenty-five, Napoleon (already / make) his name as a soldier.

2. When French troops (enter) Moscow, they found that the Russians had set it on fire.

3. He (never / lose) a major campaign before the disaster in Russia.

1. had already made (earlier than "by the time he was twenty-five" → past perfect)

2. entered (the main storyline event → past simple)

3. had never lost (before another past point → past perfect)

2. Join the two events

Combine each pair into one sentence, using the past perfect for the earlier action and the linking word given.

1. The Pope brought the crown. Napoleon took it and crowned himself.
(after) After the Pope the crown, Napoleon crowned himself.

2. Europe's powers entered Paris. Then Napoleon gave up his throne.
(once) Once Europe's powers Paris, Napoleon gave up his throne.

3. He escaped from Elba. Then he marched on Paris.
(after) After he from Elba, he marched on Paris.

1. had brought (the earlier action → had + past participle)

2. had entered

3. had escaped

3. Sequence the rise and fall — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the past simple or the past perfect. Read it aloud once you're done.

By 1804, Napoleon (crown) himself Emperor. By 1810 he (build) an empire across much of Europe. Then, in 1812, he (invade) Russia — a country that (break) with his blockade. By the time he reached Moscow, the Russians (already / burn) it.

had crowned · had built · invaded · had broken · had already burned

Notice the pattern: "by 1804 / by the time…" pulls you back to the past perfect (earlier events), while the single, forward-moving action of the storyline — "in 1812 he invaded" — stays in the past simple.
