Today we are working on sound and rhythm so you can explain how a poem’s sound creates meaning, not just point out alliteration.

The rule for sound

Never write “the alliteration makes it flow”. Sound analysis has three parts: name the sound, say what it imitates or creates, and link it to the meaning. A hard, repeated consonant can imitate gunfire; a soft, long vowel can slow the poem to grief.

Read the poem aloud, twice. The second time, exaggerate the sounds. Written in 1917 during the First World War.

Anthem for Doomed Youth · Wilfred Owen (1920)

1What passing-bells for these who die as cattle?
Only the monstrous anger of the guns.
Only the stuttering rifles' rapid rattle
Can patter out their hasty orisons.
5No mockeries now for them; no prayers nor bells,
Nor any voice of mourning save the choirs,—
The shrill, demented choirs of wailing shells;
And bugles calling for them from sad shires.
What candles may be held to speed them all?
10Not in the hands of boys, but in their eyes
Shall shine the holy glimmers of goodbyes.
The pallor of girls' brows shall be their pall;
Their flowers the tenderness of patient minds,
And each slow dusk a drawing-down of blinds.

Sibilancerepeated s / sh soundshissing, whispering, or shells flying
Assonancerepeated vowel soundslong vowels can slow the line into grief
Plosivea burst sound (p, b, t, d, k)sudden force, anger, shots
Caesura / enjambmentpauses inside lines / lines that run onpace, hesitation, breathlessness

Weak versus strong

Weak: Owen uses alliteration in “rifles’ rapid rattle”, which makes it flow nicely.
Strong: The harsh, repeated /r/ and plosive /t/ sounds of “stuttering rifles’ rapid rattle” imitate rapid gunfire in the reader’s mouth, so that the funeral rites for the dead are replaced by the noise of the weapons that killed them, which unsettles the reader by making grief impossible to hear.

SPEAK: “Makes it flow nicely” is wrong for two reasons. What are they?

It describes the opposite of the actual effect (the sounds are harsh and broken, not smooth), and it names no link to meaning. The strong version names the sounds, says what they imitate, and links them to the poem’s argument.

Listen closely

1. Read aloud and say what you hear

“Only the stuttering rifles’ rapid rattle / Can patter out their hasty orisons.”

SPEAK: What is odd about “patter out … orisons”? (An orison is a prayer.)

“Patter” normally describes light rain; here the sound of rifles replaces human prayers. The soldiers’ “prayers” are only the machine-gun noise, and “hasty” suggests how quickly they die. The sound of the words and the meaning pull in the same direction.

2. Sibilance and shells

“The shrill, demented choirs of wailing shells”

SPEAK: Which sounds dominate? What picture do they make?

The sibilance (“shrill”, “shells”) and the high, piercing vowels (“shrill”, “wailing”) imitate the whistle of shells. “Choirs” is a sacred word turned into a weapon: the war has its own warped church music. The effect is to horrify the reader by corrupting the sounds of mourning.

3. The turn

Compare the sound of line 1–8 with the sound of line 9–14.

SPEAK: What happens to the sound, and what does this do to the mood?

The octave is loud, hard and public (guns, shells, bugles). The sestet turns quiet, soft and private (“eyes”, “goodbyes”, “pallor”, “patient minds”, “drawing-down of blinds”); the long vowels and gentle consonants slow the pace. The shift from noise to silence mirrors the move from battlefield to home, and from anger to grief.

Looking back

SPEAK: Did reading aloud change how you analysed the poem? How?

I can: name a sound feature, explain what it imitates or creates, and link it to a poem’s meaning.
onomatopoeia (noun) — a word that imitates the sound it names. Example: "Rattle is an example of onomatopoeia."
sibilance (noun) — repeated s or sh sounds. Example: "The sibilance imitates the hiss of shells."
assonance (noun) — repetition of vowel sounds in nearby words. Example: "The assonance of long vowels slows the line."
plosive (noun) — a consonant made with a sudden burst of air, such as p, t or k. Example: "The plosives sound like gunshots."
octave (noun) — the first eight lines of a sonnet. Example: "The octave is loud and public."
sestet (noun) — the last six lines of a sonnet. Example: "The sestet is quiet and private."
elegy (noun) — a poem of mourning for the dead. Example: "The poem is an elegy for soldiers."
imitate (verb) — to copy the sound or form of something. Example: "The sounds imitate gunfire."
