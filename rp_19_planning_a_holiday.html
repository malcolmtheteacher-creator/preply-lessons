Planning a Holiday

Grey skies, a modest budget, and a powerful urge to be somewhere else. Time to turn a daydream into two tickets.

New to English? Try the easier A2/B1 version →

The Scene 🎬

It's the sort of week where the rain hasn't stopped and neither has your longing to be anywhere warmer. On the way home you pass a travel agent's — a proper old-fashioned one, with faded sunsets in the window — and something makes you push the door open.

You don't know exactly what you want yet: somewhere with a bit of sun, a bit of culture, and nothing you'll regret when the credit-card bill lands. The budget is real and the choices are many. Getting the right holiday today won't come from taking the first thing offered — it'll come from describing what you're really after, weighing the options honestly, and knowing which questions to ask before you commit.

Behind the desk sits someone who's sent a thousand people somewhere sunny — warm, quick, and refreshingly honest about which trips are worth the money and which are just a nice brochure.

Warm up — talk for a minute or two

  1. Think of the best holiday you've ever had. Was it the place itself that made it — or the people, the timing, or something you couldn't have planned for?
  2. You've got one week and a sensible budget. Be honest: are you a lie-on-the-beach person, a see-everything person, or a find-the-best-food person — and can any single trip keep all three of you happy?

Who's who

You
The Traveller 🧳

Dreaming of sun, guarding a budget. You want the right trip, not just any trip — so you'll describe, compare and question before you part with a penny.

The other voice
The Travel Agent 🌍

Has sold every kind of holiday there is. Knows what's genuinely worth paying for and what's just a pretty photo — and will tell you, if you ask the right way.

The Conversation 💬

Read it out loud, one role each — then swap. Listen for how the traveller keeps every option open, pins down the budget, and only commits once they've asked the questions that matter.

AgentCome in, come in — mind the puddle. Escaping somewhere warm, I hope? Where can I send you?
YouThat's the dream. Honestly, we're just at the ideas stage — somewhere with a bit of sun, a bit of culture, and not completely overrun. Does such a place exist on a normal budget?
AgentIt does, if you know where to look. What sort of figure did you have in mind — roughly?
YouWe'd rather not go much above fifteen hundred for the two of us, all in, if we can help it. I know that doesn't buy the moon.
AgentYou'd be surprised. For that I could do you a week in southern Portugal, or stretch a little to the Greek islands. Both tick your boxes.
YouNow you're talking. What's the difference in practice — beyond the extra cost, I mean?
AgentPortugal's the safer bet: shorter flight, everything walkable, easy on the wallet once you're there. Greece is the more romantic of the two — but you'll spend more on ferries and eating out.
YouHmm. I'm rather torn, to be honest. Can I just check — when you say fifteen hundred, what's actually included in that?
AgentFlights, hotel and transfers. Not the extras — food, excursions, the odd cocktail at sunset. Those are on you.
YouFair enough. And is it worth paying the bit extra for the sea view, or is that just clever marketing?
AgentBetween you and me? Save your money. You'll be on the beach all day and asleep by the time it's dark. Put it towards a boat trip instead — that's the memory you'll actually keep.
YouThat's good to know. Look — I think we'd probably give Greece a miss this time and keep it simple. If it were you, would Portugal be the one?
AgentFor a first trip on that budget? Without a doubt. You'll come back for Greece another year — they all do.
YouRight. In that case, could I have a quick think and pop back this afternoon to book it? I'd hate to commit and then spot something we've forgotten.
AgentOf course — no rush at all. I'll hold the price till five. Mind you, between us, the good rooms go fast in weather like this — half the street's dreaming of Portugal today.
YouThen let's not tempt fate — you've talked me into it. Let's go ahead and book the Portugal week. You've been a real help; I'll see you at five.

From memory — no peeking back up!

1. The traveller walks in "just at the ideas stage" and walks out having booked. What finally made them commit — and why does the agent bring up the weather right at the end?

2. Two destinations were on the table for roughly the same budget. What was the real trade-off between them — the difference that mattered once you were actually there?

3. The agent talks the traveller OUT of paying for the sea view. Why would someone selling holidays do that — and what does the traveller gain by trusting the advice?

1. They committed after the agent quietly hinted the good rooms sell out fast in this weather — "half the street's dreaming of Portugal today." It's a gentle nudge of scarcity: no hard sell, just a reason not to dawdle. The traveller reads it, decides not to "tempt fate", and books.
2. Same headline price, very different reality. Portugal: shorter flight, walkable, cheap once you're there — the easy, safe first trip. Greece: more romantic but pricier in practice, thanks to ferries and eating out. The trade-off was comfort and cost versus romance.
3. Steering them off the sea view makes the agent look honest — so the traveller believes the rest of the advice (spend it on a boat trip). The traveller gains a better memory for the same money, and the sense the agent is genuinely on their side rather than just selling.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases that turn a vague daydream into the right holiday — here and anywhere you're spending money on a choice. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it. Guessing first is how they stick.

🧭 Opening & keeping your options open

Phrase 1

You've come in to explore, not to buy today — and you don't want to be steered into the first thing that's pushed at you. Which opener keeps every option (and your wallet) open?

A. "I want to book a holiday to Portugal."
B. "What's your cheapest deal?"
C. "We're just at the ideas stage, really — nothing fixed yet."
C — "We're just at the ideas stage, really — nothing fixed yet."
It signals you're exploring, invites the agent to guide rather than sell, and commits you to absolutely nothing. Cousins: "We're keeping our options open" and "We're not set on anywhere yet."
Phrase 2

You want to describe the holiday you're dreaming of WITHOUT naming a single place — leave the expert to match it. There's a four-word frame that hands over your wish list: "___ ___ really after is somewhere with sun and a bit of culture."

"What we're really after is…"
It names the feeling, not the destination, and lets the agent do the matching for you. Handy relatives: "What we've got in mind is…" and "Ideally, we're looking for somewhere that…"

💰 Talking money & ruling things out

Phrase 3

You need to set a firm ceiling on the price — but without sounding tight-fisted or killing the conversation. Which lands better?

A. "I'm not spending more than fifteen hundred."
B. "We'd rather not go much above fifteen hundred, if we can help it."
B — "We'd rather not go much above fifteen hundred, if we can help it."
"…if we can help it" softens a hard limit into a preference — still firm, but it leaves the agent room to work with you. A is a wall; B is a line you'd rather not cross, which is far easier to negotiate around.
Phrase 4

One option just isn't for you, and you want to take it off the table politely — no criticism of the place, just "not for us". Say it kindly, in a handful of words. What would you say?

"I think we'd probably give that one a miss."
"Give it a miss" declines without insulting the choice — gentle, idiomatic, blame-free. Close cousin: "That's not really our sort of thing." Both let you say no to the option without saying anything bad about it.

⚖️ Weighing the options

Phrase 5

You genuinely can't choose between the two on offer — and you want to say so out loud, inviting the agent to help tip the balance. What's your line?

"I'm rather torn, to be honest."
It admits indecision honestly and quietly asks for a steer, without committing you either way. Variants worth having: "It's a toss-up between the two" and "I keep going back and forth."
Phrase 6

The headline price is one thing — you want to know exactly what it covers before you trust it. Put the pieces in order:

what's actuallyincluded in that?can I justcheck
"Can I just check what's actually included in that?"
"Can I just check…" is the polite way to interrogate a price without sounding suspicious; "actually included" quietly signals you know there are usually extras hiding underneath. Perfect for any quote, deal, or bill.

🔍 Asking the smart questions

Phrase 7

You're being offered an upgrade — the sea view — and you suspect it isn't worth it. Ask whether the extra money buys anything real, WITHOUT accusing them of upselling. What do you ask?

"Is it worth paying the bit extra for…?"
It puts the value question directly but pleasantly, and invites an honest answer instead of a sales pitch. The cheeky follow-up that gets the truth out: "…or is that just clever marketing?"
Phrase 8

You trust this agent now — so ask for the honest, personal steer they'd give a friend, not a customer. Build the question:

which wouldyou go for?if it were you,
"If it were you, which would you go for?"
The magic question that turns a salesperson into an adviser — it's very hard to answer with a brochure line. Relatives: "What would you do in my shoes?" and "Which would you book yourself?"

✅ Deciding — buy time, then commit

Phrase 9

You're nearly there, but you want to think before you commit — and come back to book. Which line buys you time without offending or losing the deal?

A. "I'll think about it." (turning to leave)
B. "Could I have a quick think and pop back this afternoon to book it?"
B — "Could I have a quick think and pop back this afternoon to book it?"
Naming a time ("this afternoon") and your intention ("to book it") shows you're serious, not stalling — so the agent will happily hold it for you. A is the polite version of "no", and everyone knows it.
Phrase 10

Decision made — you want to commit warmly and humanly, not with a flat "yes". What's the line that closes the deal with a smile and credits the person who helped you?

"You've talked me into it — let's go ahead and book it."
It credits the agent for the decision and sounds delighted rather than resigned. Warm cousins: "You know what, let's just do it" and "Right — you've sold me."
Quick test: close your eyes and run the whole visit out loud using all ten, in order — open softly → describe the dream → set the budget → weigh the two → ask the smart questions → buy time → commit. When it flows, you're ready for Your Turn.

The Twist

🌴 Three hundred over

One more round — no script, no safety net. You've found the holiday that's everything you wanted… and it's three hundred pounds more than you swore you'd spend. There's a cheaper trip that's almost as good, but not quite. Start the timer and talk it through with the agent: is the extra worth it? Can anything be shaved — fewer nights, a different airport, a quieter week? Would you rather have the perfect trip and go without elsewhere, or the good-enough one and money to spare? Explore every angle out loud — and one rule: you must actually decide before the timer runs out, and be able to say why. No "we'll see".

The Echo 🕰️

Over two thousand years ago, Greek writers drew up a list of the seven most astonishing things a traveller could hope to see — a pyramid that scraped the sky, gardens that seemed to hang in the air, a statue of Zeus so vast it looked ready to stand up and walk out of its temple. It was, in effect, the world's first bucket list.

They had no travel agents, no all-inclusive deals, no price held till five o'clock. A journey to see even one wonder could take months, cost a small fortune, and quite possibly kill you. And still people went — because some sights, they decided, were simply worth the whole trip.

You've just weighed sun against culture, a hotel view against a boat trip. The instinct is ancient: we have always been willing to travel absurd distances, and pay more than we meant to, just to stand in front of something wonderful.

Two bigger questions

  1. The ancient Seven Wonders were chosen by a handful of Greek writers who had never seen most of the world. If you drew up seven wonders of today — places worth crossing the planet for — what would make your list, and who should get to decide?
  2. A holiday, like a wonder, is partly the thing itself and partly the story you tell about it afterwards. Do we travel to see places, or to become the kind of person who's been there? Be honest.
