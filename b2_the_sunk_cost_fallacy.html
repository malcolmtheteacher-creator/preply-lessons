The Sunk Cost Fallacy

B2 Level · Why we keep doing what we should have stopped

The trap of "I've already…"

You're forty minutes into a film you no longer enjoy. You've eaten half a meal at a restaurant that turned out to be terrible. You've been working for two years on a project that, deep down, you suspect isn't going anywhere. In each of these cases, the rational move is to stop — but most of us keep going. Economists have a name for the trap, and the name itself explains why we fall into it: the sunk cost fallacy. We confuse what we've already spent — money, time, energy — with what we should do next.

Speak · first reactions

Two or three full sentences per answer. Take your time.

  1. When was the last time you carried on with something you should have stopped — a film, a meal, a meeting? What kept you going?
  2. We say "I've already paid for this" or "I've been working on this for ages." Both are facts. But are they reasons to continue, or are they just facts?
  3. Some people seem to walk away from things easily; others can't. Is one of these a strength?
  4. Most projects, businesses, even relationships hit a moment when "we've already invested too much" becomes the loudest argument in the room. What does it cost to listen to that argument when it isn't true?

Eight chunks to put to work

Click each card; read it; close it; move on. You'll meet all eight again in the reading, and you'll need them in the speaking work.

a sunk cost Money, time or effort already spent that you cannot get back — and that, logically, should not affect what you do next.
to throw good money after bad To keep investing in something that has already failed, hoping the next push will save it. Almost never does.
to cut one's losses To stop a failing project or activity now, and accept the loss, rather than make it bigger by continuing.
the point of no return The moment when stopping feels almost as costly as continuing. Often passed long before we notice.
to be heavily invested in To have put a lot of time, money or energy into something already — and to feel the weight of that investment in every decision about it.
the rational move What the logic says you should do — often different from what feels right in the moment.
to walk away (from) To stop and leave a project, decision or relationship behind — cleanly, without dragging it on.
to dig in To commit harder to a position even when the evidence says you shouldn't. The opposite of cutting your losses.

One paragraph. Then we talk.

Read this aloud, slowly. It's here to give us shared ground — not to be studied to death.

The American economist Richard Thaler — who later won a Nobel Prize — built much of his career on a strange observation about how human beings handle money. We say, perfectly often, "I've already paid for the ticket, so I have to go." We say "We've been working on this for two years, we can't stop now." Both of those sentences are facts. Neither of them is a reason. Once money or time has been spent, it is gone — it is a sunk cost — and it has no logical bearing on whether to continue. The rational move is always to ask "what's best from here?", not "what have I already spent?" And yet most of us, most of the time, can't quite do this. We keep watching the bad film because we've paid for the ticket. We keep working on the failing project because we're heavily invested in it. Sometimes we even dig in harder, throwing good money after bad, convinced that one more push will finally turn things around. We refuse to walk away from something we should have walked away from months ago, because walking away feels like admitting the past was a waste. Thaler called this the sunk cost fallacy. The trap is universal; the cost is real; and the cure — recognising the trap in yourself in real time, and being willing to cut your losses when the point of no return has long since passed — is one of the more useful pieces of mental discipline a working adult can develop.

Phrasing · read aloud, with a small pause at each comma and dash. The sentences are long; chunk them so they breathe.

Two quick orientations before we open it out:

1. Thaler distinguishes between a fact and a reason. In one sentence, what is the difference, and why does it matter for sunk costs?

Show Answer
A fact tells you what is true; a reason tells you what to do. "I have already paid for the ticket" is a fact. It is not a reason to go to a bad film, because the money is gone whether you go or not — so it should not weigh in the decision about what's best from here.

2. Why does walking away feel so difficult, even when it's the right thing to do?

Show Answer
Because walking away feels like admitting that the past was a waste. The investment is emotional as well as financial: stopping forces you to accept that the time and money you have already spent will not produce anything. That admission is more painful than the alternative — carrying on for a bit longer — and so most people quietly choose the alternative, even when they know it's wrong.
Speak · open it out

Pick the question that catches you and run with it.

  1. Pick one current situation in your own life where you might be falling for the sunk cost fallacy. Don't be defensive — just describe it honestly.
  2. The text says "what's best from here?" is always the right question. Why do you think more people don't ask it — at the dinner table, at the board meeting, at the long Tuesday planning session?
  3. Is there a meaningful difference between perseverance (often a good thing) and stubbornness (often a bad one)? Where does the line go — in practice, in your own life?
  4. We tell children to "finish what you started." Is that always wise advice, or do they need a more honest version of it?

Present perfect & present perfect continuous

Sunk-cost talk is built on two tenses. "I have already invested…", "We have been working on this for two years…", "He has never managed to walk away from a failing project…". Get these clean and you can describe any past-into-present situation precisely — which is much of business and personal English right there.

The forms: have / has + past participle (present perfect) — I have spent two years on this.   And have / has been + -ing (present perfect continuous) — I have been working on this for two years.   In both, the auxiliary have is what carries the past meaning. Never drop it under speed.

Present perfect simple

An action completed in the past, with present relevance. Often used with already, just, yet, ever, never.

We have already paid for the ticket.

He has never managed to walk away from a bad project.

Present perfect continuous

An action that started in the past and is still going (or has only just stopped). Often used with for + duration or since + point in time.

I have been working on this for two years.

We have been trying different solutions since January.

Which one when?

Use simple for completed action + present result. Use continuous for ongoing action emphasised over time.

I 've sent the proposal. (done)

I 've been writing this proposal all morning. (in progress)

Speak · oral transformation drill

Your teacher reads a flat sentence. You reformulate it on the spot, aloud, using the form indicated. Listen for the rhythm: have/has + past participle, or have/has been + -ing. Don't write. Each item has a model below.

1. Flat: "I started this project two years ago. I am still working on it."   →   use present perfect continuous

Show Answer
I have been working on this project for two years.

2. Flat: "I paid for the ticket already. The money is gone."   →   use present perfect with already

Show Answer
I have already paid for the ticket.

3. Flat: "He started looking for a solution last week. He still hasn't found one."   →   use present perfect continuous with since

Show Answer
He has been looking for a solution since last week, but he hasn't found one yet.

4. Flat: "We invested too much money in this. We can't stop now — that's how we feel."   →   use present perfect with too much

Show Answer
We have invested too much money in this to stop now — or so it feels.

5. Flat: "I'm exhausted. I worked on this all morning."   →   use present perfect continuous to explain why

Show Answer
I'm exhausted because I have been working on this all morning.

6. Flat: "We started throwing money at the problem six months ago, and we are still doing it."   →   use present perfect continuous

Show Answer
We have been throwing money at the problem for six months. (or: since six months ago)

Task A · a sunk cost you're carrying right now (3 minutes)

Pick one specific thing in your own life — a project, a habit, a subscription, a piece of work — where you suspect the only real reason you're continuing is past investment. Be honest. Cover:

  2. What signal told you it was time to walk away — and how did you ignore it?
  3. What would change if you cut your losses today?
  4. If you don't cut your losses, what's the most honest reason?

Task B · argue back (3 minutes)

A friend says to you:

"You've been working on this for two years. You can't just stop now. You've put too much in. One more push and it'll come good."

Argue against this for three minutes. Be specific about why past investment is not a reason. Use at least two present-perfect forms and at least two modal-perfect forms (should have / could have / would have) from the last lesson.

Task C · the walking-away role-play (3 minutes)

Your teacher plays a partner, a colleague or a friend who wants to keep going with something that is clearly failing. They argue from sunk cost: "we've come too far, we've already paid, we've been at it for so long." You make the case for cutting losses — gently, professionally, but clearly. Use the language from Tabs 2 and 4. Then swap, and try to argue the sunk-cost side honestly.

Final open discussion

  1. "We've already invested too much" is one of the most persuasive sentences in any language — even when it isn't a real reason. Why is it so persuasive?
  2. Some people walk away from things too quickly. Is there such a thing as too little stubbornness?
  3. If "what's best from here?" is the right question, why is it almost never the first one asked in a long meeting, a hard conversation, a difficult relationship?
  4. If you imagine yourself one year from now, which sunk cost in your current life would five-years-from-now-you most want present-you to cut today?
