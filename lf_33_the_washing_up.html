The Washing Up

Three days of somebody else's pans, a bin you have emptied every time for a year, and a housemate you genuinely like. Say it once, properly, tonight — because the alternative is becoming the person who sighs in kitchens.

The Scene 🎬

You have emptied that bin every single time for a year. You have counted.

Josh is a good housemate in every way that usually matters. He's easy company, he pays his share on the first of the month, he's never once had a party you weren't invited to. And he has never emptied the bin, never cleaned the hob, and currently has three days of pans in the sink that you have started walking past deliberately, to prove a point he has not noticed you making.

That's the actual problem: you've been having this argument alone, in your head, for about six months. He has no idea. And the longer it goes on, the more likely you are to say it badly — at eleven at night, after a drink, in the tone of someone who has been keeping score. Which you have.

So: tonight, once, calmly, and then never again. The aim isn't to win. It's to say it properly enough that you don't have to keep saying it.

  2. Why do we find it easier to be quietly annoyed for months than to say something in ten seconds?

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for saying a small domestic thing once, properly, so it doesn't become a year of sighing. Say each one out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The danger here is the tone of the martyr — the slightly wounded voice of someone who has been suffering nobly. Keep it brisk and almost cheerful, the way you'd raise a mildly annoying admin problem. The content is serious enough; if your voice is also serious, it becomes a much bigger conversation than a bin deserves.

🚪 Getting into it

Phrase 1

He's on the sofa, relaxed. You need his attention without making him think somebody's died. Put the pieces in order:

It's nothing major.Have you gottwo minutes?
"Have you got two minutes? It's nothing major."
The two halves do opposite and necessary jobs. "Have you got two minutes?" is a genuine request for attention, and naming a small number promises it'll be short. "It's nothing major" heads off the immediate spike of alarm — because an unannounced "can we talk" from a housemate sounds like rent, or moving out. Setting the size of a conversation before you start it is one of the most useful things you can do in English.
Phrase 2

Now take your share of the blame — before you make a single complaint. Which version does that without collapsing into apology?

A. "Sorry, this is probably really petty of me, ignore me if it's stupid."
B. "I've been putting this off, which is daft."
C. "I've said nothing for a year, so you can hardly blame me for being annoyed now."
B — "I've been putting this off, which is daft."
A gives him permission to dismiss it, and he will — you've told him it might be stupid. C uses your own silence as ammunition, which is the tone of someone who has been keeping score. B admits the delay was your fault and calls it daft rather than tragic, which lowers the temperature and quietly signals that this isn't an accusation. Daft is a mild, affectionate British word for silly — you'd use it about yourself far more readily than about someone else.
Phrase 3

Name the area, immediately and without a preamble, so he isn't sitting there guessing what he's done. Complete the missing word:

"It's the ___, mainly."

"It's the kitchen, mainly."
Short, factual, unfrightening. "Mainly" is quietly honest — it admits there's a bit more, without dumping the whole list on him in the first ten seconds. Naming the topic early is a kindness: the worst part of "can we talk" is the twenty seconds where the other person is mentally reviewing everything they've done wrong.

📏 Getting the size right

Phrase 4

He'll reach for the smallest possible version of the problem — a mug, one plate. Rule that out before he offers it. Complete the missing word:

"I'm not talking about the ___ mug."

"I'm not talking about the odd mug."
The odd here means occasional — the odd mug, the odd late night, the odd mistake. Pre-empting the trivial version is important, because if he gets to define the problem as one mug, then your year of resentment looks absurd and the conversation is over. You're setting the scale before he can.
Phrase 5

Now the specific: one concrete instance, with a number, and what you did about it. No adjectives. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "It's been three days, and I've ended up doing it again."
Notice there's no criticism in this sentence at all — it's two facts. "Three days" is checkable and impossible to dispute; "I've ended up doing it again" describes what happens without saying whose fault it is. "Ended up" is doing quiet work: it suggests something you didn't choose, which is exactly the complaint.

🙅 Saying why you've said nothing

Phrase 6

Explain the fear that has kept you silent for a year — because naming it stops you sounding like a nag while you're being one. Put the pieces in order:

nagging about a bin.I don't want to bethe one
"I don't want to be the one nagging about a bin."
To nag is to keep on at someone about small things — and it's a role nobody wants, which is precisely why domestic problems go unmentioned for years. Saying it out loud does two things: it explains your silence, and it makes clear this is a one-off rather than the opening of a campaign. Naming the role you're afraid of playing is usually how you avoid playing it.
Phrase 7

Now the alternative to nagging — and it's the sentence that justifies the whole conversation. Complete the missing word:

"I'd rather say it once than get ___ with you about it."

"I'd rather say it once than get funny with you about it."
To get funny with someone is to go cold or off with them without saying why — the silent treatment, the pointed sigh, the not-quite-friendly kitchen. It's exactly what's been happening, and admitting it is disarming: you're confessing to the passive-aggressive version before he accuses you of it. The sentence also frames speaking up as the kinder option, which it is.

📋 Fixing it

Phrase 8

Propose something practical — and make it a shared system rather than a set of instructions for him. Which version gets agreement?

A. "Could you just do the bin and the hob from now on?"
B. "Could we not just do a rota? A proper one."
C. "I just think everyone should clean up after themselves, really."
B — "Could we not just do a rota? A proper one."
A hands him a chore list, which makes you his manager. C states a principle — and principles are impossible to act on and easy to agree with while changing nothing. B proposes a system that binds you both equally, which is why it's acceptable. "Could we not just…" is a very British way of floating an idea: it sounds almost like an objection, but it means wouldn't it be simpler if we… — casual, low-stakes, easy to say yes to.
Phrase 9

Open the door the other way — invite the complaint you haven't heard. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "And if I'm the annoying one about something, tell me."
This is what turns a complaint into an arrangement. It also protects you from the exact thing you're complaining about — him quietly resenting you for a year — and in the conversation he takes it up straight away, which proves it was needed. Only say it if you'll accept the answer without arguing; a false invitation is worse than none.
Phrase 10

Close it, and promise it's over — because the fear he now has is that this is the first of many. Complete the missing word:

"Right — that's it said. I'm not going to keep ___ it up."

"Right — that's it said. I'm not going to keep bringing it up."
To bring something up is to raise it in conversation — and the promise not to keep doing it is what makes tonight bearable for him. "That's it said" is a lovely two-word full stop: the thing existed, it has now been spoken, and it doesn't need to exist again. It also puts you under a useful obligation — you now have to let it go and let the rota do the work.

🔑 Make them yours

These work for any small domestic irritation with anyone you live with — a partner, a flatmate, a grown-up child, a parent you've moved back in with. Pin three of them to your own life. Say each answer out loud before you type it.

"I've been putting this off, which is daft." — what have you not said, and for how long?

"I'd rather say it once than get funny with you about it." — where are you currently being silently annoyed?

"And if I'm the annoying one about something, tell me." — what do you suspect they'd say about you?

The Bigger Picture 🔭

Almost nobody falls out over the actual bin. They fall out over the year of not mentioning the bin — the silent tallying, the pointed sighs, the slow conversion of a person you like into a person you're keeping score against.

And there's usually an asymmetry underneath it: one person sees mess and one genuinely doesn't. Neither is lying. That's why a written list works better than a hint, and why "just be more considerate" fixes nothing — you can't be considerate about something you don't perceive.

The strange part is the maths. Six months of resentment, ten seconds of saying it, and a piece of paper on a fridge.

  1. Is it fair to be annoyed at someone for a year for something you never told them about? Where does the responsibility actually sit?
  2. In a shared house, should the standard be whatever the tidiest person wants, or the messiest? Who should have to change?
