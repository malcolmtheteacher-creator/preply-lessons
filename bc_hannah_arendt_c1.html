Hannah Arendt

The philosopher who looked at one of history's worst criminals and saw not a monster but an ordinary man who had simply stopped thinking.

A quick sketch of the woman

Now read this, then tell me what surprises you and what you already knew.

Hannah Arendt was born in 1906 into a German-Jewish family. She studied philosophy under the most celebrated minds of her generation, and looked set for a quiet academic life of pure ideas. History had other plans. When the Nazis came to power, she was briefly detained for researching antisemitic propaganda, and fled Germany. After years as a refugee she was interned in a French camp, escaped, and finally reached the United States in 1941 — stateless, nearly penniless, starting again in a new language.

That experience of statelessness never left her. She had learned, in her own body, what it means to be a person whom no country will protect — to have, as she put it, lost the very right to have rights. From then on her great subject was not abstract philosophy but the concrete, dangerous business of living together: power, freedom, politics, and how ordinary societies slide into horror.

In America she became a public thinker of rare force. Her books were demanding and her opinions sharp; she made enemies as easily as admirers. She refused to be comforting. When she covered the trial of a senior Nazi organiser in 1961, she reached a conclusion that enraged half the world — and it is that conclusion we are going to wrestle with today.

The most unsettling thing, she suggested, was not how monstrous the criminal seemed, but how disturbingly ordinary he was.

Over to you:
  • What in that sketch surprised you? Did anything shift how you picture her?
  • She insisted philosophy should deal with how we actually live together, not just abstract ideas. Do you find that more or less interesting than "pure" philosophy?
  • Her years as a stateless refugee shaped everything she wrote. Can you think of a thinker, artist or leader whose ideas clearly grew out of what happened to them?

The banality of evil — the idea that made everyone furious

This is the heart of the lesson. Here is the idea in my own words — read it, then we will pull it apart together.

The trial

In 1961, Arendt travelled to Jerusalem to report on the trial of Adolf Eichmann, the bureaucrat who had organised the transport of millions of people to the death camps. Everyone expected to see a monster in the dock — a fanatic, a sadist, a figure of pure hatred. That is what evil on that scale ought to look like.

What Arendt saw instead unsettled her far more. Eichmann struck her as strikingly mediocre: not a brilliant ideologue, not a frothing hater, but a careerist who spoke in clichés, who seemed proud of doing his job well, who could not appear to grasp what he had actually done. He was, in her phrase, terrifyingly normal.

The claim — and why it cuts so deep

From this she drew a claim that still divides people. The most catastrophic evil, she argued, does not always come from monstrous, demonic figures. It can come from utterly ordinary people who stop thinking — who never pause to ask whether what they are doing is right, who hide inside their job description, who let the system do their moral reasoning for them. Eichmann's great failing, in her reading, was not bottomless hatred but a kind of thoughtlessness: an inability to think from anyone else's point of view.

This is what she meant by "the banality of evil". Notice what it does not mean. She was not saying his crimes were small, or excusable, or that he was innocent — she thought he deserved to hang, and said so. She was saying something more frightening: that the capacity to take part in monstrous things may not require monstrous people at all. It may only require enough ordinary people who decline to think. The book also caused a separate firestorm with her hard, contested remarks about the role of some Jewish community leaders under the Nazis — a wound that has never fully healed.

The horror, she suggested, was that evil on this scale could be administered by a man who was less a monster than a deeply unthinking clerk.

This is the heart of the lesson — talk it through:
  • Is Arendt's idea more or less frightening than the idea that evil comes from monsters? Why?
  • She says the failure was a refusal to think — to imagine another person's point of view. Can ordinary thoughtlessness really be that dangerous, or is that letting people off the hook?
  • "I was only doing my job." Where do you hear that defence today — in workplaces, institutions, online? Is it ever legitimate?
  • Many people were enraged that she made a mass murderer sound ordinary. Do you think naming evil as "banal" minimises it, or does it actually help us prevent it?

Vocabulary — click each card to reveal

Ten words you need to discuss Arendt properly — and that work just as well for any serious conversation about ideas. Tap to reveal the meaning and an example.

banality
noun
The quality of being utterly ordinary, dull or unremarkable.
"Arendt was struck by the banality of the man on trial, not his menace."
complicity
noun
Involvement in wrongdoing, often by helping or simply not objecting.
"She forced readers to think about the complicity of ordinary officials."
to abdicate (responsibility)
verb
To give up or refuse a duty that is rightly yours.
"He abdicated all moral responsibility by hiding behind orders."
conscience
noun
The inner sense that tells you whether something is right or wrong.
"For Arendt, thinking is what keeps the conscience awake."
to dehumanise
verb
To treat people as if they were less than human.
"Bureaucratic language can dehumanise victims into mere figures."
thoughtlessness
noun
A failure to reflect or to consider others — not stupidity, but a refusal to think.
"She located the danger in sheer thoughtlessness, not in evil genius."
plurality
noun
The fact that we live among many different people, not as identical copies.
"Politics, for Arendt, begins with plurality — with the reality that we are many."
totalitarian
adjective
Describing a regime that demands total control over every part of life.
"A totalitarian state attacks not just freedom but truth itself."
moral (courage)
adjective
Concerned with right and wrong; moral courage is doing right despite the cost.
"It takes moral courage to refuse an order everyone else obeys."
to scapegoat
verb
To blame a person or group unfairly for a wider problem.
"Totalitarian movements thrive by scapegoating a chosen enemy."

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that most ordinary people, placed in that machine, would have done exactly as Eichmann did.
  • It might well be that thoughtlessness does more damage in the world than hatred.
  • I suspect the truth is somewhere in between.
  • You could argue that the bureaucracy, not the man, was the real criminal.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say the simple capacity to think is itself a moral defence.
  • What strikes me most is how unremarkable he seemed in the dock.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, her warning feels more urgent now than in 1963.

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure I fully buy that.
  • I take the point, but doesn't that assume…?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…

A · The Eichmann Test (3–4 min)

Picture an ordinary, competent person in a large organisation today — a clerk, a coder, a manager — who does small, legal, routine tasks that quietly add up to something harmful. They are polite, hard-working, and never break a rule.

Argue one side: are they morally responsible, or is the blame really with the system above them? Build a real case — give a concrete example, raise the strongest objection to your own view, then answer it.

B · The Duty to Think (3–4 min)

Arendt believed the antidote to "banal" evil was simply thinking — the habit of stopping to ask whether what you are doing is right, and of imagining how it looks from someone else's side.

Take your own life — work, family, the internet. Where do people around you stop thinking and just go along with things? And is Arendt right that thinking is enough to stop it? Pick one example and make the case.

C · Build Your Own (4–5 min)

Arendt warned that totalitarian power survives by attacking truth until people no longer know what is real. Your turn: invent a modern thought experiment. Describe one realistic situation — "imagine a society where…" — in which the line between true and false quietly disappears.

Open discussion
