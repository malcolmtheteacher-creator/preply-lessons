Before you defend your opinion, should you be able to explain the best argument against it?

Two friends are arguing online about whether cars should be banned from city centres. Neither has really listened to the other; they just repeat their own view, louder each time. What if each of them had to explain the other's best argument first? Nearly eight hundred years ago, a quiet Italian friar built an enormous book on exactly that rule.

Today: You'll meet Thomas Aquinas and the way he built his arguments, so you can give your reasons in a clear order and reply to the strongest objections to your view.

Meet Aquinas

Read one part at a time. After each part, close your eyes and answer the question before you check.

🏰

Locked in a castle

Thomas Aquinas was born in 1225 near Aquino, in Italy, into a noble family. They hoped he would become the head of a rich monastery. Instead, at about nineteen, he joined the Dominicans, a new group of poor friars devoted to study and preaching. His family were furious. His brothers caught him on the road and kept him locked in the family castle for about a year. He still did not change his mind.

Why did Aquinas's family lock him in a castle?

Because he joined the Dominicans, a group of poor friars, instead of becoming the head of a rich monastery as they wanted.
🐂

The dumb ox

Aquinas studied with Albert the Great, a famous teacher. He was big, quiet and slow to speak, so, according to an old story, his classmates called him 'the dumb ox'. Albert is said to have replied that one day this ox's voice would be heard all over the world. He was right. Aquinas became a star teacher at the University of Paris and wrote millions of words, using Aristotle to explain and defend the Christian faith.

What did Albert say about 'the dumb ox'?

That one day the ox's voice would be heard all over the world, and it was.
📖

The great Summa

His biggest work, the Summa Theologica, is built from thousands of questions, from 'Does God exist?' to 'Is it ever right to lie?' Each one follows the same pattern. First he lists the strongest objections against his own view. Then he gives his answer and his reasons, and finally he replies to each objection, one by one. In 1273, according to his friends, he stopped writing, saying his work seemed like straw. He died in 1274, leaving the Summa unfinished.

What does Aquinas always do before giving his own answer?

He lists the strongest objections against his own view, and later he replies to each one.
Talk: Aquinas always started with the best arguments against himself. Why do you think he did that? Does it make you more convinced by his answer, or less?

Key words

Each word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means. Then reveal and compare.

1. Nobody raised any objections, so the new rule was accepted at once.
objection (n) — a reason that you give for disagreeing with an idea or a plan. Example: "My main objection to the plan is the cost."
2. His reasoning was simple: the shop is cheaper and closer, so why go anywhere else?
reasoning (n) — the process of thinking about something in a logical way to reach a conclusion. Example: "I don't agree with her, but I can follow her reasoning."
3. She refuted every rumour about her by showing the real emails, one by one.
refute (v) — to prove that a statement or an argument is wrong. Example: "The scientist refuted the claim with clear evidence."
4. After a long debate, he finally conceded that his opponent had a good point about taxes.
concede (v) — to admit, often unwillingly, that something is true or that the other side has a point. Example: "I concede that your plan is cheaper, but mine is faster."
5. Instead of studying randomly, she took a systematic approach: one chapter a day, with a test every Friday.
systematic (adj) — done carefully, following a fixed plan or method. Example: "The police made a careful, systematic search of the house."
6. He was a devout man who prayed every morning and every evening.
devout (adj) — believing strongly in a religion and following its practices carefully. Example: "Her grandparents were devout and never missed a religious festival."
7. Nothing could change his conviction that education is the key to a better life.
conviction (n) — a strong belief or opinion. Example: "She spoke with real conviction about protecting the forest."

The idea: The Summa method

💬

🔁
RepliesAnswer each objection, one by one.

See it in action

Here is the method in action. Question: Should cars be banned from city centres? Objection 1: older and disabled people need cars to get around. Objection 2: shops may lose customers. My answer: yes, mostly, because the air would be cleaner and the streets safer. Reply to 1: allow special permits and better buses for people who need them. Reply to 2: in some car-free streets, shops have done well, because people walk and browse more. Every objection gets an answer, not silence.

Your turn

Now you: explain Aquinas's method to a friend who always argues by talking louder. Speak for about a minute. Use the starters if they help.

Aquinas always started by...Then he...Finally, he...This works because...
Aquinas always started by asking a clear question and listing the strongest objections against his own view. Then he gave his own answer, with his reasons in order. Finally, he replied to every objection, one by one. This works because you show that you have really listened, so your answer is stronger and harder to dismiss.

Think it through

Thought experiment: The other side's best argument

Imagine a new rule for every TV debate: before you can give your opinion, you must explain your opponent's best argument so well that your opponent says, "Yes, that's exactly what I think." Debates would be slower, but perhaps fairer.

Give two reasons for your choice. Then argue for the other side for thirty seconds. What do you think Aquinas would say about this rule?

Useful language: Giving reasons in order

Firstly...Firstly, starting with the objections shows that you have really listened.
Secondly...Secondly, answering each objection makes your own argument stronger.
Another reason is...Another reason is that the other side is more likely to listen to you.
Finally...Finally, you might discover that the other side was partly right.

Compare

Averroes and Aquinas: Aquinas read Aristotle with the help of Averroes's commentaries, and both believed that faith and reason can agree. But Aquinas also wrote against some of Averroes's ideas. Can you learn a lot from someone and still argue with them?
Yes. Aquinas respected Averroes as the great explainer of Aristotle, but he disagreed with some of his conclusions about the human mind. Learning from someone doesn't mean agreeing with everything; often the best way to respect a thinker is to argue with them seriously.
Aristotle and Aquinas: Aquinas called Aristotle simply 'the Philosopher'. Why might a Christian friar admire a Greek thinker who lived more than three hundred years before Christ?
Because Aquinas believed reason belongs to all human beings, not only to believers. Aristotle had used reason brilliantly to study nature, ethics and logic, and Aquinas thought this could help to explain and support faith, not threaten it.

Remember earlier thinkers

No looking back. Answer from memory first, then check.

From Averroes (Ibn Rushd)

What did Averroes believe about faith and reason?

That truth cannot contradict truth: real faith and careful reasoning must agree, and if they seem to clash, the words of scripture should be interpreted more deeply.
From Boethius

What did Lady Philosophy teach Boethius about Fortune?

That Fortune is a turning wheel: it gives and takes away money, power and fame, but it can never take what is inside you, like your mind and your goodness.
From Zeno of Citium

What was Zeno's main lesson about things we can't change?

Divide things into what is up to you and what is not. Accept calmly what is not up to you, and put your energy into your own choices.
From Parmenides

What did Parmenides argue about change, and why?

That change is impossible, because nothing can come from nothing or turn into nothing. So reality is one unchanging whole, and the change we see is an illusion.

That faith and reason can work together: reason can explain and support faith instead of fighting it.
