Hinge Moments

One night in 1983, one man had a few minutes and one decision. Hear the true story, learn the grammar of “what if”, and then find the hinge moments in your own life.

Today: you will tell the true story of a night when the world waited on one decision, practise talking about what could have happened — If he hadn’t…, we wouldn’t… — and then find a hinge moment of your own.
Before we start. Let’s see what comes to mind — have a go, and don’t worry about mistakes.
Say it out loud:

  • Think of a small decision — a train you caught, a message you sent — that turned out to matter much more than you expected. What happened?

Words you will need

Ten words and phrases, in three short activities. Say your guess out loud before you reveal it.

A · What does the word in bold mean? Guess from the sentence

1. The whole argument hinges on one question: was the witness telling the truth?
hinge on (phrasal verb) — to depend completely on something. A hinge moment is a moment that everything afterwards depends on. The match hinged on a single penalty.
2. The siren rang at three in the morning, but it was a false alarm: someone had burnt the toast.
a false alarm (noun phrase) — a warning of danger that turns out to be a mistake. The smoke detector keeps giving false alarms.
3. Hospital protocol says that two nurses must check every dose of strong medicine.
protocol (noun) — the official set of rules for how something must be done. Follow the protocol; break the protocol.
4. In the army there is a clear chain of command: a soldier reports to a sergeant, the sergeant reports to an officer, and so on upwards.
chain of command (noun phrase) — the line of authority, from the lowest rank to the highest, through which orders and information pass.
5. The argument began over a parking space, but it quickly escalated into a fight between two families.
escalate (verb) — to become, or make something, more serious or more intense. Tensions escalated.

B · Five more — guess again

6. The government warned that it would retaliate if its ships were attacked again.
retaliate (verb) — to hit back; to answer an attack with another attack. Noun: retaliation.
7. “In hindsight, we should have left earlier — but at the time nobody knew about the traffic.”
in hindsight / with hindsight — understanding a situation only after it has happened. Hindsight is a wonderful thing.
8. The manager reprimanded him for arriving late for the third time that week.
reprimand (verb / noun) — to tell someone officially that they have done something wrong. He received a formal reprimand.
9. For years people laughed at her theory. Then the new data arrived, and she was completely vindicated.
vindicate (verb) — to show that someone was right, after people said they were wrong.
10. Nobody remembers the nurse who spotted the error. She is an unsung hero.
unsung hero (noun phrase) — someone who did something brave or important and never received the praise they deserved.

The man who waited

The story comes in four moves. After each move, stop and speak before you go on.

Move 1 · The world on edge

In 1983 the Cold War was more dangerous than it had been for twenty years. Three weeks earlier, a Soviet fighter plane had shot down a South Korean passenger jet, killing all 269 people on board. The United States was preparing to put new missiles in Europe. Leaders on both sides were talking openly about the possibility of war.

Stanislav Petrov, a lieutenant colonel in the Soviet air defence forces, was on duty in a secret bunker near Moscow. His job was to watch the screens of Oko, a brand-new satellite system built to spot missiles launched from the United States. If it ever reported an attack, he was supposed to pass the news up the chain of command immediately.

Pause · speak

How might the news of those weeks have changed the way an officer read a warning on a screen? Use might have or would have.

Move 2 · The siren

Just after midnight on 26 September, the alarm sounded. On the main screen a single word glowed red: LAUNCH. The computer reported that an American missile was heading for the Soviet Union. Minutes later it reported four more — five in total. The system showed no doubt at all.

If the missiles were real, they would reach Moscow in well under half an hour. Around Petrov, the other officers were waiting for him to act. Every second made the decision heavier.

Pause · you are Petrov

The screen says five missiles. What do you want to know first? What would you do in the next sixty seconds? Say it out loud, then open the box below.

What went through his mind. He later explained three reasons for doubt. Which do you think was the strongest?

1. The number was wrong. A real American first strike would have meant hundreds of missiles, not five. He reportedly said that when people start a war, they don’t start it with only five.

2. The machine was new. He did not completely trust a system that was so young and had not been tested in real life.

3. Nothing had been confirmed. The ground radar had not yet detected anything — although it might not have seen anything for several more minutes.

Move 3 · The decision

Petrov picked up the phone and told his superiors that the system was malfunctioning. He could not be completely sure — it was a judgement, and he knew it was a risk. If the radar confirmed an attack, the delay might cost the country its chance to respond. Then he waited.

Minutes passed. Nothing arrived. Later investigators found the cause: sunlight had reflected off high-altitude clouds in exactly the wrong way, and the satellites had mistaken the glare for missile engines.

Pause · speak

Tell the last two minutes of this move again using the past perfect at least once: by the time…, the clouds had already…, he had decided…

Move 4 · After

Petrov was not given a medal. He said that he was questioned about the night, and that he was reprimanded for the way he had kept his records. The whole incident was kept secret, and the world did not learn about it until the late 1990s. Years later he was honoured by peace organisations, and when he died in 2017, at the age of 77, it was months before the news reached the wider world.

He always insisted that he had simply been doing his job.

Pause · speak

Why do you think the system punished him and not the machine? Is “I was just doing my job” a modest answer or an unfair one? Give your opinion with a reason.

Check what you remember — answer aloud, then reveal

1. What exactly did the computer report, and how many times?
An American missile launch — first one missile, then four more, five in total.
2. Give two of the reasons Petrov doubted the system.
Any two of: five missiles was far too few for a real attack; the system was new and untrusted; the ground radar had not confirmed anything.
3. What caused the false alarm?
Sunlight reflecting off high clouds, which the satellites read as missile launches.
4. What happened to Petrov afterwards?
He was questioned and reprimanded over his record-keeping, not praised; the story stayed secret until the late 1990s, and he was honoured years later.
A note on the facts. This account follows widely reported versions of the 1983 incident, including Petrov’s own later interviews. Some small details are still debated — which is part of what makes the story worth discussing.

Grammar: the language of “what if”

Today’s target: talk about things that did not happen, and what they would have changed — then and now.

1 · Pattern hunt — what do these have in common?

Read the five sentences about the night. Find the pattern before you open the explanation.

A. If Petrov had reported an attack, the leaders might have ordered a counterattack.

B. If he had obeyed his orders that night, the world would be a very different place today.

C. If he were the kind of person who always follows orders, he wouldn’t have made that call.

D. Had he trusted the satellites, he would have reported an attack.

E. If it hadn’t been for his calm judgement, the night might have ended very differently.

A · Third conditional — imagined past → imagined past result

if + had + past participle→would / might / could + have + past participle

B · Mixed (past → now) — something that did or didn’t happen then changes the situation today

if + had + past participle→would + base verb (now)

C · Mixed (now → past) — a permanent quality or fact would have changed what happened then

if + past simple (were)→would + have + past participle

D · Inversion — formal; drop if and put had first. Negative: Had he not waited…

E · If it hadn’t been for… — “without this thing”, for a past event. Formal: Had it not been for… or But for…

2 · What-if chains — build them together

One person says a sentence, the next person answers with the result of the result. Try each chain for at least three links.

Start: If the clouds hadn’t been so bright that night, the satellite wouldn’t have made a mistake…

…so there would have been no alarm. If there had been no alarm, Petrov would never have had to decide. If he hadn’t had to decide, nobody would be talking about him today.

Start: If you had missed your last flight home…

…I would have spent the night at the airport. If I had spent the night there, I might have met someone interesting. If I had met them, my life would be different now.

Start: If I weren’t so curious… (present trait → past result)

…I wouldn’t have read that article. If I hadn’t read it, I wouldn’t have learnt about this story.

3 · Error hunt — which ONE sentence is correct?

Three of these have a mistake. Pick the correct one, then say how you would fix the others.

1. If he had reported an attack, the leaders could have retaliated. (not would have in the if-clause)

2. If it hadn’t been for his calm, we might not be here today. (not wouldn’t have been)

3. Correct: Had he not hesitated, the officers above him would have been told at once.

4. If he hadn’t been so experienced, he would have reported it. (not will have)

4 · Mixed practice — choose the right form

Different structures are mixed together on purpose. First decide which type of “what if” it is.

5 · Transformation — say it, then write it

Turn each fact into a “what if” sentence. Say it aloud first, then type it.

If he had reported an attack, the leaders would have retaliated. Also possible: Had he reported an attack, …
If it hadn’t been for the clouds, there wouldn’t have been a false alarm. Also: Had the clouds not fooled the satellite, …
If he weren’t such a thoughtful man, he would have panicked. (present quality → past result)
Teach it back · 60 seconds

Explain to me, as if I knew nothing, the difference between “If he had obeyed, the world would have ended” and “If he had obeyed, the world would be different today.” Give a new example of each, about anything except Petrov.

Other hinge moments

Petrov was not the only person whose single decision mattered. Here are four more true cases. For each, say a “what if” sentence first, then reveal a model.

October 1962

Vasili Arkhipov and the submarine

During the Cuban Missile Crisis, American ships dropped practice depth charges on a Soviet submarine to force it to the surface. The submarine had lost contact with Moscow, and the captain wanted to fire a nuclear torpedo. A launch needed the agreement of three senior officers. Arkhipov refused.

If Arkhipov had agreed, the crew might have fired the torpedo, and a nuclear war could have begun.
September 1928

Alexander Fleming and the dish

Fleming came back from a holiday and found that mould had killed the bacteria around it in a dish he had left on his bench. Many people would have thrown the dish away. He looked more closely. The mould turned out to produce penicillin.

If Fleming had thrown the dish away, penicillin might not have been discovered for many years.
January 2009

Chesley Sullenberger and the river

Shortly after take-off from New York, birds hit both engines of a passenger jet. Captain Sullenberger decided that there was no time to return to an airport, and landed on the Hudson River. All 155 people on board survived.

If he had tried to fly back to the airport, the plane might have crashed in a built-up area.
July 1969

Neil Armstrong and the rocks

As the Apollo 11 lunar module came down, its automatic guidance was taking it towards a field of large rocks. Armstrong took manual control and flew on until he found a flat place to land, with very little fuel left.

If Armstrong hadn’t taken manual control, the lander might have come down among the rocks.

Ranking challenge — there is no single right answer

Include Petrov as the fifth case. For each question, choose one case, then defend your choice aloud in two sentences.

Odd one out:
  • Four of these five cases are about making a decision under pressure. One is mostly about noticing something. Which one, and why does that difference matter?
  • In which cases did someone refuse to do something? Is refusing a decision, or the absence of one?
Fleming’s case is mostly about noticing: nobody was under time pressure, but he chose to pay attention to something other people would have thrown away. Petrov and Arkhipov both refused to take the step the system expected of them — which shows that “doing nothing” can be a very active decision.

2 · Tell it in five steps

Task C · Debate: machines or people? (4 minutes)

First argue that important decisions should be left to clear rules and machines. Then argue that they should be left to human judgement. Finally, say what you really think.

  • For rules and machines: people get tired, frightened and biased; Petrov himself could not be sure — a different officer with the same “gut feeling” could have been wrong in the other direction; rules protect us when nobody is calm.
  • For human judgement: Petrov and Arkhipov each saw something the system could not; no protocol can imagine every situation; a person can ask “does this make sense?”
  • A complication: historians point out that history rarely turns on one person alone — other officers, other checks and other chances may also have mattered. Does that make the hinge story less true, or just more complicated?
Task D · Choose one
  • Two notes about one night. Write and read aloud (a) the official reprimand Petrov received and (b) the thank-you note he should have received. Same night, two completely different voices.
  • The audio guide. A museum wants a 60-second spoken description to go beside one of the five hinge moments. Choose one and record it aloud: set the scene, say what hung on the decision, and end with a “what if”.
  • The panel. You are an expert on decision-making under pressure. Answer three questions from the audience: How do you decide when you have no time? When should you trust a machine? How do you know your gut feeling is right?
  • Hinge in reverse. Describe a small decision that seemed huge at the time but turned out not to matter at all. Why do we get that wrong?

To finish — the big questions

  1. Petrov said he was just doing his job. Is it more admirable to be a person who breaks the rules when it matters, or one who builds a system where nobody has to?
  2. Most hinge moments are never recorded. What does that suggest about how many there have been?
  3. If a hinge moment is only visible in hindsight, how can anyone act well at the time?

Recall zone — from earlier learning

Answer aloud without looking anything up. Then reveal.

1. What are three different ways to talk about the future in English?
will (decisions, predictions) · be going to (plans, evidence) · present continuous (arrangements) · future perfect (by Friday I will have finished) · be about to.
2. What is the difference between used to and would for past habits?
Used to works for past habits and past states (I used to live in…). Would works only for repeated actions, not states (Every summer we would go to the coast).
3. Give two ways of disagreeing politely.
I see your point, but… · I’m not sure I agree, because… · That’s true up to a point, however… · I take your point, although…
