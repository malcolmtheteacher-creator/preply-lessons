A. Guess from the sentence

Read the sentence. What does the bold word mean? Then reveal.

1. In the wine shop, working men stood at the counter and drank a bowl of warm rice wine after work.
a wine shop (n) — a small shop or bar where people buy and drink wine. (In China, rice wine.)
2. The rich, educated men wore a long gown — a long, loose piece of clothing down to the feet. Working men wore short jackets.
a gown (n) — a long, loose piece of clothing. In old China, only educated men wore long gowns.
3. He had no money, so sometimes he stole things — a book, a pen — from people's houses.
to steal (v) — to take something that is not yours. steal – stole – stolen
4. He gave the children aniseed beans to eat, one each.
beans (n, plural) — small seeds that you can eat. Aniseed beans are a cheap snack with wine.

Read the meaning. Say the word. The first letter helps you.

5. Having very little money. It begins with p.
poor (adj). Kong Yiji was very poor.
6. To make a sound with your face and voice because something is funny. It begins with l.
to laugh (v) / a laugh (n). Everybody laughed at him.
7. Someone who studied a lot and knows old books. It begins with s.

Read the sentence and choose a, b or c. Then reveal.

8. He still owed the shop nineteen coins. — to owe means: (a) to have money that you must pay back to someone (b) to give a present (c) to lose

The story, in three parts

This is a famous Chinese story, told here in simple English. A boy who works in a wine shop tells it, many years later. Read each part, then try the questions from memory.

Part 1 — The man in the long gown

In our town, the wine shops have a big counter facing the street. Working men come after work and stand at the counter. They pay four coins for a bowl of warm rice wine, and one more coin for a plate of beans. Rich, educated men wear long gowns. They go inside, sit down, and order food and wine.

When I was twelve, I started to work in a wine shop. The boss said I looked too stupid to serve the rich customers, so I worked outside. Then he said I was bad at that too. So I got the most boring job: warming the wine. All day I stood behind the counter. The boss had an angry face. The customers were not friendly. Only when Kong Yiji came could I laugh a little. That is why I still remember him.

Kong Yiji was the only man who wore a long gown and stood at the counter with the working men. He was tall and big, with a pale face, scars, and a grey, untidy beard. His gown was dirty and torn — maybe ten years without washing. When he talked, he used old words from old books, and nobody understood him.

When he came in, everybody laughed. "Kong Yiji! You've got a new scar on your face!" He did not answer. "Two bowls of wine, and a plate of beans," he said, and put nine coins on the counter. "You've been stealing again!" they shouted. "I saw you! You stole books from the He family, and they beat you!" Kong Yiji went red. "Taking books is not stealing! A scholar… taking books… that is not stealing!" Then he said more old words, and the whole shop laughed and laughed.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is different about Kong Yiji?

2. What does he say when they say he stole books?

1. He wears a long gown, like a rich educated man, but he stands at the counter with the working men. His gown is dirty and torn.

2. 'Taking books is not stealing!' — because he is a scholar. Then he uses old words nobody understands, and everybody laughs.

Discuss:
  • The boy says: 'Only when Kong Yiji came could I laugh.' Is that a nice memory or a bad one?

Part 2 — Four ways to write a word

People said Kong Yiji studied for many years, but he never passed the exams. He could not get a job, so he became poorer and poorer. He wrote beautifully, so people paid him to copy books. But he loved wine and hated work. After a few days, he disappeared — with the books, the paper and the pens. So nobody gave him work any more, and sometimes he stole. But in our shop, he was the best customer: he always paid. If he had no money, the boss wrote his name on the board, but in a month he always paid, and his name was cleaned off.

After half a bowl of wine, the men started again. "Kong Yiji, can you really read?" He looked at them as if the question was stupid. "Then why did you never pass even the smallest exam?" Kong Yiji's face went grey. He said some old words. Nobody understood. Everybody laughed. The boss laughed too.

Kong Yiji could not talk to the men, so he talked to children. One day he said to me: "Have you been to school? Then I'll test you. The word 'hui' in 'aniseed beans' — how do you write it?" I thought: a beggar wants to test me? I turned my face away. He waited, and then said, very kindly: "You can't write it? I'll teach you. You must remember it. When you are a boss, you will need it for the accounts." I said lazily: "Who needs you? It's 'grass' on top and a box under it." Kong Yiji was very happy. "Yes! Yes! And did you know there are four ways to write it?" I walked away. He sighed, and looked very sorry.

Sometimes the children came to laugh at him. He gave them beans, one each. Then they looked at the plate for more. He put his hand over the plate. "Not many left! Not many, not many!" he said in his old-book words, and the children ran away laughing.

Kong Yiji made people laugh. But without him, life was just the same.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did nobody give Kong Yiji work?

2. What did he try to teach the boy?

1. Because he loved wine and hated work. After a few days he disappeared with the books, paper and pens.

2. How to write the word 'hui' in 'aniseed beans' — and that there are four ways to write it. The boy was not interested.

Discuss:
  • Kong Yiji is kind to the boy. The boy is not kind to him. Why?

Part 3 — Nineteen coins

One day in autumn, the boss looked at the board and said: "Kong Yiji hasn't come for a long time. He still owes nineteen coins!" A customer said: "He can't come. They broke his legs." "Oh!" said the boss. "He stole again — from the Ding family, the rich scholar's house. They beat him half the night and broke his legs." "And then?" "Who knows? Maybe he's dead." The boss said nothing more and went back to his accounts.

Winter came. One afternoon the shop was empty. Suddenly I heard a quiet voice: "Warm a bowl of wine." I looked over the counter. Kong Yiji was sitting on the ground by the door. His face was dark and thin. He sat on a straw mat, and he had a rope over his shoulders to hold it. "Warm a bowl of wine," he said again.

The boss looked out. "Kong Yiji? You still owe nineteen coins!" "Next time," said Kong Yiji. "This time I have money. Make the wine good." The boss laughed, as always. "Kong Yiji, you've been stealing again!" This time Kong Yiji only said: "Don't laugh at me." "Why are your legs broken, then?" "I fell," he said quietly. "I fell…" His eyes said: please, don't talk about it. But the men laughed.

I warmed the wine and put it on the step. He took four coins from his pocket and put them in my hand. His hand was covered in mud. He had walked here on his hands. He drank the wine. Then, slowly, on his hands, he went away, and the men laughed.

At the end of the year, the boss said: "Kong Yiji still owes nineteen coins!" In summer, he said it again. Then he stopped saying it.

I never saw Kong Yiji again. He is probably dead.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to Kong Yiji's legs?

2. How does he come to the shop the last time, and how does he pay?

1. He stole from the Ding family, a rich scholar's house. They beat him and broke his legs.

2. On his hands, sitting on a straw mat. He pays four coins with a hand covered in mud.

Discuss:
  • The boss remembers only the nineteen coins. Is that all that is left of Kong Yiji?

The grammar this story runs on: but, because and so

Kong Yiji is full of opposites: a scholar, but poor; he steals, but he pays. To tell his story, you need three small words that join ideas: but (opposite), because (reason), so (result).

but — two ideas that are opposite.
• "He wore a long gown, but he was very poor." • "He stole, but he always paid his bill."
because — the reason (why?).
• "He was poor because he never passed the exams." • "Nobody gave him work because he disappeared with the books."
so — the result (what happened next?).
• "He never passed the exams, so he was poor." • "He could not walk, so he came on his hands."
Notice: because and so say the same thing in different directions: A because B = B, so A.

Why people still read this story

Lu Xun (1881–1936) is the most important Chinese writer of the twentieth century. He studied to be a doctor, but he stopped, because he wanted to 'cure' China's spirit, not its body. He wrote this story in 1919. He said it was his favourite. In old China, only men who passed very hard exams could wear a long gown and get a good job. Those exams stopped in 1905. Kong Yiji is a man from a world that has already died.

Nobody in the story hates Kong Yiji. They enjoy him. The boss, the customers, the children, and the boy who tells the story — they all laugh. Lu Xun wanted to show a country that watches suffering like a show. Kong Yiji's only good thing is that he pays his debts. His only kind act is to try to teach a boy a word. And all that is left of him is nineteen coins on a board.

Look at the four coins. On his last visit, with broken legs and a muddy hand, he pays. Why does he say 'This time I have money'?
