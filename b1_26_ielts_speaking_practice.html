Speaking Toolkit: Discourse Markers

When you speak under pressure, your brain can freeze. Discourse markers are words and phrases that buy you thinking time and make your speech flow naturally. They show you're thinking, not just stuck.

Click each card to reveal its meaning and common use:

actually
to be honest
the thing is
what I mean is
for instance
on the other hand
all in all

What makes a good speaker?

These discourse markers work because they:

  • Buy thinking time — Fill silence naturally instead of saying "um" or "uh"
  • Show your thinking — Reveal how your mind works, not just the answer
  • Create flow — Link ideas together so you sound organized
  • Add authenticity — Show genuine, natural speech instead of memorized answers
  • Extend answers — One word or phrase can turn a 5-second answer into 30 seconds

Task: As you move through the other sections, try using at least one discourse marker in each answer. Notice which ones feel most natural to you.

Quick Questions: How to Extend Your Answers

In speaking tests, short answers don't score well. The key is to use extension techniques to turn a 3-second answer into 15-30 seconds. Here's how:

Extension Framework (WEDDING):

  • Why? Explain your reason
  • Example? Give a specific instance
  • Details? Add descriptive information
  • Difference? Compare or contrast
  • Impact? Say what effect it has
  • Nuance? Add "but" or "although" to show complexity
  • Gateway? Link to the next point

Example Question: "What's your favorite hobby?"

Short answer (bad): "I like playing football."

Extended answer (good):

"I really enjoy playing football, actually. The thing is, I've been playing since I was about eight years old, so it's been part of my life for quite a long time. I usually play with friends on weekends, and what I mean is, it's not just about the sport itself — it's a great way to relax after a busy week. You know, it helps me stay fit, but on the other hand, it's also about spending time with people I care about. I suppose the best thing is that feeling when you score or when the team works really well together."

What happened? Notice the extensions:

  • "The thing is" + timeline (since I was eight)
  • "What I mean is" + real reason (relaxation + friendship)
  • "On the other hand" + another benefit (social element)
  • "I suppose" + specific detail (the feeling of success)

Six Practice Questions — Try Extending Each One

1. What hobbies do you enjoy in your free time?

Extension tip: Pick one hobby. Say when you started, why you like it, how often you do it, and how it makes you feel.

See Model Answer
"I'm really passionate about reading, to be honest. I've been reading novels since I was quite young, and I find it's a wonderful escape from daily stress. I try to read at least a couple of hours each week, usually in the evenings. The thing is, it's not just about the story — it's about discovering different perspectives and ideas. I particularly enjoy historical fiction because it combines entertainment with learning. All in all, it's become an essential part of my routine."

2. Can you describe your typical daily routine?

Extension tip: Don't just list activities. Say why you do them, how long they take, and how you feel about them.

See Model Answer
"Well, I wake up around seven, and I usually start my day with coffee and breakfast. What I mean is, those first thirty minutes are really important for me to feel ready. Then I head to work, which takes about forty minutes. During the day, I try to take a break at lunchtime — actually, I think it's crucial to step away from your desk. In the evening, I typically cook dinner and spend time with family or friends. On the other hand, if I've had a particularly stressful day, I prefer to relax at home. All in all, I try to keep things balanced."

3. Where are you from, and what do you like about your hometown?

Extension tip: Describe a specific feature. Give an example. Say why it matters to you.

See Model Answer
"I'm from a small city in the south, and to be honest, I have really fond memories of growing up there. The thing is, it's known for its beautiful beaches and friendly atmosphere. For instance, people actually know their neighbors, which is becoming rare these days. What I particularly love is the way the community comes together for local festivals. On the other hand, it can feel a bit quiet for young people. But all in all, it shaped who I am, and I'm grateful for that foundation."

4. How do you usually spend your weekends?

Extension tip: Give two or three activities. Explain why you choose them. Say how they make you feel.

See Model Answer
"Well, my weekends are pretty varied, actually. I usually try to meet friends for coffee or maybe go to a restaurant, which is a nice way to catch up. What I mean is, I really value that social time. I also dedicate some time to personal projects or hobbies — for instance, I've been learning guitar lately. The thing is, I also make sure to rest, because the week can be quite demanding. On the other hand, I don't like to be completely lazy. All in all, it's about finding that balance between activity and relaxation."

5. What kind of food do you enjoy eating?

Extension tip: Name a specific type. Say where you eat it. Explain why you like it.

See Model Answer
"I have quite a diverse palate, to be honest. I'm particularly fond of Italian food — actually, there's something about fresh pasta that's just wonderful. For instance, I could eat risotto every week. The thing is, I also really enjoy trying new cuisines, especially when I'm traveling. On the other hand, I'm quite adventurous, but I do have some comfort foods I return to. What I mean is, Italian and Japanese are my top two, but I'm always open to discovering new flavors. All in all, good food is one of life's great pleasures."

6. What do you hope to achieve in the next five years?

Extension tip: Say your main goal. Give specific details. Explain why it matters to you.

See Model Answer
"Well, I think achieving a better work-life balance is really important to me. What I mean is, I want to progress in my career, but not at the expense of my personal relationships. For instance, I'm hoping to get a promotion within the next couple of years, and I'm willing to invest in additional training. On the other hand, I also want to travel more — actually, I've made a list of places I'd like to visit. The thing is, these goals aren't separate; they're interconnected. All in all, in five years, I'd like to be more fulfilled both professionally and personally."

The Long Turn: Extended Speaking

In IELTS Speaking Part 2, you receive a task card and have 1 minute to prepare. Then you speak for 2 minutes without interruption. This is harder than it sounds.

The Task Card

Describe something you own which is very important to you.

You should say:

  • What it is
  • How long you have had it
  • What you use it for
  • Why it is important to you

And explain: Would it be easy to replace?

Step 1: Planning (1 Minute)

When you read this card, you have 60 seconds to plan. Here's the smart approach:

Planning Framework

  • Seconds 0–10: Choose your object. Pick something specific and meaningful, not generic. (Avoid: "my phone" — too common. Better: "a watch my grandfather gave me")
  • Seconds 10–30: Sketch the 4 main points. One sentence each in notes.
  • Seconds 30–45: Think of 2–3 specific details for each point. Why these details matter.
  • Seconds 45–60: Plan your opening and closing. Decide how you'll introduce the object and wrap up your final thought.

Step 2: Speaking Structure (2 Minutes)

Don't memorize. Plan a structure and speak naturally into it.

Useful Opening Phrases

  • "I'd like to tell you about..."
  • "The thing I want to describe is..."
  • "What I'm going to talk about is..."
  • "Let me tell you about something really important to me..."

Sample Answer — Listen to the Structure

Object: A leather journal

"I'd like to tell you about a leather journal that I've had for quite a number of years now. It's a brown leather-bound notebook, fairly small so it fits in my bag, and it has become incredibly important to me.

How long: I've actually had it for about seven years. I received it as a gift from my father when I was going through a difficult time in my life, and that's why I've kept it ever since.

What I use it for: The thing is, I use it primarily as a journal for writing down my thoughts, reflections, and sometimes dreams. I might write in it several times a week, depending on what's going on in my life. Occasionally, I also use it to sketch ideas or to record meaningful quotes that inspire me.

Why it's important: Well, it's important for several reasons. Firstly, it's a physical connection to my father, who passed away a few years ago. Secondly, the act of writing in it has helped me through challenging periods — it's therapeutic, you know? And thirdly, when I look through the pages, I can see my personal growth over the years. It's like a visual record of who I was and who I've become.

Would it be easy to replace? Actually, no, it wouldn't be easy to replace at all. On the other hand, I could buy another journal, but it wouldn't be the same. The sentimental value is irreplaceable because of the memories it holds and the person who gave it to me. So while I could technically replace the object itself, I could never replace what it represents to me."

What's happening here?

  • Clear structure that follows the task card points
  • Specific details (brown leather, fits in bag, seven years, father's gift)
  • Discourse markers throughout (the thing is, well, actually, on the other hand)
  • Direct answer to the follow-up question
  • Natural flow despite being prepared

Key Phrases for Each Section

Describing What It Is

  • "It's a... (object) that is... (color/material/size)"
  • "It's one of those things that..."
  • "Imagine a... (simple description)"

Explaining How Long You've Had It

  • "I've had it for approximately... (time period)"
  • "I received it as a gift about... (when)"
  • "I've owned it since..."
  • "For the past... years, I've had..."

Describing What You Use It For

  • "The thing is, I use it to..."
  • "I use it quite regularly for..."
  • "What I mean is, it serves the purpose of..."
  • "I might use it... (frequency) to..."

Explaining Why It's Important

  • "The main reason it's important is..."
  • "To be honest, it represents..."
  • "There are several reasons, actually. Firstly... Secondly... And finally..."
  • "It holds sentimental value because..."

Deep Discussion: Part 3

In IELTS Speaking Part 3, you have a 4–5 minute conversation about abstract ideas related to Part 2. You need to give longer answers with personal opinions, examples, and nuanced thinking.

The Challenge

Part 3 isn't just describing — it's analyzing, comparing, and thinking critically. You'll be asked:

  • "Why do you think...?"
  • "Do you agree that...?"
  • "How has this changed...?"
  • "What's the difference between...?"

Key Skills: Building an Argument at Length

Formula: Opinion → Reason → Example → Nuance → Conclusion

Opinion Starters

  • "I think... because..."
  • "In my view, ..."
  • "To be honest, I believe..."
  • "I'd say that..."
  • "From my perspective, ..."

Adding Reasons & Examples

  • "The main reason is that..."
  • "For instance, ..."
  • "In my experience, ..."
  • "What I mean is, ..."
  • "For example, when I..."

Adding Nuance (Shows Complex Thinking)

  • "On the other hand, ..."
  • "Although, ..."
  • "That said, ..."
  • "To some extent, ..."
  • "It depends on..."

Concluding / Summarizing

  • "So, all in all, ..."
  • "In conclusion, ..."
  • "Ultimately, ..."
  • "I think that's the key point, really."

Four Discussion Questions — About Possessions & Materialism

Question 1: "Why do you think people form attachments to objects?"

Suggested angle: Emotional connection, memory, identity, security.

See Sample Response
"I think people form attachments to objects because they often represent something beyond the physical item itself. For instance, a gift from someone you love becomes about the relationship, not the thing. What I mean is, we invest objects with emotional weight. In my experience, when I own something connected to a meaningful memory or a person I care about, it becomes part of my identity in a way. On the other hand, some people might attach themselves to objects for security or comfort — a childhood toy, for example, can be a source of reassurance. I'd say that's particularly true during difficult times. So, all in all, objects are really symbolic repositories for our memories and emotions."

Question 2: "Do you agree that we own too many things in modern society?"

Suggested angle: Consumerism vs. necessity, environmental impact, minimalism trend.

See Sample Response
"I'd say that's partially true, actually. To be honest, I think it depends on the person and their circumstances. The thing is, in affluent Western societies, there's definitely a culture of accumulation — we buy things we don't need because they're marketed to us. For instance, how many people have kitchen gadgets they've never used? On the other hand, not everyone has the luxury of owning 'too much.' Some people are just trying to meet basic needs. What I mean is, it's not a simple yes or no. But I do think that environmentally and psychologically, we'd benefit from owning less and choosing quality over quantity. That said, some possessions are genuinely valuable and worth keeping. All in all, I think the ideal is intentionality — owning things that serve a purpose or bring real joy, not just accumulating for the sake of it."

Question 3: "How do you think people's relationship with possessions has changed over the last few decades?"

Suggested angle: Technology, social media, convenience, throwaway culture, nostalgia.

See Sample Response
"Well, I think it's changed quite dramatically, actually. In my view, technology has fundamentally altered how we relate to things. The thing is, objects used to last longer, and people invested in them more carefully. For instance, my grandparents would repair items rather than replace them. What I mean is, there was a sense of stewardship. Nowadays, we have a throwaway culture — things are cheap and disposable, so we buy and discard more freely. On the other hand, there's also a counter-movement now toward sustainability and vintage goods, which is interesting. To be honest, I think social media has complicated things too. We now care about possessions partly because of how they look online. That said, there's also growing awareness of minimalism and the psychological benefits of owning less. All in all, while we own more, we might actually value individual items less than previous generations did."

Question 4: "What's the difference between collecting something as a hobby and hoarding?"

Suggested angle: Intentionality, organization, purpose, psychological health, social norms.

See Sample Response
"That's an interesting question, actually. I think the key difference is intention and control. Collecting, in my view, is a deliberate, organized hobby. For instance, someone who collects vinyl records knows exactly what they have, displays it thoughtfully, and derives genuine pleasure from it. What I mean is, there's a purpose and a system. Hoarding, on the other hand, is more compulsive and often linked to anxiety or emotional struggles. The thing is, hoarders often lose track of what they own, and it ends up controlling them rather than the other way around. To be honest, it can also impact health and relationships. The distinction really comes down to: Does the person enjoy their possessions and maintain them responsibly? Or are they accumulating out of fear or inability to let go? On the other hand, I think there's also a social and cultural element — what counts as 'collecting' versus 'hoarding' depends on context. All in all, it's about agency and intention."

Final Thought

Strong speaking isn't about perfection. It's about showing you can think, explain, and engage with ideas in real time. Every pause to gather your thoughts, every "well" or "the thing is," every time you say "on the other hand," you're demonstrating that you're a thoughtful, reflective speaker.
