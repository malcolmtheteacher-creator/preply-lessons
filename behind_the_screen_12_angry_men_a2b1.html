12 Angry Men

Twelve men must decide if a boy is guilty. Eleven say yes. One man says no — and slowly, he changes their minds.

The film, in three parts

Sidney Lumet's 12 Angry Men (1957). Read each part. Then try the questions from memory, and talk about the last one. (There are spoilers — that is fine, we want to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A boy on trial

Twelve men sit in a small, hot room. It is 1957, in New York. They must decide: is an eighteen-year-old boy guilty of killing his father, or not? If they say "guilty," the boy will die. This is a big decision.

First, they vote. Eleven men say "guilty." Only one man, Juror 8, says "not guilty." He does not say the boy is innocent. He only says: "I am not sure. And if I am not sure, I cannot vote guilty."

The other men are angry. One man wants to leave quickly — he has tickets for a baseball game. Another man does not like poor people from the boy's neighbourhood, and he is sure the boy is guilty just because of that. But the leader of the group says: "OK. Let's talk about it. Each person can explain why he thinks 'guilty.'"

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How many men vote "guilty" at first, and how many vote "not guilty"?

2. Why does Juror 8 vote "not guilty"?

1. Eleven vote guilty; one, Juror 8, votes not guilty.

2. He is not saying the boy is innocent — he says he is not sure enough to say "guilty."

Talk about it:
  • Could you say "no" if eleven other people said "yes"? Why, or why not?

Part 2 — The evidence

Juror 8 starts to ask questions about the evidence. The police say the knife is special — there is only one knife like it in the shops near the boy's home. But Juror 8 goes out and buys the exact same knife, at a shop near the boy's home! Now they know: the knife is not special. Maybe someone else had a knife just like it.

Next, they think about an old man who lives under the boy's flat. He says he heard the boy shout "I will kill you!", heard a body fall, and then walked to his door in fifteen seconds to see the boy run down the stairs. But the old man is not fast — he had a stroke, and he walks slowly. The men time how long it really takes to walk to the door. It takes more than forty seconds!

Also, a loud train passes near his window at that exact time every night. Could he really hear the shout so clearly?

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Juror 8 do to show the knife is not special?

2. What two problems do they find with the old man's story?

1. He buys the exact same knife near the boy's home, showing that more than one knife like this exists.

2. It takes much longer than fifteen seconds to walk to his door; and a loud passing train would make it hard to hear a shout clearly.

Talk about it:
  • Which piece of evidence surprised you the most — the knife, or the old man's story?

Part 3 — The room changes

One by one, the men change their vote. One man says bad, unfair things about poor people — the other men stand up and turn their backs on him. They are ashamed of him. A woman across the street said she saw the murder through a train window at night — but she needs glasses, and she was not wearing them in court! Maybe she could not see clearly.

Only Juror 3 still says "guilty." But we learn something: Juror 3 has a son he does not talk to anymore. He is really angry about his own son — not about this boy. Finally, he looks at a photo of his son, and he cries. He changes his vote to "not guilty."

Now all twelve men agree: not guilty. Outside, on the courthouse steps, two of the men — Juror 8 and Juror 9 — say their real names to each other for the first time.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why does Juror 3 finally change his vote?

2. What is surprising about the last scene, on the steps?

1. He realises his anger is really about his own son, not about the boy on trial, and he breaks down and votes not guilty.

2. The two men say their names for the first time — all through the film, we never knew any juror's real name.

Talk about it:
  • The film never says for certain if the boy is innocent — only that there is doubt. Do you like this kind of ending?

Grammar: asking questions

This film is full of people asking questions — about the knife, the witnesses, and each other. Let's make sure you can ask a question the correct way.

Yes/No questions: Do / Does / Did + subject + verb. "Did you see the boy?" "Does he live near here?"
Wh- questions: question word + do/does/did + subject + verb. "Why did you vote guilty?" "What did the witness see?"
Feel the difference:
• Use who, what, where, when, why, how, which to ask for information.
• Use do/does/did alone (with no question word) to ask for yes or no.

1. Make the question — use do, does or did

Complete each question.

1. you hear the man shout that night?

2. she wear glasses in court?

3. juries usually agree quickly?

1. Did (past — did you hear)

2. Did (past — did she wear)

3. Do (present habit — do juries)

2. Add the question word

Complete each question with a question word and did.

1. the old man walk to the door? (asking about time)

2. she see the murder? (asking in what way)

3. the men change their vote? (asking for the reason)

1. How long did (time)

2. How did (way/manner)

3. Why did (reason)

3. Complete the short conversation

Fill each gap with the correct question. Read it aloud when you finish.

" you sure?" asked Juror 9. "Yes," said Juror 3. "But you see his face clearly?" "Well…" said Juror 3. " the old man really hear the shout?" asked Juror 8.

Are (you sure) · did (you see) · Did (the old man really hear)

Use "be" (am/is/are) alone for questions with "be"; use do/does/did for other verbs.

The Real Story — is any of this true?

Here is the surprising part: this story is based on something real, and a famous science experiment explains why one man could change the whole room.

⚖️ Is this true? The rules of a real jury

In the American justice system, twelve ordinary people (a jury) decide if someone is guilty. To say "guilty," all twelve people must agree — this is called a unanimous decision. And the rule is: if there is a real, reasonable doubt, the jury must say "not guilty." Even if some jurors think the person probably did it, "probably" is not enough.

The writer of this story, Reginald Rose, was once a real juror himself, in a real trial in 1954. His own experience gave him the idea for this story.

A famous experiment about groups

In the 1950s — around the same years as this story — a scientist named Solomon Asch did a famous experiment. He asked people to say which of three lines was the same length as another line. It was a very easy question. But some people in the room were actors, and they all gave the same wrong answer on purpose.

About one in three people agreed with the wrong answer, just because everyone else said it! But when only one other person gave the correct answer, most people felt free to say the truth too. This is exactly what happens in the film: one man says "I don't agree," and slowly, the others feel free to think again too.

Is the ending realistic? Not really. Studies of real juries show that the first vote decides the final result about 90% of the time. One person changing everyone's mind, like in this film, is possible — but very rare in real life.

Talk about it:
  • Before this lesson, did you think the ending was realistic, or too perfect for real life?
  • Do you ever agree with a group, even when you are not sure they are right? Why?
  • Would you want to be a juror and decide if someone is guilty?
