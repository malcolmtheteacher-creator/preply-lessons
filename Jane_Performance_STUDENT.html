We grew up inside a birdcage.
A birdcage built from paper crowns and origami dreams.
The bars seemed like protection,
And they felt like love,
Just for the reason of being gold.

Wrapped in report cards and ribboned expectations
Glass cases filled with plastic medals and borrowed victories,

Step 1 of 9

The Performance Read

Before we change anything about how you deliver, let's hear where you are right now. This is your baseline.

What to do
Stand up. Read the whole poem aloud at performance pace — including your lift moves and your ending. Don't try to "act." Just speak the words. Record it on your phone if you can.

After the read, write down: how long did it take? Where did you feel strong? Where did you feel like you were just reading?

Step 2 of 9

The Volume Map

Every slam performer maps the volume of their poem in advance. Whisper, normal, loud, sudden-hush — these are CHOICES, not accidents. A poem read at one steady volume is a poem that loses the audience.

Suggested volume map for your poem
Mark these on your printed copy. The colour code helps you find your place at a glance.
QUIET"We grew up inside a birdcage…" — confide, draw the audience in close
BUILD"My childhood was wrapped… Wrapped in report cards…" — energy rising
LOUD"Run faster / Study harder / Smile wider / Be better" — the climax, push it
HUSHThe multilingual stanza — sudden drop in volume, almost a whisper. The most personal moment
BUILD"Somewhere between four languages…" — emerging from the hush, restrained but growing
STRONGYour volta line — declarative, the turn lands here
INTIMATE"I'm trapped in a mirage of my own fears…" — vulnerable, close
RESTRAINEDThe three-line return to the birdcage — controlled, deliberate
SILENCEThe pause before the closing beat — count one Mississippi in your head
FINALYour closing beat — full voice if "Watch me", quiet-strong if "Still"
Step 3 of 9

Pace + Pauses

Pace is how fast you go. Pauses are where you stop. Both are weapons — most school performers use neither.

Pace map
Slow — opening stanza. Let the audience settle.
Moderate — the "wrapped" section.
Accelerate — the "Run faster / Study harder…" climax. Push the speed.
Slow down sharply — the multilingual stanza. Let each language land.
Moderate — the volta and the trapped section.
Slow — the final three-line ending.
Dead stop — before the closing beat.
The four pauses that matter most
1. After "Don't embarrass the family." — one beat. Let it sit.
2. After EACH multilingual phrase — one beat each. Don't run them together.
3. Before your volta line — half a beat. The turn needs space.
4. Before your closing beat — the BIG one. A full second of silence. This is the most important silence in the whole poem.
Why the closing-beat pause matters so much

After your three-line ending lands, the audience THINKS the poem is over. They start to applaud or to shift in their seats. The silence you hold then is what makes them lean back in — and the final beat (Watch me / Still) becomes the thing they erupt over.

If you rush this pause, the closing beat is just another line. If you HOLD it, it's the moment of the whole performance.

Step 4 of 9

The One Gesture

One deliberate physical movement, tied to ONE specific line. Not five gestures. One. The principle: economy of motion. Most school performers either stand stiff or wave their hands around the whole way through. One deliberate gesture, in one chosen place, makes you look trained.

Try this
Pick ONE line for your one gesture. The best choices tie to the birdcage — the central image. Practise the gesture in the mirror until it feels natural and unhurried.
Options that work for your poem
  • Hands closing into fists on "kept me trapped" — the cage closing
  • Open palms turning upward on "Watch me" — the bird ready to fly (if you chose the hopeful ending)
  • Hands by your sides, palms down on "Still." — surrender (if you chose the resigned ending)
  • One hand to your chest on the volta line — locating the change inside you

If in doubt: open palms outward on the closing beat. It's the highest-impact moment and the gesture there carries the lift.

Rules:

  • Slow movement, not fast. Trained looks slow.
  • One time only. Don't repeat.
  • Don't add extras when you get excited.
Step 5 of 9

Voice Characters

The "Don't embarrass the family" stanza and the multilingual lines are the most personal moment in your poem. They can each carry a different speaker — the family member who actually said something like it. Your voice will shift slightly without you trying. The audience feels that shift.

Try this
For each foreign-language line, decide whose voice it is. Don't imitate — no accents, no caricature. Just think of that person as you speak the line. Your tone changes naturally.
How to assign the voices
  • "Don't embarrass the family" — whoever in your real family says it most. A mother? An auntie? Picture them.
  • The Indonesian line — your mother? Your grandmother on that side?
  • The Korean line — a different family member. A relative who said something like that to you.
  • The Chinese line — yet another voice. A neighbour? A school friend's mother? A teacher?

You don't have to tell the audience who each voice is. They feel the difference. That's enough.

Step 6 of 9

Duo Coordination — Working with Jesslyn

You have two voices. Most school duos either talk OVER each other or leave gaps between the handovers. Strong duos rehearse the choreography until the two voices move like one performer.

Three things to drill
1. Together-lines in unison. The opening, the climax, the ending — when you both speak, it must be CLEAN unison. Practice with a metronome if needed. Aim for 0ms gap.

2. Handovers without a gap. When one of you finishes a section and the other starts, the second voice begins BEFORE the first voice has fully landed — like passing a baton in a relay. Drill it.

3. Stage positioning. Stand side by side, slightly angled toward each other. Not directly facing each other (that excludes the audience). Not standing far apart (that breaks the connection).
When to face the audience vs each other

Face the audience for: the opening stanza, the climax ("Run faster…"), the ending, the closing beat. Always for the moments meant for them.

Face each other for: the most intimate moments. The multilingual stanza could work as you facing each other, sharing the family memory. "Encouragement became expectation" could be exchanged eyes-to-eyes.

Switch back on the volta. The turn pulls you out of your private exchange and back to the audience.

Step 7 of 9

The Breath Before

How you START is half the performance. Most school performers either bolt out of the gate or hesitate. Slam poets have a small ritual that calms the nerves and signals to the audience: watch me, this is going to matter.

The opening ritual — five seconds
1. Stand still. Feet shoulder-width.
2. Slow deep breath in through your nose.
3. Find ONE person in the front row. Lock eyes briefly.
4. Slow exhale.
5. Begin on your next breath.

Do it with Jesslyn — you breathe together, you start together. Synchronizes you. The audience feels you're in control before you've spoken a single word.

Step 8 of 9

Memorisation — The Named Chunks Method

Memorising 40 lines is hard. Memorising 9 named chunks is easy. Break the poem into chunks with names — when you blank, you remember the NAME, and the lines come back.

Your nine chunks
Memorise the labels in order. Then attach each label to its lines.
Click to see the named chunks
  1. THE CAGE — "We grew up inside a birdcage…" (the opening 5 lines)
  2. WRAPPED — "My childhood was wrapped…" through Gap 1
  3. COMPETE — "Because we learned how to compete… burdens I can't bear"
  4. FOUR LANGUAGES — the multilingual stanza
  5. DIRECT ADDRESS — your lift move 1 line
  6. THE LESSON — "Somewhere between four languages… sounding like pressure"
  7. THE VOLTA — your lift move 2 line
  8. TRAPPED — "The walls of this birdcage… overflow with mistakes" (with Gaps 2 & 3)
  9. THE RETURN + BEAT — your three-line ending + the closing beat

Practise saying the chunk names aloud in order. Then drill each chunk on its own. Only after you can do each chunk fluently do you stitch them together.

Step 9 of 9

The Final Run-Through

Now everything together. Volume map, pace, pauses, your one gesture, voice characters, the breath before, and the chunks in order. Time it. Record it.

Self-review questions after you record
1. Where did your volume choices land? Did the QUIET feel actually quiet?
2. Did you hold the closing-beat pause for a full second?
3. Did your one gesture land naturally — or did it look forced?
4. Did the multilingual lines feel like different people, or one continuous voice?
5. Was the time inside the slam window (2–3 minutes)?

Anything that didn't land — drill that one chunk separately, then run the whole thing again.
