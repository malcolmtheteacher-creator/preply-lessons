MINDS WORTH MEETING · C1 · 50 min

Zhuangzi

The ancient Chinese thinker who dreamed he was a butterfly — and woke up unable to say for certain which of them was dreaming.

You talk first — before you read anything

This is a talking lesson, not a reading one. So before a single fact, the floor is yours.

Start here · 2 minutes

Just his name and one image: a man wakes from a dream of being a butterfly, and wonders whether he is now a butterfly dreaming he is a man. Have you met this idea before — in a film, a philosophy class, a meme? Does it strike you as deep, playful, or just a clever trick? Just talk.

Now meet him — one beat at a time

Three short beats. After each one, you do the talking.

Zhuangzi — also written Chuang Tzu — lived around the 4th century BCE, during China's Warring States period, when rival kingdoms were fighting for power. He gave his name to the Zhuangzi, which sits beside the Tao Te Ching as one of the two founding texts of Taoism. Here is the surprising part: we know almost nothing reliable about him. The only account of his life is a short sketch by the historian Sima Qian, written centuries later, which says he was a minor official from a place called Meng, in the state of Song. One modern scholar, Russell Kirkland, says there is “no reliable historical data at all”.

Over to you:
  • One of the most influential thinkers in history may be almost a ghost. Does it matter to you whether a wise book was written by one real person — or by a group of followers over generations?

The book tells this story about him. He was fishing when two high officials arrived from a powerful prince, offering him the job of running the state. Without turning his head, he asked: would a sacred tortoise, dead for three thousand years and kept in a chest in the temple, rather be honoured as a corpse — “or be alive and wagging its tail in the mud?” The officials agreed it would rather be alive. “Begone!” he said. “I too will wag my tail in the mud.” (Sima Qian also says he turned down an offer from King Wei of Chu because he valued his freedom.)

Over to you:
  • Prestige and power on one side, freedom and mud on the other. Would you turn down the top job to stay free? Has anyone you know actually done it?

Another story: when his wife died, the philosopher Huizi came to offer sympathy — and found him sitting on the ground, singing and beating time on a bowl. Huizi was shocked. Zhuangzi explained that at first he could not help being affected by her death; then he reflected that she had passed from one state to another, “like the sequence of spring, summer, autumn, and winter”, and that to go on weeping would show he did not understand how nature works.

Over to you:
  • Is singing at your wife's funeral wisdom, coldness, or performance? How does your own culture expect people to grieve — and has anyone you know broken the rules?

The butterfly dream — and you do the thinking out loud

His most famous passage is only a few lines long, but people have argued about it for more than two thousand years. I will hand it to you in small pieces. After each piece, you talk — I will mostly listen.

Here it is, in Herbert Giles's 1889 translation: “Once upon a time, I, Chuang Tzŭ, dreamt I was a butterfly, fluttering hither and thither, to all intents and purposes a butterfly… Suddenly, I awaked, and there I lay, myself again. Now I do not know whether I was then a man dreaming I was a butterfly, or whether I am now a butterfly dreaming I am a man.”

Put yourself there

Before I tell you what people make of it: how do you know you are not dreaming right now? Try to give me one argument that actually works — and then try to break it yourself.

The passage does not end with “so nothing is real”. Its last lines insist that there is a difference between a man and a butterfly — and call the change between them a transformation. That fits the book as a whole, which argues that humans keep inventing sharp distinctions — good and bad, large and small, useful and useless — that cut us off from the natural flow of the world, and that we would be happier if we held those distinctions more loosely.

Over to you:
  • Which of your own “sharp distinctions” — success and failure, work and play, useful and useless — would you find hardest to hold loosely?
  • Is this a recipe for freedom, or for never taking a stand on anything?

The book loves to catch confident people off guard. Zhuangzi and Huizi are on a bridge over the river Hao. “See how the minnows are darting about! That is the pleasure of fishes,” says Zhuangzi. Huizi objects: you are not a fish — how can you know what fish enjoy? Zhuangzi replies: “And you not being I, how can you know that I do not know?” And then he plays one last trick, going back to Huizi's original question: by asking how I knew, you already accepted that I knew.

Certainty, for Zhuangzi, is often just a view from one place — and the wise move is to notice where you are standing before you tell anyone else what is true.

Take a side · 3–4 minutes

“Most of the categories that rule our lives — success and failure, useful and useless — are things we invent, not things we find.” Argue for it or against it — and bring real examples from your work, school or culture. Build your case, then I will push back and you defend it.

The rest of the toolbox

The Zhuangzi works through short stories rather than arguments. The first seven chapters have traditionally been credited to him, and many modern scholars still accept that; who wrote the rest is disputed. Glance at these, then tell me which one you would want to discuss first, and why.

Vocabulary — click each card to reveal

Ten words you need to discuss Zhuangzi properly — and that work just as well for any serious conversation about perception, freedom and how we know things. Tap to reveal the meaning and an example.

paradox / paradoxical
noun / adjective
A statement or situation that seems to contradict itself but may still be true.
"“I dreamt I was a butterfly — or am I a butterfly dreaming?” is a paradox students still argue about."
sage
noun
A person respected for great wisdom, especially in ancient traditions.
"The officials came to the sage by the river expecting him to be honoured by their offer."
spontaneity / spontaneous
noun / adjective
Acting naturally, without forcing or over-planning.
"For Zhuangzi, spontaneity is not laziness — it is moving with things instead of against them."
perspective
noun
A particular way of seeing something, shaped by where you stand. Collocations: from my perspective; put things in perspective.
"From the frog's perspective at the bottom of the well, the sky is a small circle."
relative / relativism
adjective / noun
Depending on comparison or point of view, rather than fixed and absolute.
"Big and small are relative: a pond is huge to a minnow and tiny to a heron."
conventional
adjective
Following accepted customs and expectations.
"A conventional man would have taken the job; Zhuangzi went back to his fishing."
detachment / detached
noun / adjective
Emotional distance; not being controlled by desire or worry.
"Singing at his wife's funeral looked like coldness, but he called it a kind of detachment."
to unsettle
verb
To make someone feel less certain or comfortable.
"The butterfly story is meant to unsettle you, not to give you an answer."
whimsical
adjective
Playful and unusual in a light, imaginative way.
"His stories are whimsical — a frog in a well, a sacred tortoise — but the questions are serious."
equanimity
noun
Calmness and balance, especially in difficult situations.
"He met loss with equanimity, comparing death to the change of the seasons."

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that the butterfly story is a joke as much as a philosophy.
  • It might well be that “Zhuangzi” is really the name of a whole school of thinkers.
  • I suspect his calm at the funeral hid more pain than he admitted.
  • You could argue that he is the first great sceptic about certainty.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say his refusal of the job was the wisest thing in the book.
  • What strikes me most is how funny he is.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, we need his doubt more in an age of loud opinions.

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure detachment is always a virtue.
  • I take the point, but doesn't that make every opinion equally valid?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…

I'll make a bold claim — “Zhuangzi is just clever word games; there is nothing you can actually live by.” Answer me using at least one phrase from each box above: first speculate, then take a position, then complicate it. Keep going until you've used all three.

A · The Butterfly Test (3–4 min)

Think of the most vivid dream you have ever had — one that felt completely real while it lasted.

Argue one side: is there any experience you can point to that proves you are awake right now — or is Zhuangzi right that we cannot be completely sure? Build a real case — give your argument, raise the strongest objection to your own view, then answer it.

B · The Tortoise Offer (3–4 min)

You are offered a job with a huge salary, a famous title and a lot of power — but you would lose control of your time and your opinions. The alternative is a modest, free life “wagging your tail in the mud”.

Which do you take — and what would have to change for you to choose the other? Make the case with one concrete example from your own life or someone you know.

Your take — the conversation that matters most

You introduced me to Zhuangzi, so you get the last word. Pick the questions that grab you — there are no model answers here.

Open discussion

  1. After today, has anything shifted in how you see Zhuangzi — or in how sure you feel about your own certainties?
  2. He holds our distinctions loosely — useful and useless, success and failure. Is that wisdom we need, or a luxury for people with nothing at stake?
  3. He refused power to stay free. In your experience, does success usually cost freedom — or can you have both?
  4. His reaction to his wife's death shocks many readers. Is there something in it you would like to learn, or something you would never want to learn?
  5. If you could ask Zhuangzi one question, what would it be? And what strange little story do you think he would tell you instead of answering?
  6. Who is the next writer or thinker you would put in front of me — and what is the one idea of theirs I need to understand?
