Managing Remote & Hybrid Teams

C1 Level · Modern Management

The New Normal

If you're managing knowledge workers in 2026, you're likely managing a mix: people in offices, working from home, distributed across time zones, and some who travel. This isn't temporary. It's how work happens now.

Reflect:

  • How has remote work changed the way you manage?
  • What do you miss about in-person teams? What do you prefer about remote?
  • How do you keep a distributed team feeling connected?
  • Where's the tension in your hybrid arrangement?

The Case for Remote: What Works

Deeper focus: No commute, fewer interruptions, control over your environment. People get more meaningful work done.

Expanded talent pool: You're not limited to who lives in your city. You can hire the best person for the role, regardless of location.

Flexibility for life: Parents, caregivers, people with disabilities, and people managing health conditions can participate fully.

Reduced overhead: Less office space needed, lower utilities, reduced infrastructure costs.

Environmental benefit: Less commuting, lower carbon footprint.

The Challenges: What's Hard

Proximity bias: People who are in the office get more visibility, informal mentoring, and chances. Remote workers get overlooked for promotions.

Accidental exclusion: Hybrid teams often create a two-tier system where in-office people are "in the loop" and remote people are peripheral.

Digital fatigue: Video calls are exhausting. There's no water cooler chat to break the tension. Small talk has to be engineered.

Time zones: Finding meeting times becomes a puzzle. Someone's always in an inconvenient slot.

Onboarding: New hires miss informal learning. They have to schedule everything instead of being naturally included.

Culture and belonging: Real culture is hard to build remotely. You have to be intentional.

Consider: In your team, do remote and in-office workers have equal standing? Be honest.

Vocabulary & Key Concepts

Understand the language of modern work arrangements.

Hybrid Work arrangement combining in-office and remote work; flexible schedule for different days or roles.
Asynchronous Work communication and collaboration that doesn't require real-time participation; happens on your timeline.
Co-located Team members in the same physical location; sitting together in an office.
Digital Nomad Someone who works remotely while traveling; no fixed office, working from various locations.
Hot-desking Shared office spaces without assigned desks; first-come, first-served seating for flexible workers.
Virtual Team Team members primarily or entirely distributed; interaction happens mostly through digital tools.
Time Zone Geographic band where local time is the same; managing across zones is a key challenge for distributed teams.
Work-Life Boundary Separation between work and personal time; harder to maintain when home is the office.

Building Culture Without a Physical Office

Intentionality is everything: Culture doesn't happen by accident in remote settings. You have to design it. That means:

Clear communication norms: Which channel for what? How quickly do people respond? What meetings are synchronous vs. asynchronous?

Regular all-hands meetings: Not just status, but connection. Share wins, celebrate people, build shared purpose.

Subgroup connections: Pods, interest groups, social calls. Don't force it, but enable it.

In-person time: When possible, bring the team together occasionally. It matters. People need face-to-face time, especially new team members and in moments of change.

Equal treatment: If you have both remote and in-office people, everyone should be remote. This prevents a two-tier system where some people are central and others are peripheral.

Documentation: Decisions, processes, context—write it down. This benefits remote workers, new hires, and people who can't attend meetings.

Scenario: Draft Your Hybrid Policy

Your company is moving to a hybrid model. You have flexibility in how your department structures it. You need to draft a policy that balances flexibility with collaboration needs, and that works for your team and business.

10:00

Part 1: Your Framework

Consider these questions and draft a simple hybrid policy:

  • How many days in-office per week? (Full-time remote? Specific days? Flexible?)
  • Are there roles that need to be in-office? Why?
  • How will you handle time-zone differences?
  • What's required for collaboration and what can be async?
  • How do you maintain culture and inclusion?

Example structure: "Tuesday and Thursday are core collaboration days when the team is expected in-office (unless working across time zones, which requires different accommodation). Monday, Wednesday, Friday are flexible—work from home or office based on your preference and work needs. We'll hire globally as long as there's meaningful overlap with core hours. Documentation is mandatory; if it's not written down, assume someone doesn't know it."

Part 2: Addressing the Hard Cases

Your policy will be tested. How do you handle:

  • Someone who doesn't want to come in on office days?
  • People across multiple time zones wanting "core hours"?
  • New hires who are entirely remote and need onboarding?
  • Concerns that remote workers get overlooked for advancement?

Write out your approach to one of these. How would you handle it? What principles guide your decision?

Example (someone resisting office days): Have a conversation. Understand the reason. If it's about productivity, that's worth taking seriously—but the office isn't just about productivity, it's about connection. If it's about hardship (long commute, caregiving), address that. Be flexible where possible. But also be clear: if collaboration is important and they're refusing the minimum in-office time, that's a real issue to work through.

Example (remote workers and advancement): Explicitly monitor this. Make it a conscious decision to ensure remote workers get equal opportunity. Have them present in leadership meetings. Include them in strategic projects. Make visibility and growth possible remotely, or you'll lose your best distributed talent.

Part 3: The Communication

How would you present this policy to your team? What would you emphasise? What concerns would you preempt? Write the opening message or talking points.

Key elements of communication: Lead with the "why"—what problem are we solving? Emphasise flexibility and trust. Acknowledge tradeoffs (collaboration vs. flexibility). Be clear about what's non-negotiable and where there's room to adjust. Invite feedback and signal you'll refine this over time. Show you've thought about equity (remote and in-office workers treated equally). And be clear: this is an experiment. We'll learn and adjust.

The Deeper Conversations

Remote and hybrid work aren't just logistical questions. They touch on trust, autonomy, culture, and what it means to work together.

Reflection questions:

  • How do you define "presence"? Does it require being in a room, or is it about contribution?
  • How much of your preference for in-office work is about productivity vs. control?
  • What's the relationship between surveillance and remote work? Should you monitor people's activity?
  • How do you build trust with people you rarely see in person?
  • What's the responsibility of the company vs. the individual for maintaining work-life balance?

Surveillance vs. Trust

Some organisations try to manage remote workers with activity tracking, screenshot tools, and constant monitoring. This is counterproductive.

It signals distrust, erodes culture, breeds resentment, and doesn't actually improve productivity. People are less likely to do their best work when they feel surveilled.

Better approach: focus on output, not activity. If someone is delivering great results, does it matter when they work? If they're not delivering, that's a conversation to have.

The Future of the Office

The office isn't going away, but its purpose is changing. It's less about getting work done (because you can do that anywhere) and more about collaboration, connection, and culture.

The best offices now are designed for collaboration and community, not individual focus. They're places to come together, build relationships, innovate together, and feel part of something bigger.

If your office doesn't serve that function, it's just expensive real estate.

Remote Work Inequality

There's a dark side to remote work: it can mask and magnify inequalities. People with caregiving responsibilities (often women) might need remote work to participate at all. But if career advancement happens in-office, remote work becomes a trap.

As a leader, be aware of this. Make sure remote work is truly flexible and doesn't create a barrier to opportunity.

Action step: If you manage remote or hybrid workers, audit the fairness of your current arrangement. Are remote and in-office workers equally visible, equally developed, equally promoted? If not, what needs to change?
