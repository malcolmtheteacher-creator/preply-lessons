The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A lump of bronze from the sea

In the spring of 1900, a boat of Greek sponge divers was driven off course by a storm and sheltered near Antikythera, a tiny, wind-battered island between Crete and the mainland. When the weather cleared, one of the divers went down in a heavy helmet and canvas suit to look for sponges — and surfaced in a panic, babbling about a heap of dead bodies on the seabed. What he had actually found were bronze and marble statues, scattered across the wreck of a large Roman-era cargo ship that had gone down around 60 BC.

Over the following months, the Greek authorities organised one of the first major underwater archaeological recoveries in history. Divers brought up exquisite statues, coins, jewellery and glassware — a haul so rich it filled a museum in Athens. Among the treasures, almost ignored, was a shapeless, corroded lump of bronze about the size of a shoebox. It was set aside with the rest of the finds and, for a while, no one paid it much attention.

Then, in 1902, the lump split apart as it dried. Inside the crust of green corrosion, an archaeologist noticed something impossible for its age: a precise, finely toothed gear wheel. Machined gears were not supposed to exist in the ancient world. The object had been made more than two thousand years earlier, yet it looked, unmistakably, like a piece of clockwork.

Part 2 — Decades of puzzlement

For most of the twentieth century, the Antikythera mechanism remained a genuine mystery. Some scholars refused to believe it was really that old, suspecting a later object had fallen onto the wreck by accident. Others could see the gears but had no idea what such a thing was for. The fragments — by now broken into dozens of encrusted pieces — sat quietly in the National Archaeological Museum in Athens, guarding their secret.

The breakthrough came from imaging. In the 1970s a British physicist, Derek de Solla Price, used X-ray photography to peer inside the fused mass and count the teeth on gears no eye could see. What he found astonished him: the device contained not one or two wheels but a whole train of them, meshing together in a design of startling sophistication. He argued that it was a calculating machine that modelled the motions of the heavens.

Decades later, in the 2000s, an international team went much further. Using high-resolution 3D X-ray CT scanning — the same technology used in hospitals — and advanced surface imaging, they read faded inscriptions and mapped more than thirty surviving gears, with signs that others had been lost. Slowly, from a rusted ruin, the true instrument came back into focus: knobs to turn, dials to read, and pointers that once traced the sky.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did the mechanism stay a mystery for so long, and what did some scholars suspect?

2. What role did imaging technology play in finally understanding it?

1. The object was broken and encrusted, and machined gears seemed impossible for its age; some suspected it was a later object that had landed on the wreck by chance.

2. X-ray photography (1970s) revealed a whole gear train; later 3D CT scanning and surface imaging read hidden inscriptions and mapped more than thirty gears.

Discuss:
  • The mechanism was understood only when new technology arrived to read it. What other secrets of the past might still be waiting for a tool that hasn't been invented yet?

Part 3 — The machine that modelled the sky

What the imaging revealed was extraordinary. The Antikythera mechanism was a hand-cranked astronomical calculator — an analogue "computer" of bronze gears. Turn the handle on the side, and dials on the front and back moved together to show the position of the Sun and Moon against the stars, the changing phase of the Moon, and the dates of eclipses years into the future. Ingenious details, such as a pin-and-slot arrangement that reproduced the way the Moon appears to speed up and slow down in its orbit, show an astonishing grasp of astronomy.

It tracked human time as well as celestial time. One dial followed the cycle that governed the ancient Greek games, naming the four-year Olympiad and even the individual festivals, including the Olympics. In effect, a single geared box held a working model of the Greek cosmos and calendar — the kind of understanding usually credited to much later ages.

That is the long echo. Before this object surfaced, the ancient Greeks were admired for their philosophy, art and geometry, but were assumed to lack precision engineering; nothing of comparable complexity is known for well over a thousand years, until the geared clocks of medieval Europe and the Islamic world. The Antikythera mechanism forced a rewriting of the story of technology, proving that a civilisation could invent a sophisticated machine, use it, and then let the knowledge slip away entirely. It stands as a warning and a wonder — a reminder that progress is neither steady nor guaranteed, and that what is known can also be forgotten.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What could the mechanism actually do when you turned its handle?

2. Why did the discovery force historians to rethink ancient technology — what is its "long echo"?

1. It showed the positions of the Sun and Moon, the Moon's phase, the dates of eclipses, and calendar cycles including the four-year Olympiad — a working model of the heavens.

2. The Greeks had not been thought capable of such precision engineering; nothing this complex reappears for over a thousand years, showing that advanced knowledge can be invented and then lost.

Discuss:
  • A whole civilisation built this, used it, and then forgot how. What does that suggest about how fragile our own knowledge and technology might be?

Grammar — the passive voice

Notice how this whole story is told: the wreck was discovered, the gears were revealed, the inscriptions have been read. We rarely know, or care, exactly who did each thing — what matters is what was done to the object. That is precisely what the passive voice is for, which makes it the natural grammar of history, science and discovery.

How the passive is built: a form of be + the past participle (the third form of the verb). The object of the action becomes the subject of the sentence.
• Active: Divers recovered the statues. → Passive: The statues were recovered (by divers).
• The doer (the "agent") is often dropped, or added with by only when it matters: The gears were mapped by an international team.
It works in every tense:
• Present simple: The fragments are kept in Athens.
• Past simple: The mechanism was made around 60 BC.
• Present perfect: Thirty gears have been identified.
• Modal: Others may have been lost.
Use the passive when the doer is unknown, obvious, or simply less important than the thing acted upon.

1. Make it passive

Rewrite the underlined active clause in the passive, keeping the same tense. Drop the doer if it isn't needed.

1. Sponge divers found the wreck. → The wreck near Antikythera. (past simple)

2. A physicist X-rayed the fragments. → The fragments in the 1970s. (past simple)

3. Researchers have mapped the gears. → More than thirty gears . (present perfect)

1. was found (was + past participle)

2. were X-rayed (were + past participle)

3. have been mapped (have + been + past participle)

2. Fix the form

Each sentence uses the passive but the participle or the form of be is wrong. Write the corrected passive verb.

1. The device was (make) more than two thousand years ago.

2. For decades the object was (mistake) for worthless scrap.

3. The inscriptions have recently been (read) using new imaging.

1. made (was made)

2. mistaken (was mistaken for)

3. read (have been read — participle of "read", pronounced "red")

3. Complete the account

Fill each gap with the correct passive form of the verb in brackets (mind the tense). Read it aloud once you're done.

The mechanism (recover) from a shipwreck in 1901. For years its purpose (not / understand). Only when it (scan) with modern technology were its gears finally counted, and today the fragments (display) in Athens.
