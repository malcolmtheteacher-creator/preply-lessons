Lesson 1 — Answer Key & Teaching Flow

Quick reference for the 50-minute slot. Upgraded sentences, anticipated student responses, and timing.

50-minute flow at a glance

0–5 min
Tab 0 — What Examiners Want. Read "The single biggest secret" aloud with her. Ask: "What do you think Criterion A actually rewards?" Listen, then show her the three words to underline forever. Do not linger — this is scene-setting.
5–15 min
Tab 1 — The Ladder. Walk the student up the three rungs. Pause at each rung and ask: "Can you see what's new compared to the one below?" End on the self-check table — the student should be able to state aloud which rung they were on in their most recent essay (typically rung 1–2).
15–25 min
Tab 2 — Weak vs Strong. Do example 1 together: read the weak version, read the strong version, ask her: "What did the strong column add?" Then let her try example 2 by herself — she predicts what the strong version will name as the technique before you reveal it. Repeat for example 3.
25–45 min
Tab 3 — Your Turn. The core of the lesson. Work through each of her three sentences in order. For each: (1) read her original, (2) agree on the diagnosis, (3) let her type her own upgrade in the textarea, (4) click reveal, (5) compare. See detailed upgrade notes below.
45–50 min
Tab 4 — Cheat Sheet. Tell her to screenshot it. Read the four self-check questions together. End with: "Next lesson we build your technique library — the 15 techniques you'll spot again and again."

Tab 3 "Your Turn" — upgrade notes

These are the three sentences of hers that appear in the lesson. Use these notes to coach her before she sees the reveal.

Sentence 1 — Napoleon's greed

"Napoleon is the mighty leader who in fact was greedy to keep the other animals poor and ignorant so he could thrive."

Diagnosis: Rung 1 (Identification). The student names what Napoleon does (greedy, keeps others ignorant) but names no technique Orwell uses, and no effect on the reader.

Coach prompts (use these in order):
  • "How does Orwell make us see Napoleon as a leader before we ever see him do anything cruel? Look at the words around his name."
  • "What changes when the pigs start calling him 'Leader' or 'Comrade Napoleon' instead of just 'Napoleon'?"
  • "What is the technique when a writer uses a capitalised title or honorific to ritualise authority through language alone?" (Target answer: capitalised honorific / loaded vocabulary / linguistic elevation.)
  • "What effect does it have on you as a reader before any violence is named?" (Target: we distrust the new vocabulary; the words have already done the work.)

"Orwell's capitalised honorific — 'our Leader, Comrade Napoleon' — ritualises authority through language alone, conscripting even 'Comrade' into the machinery of hierarchy. The reader is positioned to distrust the farm's new vocabulary before any violence has been named, because the words themselves have already done the work."

What the upgrade adds: named technique (capitalised honorific / loaded vocabulary), direct quotation, effect on reader ("positioned to distrust"), bigger meaning (language pre-empts violence).
Anticipate: the student may say "Orwell shows that Napoleon is bad" — accept this as a start, then push: "What words does Orwell put around Napoleon's name? What effect do those choices have on us?" until the student reaches loaded vocabulary or capitalised honorific.

Sentence 2 — Boxer's loyalty

"Boxer's strength made him think his only job was to work harder and harder, and his loyalty has ruined him."

Diagnosis: Rung 2 (Description). The student describes Boxer's psychology, but does not link this to Orwell's writing choices, and blames Boxer for being ruined — which is not what Orwell's text actually positions us to feel.

Coach prompts:
  • "Boxer only ever says two things across the whole book — what are they?" (Target: 'I will work harder' and 'Napoleon is always right'.)
  • "Does Orwell give Boxer any other thoughts? Why does he reduce Boxer to just two mottos?"
  • "When Orwell shrinks Boxer's whole inner life to two slogans, does that make us angry at Boxer — or sorry for him?"
  • "If we feel sorry for Boxer, where does Orwell redirect the blame?" (Up, to the pigs and the system.)
  • "What's the technique when a character is reduced to a couple of repeated sayings to show their lack of agency?" (Target: reduction / motto repetition / characterisation through diminished speech.)

"Orwell's repeated reduction of Boxer to two mottos — 'I will work harder' and 'Napoleon is always right' — frames him as a victim of simplicity rather than a moral failure. The reader is invited to sympathise: Boxer obeys because the system has made him exactly the kind of worker who would, which redirects blame upward toward the pigs."

What the upgrade adds: names a technique (reduction / motto repetition), corrects the misread direction (sympathy not blame), links to the bigger meaning (blame travels up the hierarchy).
Anticipate: the misread is the teaching moment. The student may resist this — they have been reading Boxer as the one who "ruined himself" through blind loyalty. Gently: "The text agrees with you that his loyalty destroys him. But look at whose advantage that loyalty serves. Whose fault is it that the system rewards a horse who will not stop working?" (The answer points up — to the pigs.)

Sentence 3 — Animal Farm commandments

"This can be seen in Animal Farm, from the 7 commandments which stated 'all animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others', referring to how the pigs take control."

Diagnosis: Rung 1–2. The student has the right quote, describes what it means, but does not name Orwell's technique or its effect on us as readers.

Coach prompts:
  • "When Orwell changes the commandments, does he change them all at once — or gradually, one at a time?"
  • "How do the animals on the farm notice each change? Do they notice it fast or slowly?"
  • "And as readers, do we notice each change the first time it's rewritten?"
  • "What is the technique of making the reader watch a slow corruption happen piece by piece?" (Target: gradual revision, creeping satire, or satirical allegory.)
  • "What does it do to us emotionally to see it happen slowly instead of all at once?" (Target: complicity — we feel like we should have stopped it.)

"Orwell's gradual revision of the Seven Commandments — until only the contradictory 'all animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others' remains — uses creeping satirical allegory to make the reader complicit in the corruption. We notice each small change too late, mirroring the animals on the farm and amplifying the betrayal."

What the upgrade adds: names two techniques (gradual revision + satirical allegory), keeps the student's quote, explains effect on reader (complicity), links to the bigger meaning (betrayal).
Anticipate: the student will reach for "Orwell uses a metaphor" or "Orwell uses symbolism." Accept, then push: "What kind of metaphor? What's the special name for a metaphor that works across a whole book, where every character stands for something in real history?" (Allegory.)

If she is flying — reserve practice

If she reaches the end of Tab 3 before 40 minutes, use this extra passage. Same task: diagnose the rung, write the upgrade.

Reserve sentence

"The sheep keep saying 'Four legs good, two legs bad' to help the revolution."

Diagnosis: Rung 1 (Identification) — pure summary, misreads the effect.

Original Orwell detail: "The sheep were the greatest devotees of the Seven Commandments… they would bleat it for hours on end, never growing tired of it."

"Orwell's insistent repetition of the sheep's bleat — 'Four legs good, two legs bad' — reinforces the slogan's function as thought-terminating ritual, inviting the reader to feel how a rhythm, repeated often enough, can substitute for argument. The bleating is not support of the revolution but a sound that drowns out the chance of disagreement."

What the upgrade adds: named technique (repetition / ritual chant), quoted evidence, effect on reader (we feel the hypnotic pull), links to bigger meaning (rhythm replacing reasoning).
