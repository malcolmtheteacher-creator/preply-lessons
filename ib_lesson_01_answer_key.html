Lesson 1 — Answer Key & Teaching Flow

Quick reference for the 50-minute slot. Upgraded sentences, anticipated student responses, and timing.

50-minute flow at a glance

0–5 min
Tab 0 — What Examiners Want. Read "The single biggest secret" aloud with her. Ask: "What do you think Criterion A actually rewards?" Listen, then show her the three words to underline forever. Do not linger — this is scene-setting.
5–15 min
Tab 1 — The Ladder. Walk her up the three rungs. Pause at each rung and ask: "Can you see what's new compared to the one below?" End on the self-check table — she should be able to state aloud which rung she was on in her Pandora essay (rung 1–2).
15–25 min
Tab 2 — Weak vs Strong. Do example 1 together: read the weak version, read the strong version, ask her: "What did the strong column add?" Then let her try example 2 by herself — she predicts what the strong version will name as the technique before you reveal it. Repeat for example 3.
25–45 min
Tab 3 — Your Turn. The core of the lesson. Work through each of her three sentences in order. For each: (1) read her original, (2) agree on the diagnosis, (3) let her type her own upgrade in the textarea, (4) click reveal, (5) compare. See detailed upgrade notes below.
45–50 min
Tab 4 — Cheat Sheet. Tell her to screenshot it. Read the four self-check questions together. End with: "Next lesson we build your technique library — the 15 techniques you'll spot again and again."

Tab 3 "Your Turn" — upgrade notes

These are the three sentences of hers that appear in the lesson. Use these notes to coach her before she sees the reveal.

Sentence 1 — Jupiter's greed

"The mighty king who in fact was greedy to make the means poor and ignorant for him to live this and happy."

Diagnosis: Rung 1 (Identification). She names what Jupiter does but names no technique and no effect.

Coach prompts (use these in order):
  • "Who is speaking when Jupiter says these words — is it the narrator, or Jupiter himself?"
  • "What does it tell us that Baldwin lets Jupiter speak his own cruelty, instead of the narrator describing it?"
  • "What technique is it called when a character condemns himself through his own words?" (Target answer: dramatic irony / self-incrimination.)
  • "What effect does it have on you as a reader?" (Target: we judge him directly; no narrator needed.)

"Baldwin's choice to voice Jupiter's cruelty in the tyrant's own words — 'poor and ignorant, that so we Mighty Ones may thrive' — uses dramatic irony to expose the king's greed without the narrator ever needing to condemn him. The reader is invited to judge Jupiter directly from his own confession."

What the upgrade adds: named technique (dramatic irony), direct quotation, effect on reader ("invited to judge directly"), bigger meaning (the tyrant reveals himself).
Anticipate: she may say "Baldwin uses dialogue" — accept this as a start, then push: "What kind of dialogue? What effect does letting the villain speak create?" until she reaches dramatic irony or self-incrimination.

Sentence 2 — Pandora's curiosity

"The power of Pandora being pretty made her think she… [had] no use [except] to [open the box]… her curiosity has corrupted her."

Diagnosis: Rung 2 (Description). She describes Pandora's psychology, but misreads the moral direction of the text — Baldwin does not blame Pandora; he redirects blame to the gods.

Coach prompts:
  • "When Baldwin calls Pandora 'the all-gifted', does that make you feel angry at her, or sorry for her?"
  • "What does the text keep reminding us about who gave her those gifts?" (The gods designed her this way.)
  • "If the text positions us to sympathise with Pandora, where does the blame actually go?" (Up, to Jupiter.)
  • "What's the technique when a character is built up through layers of descriptive labels?" (Target: characterisation through accumulated epithets, or simply characterisation.)

"Baldwin's repeated emphasis on Pandora as 'the all-gifted' — beautiful, curious, charming — frames her as a victim of her own design rather than a moral failure. The reader is invited to sympathise: she opens the casket because the gods made her exactly the kind of person who would, which redirects blame upward toward Jupiter."

What the upgrade adds: names a technique (characterisation through accumulated epithets), corrects the misread direction (sympathy not blame), links to the bigger meaning (the gods are the villains).
Anticipate: the misread is the teaching moment. She may resist this — she's been reading Pandora as the one who "caused" the evil. Gently: "The text agrees with you that she caused it. But look at whose fault Baldwin keeps pointing back to. Whose fault does the last sentence of the passage say it is?" (The answer is Jupiter's — "this was the way in which Jupiter sought to make mankind more miserable.")

Sentence 3 — Animal Farm commandments

"This has also be[en] seen in Animal Farm, from the 7 commandments [that] stated 'animals are all equal, but some animals are more equal,' referring [to] some animals to control the other creatures."

Diagnosis: Rung 1–2. She has the right quote, she describes what it means, but she doesn't name Orwell's technique or its effect on us as readers.

Coach prompts:
  • "When Orwell changes the commandments, does he change them all at once — or gradually, one at a time?"
  • "How do the animals on the farm notice each change? Do they notice it fast or slowly?"
  • "And as readers, do we notice each change the first time it's rewritten?"
  • "What is the technique of making the reader watch a slow corruption happen piece by piece?" (Target: gradual revision, creeping satire, or satirical allegory.)
  • "What does it do to us emotionally to see it happen slowly instead of all at once?" (Target: complicity — we feel like we should have stopped it.)

"Orwell's gradual revision of the Seven Commandments — until only the contradictory 'all animals are equal, but some animals are more equal than others' remains — uses creeping satirical allegory to make the reader complicit in the corruption. We notice each small change too late, mirroring the animals on the farm and amplifying the betrayal."

What the upgrade adds: names two techniques (gradual revision + satirical allegory), keeps her quote, explains effect on reader (complicity), links to the bigger meaning (betrayal).
Anticipate: she will reach for "Orwell uses a metaphor" or "Orwell uses symbolism." Accept, then push: "What kind of metaphor? What's the special name for a metaphor that works across a whole book, where every character stands for something in real history?" (Allegory.)

If she is flying — reserve practice

If she reaches the end of Tab 3 before 40 minutes, use this extra passage. Same task: diagnose the rung, write the upgrade.

Reserve sentence

"The evils flew into every house and made people sick."

Diagnosis: Rung 1 (Identification) — pure summary.

Original Baldwin line: "They fluttered for a little while about the room, and then flew away to find dwelling-places wherever there were homes of men. They were diseases and cares."

"Baldwin's use of the verb 'fluttered' — a word we normally associate with butterflies or moths — creates a jarring dissonance between the delicacy of the movement and the monstrous harm it delivers. The reader is unsettled by the beauty of evil, preparing us for the text's argument that corruption often arrives disguised as something innocuous."

What the upgrade adds: named technique (verb choice / dissonance / juxtaposition), quoted evidence, effect on reader (unsettlement), links to bigger meaning (corruption disguised as innocuous).
