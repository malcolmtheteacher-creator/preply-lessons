CAE Writing Part 2

Reports & Proposals - Master the Format

Cambridge C1 Advanced

The Real Challenge

Reports and proposals are two of the most common writing tasks in Part 2 of the CAE Writing exam. They look similar, but understanding the key differences is what separates Band 6 from Band 8.

The Question You Must Ask: Am I describing what IS (report) or suggesting what SHOULD BE (proposal)?

What's the Difference?

REPORT

  • Describes a current situation
  • Based on research, surveys, or observations
  • Presents findings objectively
  • Makes recommendations at the end
  • Tone: Informative and factual

"Here's what we found, and here's what we suggest."

PROPOSAL

  • Suggests future action or changes
  • Aims to persuade the reader
  • Focuses on benefits
  • Recommendations throughout
  • Tone: Persuasive and forward-looking

"Here's what we should do and why it will work."

What They Have in Common

  • Clear sections with headings - This is NOT optional
  • Formal register - No contractions, no colloquialisms
  • Recommendations - Both end with suggestions for action
  • Structured introduction - States the purpose clearly
  • 220-260 words - Same word count requirement

Band 6 vs Band 8: Same Task, Different Results

Task: Write a report for your college principal about facilities that could be improved.

Band 6

Problems:

  • No clear section headings
  • Mixes informal language ("stuff", "a lot")
  • Vague recommendations ("things should be better")
  • No clear structure
  • Doesn't fully address all points
"The library needs a lot of stuff to be fixed. Students don't like it because it's not very good..."
Band 8

Strengths:

  • Clear headings for each section
  • Consistently formal register
  • Specific, actionable recommendations
  • Logical flow with cohesive devices
  • Addresses all required points
"Introduction: The purpose of this report is to evaluate current facilities and suggest improvements..."
The Difference: Band 8 answers show clear organization, appropriate register, and specific detail. It's not about being "better at English" - it's about knowing the FORMAT.

Decision 1: Understand the Task

Before you write a single word, you must analyze the task carefully. This takes 2-3 minutes but saves you from disaster.

Question 1: Who is the Reader?

The reader determines your tone and level of formality.

Reader What This Means
Your boss / manager Very formal, respectful, professional
College principal / director Formal, academic tone
Town council / committee Formal, public-facing language
Club members / colleagues Semi-formal, still professional

Question 2: What's the Purpose?

  • To inform - Present facts and findings (reports)
  • To recommend - Suggest actions based on evidence (reports)
  • To persuade - Convince someone to approve a plan (proposals)
  • To request - Ask for resources or approval (proposals)

Question 3: What Points MUST You Cover?

CAE tasks always include bullet points or specific requirements. Miss one = lose marks.

Warning: Examiners check that you've addressed ALL the points. Even a brilliant answer that misses one requirement will be marked down.

Decision 2: Structure It Right

Both reports and proposals follow a similar structure. Master this template and you're halfway to Band 8.

Target word count: 220-260 words Aim for 240 words - gives you flexibility

The Standard Structure

INTRODUCTION (30-40 words)

State the purpose of the report/proposal. What is it about and why are you writing it?

MAIN BODY - Section 1 (50-60 words)

First main point with a clear heading

MAIN BODY - Section 2 (50-60 words)

Second main point with a clear heading

MAIN BODY - Section 3 (optional) (40-50 words)

Third point if needed

CONCLUSION / RECOMMENDATIONS (40-50 words)

Summary and recommendations for action

Section Heading Examples

Use clear, informative headings. Here are examples that work:

Introduction Current Situation Findings Staff Feedback Main Issues Proposed Changes Benefits Potential Challenges Recommendations Conclusion
Avoid: Vague headings like "Part 1", "Main Part", or "The End". Your headings should tell the reader what each section contains.

How to Write the Introduction

Your introduction must state the PURPOSE clearly. Use these templates:

For Reports:

"The purpose of this report is to evaluate/assess/examine..."

"This report aims to present findings on..."

"The aim of this report is to provide an overview of..."

For Proposals:

"The purpose of this proposal is to suggest/recommend..."

"This proposal outlines a plan for..."

"The aim of this proposal is to put forward ideas for..."

How to Write Recommendations

Use modal verbs to show degrees of certainty and politeness:

Modal Strength Example
should Strong recommendation The company should invest in new equipment.
would Conditional/polite It would be advisable to review the policy.
could Suggestion/possibility The college could consider offering evening classes.
might Tentative suggestion Management might wish to explore alternatives.
Pro Tip: Mix different modals in your recommendations section to show range. Don't use "should" for every sentence.

Decision 3: Use Formal Language

Reports and proposals require consistent formal register. One informal phrase can cost you marks.

Passive Voice for Objectivity

The passive voice creates distance and sounds more objective:

"We surveyed 50 students."
→
"50 students were surveyed."
"People have complained about noise."
→
"Complaints have been received regarding noise levels."
"We need to fix this problem."
→
"This issue needs to be addressed."

Formal Linking Words

Adding Information:

Furthermore, Moreover, In addition, Additionally, What is more

Contrasting:

However, Nevertheless, On the other hand, Conversely, Nonetheless

Explaining Results:

Therefore, Consequently, As a result, Thus, Hence

Giving Examples:

For instance, For example, Such as, Namely, In particular

Hedging Language

Hedging makes your writing sound more academic and less absolute:

Too Direct Hedged (Better)
"Students don't like the cafeteria." "Students appear to be dissatisfied with the cafeteria."
"This will solve the problem." "This is likely to address the issue."
"Everyone agrees." "There seems to be general agreement."
"The system fails." "The system tends to experience difficulties."
Useful hedging phrases: appears to, seems to, tends to, is likely to, may/might, could potentially, it is possible that, evidence suggests that

Recommendation Phrases

Strong Recommendations:

"It is recommended that..."

"I strongly recommend that..."

"It is essential that..."

Suggestions:

"I would suggest that..."

"It would be advisable to..."

"It might be worth considering..."

Conditional Recommendations:

"If resources permit, it would be beneficial to..."

"Should funding be available, the organization could..."

"Provided that there is sufficient support..."

Practice: Transform Informal to Formal

Exercise: Rewrite these sentences formally

1. "Lots of people said the food is bad." ▼

"A significant number of respondents expressed dissatisfaction with the quality of food."

Changes: "lots of" → "a significant number of", "said" → "expressed", "bad" → "dissatisfaction with the quality"

2. "We should definitely do something about this." ▼

"It is strongly recommended that action be taken to address this matter."

Changes: Passive construction, "definitely" → "strongly", "do something" → "action be taken", "this" → "this matter"

3. "The wifi is really slow and it's annoying everyone." ▼

"The wireless connection appears to be inadequate, which has caused considerable frustration among users."

Changes: "wifi" → "wireless connection", "really slow" → "inadequate", "annoying everyone" → "caused considerable frustration among users"

4. "I think we could maybe try having more breaks." ▼

"It might be worth considering the implementation of additional break periods."

Changes: "I think we could maybe try" → "It might be worth considering", "having more breaks" → "the implementation of additional break periods"

Decision 4: Reports vs Proposals - Key Differences

Now let's look at when to use each format and how they differ in practice.

Reports: The Basics

  • Focus: Factual, based on research, surveys, or observations
  • Tone: Objective and informative
  • Purpose: Describes current problems or situations
  • Structure: Findings THEN recommendations
  • Language: Passive voice, hedging, formal register
Key question for reports: "What did we find out?" The answer forms your main body.

Typical Report Tasks:

  • Evaluate the effectiveness of a service/facility
  • Describe the results of a survey
  • Assess the current situation and recommend changes
  • Report on problems that have been identified

Proposals: The Basics

  • Focus: Persuasive, forward-looking, solution-oriented
  • Tone: Enthusiastic but professional
  • Purpose: Sells the benefits of an idea
  • Structure: Ideas + benefits woven together
  • Language: Modal verbs, conditional language, benefit-focused
Key question for proposals: "Why should they say yes?" The answer shapes your entire response.

Typical Proposal Tasks:

  • Suggest improvements to a facility/service
  • Propose a new event, programme, or initiative
  • Put forward ideas for changes
  • Recommend a course of action for the future

Quick Decision: Report or Proposal?

Identify the text type:

1. "Your manager wants to know about customer satisfaction levels..." ▼

REPORT - You're describing findings about the current situation (satisfaction levels).

2. "Your college wants students to suggest ways to improve campus life..." ▼

PROPOSAL - You're suggesting future improvements and persuading.

3. "Write about the results of a survey on transport options..." ▼

REPORT - Based on survey results = factual findings.

4. "Your company is considering expanding into new markets. Suggest..." ▼

PROPOSAL - Forward-looking, suggesting a course of action.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1: No headings
Reports and proposals MUST have section headings. No headings = automatic mark reduction.
Mistake 2: Mixing registers
One "gonna" or "lots of" in an otherwise formal text stands out badly. Stay consistent.
Mistake 3: Missing task points
Check the bullet points. Address ALL of them. Examiners specifically look for this.
Mistake 4: Confusing reports and proposals
Reports = what IS. Proposals = what SHOULD BE. Know which you're writing.
Mistake 5: Weak recommendations
"Things should be better" is vague. "The company should implement flexible hours" is specific.
Mistake 6: Wrong word count
Under 200 or over 280 words will cost you marks. Count carefully.
