Group Harmony or Individual Voices?

“The nail that sticks out gets hammered down.” Is that how East Asians really live — and what is changing?

The words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. Some workers feel pressure to conform to the group's style.
conform (v) — to behave in the way that other people expect. Example: “Some workers feel pressure to conform to the group's style.”
2. In Japanese culture, harmony is a high value.
harmony (n) — a situation in which people live and work together without conflict. Example: “In Japanese culture, harmony is a high value.”
3. Collective decisions can take longer to reach.
collective (adj) — shared by a group of people. Example: “Collective decisions can take longer to reach.”
4. An assertive manager says clearly what she needs.
assertive (adj) — confident and willing to say what you want. Example: “An assertive manager says clearly what she needs.”
5. The team worked until it reached a consensus.
consensus (n) — an agreement reached by a whole group. Example: “The team worked until it reached a consensus.”
6. Family obligations often come before personal plans.
obligation (n) — something you must do because it is your duty. Example: “Family obligations often come before personal plans.”
7. Loyalty to a company used to last a whole working life.
loyalty (n) — support for a person or group that you do not change. Example: “Loyalty to a company used to last a whole working life.”
8. Researchers talk about tendencies, not rules.
tendency (n) — a way of behaving that is likely to happen. Example: “Researchers talk about tendencies, not rules.”

The stereotype: “East Asians have no individuality — everyone obeys the group.” Let's find out what's really true.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — The nail

A well-known Japanese proverb says that “the nail that sticks out gets hammered down”. Researchers often use scales of “individualism” to compare countries. On the best-known scale, created by Geert Hofstede, the USA scores about 91, Japan about 46, and China and South Korea about 20 or lower. Such scores describe averages and tendencies, not the way that every person thinks, and some scholars say a single number is too simple.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the Japanese proverb about the nail mean?

2. Do the scores describe every person?

1. People who stand out are pressured to conform.

2. No — they describe averages and tendencies.

Discuss:
  • Is there a proverb in your language about standing out or fitting in?

Part 2 — Harmony in practice

In Japan, “wa” — harmony — means avoiding open conflict, and teams often build consensus before a formal meeting, a process called nemawashi. In Korea, nunchi is the skill of reading a room and sensing what other people feel. In China, guanxi — networks of trusted relationships — helps people get things done. Belonging to a group brings support, but also obligations.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What is nunchi?

2. What does belonging to a group bring?

1. The skill of reading a room and sensing what others feel.

2. Support, but also obligations.

Discuss:
  • What do you gain and what do you give up when you belong to a group?

Part 3 — Individuals everywhere

People are not copies of each other. Japan has produced manga artists, chefs and architects with very personal styles; Korea has an energetic independent music scene; China has creative entrepreneurs who started with nothing. Researchers say younger people in all three countries put more value on personal choice than their grandparents did. And in a group-focused culture, standing out can simply happen in another way — through skill, humour or generosity.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Which groups of people in East Asia have very personal styles?

2. What do younger people value more than their grandparents did?

1. Manga artists, chefs, architects, musicians and entrepreneurs.

2. Personal choice.

Discuss:
  • Which famous person from East Asia stands out most to you?

Grammar — Contrast — Although, Whereas, However

A stereotype and the reality rarely match. This lesson's grammar gives you the structure to describe the difference.

How it works:
Contrast:
• although + clause: “Although harmony is valued, individuals still stand out.”
• whereas / while compares two things: “The USA scores about 91, whereas China scores about 20.”
• however begins a new sentence: “Groups matter. However, individuals matter too.”
• despite + noun: “Despite the pressure, many people speak up.”

Going deeper

In one famous experiment, researchers showed Japanese and American students a picture of an underwater scene. The Americans tended to talk about the biggest fish; the Japanese tended to describe more of the background and the relationships between the objects. Some researchers say this reflects a habit of looking at the whole picture. Others say it is too early to be sure. Either way, tendencies in the way a group thinks do not mean that its members are the same.

Worth noticing: Contrast: although / though (+ clause), whereas / while (compares two things), however (starts a new sentence, with a comma), despite / in spite of (+ noun).
For today — practical cross-cultural tips: In a group meeting with East Asian colleagues, don't expect a loud argument: ideas are often raised beforehand or in small conversations. Ask quiet questions after the meeting, and don't mistake silence for lack of ideas. Praise the team's work, but also notice individual contributions in private.
Dig into it — talk these through:
  • Is it easy to disagree in groups you belong to?
  • What is better for a company: a team that agrees quickly, or one that argues openly?
  • Are young people more individualistic everywhere, or only in some countries?
