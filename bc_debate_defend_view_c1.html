Debate Sparks: Defend Your View

C1 Advanced · Fluency & Advanced Language Structures

Warm-Up: Instant Opinions

Answer each question rapidly with a brief reason for your position. Don't overthink—this is about speaking naturally and confidently under time pressure.

  1. Should smartphones be banned at dinner?
  2. City or countryside living?
  3. Should kids choose their bedtime?
  4. Is money the main measure of success?
  5. Should we all be vegetarians?

Notice how you naturally defend your position. Today we'll add structure and sophistication to these natural responses.

Sound Thoughtful: Opening an Opinion

These phrases help you express your view with confidence and nuance:

1. To be honest...

Signals authenticity and a personal perspective. Shows you're being genuine.

Example: "To be honest, I think remote work has changed how we value time at the office."

2. From my perspective...

Acknowledges your viewpoint is one of many possible ones. Shows intellectual humility.

Example: "From my perspective, the best managers are those who trust their teams."

3. In my view...

A clear, direct way to introduce your personal stance. Neutral and professional.

Example: "In my view, technology should enhance human connection, not replace it."

4. I'd argue that...

Slightly stronger and more confident. Shows you're willing to defend your position logically.

Example: "I'd argue that artificial intelligence will create more jobs than it destroys."

Stay Polite: Acknowledging Other Views

Respectful debate requires you to show you've heard and understood the other side:

Although I see the point...

Shows you understand the opposing view before presenting your own. Demonstrates respectful listening and balanced thinking.

Example: "Although I see the point that social media connects people globally, I think it can be isolating locally."

It's true that..., but...

Acknowledges validity in the other position while maintaining your stance. Creates a bridge between opposing viewpoints.

Example: "It's true that automation increases efficiency, but it also raises questions about job security."

I can understand why...

Shows empathy for the reasoning behind different opinions. Softens disagreement while maintaining your position.

Example: "I can understand why someone might prefer office work, but I've found flexibility essential."

Be Convincing: Contrasting Your Ideas

Move smoothly from acknowledging another view to presenting your own:

On the other hand...

Introduces a contrasting perspective smoothly. Natural conversational flow.

That said...

Pivots to an alternative viewpoint concisely. Sophisticated and natural.

However...

Creates a clear opposition between points. Universal and effective.

Conversely...

Signals a completely opposite perspective is coming. More formal and emphatic.

Add Impact: Making Your Point Unforgettable

What people often forget is...

Suggests you're revealing an overlooked but important insight.

Example: "What people often forget is that technology changes faster than our ability to regulate it."

It's absolutely essential that...

Creates urgency and highlights critical importance.

Example: "It's absolutely essential that we invest in education now to prepare for future jobs."

The crucial point here is...

Directs attention to your central argument.

Example: "The crucial point here is that motivation matters more than natural talent."

We can't ignore the fact that...

Presents your point as undeniable and significant.

Example: "We can't ignore the fact that climate change is already affecting our economy."

Hedging: Softening Your Stance

Sometimes you want to sound thoughtful and cautious rather than absolute:

1. I believe that... (Most direct)

Clear, confident statement of your position.

2. It seems to me that... (Somewhat softened)

Invites consideration without being absolute.

3. Perhaps we should consider... (Quite tentative)

Opens discussion rather than asserting truth.

4. I wonder if it might be possible that... (Very cautious)

Extremely open and exploratory. Good for sensitive topics.

Advanced Grammar for Debates

Concessive Clauses: Acknowledging Complexity

These structures help you acknowledge other viewpoints while maintaining your position:

Structure: Although / Even though + clause

Example: "Although I agree that remote work is convenient, I think it's impractical for team cohesion."

Example: "Even though it helps productivity, it causes isolation."

Concessive clauses help you acknowledge other viewpoints while maintaining your position. They show you're thoughtful and fair-minded.

Cleft Sentences: Emphasizing Key Points

Use these to direct attention to what you consider most important in your argument:

What + clause + is...

Example: "What really matters is trust."

Example: "What distinguishes successful entrepreneurs is resilience, not talent."

The thing + clause + is...

Example: "The thing I find surprising is the speed of change."

Example: "The thing about leadership is that it requires both vision and humility."

It is/was + emphasis + that...

Example: "It is the environment that suffers most."

Example: "It was his attention to detail that made the difference."

Cleft sentences help emphasize key points in your argument. They direct your listener's attention to what you consider most important.

Conditional Clauses: Making Arguments with "If"

Real Conditions (likely)

Structure: If + present, will + base

Example: "If people work from home, cities will change."

Example: "If we invest in education now, we'll see economic growth later."

Hypothetical Conditions (possible)

Structure: If + past, would + base

Example: "If I had more time, I'd learn coding."

Example: "If companies valued work-life balance more, employees would be happier."

Impossible Conditions (counterfactual)

Structure: If + past perfect, would have + past participle

Example: "If we had acted earlier, we would have prevented this crisis."

Example: "If technology had been regulated properly, we wouldn't be facing these problems now."

Speed Debate: Three Rounds

You'll practice expressing and defending your viewpoint with increasing sophistication across three timed rounds.

Round 1: Present (60 seconds)

Deliver your view clearly and confidently. Focus on your main points without excessive detail.

  • Open with one of your opinion-starters (To be honest, From my perspective, etc.)
  • Give 1–2 main reasons
  • Use confident language (I'd argue that, The crucial point is, etc.)

Round 2: Defend (varies)

Respond to teacher challenges. Use concessive clauses and hedging to show you've heard the other side while maintaining your stance.

  • Acknowledge the opposing view (Although I see the point..., It's true that..., but...)
  • Contrast with your position (On the other hand, That said, Conversely...)
  • Use hedging if needed to avoid sounding absolute

Round 3: Refine (varies)

Rephrase with stronger fluency and structure. Add cleft sentences and conditional clauses to show depth.

  • Use cleft sentences to emphasize your key point (What really matters is...)
  • Use conditionals to explore consequences (If..., then...)
  • Speak with more natural pacing and confidence
