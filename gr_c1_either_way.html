Today we’re working on choosing between two options and defending the choice, so you can push back, give ground, weigh things up and say what your answer depends on, in natural English.

Every question here is a choice between two things. There is no right answer and nothing to win. What changes from round to round is what you do with your answer.

How it sounds

First speakerOn balance, I’d go for the four-day week. Time is the one thing you can’t earn back.

Second speakerThat’s assuming you would actually rest. Wouldn’t that mean five days’ work squeezed into four?

First speakerFair point, though I’d still take it. A long weekend every week is worth a few late evenings.

Key words

Eight words and phrases for weighing things up. You will want them in the questions that follow. Read each one aloud, then give an example of your own.

trade-off (noun) — a situation in which you accept less of one good thing in order to get more of another. Example: "Working from home is a trade-off: you gain time but lose company."
deal-breaker (noun) — the one thing that makes you reject an offer, however good the rest of it is. Example: "The salary was fine, but the two-hour journey each way was a deal-breaker."
toss-up (noun) — a choice between two options so equal that you could decide by throwing a coin. Example: "It's a toss-up between the two flats; I like them both."
on balance (phrase) — after considering all the points for and against. Example: "On balance, I think we made the right decision."
marginally (adverb) — by a very small amount. Example: "The second offer was only marginally better than the first."
concede (verb) — to admit, often unwillingly, that the other person is right about something. Example: "He conceded that the plan would cost more than he had first said."
lesser of two evils (phrase) — the less unpleasant of two bad choices. Example: "Neither option appealed to me, so I picked the lesser of two evils."
hedge (verb) — to avoid giving a direct answer, so that you cannot be proved wrong later. Example: "Asked which side she was on, she hedged and said both had a point."

Three routes · about 50 minutes each

Recall zone

Three quick ones from further back. Without looking anything up, say each answer aloud, then check.

1. What is the difference between “I’d rather stay” and “I’d rather you stayed”?

“I’d rather stay” is about what I want to do myself: would rather + the plain verb. “I’d rather you stayed” is about what I want somebody else to do: would rather + person + a past form, although the meaning is present or future.

2. What is the difference between “I used to work late” and “I’m used to working late”?

“I used to work late” is a past habit that has stopped. “I’m used to working late” means it is normal for me now and no longer feels difficult. After be used to, the verb takes -ing.

3. Give three ways to disagree with a colleague, from gentle to firm.

For example: “I see it slightly differently.” · “I’m not sure I agree with that.” · “I’m afraid I can’t go along with that.”

Explain it

Why does “Fair point, though I’d still take it” sound stronger than “Yes, but I still want it”? Explain it in your own words.
“Fair point” shows that you have really listened and accepted part of the other argument, so your final choice sounds considered, not stubborn. “Yes, but” gives nothing away: the listener hears only the “but”.
