Here's the thing...

You just answered three of the biggest questions human beings have ever asked. You already have views about the origin of the universe, the meaning of death, and the nature of right and wrong.

That means you already have a worldview. You might not have known it had a name — but it does. Let's find out what that means.

2. Encounter

KEY TERM

Worldview (noun)

A person's overall way of understanding the world, their place in it, and how they should live. It includes their beliefs about God (or no God), right and wrong, the meaning of life, and what happens after death.

Think of it as: the invisible glasses you look through every day. You don't usually notice them — but they shape everything you see.

The word comes from the German Weltanschauung (velt-AN-show-ung), meaning "view of the world." The philosopher Immanuel Kant first used it in 1790. Everyone has one — even people who say they "don't believe in anything."

A worldview in action

Watch how the same event looks completely different through different worldview "glasses":

The situation:

A terrible earthquake destroys a city. Thousands of people die.

╋ Through Christian glasses

"God allows suffering for reasons we may not understand. We must help the victims and trust in God's plan. This is a fallen world, but heaven awaits."

☙ Through Buddhist glasses

"Suffering (dukkha) is part of all existence. Nothing is permanent. Our response should be compassion (karuna) for all who suffer."

△ Through Humanist glasses

"There is no divine plan — earthquakes are natural events. What matters now is the human response: science to understand, compassion to help."

☮ Through Muslim glasses

"This is a test from Allah. Everything that happens is part of God's will (qadr). Muslims must respond with patience (sabr) and charity (sadaqah)."

Same earthquake. Four completely different ways of making sense of it. That's what worldviews do — they don't change the facts, they change how you interpret the facts.

• • •

Two types of worldview

🕌

Religious Worldviews

Include belief in God, gods, or a spiritual reality beyond the physical world. Life has a sacred purpose.

Examples: Christianity, Islam, Hinduism, Buddhism, Judaism, Sikhism

🔬

Non-Religious Worldviews

Do not include belief in God or the supernatural. Meaning and morality come from human reason and experience.

Examples: Humanism, Atheism, Agnosticism, Secularism

3. Explore

Now let's go deeper. A worldview isn't just about God or no God — it's a whole system of beliefs that fit together.

The Seven Dimensions of Religion

In 1989, the scholar Ninian Smart suggested that every worldview has up to seven "dimensions" — like seven sides of a diamond. Not every worldview has all seven, but most have several.

📚

Narrative

Stories & myths

💡

Doctrinal

Beliefs & teachings

⚖

Ethical

Rules for living

🎶

Ritual

Practices & worship

💜

Experiential

Feelings & experiences

🏛

Institutional

Organisations & leaders

🎨

Material

Buildings, art, symbols

Let's apply the dimensions

Here's how the seven dimensions might apply to Christianity as an example:

Christianity through Smart's Seven Dimensions

Narrative: The story of Jesus — birth, death, resurrection
Doctrinal: God is a Trinity — Father, Son, Holy Spirit
Ethical: "Love your neighbour as yourself"
Ritual: Baptism, Communion, prayer
Experiential: Feeling the presence of God, being "born again"
Institutional: The Church — Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox
Material: Cathedrals, crosses, stained glass, the Bible

Can Smart's dimensions apply to football fandom? This isn't a joke — think carefully about each one.

Possible answers:
Narrative: "That time we won the cup in '66..." — fans tell origin stories, legends, and myths
Doctrinal: "You never walk alone" — core beliefs about loyalty and identity
Ethical: "You never support two clubs" — unwritten rules of fandom
Ritual: Wearing the shirt, chanting, pre-match traditions
Experiential: The roar of a last-minute goal — transcendent emotion
Institutional: FIFA, the FA, the club itself — hierarchies and authority
Material: Stadiums, scarves, trophies, statues outside grounds

The point isn't that football IS a religion — it's that Smart's dimensions help us understand what worldviews are made of, and they apply more widely than you might expect.

Strong evaluations include:
• Evidence from what you've learned (Smart's dimensions, key terms)
• Acknowledgment of the other side before giving your view
• A clear reason for your position — not just "I think" but "I think because"

For example: You might argue that religious worldviews address the "experiential" and "narrative" dimensions more fully — or you might argue that Humanism has strong ethical, institutional, and material dimensions without needing the supernatural.

The quality of your reasoning matters more than which side you choose.
