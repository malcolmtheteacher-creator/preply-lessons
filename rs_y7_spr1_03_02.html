Many people know a few Bible stories (Adam and Eve, Jesus, Jonah and the whale) but don't realize the Bible is actually 66 separate books written by different people over 1,500 years. Today you'll discover its structure and why Christians see it as so important.

The Structure of the Bible

The Bible has two main parts:

Old Testament: 39 Books

Written before Jesus was born. Covers the history of Israel, God's laws, poetry, and prophecies about a future Messiah.

Main sections:

  • The Torah (Law): First 5 books (Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy)
  • History: Joshua, Judges, 1&2 Samuel, 1&2 Kings, etc.
  • Poetry & Wisdom: Psalms (prayers and hymns), Proverbs, Job
  • Prophecy: Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, etc.

New Testament: 27 Books

Written after Jesus's life. Focuses on Jesus, the early Church, and Christian teachings.

Main sections:

  • The Gospels: Matthew, Mark, Luke, John (4 accounts of Jesus's life)
  • Acts: History of the early Church after Jesus's resurrection
  • Paul's Letters: Romans, 1&2 Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, etc. (theology and advice to churches)
  • Other Letters: James, 1&2 Peter, 1&2&3 John, Jude
  • Revelation: A prophetic vision of the end times
Quick fact: 66 books total. Different Christian traditions include different numbers of books (Catholics include extra books called the Apocrypha), but the 66-book version is most common in Protestant churches.

Types of Writing in the Bible

The Bible isn't one type of book. It's a collection of many different kinds of writing:

History

Stories of what God did and what happened to God's people. Examples: Genesis (creation, Abraham), Exodus (Moses, freedom from Egypt), Kings (history of Israel's kingdoms).

Law

God's commandments and rules. The most famous: the Ten Commandments. But the Torah contains 613 commandments covering everything from worship to food to business.

Poetry

The Psalms are hymns and prayers, full of emotion and beauty. Used in worship. Not meant to be read literally—they use metaphor and emotion.

Wisdom Literature

Proverbs and Job explore questions about suffering, meaning, and how to live. Practical advice mixed with deep questions.

Gospel

Stories of Jesus's life, teachings, miracles, death, and resurrection. Four different accounts (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John) tell slightly different stories from different angles.

Letter

Paul and other leaders wrote letters to churches, answering questions and giving instruction. These became Scripture.

Prophecy

Messages from God about future events or calls to repentance. Often mysterious and symbolic (like Revelation).

Apocalyptic

Visions of the end of the world. Revelation is the main example: cosmic battles, symbols, and mysterious imagery.

Why this matters: You can't read a Psalm the same way you read a law code. You can't read a poem the same way you read history. Understanding the genre helps you understand what the Bible is claiming.

Key Books You Should Know

Genesis

What: The creation story, Adam and Eve, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob. Foundational stories for Judaism and Christianity.

Type: History/narrative with theology mixed in.

Exodus

What: Moses leads the Israelites out of slavery in Egypt. God gives the Ten Commandments. The founding story of Israel.

Type: History, law, and theology.

Psalms

What: 150 hymns and prayers. Express joy, sorrow, anger, praise. Used in worship for 2,500+ years.

Type: Poetry/liturgy.

The Four Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John)

What: Four accounts of Jesus's life, teachings, and resurrection. Each Gospel has a different audience and angle.

Type: Biography/narrative (though not like modern biography).

Romans

What: Paul's letter to the Church in Rome. His most important theological letter, explaining faith, grace, and salvation.

Type: Letter/theology.

Revelation

What: A prophetic vision of cosmic battles, judgment, and a new heaven and earth. Often interpreted symbolically.

Type: Apocalyptic vision.

How Christians Use the Bible

Read in Worship

Churches follow a lectionary (schedule) that reads through the Bible over a 3-year cycle. A passage is read aloud in every service.

Personal Devotion

Many Christians read the Bible alone each day for spiritual growth and connection to God.

Source of Moral Guidance

When facing decisions, Christians ask: "What does the Bible say about this?"

Memorised Verses

Many Christians have Bible verses memorised. "Jesus wept" (shortest verse). John 3:16 is probably the most famous verse.

Study Groups

Churches run Bible study groups where members discuss passages together and explore meaning.

Sung (Psalms)

The Psalms are sung in worship. Many hymns are based on Bible passages.

Views on Biblical Authority: The Spectrum

Key verse: 2 Timothy 3:16 says "All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for teaching, rebuking, correcting and training in righteousness."

All Christians agree this verse is important. But they interpret what "God-breathed" means very differently.

Fundamentalist / Literal View:

Every word of the Bible is exactly as God intended. It's inerrant (without error). Should be read literally. No contradictions exist—if there seem to be, we're just misunderstanding.

Liberal / Interpreted View:

The Bible is inspired by God, but written by humans in specific historical contexts. It contains eternal truth, but we interpret it for our time. Some parts are metaphorical. Contradictions exist and that's OK—they show different perspectives.

Most Christians:

Fall somewhere in the middle. They see the Bible as authoritative and God-inspired, but also recognize it was written by humans and requires interpretation. They take some parts literally (especially moral laws and Jesus's teachings) but interpret other parts (like apocalyptic visions) more freely.

Catholic View:

The Bible is sacred, but not the only source of authority. The Church Tradition (teachings of the Magisterium) and the Pope also have authority. Scripture + Tradition = complete revelation.

Activity 2: Identify the Genre

Read these three short Bible passages and identify what type of writing each is. What does that tell you about how to read it?

Passage 1: Genesis 1:1

"In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth."

What type of writing is this? Narrative/history (though some see it as theological poetry)

What does that mean for how we read it? It's presenting a foundational story. Different Christians interpret whether it's meant to be scientifically precise or theologically true (or both).

Passage 2: Psalm 23:1-2

"The Lord is my shepherd, I lack nothing. He makes me lie down in green pastures, he leads me beside quiet waters."

What type of writing is this? Poetry/metaphor

What does that mean for how we read it? It uses metaphor (God is like a shepherd). It's expressing emotion and spiritual truth, not literal facts. You're meant to feel comforted, not imagine God literally herding you like sheep.

Passage 3: Romans 3:28

"For we maintain that a person is justified by faith apart from the works of the law."

What type of writing is this? Letter/theology

What does that mean for how we read it? Paul is arguing a theological point to a church. It's meant to explain Christian belief, not tell a story. You're supposed to think critically and understand the logic.

Discussion Questions

Pick at least 2 and write 3-4 sentences for each:

Question 1: If the Bible was written by humans, can it still be the "word of God"?

Christians believe the Bible is "God-breathed," meaning God guided the authors. But humans still made choices about what to include. Does that make it less divine? Or can God work through human writers?

Question 2: Why are there so many different translations?

The Bible was written in Hebrew (Old Testament) and Greek (New Testament). Translation requires choices. Should translators be literal or readable? Does it matter which translation you read?

Question 3: Does it matter if the creation story in Genesis isn't scientifically accurate?

Science says the universe is 13.8 billion years old. Genesis says creation was 6 days. Can both be true? Does Genesis have to be scientifically accurate to be spiritually true?

Question 4: Who makes the better case?

A Christian says "the Bible is all I need for guidance." A Humanist says "I can find guidance through reason, ethics, and science without any sacred text." Who do you find more convincing, and why?

The Big Question

"The Bible is the most important book ever written."

Decide: Do you agree or disagree? Then explore both sides.

Arguments FOR (Why someone might agree):

  • The Bible has shaped Western civilization more than any other book. Laws, ethics, art, literature—all influenced by Christianity.
  • Over 2 billion Christians believe it's the word of God. Its influence on human life is enormous.
  • It's been translated into nearly every language and is the world's best-selling book. No other book has this reach.
  • For believers, it's not just important—it's the foundation of meaning, ethics, and relationship with God.

Arguments AGAINST (Why someone might disagree):

  • "Most important" is subjective. Is the Bible more important than science (which changed how we understand reality)? Philosophy? Literature?
  • The Bible has also been used to justify slavery, oppression of women, and violence. Its influence isn't always positive.
  • Most of the world (5+ billion people) doesn't follow Christianity. For them, the Qur'an, Torah, Vedas, or other texts are more important.
  • We can learn wisdom and meaning from many sources—the Bible is one of many, not uniquely supreme.
Your turn: Write 3-4 sentences about which side is more convincing to you, and why.
