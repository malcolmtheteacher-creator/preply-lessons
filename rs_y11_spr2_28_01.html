Peace, Justice, and Reconciliation

Year 11 — Unit 28: Themes D & E

What You'll Learn

Understand Christian and Muslim approaches to peace, justice, and reconciliation. Explore peacemaking figures, scriptural foundations, and the role of forgiveness in conflict resolution.

What's the difference between peace and justice?

Answer: Peace is the absence of conflict; justice requires addressing wrongs. True reconciliation needs both.


Name one religious peacemaker.

Answer: Quaker peace activists, Desmond Tutu (South Africa), Martin Luther King Jr., or Muslim leaders like Ali Shariati.


What does 'pacifism' mean?

Answer: Belief that violence is morally wrong; refusal to participate in war, even in self-defence.

Christian Views on Peacemaking

The Beatitudes: Matthew 5:9

"Blessed are the peacemakers, for they will be called children of God."

Jesus elevates peacemaking to a spiritual virtue. The Beatitudes present peace not as passive avoidance of conflict, but as active work towards reconciliation—this is a central Christian ethical principle.

Quaker Peace Testimony

Founded in the 1650s, the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers) rejected all forms of violence and warfare. They are known historically for: - Absolute pacifism and conscientious objection - Peace activism and humanitarian work - Running peace organisations and conflict resolution programs - Belief that God (the "Inner Light") speaks to all people, making war unjust

Just War Theory (Aquinas)

Thomas Aquinas developed Just War Theory criteria (13th century): - Just cause (e.g., self-defence) - Legitimate authority (state, not individuals) - Right intention (not revenge, greed, or power) - Last resort (all peaceful options exhausted) - Probability of success - Proportionality (outcome justifies means) - Discrimination (protect civilians) Aquinas argued some wars could be morally justified.

Muslim Views on Peacemaking

Surah 8:61 (The Negotiation)

"And if they incline towards peace, then incline towards it [also] and rely upon Allah."

The Qur'an teaches that Muslims must accept and pursue peace whenever possible. This is not weakness but obedience to Allah's will.

Greater and Lesser Jihad

Greater Jihad: The internal spiritual struggle against evil desires, ignorance, and sin. This is the primary form of jihad emphasized in Islamic teaching.

Lesser Jihad: Physical struggle, including military defence. Permitted only: - In self-defence against aggression - To protect the Islamic community - Under the authority of a just leader - With strict rules protecting civilians

Many Islamic scholars argue that Lesser Jihad has become a misunderstood term, often mistranslated as "holy war" in Western contexts.

Forgiveness and Reconciliation

The Lord's Prayer (Christian)

"Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors." - Forgiveness is not optional; it's central to Christian ethics - Jesus taught radical forgiveness: "Forgive seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22) - Forgiveness doesn't erase consequences but removes bitterness - Enables healing and restoration of relationships - Modelled by Jesus forgiving those who crucified him (Luke 23:34)

Surah 42:40 (Islamic)

"The recompense for an injury is an injury equal to it: but those that forgive and make amends will have their reward from Allah." - Forgiveness is presented as superior to retaliation - Shows trust in Allah's justice - Breaks cycles of violence - Encourages reconciliation - Many Islamic cases emphasize forgiveness (e.g., Surah 9:102)

Case Study: Desmond Tutu and the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (South Africa)

After apartheid ended (1994), South Africa faced a choice: punitive justice or reconciliation. Archbishop Desmond Tutu led the TRC, which prioritized: - Truth-telling: victims and perpetrators shared their stories - Accountability without vengeance - Forgiveness and healing over retribution - Community restoration rather than individual punishment

Key insight: Religious leaders can broker peace when secular justice fails. Tutu's Christian theology of Ubuntu ("I am because we are") shaped a new model of justice.

Summary: Peace, Justice, and Reconciliation

Christian perspectives: Emphasize peacemaking as a spiritual calling. Quakers practice absolute pacifism; mainstream Christianity uses Just War framework. Jesus taught radical forgiveness. Churches are key agents of reconciliation.

Muslim perspectives: Prioritize peace over conflict (Surah 8:61). Greater Jihad (spiritual struggle) is primary. Lesser Jihad (military defence) is justified only in specific circumstances. Forgiveness honoured above vengeance (Surah 42:40).

Common ground: Both traditions value peace, forgiveness, and justice. Both recognize conditions under which conflict may be justified. Both see reconciliation as healing.
