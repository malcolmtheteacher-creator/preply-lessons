Defending the Creative Work

C1 Level · Advertising · Client Reviews

Advertising · Dewey 659.1

First Thoughts

You have spent six weeks on the campaign. The team has poured themselves into it. You present it to the client, and twenty minutes in, the marketing director says flatly: “I’m not feeling it. Can we see something safer?”

You have two bad options: cave immediately, or dig in and fight. Neither wins you this account or this campaign. The third option — the one experienced creatives reach for — is to defend the work intelligently, not defensively.

Take a moment with each question below. Share as much detail as you like — specific examples, what you would actually say, what tripped you up last time.

1. What is the difference between defending creative work and protecting your ego? How do you know which one you’re doing in the moment?

2. Why do clients often ask for ‘something safer’ — and what do they actually mean when they say it?

3. When should a creative fold on an idea, and when should they fight for it? Where is your personal line?

4. What is the real cost of agreeing to a weaker idea just to keep the room calm?

Quick opener — pick whichever you prefer:

  • The client has just said “I’m not feeling it.” What is your first sentence — before you try to change their mind?
  • A creative director once said: “The work doesn’t need defending. It needs explaining.” What did they mean?

Key Language

Useful chunks of language for this conversation. Read each phrase aloud and think about when you would actually reach for it.

Catching the feedback — before responding

  • “Thank you for being straight with us — I’d rather you said that now than three weeks from now.”
  • “Before I react to that, can I ask what specifically is making you hesitate?”
  • “Help me understand whether that’s a gut reaction or a strategic concern — both are valid, but we’d defend them differently.”

Going back to the brief

  • “Can we just go back to the brief for a moment? The problem we agreed to solve was standing out in a very crowded category.”
  • “The insight we built this on was that your audience is tired of being shouted at — this idea is a deliberate answer to that.”
  • “If we go safer, we risk solving a different problem than the one the brief set us.”

Defending the work without arrogance

  • “I want to be upfront: this is the work I believe has the best chance of actually moving the numbers you care about.”
  • “I’m not asking you to love every frame. I’m asking you to look at whether it does the job the brief asked for.”
  • “Nothing in this idea is there for our ego — I’d happily cut anything that isn’t earning its keep.”

Finding a third way

  • “Let me propose a middle path — we keep the core idea, and we stress-test two moments you’re uncomfortable with.”
  • “Before we walk away from this route, can we test it with a small group this week? That’s less expensive than replacing it.”
  • “What if we parked the ‘safer’ version as an option B, and spent our energy making option A undeniable?”

The Scenario

The situation: The main conference room at the client’s HQ. You are showing the creative for a new campaign to the CMO, the brand director, and two others. Twenty minutes in, the CMO says, calmly: “I’m not feeling it. Can we see something safer?” The room goes quiet. Your creative director looks at you.

Your task: Respond to the CMO in a way that buys the idea another genuine chance, without looking defensive, desperate, or rude. By the end of the exchange, she should feel heard, and the work should still be on the table.

Your response should include:

Step 1 · Plan your response

Use the timer. Make notes — do not write full sentences. Think about what you will say first.

Strategy time: 10:00

Step 2 · Deliver

When you are ready, role-play the conversation out loud. Be ready to handle the follow-up questions below.

Follow-up questions to handle:

  • My CEO will hate this. I know him — I know what he’ll say.
  • Can’t you just show me a version without the risky bit?
  • We paid a lot of money for this. Why do I have to argue for it?
  • If I sign this off and it tanks, what happens to me?

Reflection

The most important part. What did you notice, what surprised you, what will you try differently next week?

1. Which follow-up was the hardest to answer without sounding defensive?

2. Where in the conversation did you want to protect your team rather than the idea? What did you say instead?

3. What is the difference between a creative who defends work well and one who simply argues harder?

4. If you had to teach a junior creative one rule about client reviews, what would it be?
