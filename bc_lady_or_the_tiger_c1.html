The Lady, or the Tiger?

A 140-year-old story with no ending — and the grammar that lets you argue for one

Adapted from the short story by Frank R. Stockton (1882)

Before we open any doors

In a moment, you'll read a story that's almost a hundred and forty years old, by an American writer called Frank Stockton. It runs to about four hundred words and it has no ending. None. The author deliberately stops at the moment of the decision and turns the question back to the reader. People have been arguing about it ever since — in classrooms, in newspapers, at dinner tables — and there is, genuinely, no right answer. Before we start, let's warm up the part of you that does the arguing.

Speak · first questions

Two or three full sentences each. We're priming the muscle, not racing.

  1. Imagine you discovered that the person you love has been glancing — just glancing — at someone else, and that other person has been glancing back. Nothing has happened. What does your reaction look like, honestly, in the first three days?
  2. Is it possible to genuinely love someone and, at the same time, want them not to exist in the same room as a rival? Or does jealousy automatically mean the love is failing?
  3. Some legal systems put the accused person in front of a jury of strangers. Some used to put them in front of a fight, a trial of fire, a duel. Has the human instinct behind those old systems actually disappeared, or has it just changed clothes?
  4. If someone you loved was about to make a life-changing decision and you alone knew which choice would destroy them — would you tell them? Always? Even if telling them would also destroy you?
Speak · hot takes

Each of these is a single sentence. Pick the one that bites you most and respond — agree, push back, complicate. Then pick the one you find easiest to dismiss and try to take it seriously for sixty seconds.

1. A king who calls this "justice" is calling it "entertainment" and hoping nobody notices.
2. Whether she sent him to the lady or to the tiger, she made the decision for him — that's the real cruelty of the story.
3. The story isn't really about the princess at all; it's about what every reader instantly assumes about love.
4. A door that hides a tiger and a door that hides a wife are not as different as the king pretends.
5. If you can't decide which she chose, that says more about you than it does about her.

Eight chunks to put to work

Click each card. Read it; close it; move on. Every one of these turns up in the story on the next tab, and you'll need them in the verdict at the end — you can't argue the ending without them.

semi-barbaric Half-civilised and half-savage at the same time. Used of people, customs or systems where there's a thin layer of refinement over something genuinely brutal.
to seal someone's fate To finally decide what will happen to someone — usually something serious, often irreversible. The word fate alone means: what is destined to happen.
to be smitten with someone To be suddenly and helplessly in love. Stronger and more literary than "to fall for someone" — it sounds like the feeling has hit you like a blow.
a rival Someone competing with you for the same thing — a job, a prize, a person's love. The word always implies a contest that someone will eventually lose.
a well-placed bribe Money or a favour given to exactly the right person at exactly the right moment, to make them do something they shouldn't. "Well-placed" is the give-away word: it implies skill, not desperation.
a flicker of jealousy A brief, almost involuntary appearance of the feeling. A flicker of works with any sudden small emotion — a flicker of doubt, of recognition, of fear.
to give the slightest signal To communicate something silently with a tiny gesture — a glance, the smallest movement of a hand — that almost nobody else would notice.
unflinching Showing no hesitation, fear or doubt in a moment when most people would. Often used approvingly — but it can also be a cold word, depending on context.
Speak · rapid-fire drill

Your teacher reads each prompt aloud. Answer in one full sentence, using at least one of the chunks above. Don't write. Move quickly; rhythm matters more than the perfect answer.

  1. "Tell me about a custom in your own country that an outsider might call semi-barbaric."
  2. "Describe a moment from your life when you felt smitten with something — a person, a place, an idea."
  3. "Give me an example of a well-placed bribe — from history, from fiction, or from anything you've heard."
  4. "Describe a moment when you noticed a flicker of jealousy in yourself and were surprised by it."
  5. "Tell me about someone you've watched make an unflinching decision — and whether you admired them or were quietly disturbed."
  6. "Finish this: the most expensive signal I ever gave someone was…"

The story — read it aloud, slowly

About four hundred words. Read it as if you were reading it to someone who would only hear it once. Pause at every comma. Let the last line sit in the air before you move on.

In the very olden time, there lived a king whose ideas were semi-barbaric — partly civilised, partly savage. When one of his subjects was accused of a serious crime, the man's fate was sealed in the king's arena. The accused was led in, alone, and made to stand before two identical doors. Behind one waited a fierce tiger, the most savage that could be found. If the man opened that door, the tiger leapt out and tore him to pieces. Behind the other stood a beautiful young woman, the best the court could find. If he opened that door, she was given to him in marriage on the spot — whether he was already married or not. This was the king's idea of perfect justice, and he thought it magnificent.

This king had a daughter — beloved, beautiful, and as headstrong as her father. She had become smitten with a young man at court: handsome, brave, and far below her station. He returned her love. When the king discovered the affair, he was furious, and immediately sent the young man to the arena. The whole kingdom turned out to watch.

The princess had not been idle. By force of will and by a series of well-placed bribes, she had discovered which of the two doors hid the lady, and which the tiger. More than that — she knew the lady. She knew her to be the most beautiful woman of the court, and she had once seen her young man glance at this rival, and the rival glance back. Even now, remembering it, the briefest flicker of jealousy passed across her face.

The young man stepped into the arena and turned, as everyone always did, towards the princess in the gallery. Their eyes met. With the slightest signal of her hand, almost too small for the watching crowd to see, she pointed to the door on the right. He went to it, unflinching, and laid his hand upon the latch.

And here is the point of the story. Did the tiger come out of that door — or did the lady? The princess had thought about it for many days and many nights. To send him to his death was unbearable. But to watch him married to her rival, in front of the cheering crowd — that was unbearable too. Some have called her the most loving daughter of her time; others, the most jealous woman who ever lived. Which would you, in her place, have chosen — the lady, or the tiger?

— adapted from Frank R. Stockton, The Lady, or the Tiger? (1882)

Phrasing · read aloud at funeral pace. The story works on rhythm; rush it and the ending lands flat.

1. What does the king's idea of "perfect justice" tell us about him as a ruler?

Show Answer
He is powerful, theatrical and a little lazy all at once — he lets fate decide instead of judging, and he treats trials as public entertainment. The word "semi-barbaric" at the start is the writer's quiet warning: this is a man who dresses brutality up in fine language. Everything he does later in the story should be read through that lens, including how he treats his own daughter.

2. Why does the writer tell us, twice, that the princess "knew the lady"?

Show Answer
Because everything depends on this detail. If the lady were a stranger, the princess might more easily choose the door of the lady — survival above all. But she's not a stranger. She's the most beautiful woman of the court. She has been glanced at, and has glanced back. The princess is being asked to choose between her lover's death and her lover's life in someone else's arms. The repetition forces us to feel the weight of that.

3. The young man goes "unflinching" to the door. Why does the writer choose that word, rather than "obedient" or "trusting"?

Show Answer
Because "unflinching" cuts both ways. It can mean total faith in the princess (he doesn't hesitate because he trusts her completely). But it can also mean something colder — that he had already decided to walk through whichever door she pointed at, lady or tiger, because the alternative was unbearable to him too. The single word holds both readings; it's part of why the ending stays open.
Speak · first reactions to the ending

No writing. Two or three minutes per question. Don't commit to your final answer yet — we'll do that properly after the grammar tab.

  1. Your gut says: tiger or lady? Just your instinct, before you think. Say it out loud.
  2. What single line in the story made up your mind — or hardened a feeling you already had?
  3. If the writer had chosen any ending, the story would be far less famous than it is. Why is the silence at the end so much more powerful than a written conclusion?

Two grammars — the only honest tools for arguing this story

You're about to defend a verdict in a story whose answer no one knows. That puts you in a very specific grammatical position. You are speculating about a past event you cannot verify, and you are imagining alternative pasts that would have led to different presents. English has two purpose-built tools for exactly this work. Get them clean and your argument will sound like a barrister's. Get them sloppy and it'll sound like a guess in the dark.

Tool 1 — Speculation modals (past)

Use these when you are guessing about something that already happened. The auxiliary have is what carries the past.

The form:   modal  +  have  +  past participle.   Always have, never has.   Not "must known", not "might did" — always must have known, might have done.

must have — I'm sure (positive)

She must have hated the lady — that's why we get the flicker of jealousy.

He must have trusted her completely to walk to that door without hesitating.

can't have / couldn't have — I'm sure (negative)

She can't have signalled the lady's door — her jealousy was too strong.

He couldn't have known for certain what was behind it.

might / may / could have — possible, not sure

She might have chosen the tiger and then regretted it her whole life.

He could have suspected the truth and decided he'd rather die than be married off.

Tool 2 — Third and mixed conditionals

Use these when you want to imagine an alternative past — either a past that would have led to a different past, or a past that would have led to a different present, or a different present-self who would have acted differently in the past.

Third conditional — past condition, past result:   If she had chosen the lady, he would have lived.
Mixed (A) — past condition, present result:   If she had chosen the lady, he would be someone else's husband right now.
Mixed (B) — present condition, past result:   If she were truly heartless, she would have signalled the tiger without thinking.
Speak · oral transformation drill

Your teacher reads each flat sentence. You reformulate it on the spot, aloud, using the structure indicated. Don't write — the answers are hidden below. Listen for the rhythm.

1. Flat: "I'm sure she chose the lady, because she loved him too much."   →   use must have

Show Answer
She must have chosen the lady, because she loved him too much.

2. Flat: "I'm sure she didn't choose at random — she'd thought about it for weeks."   →   use can't have

Show Answer
She can't have chosen at random — she'd thought about it for weeks.

3. Flat: "Maybe she signalled the tiger and then immediately regretted it."   →   use might have

Show Answer
She might have signalled the tiger and then immediately regretted it.

4. Flat: "She didn't tell him the truth about the doors. That's why he walked unflinchingly to the right."   →   rebuild as a third conditional (past condition, past result)

Show Answer
If she had told him the truth, he wouldn't have walked unflinchingly to the right.

5. Flat: "She isn't completely heartless — that's why she didn't simply send him to the tiger."   →   rebuild as a mixed conditional (present condition, past result)

Show Answer
If she were completely heartless, she would have simply sent him to the tiger.

6. Flat: "He didn't escape. That's why people are still talking about him today."   →   rebuild as a mixed conditional (past condition, present result)

Show Answer
If he had escaped, people wouldn't still be talking about him today.
Speak · live micro-task

Take whichever ending feels right to you — lady or tiger — and defend it for sixty seconds, out loud, using at least two speculation modals and one mixed conditional. Don't write. The grammar is the rope you climb.

The Verdict — three speaking tasks

Door on the right

The Lady

He lives. He marries her in front of the cheering crowd. The princess watches.

Door on the right

The Tiger

He dies in front of the cheering crowd. The princess watches.

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · first verdict (4 minutes)

Choose your verdict — lady or tiger — and defend it for four minutes without interruption. You must build your argument out of the text, not out of general opinions about love and jealousy. Anchor every claim in something the story actually shows us (the flicker of jealousy, the bribes, the way the lady glanced back, the word unflinching, the king's character, the princess's character).

Required grammar — this is the rope you climb. Use at least:

  1. three speculation modals in the past (must have, can't have, might have, could have);
  2. two mixed conditionals (one in each direction);
  3. four vocabulary chunks from Tab 2.

Pace yourself. You have four minutes — you should run out of time, not out of arguments.

Task B · second verdict, opposite side (4 minutes)

Now defend the opposite ending. Same rules — same grammar quota, same vocabulary quota, same instruction to anchor in the text. The discomfort of arguing against yourself is the point. Most students discover that their second argument is, in fact, slightly stronger than their first — because they've been forced to listen to what the story is actually doing, instead of what they wanted it to be doing.

End by saying which of the two arguments you yourself find harder to refute — and why.

Task C · switch chairs (3 minutes)

You are now the young man, walking towards the door. The princess has just given you the signal. You have a few seconds before you reach the latch. Speak your thoughts aloud, in the first person, in those few seconds. What do you suspect? What do you hope? What have you already decided, regardless of what's behind the door?

Use mixed conditionals at least twice (if she really loved me, she would have…  /  if I weren't already half-resigned to death, I wouldn't be walking this calmly…) and speculation modals at least twice (she must have wanted…).

Final open discussion

  1. If you had to teach this story to a teenager who had never met it before, what would you make sure they noticed — and what would you deliberately not tell them?
  2. Stockton wrote this in 1882 and people have been arguing about it ever since. What is it about the human mind that finds an unfinished story more satisfying, in the long run, than a finished one?
  3. Imagine a sequel — just one sentence long — that closes the story. Write it in your head. Then explain why writing that sentence would, in fact, ruin the story.
  4. Which character in the story do you find yourself thinking about for longest after the lesson ends — the king, the princess, the young man, the lady? Why that one, and not the others?
