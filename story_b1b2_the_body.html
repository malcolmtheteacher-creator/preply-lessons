The Body

Bill Bryson writes funny travel books. He is not a scientist. In 2019 he wrote a book about the one place he has lived in all his life — his own body. Again and again, the experts told him the same thing: “We don’t know.”

A. Guess the word from the sentence

Read the sentence. What does the word in bold mean? Say it in your own words, then reveal.

1. The new occupant of the flat moved in on Monday. She still hasn’t met the neighbours.
occupant (n) — a person who lives in a place or is using it, like a house, a room or a seat. Example: "Every occupant of the building got a letter about the broken lift."
2. Flour, eggs, butter and sugar are the main ingredients of most cakes.
ingredient (n) — one of the things you mix together to make a food, or to make something else. Example: "This soup has only four ingredients."
3. It was an ordinary Tuesday: work, dinner, a little television, bed. Nothing special happened.
ordinary (adj) — normal; not special or different in any way. Example: "The carbon in your body is the same as the carbon in a cheap pencil — completely ordinary."
4. The table came flat in a box. It took me two hours to put it together.
put together (phrasal v) — to join all the parts of something so that it is complete and works. Example: "He had every piece of the old clock, but he couldn’t put it together again."
5. The liver is a large organ inside your body. Its job is to clean your blood.
organ (n) — a part of the body that has its own special job, like the heart, the lungs or the brain. Example: "The skin is the biggest organ of the human body."
6. When the traffic light turns green, it is a signal that you can drive on.
signal (n) — a light, sound or message that tells someone something. Example: "Your eyes send tiny electrical signals to your brain."

The story, in three parts

A real book, told in three parts: the shopping bill, the dark room, and the things nobody knows. Read one part. Then answer the quick check from memory, and talk about the question.

Part 1 — The shopping bill

Bill Bryson is an American writer. He was born in Iowa in 1951, and he has lived most of his adult life in Britain. He is famous for funny travel books. He is not a scientist, and he is not a doctor. But in 2019 he wrote a book about the human body. He called it The Body: A Guide for Occupants. The little joke in the title is this: every one of us lives inside a body every day of our lives — but most of us know almost nothing about it. Can you say where your spleen is, or what it does? Most people can’t.

The book starts with a shopping bill. In 2013 a group of British scientists, the Royal Society of Chemistry, asked a fun question: how much would it cost to buy all the ingredients for one human being? (They used the actor Benedict Cumberbatch as their model.) A body needs fifty-nine elements — simple materials like carbon, oxygen and iron. Just six of them make up more than ninety-nine per cent of you. The others are there only in very, very small amounts. Oxygen is most of your weight, and it costs almost nothing. Pure carbon is the expensive part. The total bill was a little under £97,000. A year earlier, an American TV programme did the same sum and got a very different answer: about $168.

But Bryson says the price doesn’t matter. You can buy every ingredient and pay the best scientists in the world, and you still can’t put a person together. The ingredients are ordinary — the same tiny pieces, or atoms, that you find in a piece of coal or a glass of water. There are about seven billion billion billion atoms in you. Not one of them is alive. Not one of them knows that you exist. Nobody is in charge of them. And yet, for your whole life, they stay together and make you.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is Bill Bryson, and why is it surprising that he wrote a book about the body?

2. What did the British scientists work out in 2013, and what was their answer?

3. Why does Bryson say the price doesn’t matter?

1. He is an American writer who lives in Britain and writes funny travel books. It is surprising because he is not a scientist or a doctor — he is an ordinary “occupant” of a body, like us.

2. They worked out the cost of all the ingredients (the fifty-nine elements) for one human body. The answer was a little under £97,000. (An American TV programme said about $168.)

3. Because even with all the ingredients and the best scientists, nobody can put a person together. The atoms are ordinary and not alive — the amazing thing is that they make a living person.

Discuss:
  • Was your number closer to £97,000 or to $168? Does it surprise you that you are made of very ordinary things? Does that make the body seem less special to you — or more?

Part 2 — The dark room

Of all the organs in the body, the one that amazes Bryson most is the brain. It doesn’t look special. It weighs a little less than one and a half kilos, and three-quarters of it or more is water. It is only about two per cent of your weight, but it uses about twenty per cent of your energy. Even so, it doesn’t need much food: about four hundred calories a day — about the same as one blueberry muffin. With that, it does more than any computer ever built.

What really amazes Bryson is where the brain works. It sits inside your head, in the dark and in silence. It has never seen light. It has never heard a sound. It can’t even feel pain itself: a doctor can touch the brain and the patient feels nothing. The only things that reach it are tiny electrical signals from your eyes, ears, nose and skin. From these signals, and nothing else, the brain makes your whole world — the blue sky, the smell of coffee, the voice of someone you love. The colour blue is not really “out there”. Light comes into your eye, and your brain turns it into blue.

The brain also has to cheat a little with time. Signals from your eyes need a short moment to arrive — by some estimates, about a fifth of a second. If the brain simply showed you what arrived, you would always live a little bit in the past, and you could never catch a ball. So the brain predicts. What you feel as “now” is really the brain’s best guess about what the world looks like at this moment. It does this so well that you have never noticed.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Say two numbers about the brain (its weight, its water, its energy or its food).

2. Why does Bryson say the brain works in a “dark room”?

3. Why does the brain need to predict?

1. Any two: a little less than 1.5 kilos; three-quarters or more is water; about 2% of your weight; about 20% of your energy; about 400 calories a day, like one blueberry muffin.

2. Because it is inside the head, in total darkness and silence. It never sees light or hears sound. It only gets electrical signals, and from those it makes everything we see, hear and smell.

3. Because signals from the eyes take about a fifth of a second to arrive. Without predicting, we would always live a little in the past and could never catch a ball. So “now” is the brain’s best guess.

Discuss:
  • If your brain makes the colour blue, do you and your friend see exactly the same blue? Can we ever check? Does it matter?

Part 3 — What nobody knows

Bryson spent years on this book. He read the research, and he asked many doctors and scientists his questions. He was surprised how often the answer was: “We don’t know.”

Take the yawn. Everybody yawns, and when one person yawns, the people near them often start yawning too. But nobody knows for sure what yawning is for — and it isn’t even closely connected to being tired. Or take the chin. Humans are the only apes with a chin, and nobody knows why. As far as we can tell, we are the only animals that cry because of our feelings — and why we do it is still a mystery.

There is more. About nine people in ten are right-handed, in every country that has been studied, and nobody knows why. We sleep for about a third of our lives. We know we can’t live without sleep, but scientists still don’t agree on what sleep is really for. These questions puzzle scientists not because nobody has looked, but because the body is much stranger than the simple pictures in school books.

None of this makes Bryson sad. What amazes him is how much goes right. Your heart beats about a hundred thousand times a day — about three billion times in a long life — and you never have to remember to do it. You blink about fourteen thousand times a day. Every second, without asking you, your body is building, repairing and protecting itself. We live inside the most amazing thing we will ever see. And how do most of us look after it? Bryson jokes: with crisps and a sofa. His book asks us to look at the place where we live — and to be amazed that it works at all.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Name three things about the body that nobody can fully explain.

2. Does all this make Bryson sad? What amazes him most?

1. Any three: what yawning is for; why humans have a chin; why we cry because of our feelings; why most people are right-handed; what sleep is really for.

2. No. He is amazed at how much goes right without any work from us: the heart beats about 100,000 times a day, we blink about 14,000 times a day, and the body is always building, repairing and protecting itself.

Discuss:
  • Would you prefer a doctor who sometimes says “Nobody knows”, or a doctor who always has an answer? Why?

Grammar: saying what we don’t know

Part 3 of the story is full of things nobody knows. In English there are three simple ways to say this clearly. Learn one at a time: first read the examples, then try the practice.

1. Nobody knows why…

The idea: when you put a question after Nobody knows… or I don’t know…, it is not a question any more. So the words go back to normal order: the person or thing first, then the verb. There is no do, does or did, and no question mark.
Where is my phone? → I don’t know where my phone is.
What does this word mean? → I don’t know what this word means.
Why did the bus stop? → Nobody knows why the bus stopped.

Look at the last two: does and did disappear, and the verb changes instead (means, stopped).

Your turn. Change each question into a sentence that starts Nobody knows… Say it out loud, then reveal.

1. Why do we yawn?

2. Why do humans have chins?

3. What is sleep for?

4. Why do most people use their right hand?

5. How does the brain make the colour blue?

1. Nobody knows why we yawn.

2. Nobody knows why humans have chins.

3. Nobody knows what sleep is for.

4. Nobody knows why most people use their right hand.

5. Nobody knows exactly how the brain makes the colour blue.

2. still and yet

The idea: both words mean “not up to now — but maybe later”. The difference is where they go.
• still goes before don’t / doesn’t / can’t: “I still don’t know.” “He still can’t swim.”
• yet goes at the end, usually with haven’t / hasn’t: “I haven’t finished yet.” “The pizza hasn’t arrived yet.”
We waited an hour, but the train still didn’t come.
My brother hasn’t called me back yet.

Your turn. Write still or yet. Look at where the gap is — that tells you.

1. Scientists don’t know why we cry.

2. Nobody has explained the human chin .

3. We don’t really understand what sleep is for.

4. Doctors haven’t found a cure for the common cold .

5. Bryson asked the experts about yawning, but they couldn’t tell him.

1. still (before don’t)

2. yet (at the end, with has)

3. still (before don’t)

4. yet (at the end, with haven’t)

5. still (before couldn’t)

3. A fact or a guess? is or might

The idea: when scientists have measured something, say it plainly: “The brain weighs about 1.4 kilos.” When it is only an idea that nobody is sure about, use might + the verb: “Yawning might cool the brain.”
After might: no to, and no -s. Say it might help, not it might to help or it might helps.
A factIt rains a lot in Manchester. (People have measured it for years.)
A guessIt might rain tomorrow. (Nobody is sure.)

Your turn. Choose (a) or (b). Think: did the story give this as a fact, or is it only an idea? Say your answer, then reveal.

1. Yawning (a) cools (b) might cool the brain — scientists are still testing this idea.

2. The brain (a) is (b) might be about two per cent of your body weight.

3. About nine people in ten (a) are (b) might be right-handed.

4. Crying (a) is (b) might be a way of showing other people that we need help — nobody is sure.

5. A human body (a) needs (b) might need fifty-nine elements.

1. (b) might cool — it is only an idea.

2. (a) is — it has been measured.

3. (a) are — it has been counted in many countries.

4. (b) might be — it is only an idea.

5. (a) needs — the scientists counted them.

Put all three together — about your own life

Think of something small in your life that nobody can explain — a strange habit of a pet, a noise in your home, something a friend always does. Say three sentences: one with Nobody knows why…, one with still or yet, and one with might.

Example: Nobody in my family knows why our dog barks at the fridge. We still don’t understand it, even after three years. It might be the noise the fridge makes at night.

Why this book works so well

There are hundreds of books about the human body, and most of them are by doctors and scientists. So why did millions of people read this one? Because Bryson is like the reader. He doesn’t know the answers either. He asks the questions we would ask, and when a professor tells him something amazing, he is amazed — and so are we.

Bryson’s trick: a big number becomes something from everyday life

Huge numbers are hard to feel. So Bryson changes them into a picture you know. It works in three steps:

1. The numberThe brain uses about 400 calories a day.
2. The everyday pictureThat’s about one blueberry muffin.
3. The feelingOne muffin a day — and it does more than any computer!

Your turn. Change each number into an everyday picture. Say it out loud first, then reveal one possible answer. (There are many good answers.)

1. Your heart beats about a hundred thousand times a day.

That’s more than once every second, all day and all night — even while you sleep, and even right now.

2. We sleep for about a third of our lives.

If you live to 90, that’s about 30 years in bed — longer than most people stay in one job.

3. You blink about fourteen thousand times a day.

Each blink is very short, but together they add up to many minutes a day with your eyes closed — and you never notice.

The title: your body as a home you rent

A Guide for Occupants makes your body sound like a flat, and you like a tenant — a person who rents a home. A tenant didn’t build the place. A tenant can’t see the plans. And one day, a tenant has to leave. Usually we say “my body”, as if we own it. Which way of talking feels truer to you?
