The Medici

The banking family from Florence whose money helped buy the Renaissance — and whose power has echoed through art ever since.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of a merchant family who turned a fortune in banking into some of the greatest art the world has ever seen — and you'll master the difference between finished actions (they built…, they funded…) and their living legacy (their money has shaped…).
Warm up — talk before we read.
  • Can money buy great art? The Medici were bankers who paid for some of the most famous paintings and buildings ever made. Is a rich patron good or bad for art?
  • Think of a wealthy family or company today that funds museums, sport or science. What do they hope to get back in return?
  • The Medici were never kings, yet they ran Florence for generations from behind the scenes. How can a family hold real power without a crown?

Now — the words for today's story

Ten words you'll meet in the story, in four short activities. Do the thinking first — say your answer out loud — then reveal only to check how close you were.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean here?

The situation in each sentence gives you a strong clue. Say what you think the word means, then reveal.

1. The family lent to kings and popes, held their gold in vaults across Europe, and charged for every loan; a mastery of finance made them the richest name in Florence.
finance (n) — the management of large sums of money, especially by banks, governments or companies; high-level dealing in loans, credit and capital.
2. The young painter had no money for paint or a workshop, until a rich man agreed to cover every cost so the work could be made. That man was his patron.
a patron (n) — a wealthy person who gives money and support to an artist, writer or cause.
3. Year after year the family paid for statues, frescoes, libraries and domes right across the city; nothing beautiful in Florence was built without their patronage.
patronage (n) — the money and support a patron gives; the act of funding art, artists or institutions.

4. Which two-word Latin phrase means "an artist's single greatest work — the crowning achievement of a whole career"? It starts with M___ o___.
a magnum opus (n) — the greatest and most important work an artist, writer or composer ever produces; their crowning achievement. Example: Michelangelo's ceiling for the Sistine Chapel is often called his magnum opus.
5. Which word means "rule by a small, privileged group — a few powerful families who really control a state, whatever its official form"? It starts with O___.
an oligarchy (n) — a state or system in which power rests with a small, dominant group, often a handful of wealthy families. Example: for all its republican pride, Florence was in practice an oligarchy.

the Renaissance (n) — the "rebirth" of art and learning in Europe, roughly the 14th–16th centuries. Example: Florence is called the birthplace of the Renaissance.

8. Is a feud between two families closer to a long, bitter quarrel, or to a warm friendship?
a feud (n) — a long, bitter quarrel between families or groups, often lasting years. The opposite of a friendship or alliance.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — Money, and the man who used it well

The Medici did not begin as princes. They began as merchants and money-lenders in Florence, a busy, ambitious republic in northern Italy. Their real fortune was built by Giovanni di Bicci de' Medici in the late 1300s and early 1400s, when the Medici Bank grew into one of the most powerful in Europe. It handled the finances of the Church itself, becoming banker to the popes — a position that brought both enormous profit and enormous influence.

Giovanni's son, Cosimo de' Medici, turned that wealth into quiet political control. Florence had no king; it was proud of being a republic, governed by its citizens. Cosimo held no crown and, for much of his life, no grand official title. Yet through loans, favours, friendships and careful patronage, he became the man who really decided things. He was clever enough to rule without ever seeming to rule — a true statesman.

Cosimo also understood something new: that money could buy more than power. It could buy beauty, and beauty could buy immortality. He poured funds into libraries, churches and scholars, and he paid the architect Filippo Brunelleschi to complete the vast dome of Florence's cathedral — an engineering marvel that still crowns the city's skyline today.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the Medici family first make their fortune, and who was their most important client?

2. How did Cosimo control Florence without being its king?

1. Through banking and money-lending; the Medici Bank became banker to the Church and the popes, which brought both profit and influence.

2. Florence was a republic with no king; Cosimo used loans, favours, friendships and patronage to become the real decision-maker without holding a crown.

Discuss:
  • Cosimo ruled without a crown or an official title. Is quiet, behind-the-scenes power stronger or weaker than open, official power?

Part 2 — Lorenzo the Magnificent, and a knife in the cathedral

Cosimo's grandson, Lorenzo de' Medici, took the family to its dazzling height. Charming, learned and a poet himself, he became known as Lorenzo il Magnifico — "the Magnificent." Under him, Florence became the beating heart of the Renaissance. He gathered artists and thinkers around him, and a talented boy called Michelangelo lived for a time almost as a member of the household. Sandro Botticelli, whose Birth of Venus is now world-famous, worked within the circle of Medici money and taste.

But wealth breeds rivals. A powerful rival family, the Pazzi, resented Medici dominance and conspired to destroy it. On a spring Sunday in 1478, during Mass in Florence's cathedral, assassins attacked Lorenzo and his younger brother Giuliano. Giuliano was killed; Lorenzo, wounded, fought his way to safety. The Pazzi plot collapsed, and Florence's fury fell hard upon the conspirators. Lorenzo emerged more powerful than before.

For all his cultural brilliance, Lorenzo was a shrewd political operator who kept peace among Italy's warring states through skilful diplomacy. Yet the bank that had built the family began to weaken under his rule — he spent lavishly and paid it too little attention. The Medici, it turned out, were now more devoted to art and politics than to counting coins.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who was Lorenzo the Magnificent, and which artists were connected to his circle?

2. What happened during the Pazzi plot of 1478?

1. Cosimo's grandson, the poet-statesman who led Florence at the height of the Renaissance; the young Michelangelo lived almost in his household, and Botticelli worked within the Medici circle.

2. The rival Pazzi family conspired to kill the Medici brothers during Mass; Giuliano was murdered, but Lorenzo escaped wounded and came out stronger, while the plotters were punished.

Discuss:
  • The Medici funded genius but neglected their own bank. Can a family — or a country — pour so much into culture that it forgets what pays for it?

Part 3 — Popes, crowns, and a lasting echo

The family's ambition soon reached beyond Florence to Rome itself. In time, the Medici gave the Church four popes — among them Lorenzo's own son, who became Pope Leo X. As popes, the Medici carried their love of art onto the grandest stage of all, funding work in Rome during the years when Michelangelo painted the Sistine Chapel and Raphael decorated the Vatican. Two Medici women, Catherine and Marie, even became queens of France, marrying into one of Europe's most powerful royal houses.

Back home, Florence's republic finally gave way. The Medici returned as hereditary rulers, and later became Grand Dukes of Tuscany — no longer hidden powers, but crowned princes. The dynasty ruled, on and off, for some three centuries, until the last of the direct Medici line died in the 1740s. Their story ended not with a bang but with a quiet extinction.

And yet the echo has never faded. The final Medici heir left the family's vast art collection to the city of Florence on the condition that it never leave — which is why the treasures of the Uffizi still draw millions of visitors each year. The Medici did not paint the masterpieces or carve the statues. But their money, their taste and their ambition helped make the Renaissance possible, and that legacy has outlasted every coin they ever lent.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the Medici extend their power beyond Florence?

2. Why can visitors to Florence still see the family's art collection today?

1. They gave the Church four popes (including Pope Leo X), married two women into the French royal family as queens, and later became hereditary rulers and Grand Dukes of Tuscany.

2. The last Medici heir left the family's art collection to the city on condition that it never leave Florence, so the treasures of the Uffizi remain there.

Discuss:
  • The Medici are remembered less for their banking than for the art their money paid for. Is that a fair way for history to judge a family?

Grammar — present perfect vs past simple

The Medici story lives in two time zones at once. There are the finished actions of five hundred years ago — they built, they funded, they ruled — and there is the living legacy that reaches right into today: their money has shaped the art we still queue to see. English uses two different tenses for these two ideas, and mixing them up is one of the most common mistakes at every level.

The two tenses, side by side:
• past simple — a finished action, at a finished time. We often say (or imply) when: "Cosimo paid for the dome." "The Pazzi attacked the brothers in 1478." "The last Medici died in the 1740s."
• present perfect (have/has + past participle) — a past action whose result still matters now, or an experience up to now, with no specific past time: "Their patronage has shaped Western art." "The collection has stayed in Florence ever since." "Millions of people have visited the Uffizi."
Feel the difference:
• Past simple is the tense of the closed story — dates, events, one thing after another: "He funded Brunelleschi and completed the dome."
• Present perfect is the tense of the still-open consequence: "That dome has crowned the city ever since." Note the time signals: in 1478, then, later → past simple; ever since, so far, already, never, for centuries → present perfect.

1. Which tense? Choose past simple or present perfect

Read the time signal in the sentence and complete it with the correct form of the verb in brackets.

1. In 1478, assassins (attack) the Medici brothers during Mass. (a finished time)

2. The Medici collection (stay) in Florence ever since. (result continues now)

3. Cosimo (pay) for the great dome of the cathedral. (a finished action long ago)

1. attacked (past simple — "in 1478" is a finished time)

2. has stayed (present perfect — "ever since", the result continues)

3. paid (past simple — a completed action in the distant past)

2. Correct the tense

Each sentence uses the wrong tense. Write the corrected verb form.

1. The Medici (given → ?) the Church four popes over the centuries. (experience up to now)

2. Lorenzo (has died → ?) in 1492. (a specific past date)

3. Their patronage (shaped → ?) Western art in ways we still feel today. (result reaches the present)

1. have given (present perfect — "over the centuries", no single finished time)

2. died (past simple — "in 1492" is a specific finished date)

3. has shaped (present perfect — the effect reaches into the present)

3. From finished to living — complete the paragraph

Fill each gap with the past simple or present perfect of the verb in brackets. Read it aloud once you're done.

Five hundred years ago, the Medici (fund) the artists of the Renaissance and (build) some of Florence's greatest buildings. Since then, their collection (remain) in the city, and millions of visitors (see) it with their own eyes.

funded (past simple) · built (past simple) · has remained (present perfect) · have seen (present perfect)

Notice the shift: the finished actions of the past (funded, built) give way to the living result that continues today (has remained, have seen).
