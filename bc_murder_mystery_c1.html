🔍 Murder at Ashworth House

C1 Level · Speaking & Critical Thinking

Setting the Scene

London, 1938. It is a cold November evening. Reginald Ashworth — the most powerful literary editor in England — has been found dead in his private study at Ashworth House. The cause of death: poison. The time of death: between 9 and 11pm. Every guest at that evening's dinner party is a suspect.

The detective has arrived. That detective is you.

Crime Language

Click each card to reveal the definition. Then try to use each word in a sentence about the case.

perpetrator The person who actually committed the crime — the guilty party
premeditated Planned in advance — the opposite of acting in the heat of the moment
circumstantial evidence Evidence that suggests guilt indirectly — it implies, but doesn't prove
alibi Proof that a suspect was elsewhere when the crime took place
implicate To suggest or show that someone is involved in a crime, even without proof
exonerate To officially clear someone of blame or suspicion entirely
incriminating Making someone appear guilty — incriminating evidence points the finger
motive The underlying reason — the "why" behind the crime

The Ashworth Case

Reginald Ashworth, 62, was discovered at 11:15pm by his butler, slumped at his writing desk. A half-drunk glass of brandy tested positive for a rare botanical poison. The study door had been locked from the inside, but the window latch was broken — it could be opened from outside.

Four guests had dined at Ashworth House that evening. All four had left the dining room at various points during the evening. All four had reason to want Reginald dead.

The Four Suspects

🖊️ Diana Whitmore

Novelist — Reginald's most famous client

Reginald had just rejected Diana's new manuscript — the fifth time in two years. In a letter discovered that morning, he had written that he intended to "publicly destroy her reputation" if she published elsewhere. Diana stood to lose everything. She says she was in the drawing room all evening, but no one can confirm this after 9:30pm.

💼 Charles Pemberton

Business Partner — co-owner of Ashworth & Pemberton Publishing

Three days before the dinner, Charles discovered that Reginald had been secretly embezzling funds from their publishing house — over £40,000 across four years. Reginald had threatened to frame Charles for the theft if he went to the police. Charles claims he spent the evening in the library and has no witness.

📜 Eleanor Voss

Secretary — employed by Reginald for eleven years

Eleanor recently found proof that Reginald had stolen her late husband's unpublished novel and published it under a pseudonym in 1931 — a book that made Reginald's fortune. She had confronted him that afternoon. Eleanor says she was unwell and retired to her guest room at 9pm, but a maid reports seeing her near the study corridor at 10:15pm.

🩺 Dr. Neville Cross

Family Physician — Reginald's doctor for twenty years

Reginald had recently discovered that Dr. Cross had been forging prescriptions and selling medication illegally. He had given the doctor one week to confess or face exposure. Crucially, Dr. Cross would have known exactly which poison to use — and how to obtain it. He claims he was playing cards with another guest until 10pm, but that guest left early and cannot be reached.

The Interrogation Room

You are Detective Inspector. The suspect is in the room. Your goal is to find inconsistencies, press on weak alibis, and extract a confession — or at least the truth.

Time remaining: 10:00

Choose Your Suspect

Pick one suspect to interrogate. Use the questions below as your starting point — but follow where the conversation leads. Push back. Don't accept weak answers.

🖊️ If you're interrogating Diana Whitmore:

• "You say you were in the drawing room all evening. Who last saw you there — and at what time exactly?"

• "Reginald threatened to destroy your career. How did that make you feel? Be honest."

• "Is it possible that, after everything he put you through, part of you is relieved he is dead?"

• "Where did you get the botanical knowledge to obtain such a specific poison?"

💼 If you're interrogating Charles Pemberton:

• "You discovered he was framing you for his own crimes. That is a powerful motive. Why should I believe you didn't act on it?"

• "The library has no external windows. How did you spend three hours alone there without touching a single book that was moved?"

• "If Reginald had exposed you, what would have happened to your life, your family, your reputation?"

• "You're a businessman. Was Reginald more valuable to you alive — or dead?"

📜 If you're interrogating Eleanor Voss:

• "A maid places you near the study at 10:15pm. You said you were in your room. Which is true?"

• "Your husband's work — stolen, profited from, and never acknowledged. How long have you been carrying that anger?"

• "You had eleven years of access to this house. You knew every room, every routine. Didn't you?"

• "Is it possible that justice, in your mind, looked very different from the law's definition of it?"

🩺 If you're interrogating Dr. Cross:

• "You are the only person in this house who would know exactly which poison to use. How do you explain that?"

• "Your alibi witness left early and is unreachable. That is a remarkable coincidence, isn't it?"

• "You have spent twenty years building a reputation. One week away from losing everything — what would you do?"

• "As a doctor, you took an oath to protect life. How does that sit with you tonight?"

The Reveal

The perpetrator was Eleanor Voss.

Eleanor had spent eleven years watching Reginald profit from her husband's stolen work. When she finally found proof, she knew Reginald would use his influence to discredit her — as he had done to others. She had obtained the botanical poison months earlier, during a trip abroad, waiting for her moment. The broken window latch was not a coincidence — Eleanor had broken it herself six weeks prior, during her regular duties. She entered the study through the window, added the poison to Reginald's brandy while he worked with his back turned, and returned to her room. The maid's sighting placed her at the corridor — but Eleanor claimed she had simply gone to fetch a shawl. A lie, but a plausible one.

The case rested on the maid's testimony, Eleanor's intimate knowledge of the house, and a receipt for an unusual botanical compound found pressed into the lining of her travelling case.

Beyond the Case

Was Eleanor morally justified in what she did?

Reginald stole her husband's life's work and profited from it for years. The law offered her no remedy. Does that change how you judge her?

The justice system is built on "innocent until proven guilty."

But in this case, almost everyone had a strong motive. Is it possible for a genuinely impartial verdict to exist when every path leads somewhere dark?

If you had been in Eleanor's position — what would you have done?

Suspect Scripts

Scripted answers for each suspect during interrogation. Each character answers honestly — but every answer quietly points toward Eleanor. Read your suspect's script before the interrogation begins.

🖊️ Diana Whitmore — Scripted Answers

"You say you were in the drawing room all evening. Who last saw you there — and at what time exactly?"

"I honestly cannot say. I was reading by the fire. Eleanor — the secretary — came in briefly around nine o'clock, I think, to bring Reginald a message. She looked rather pale. But after that I was quite alone. No one came in again until nearly eleven."

"Reginald threatened to destroy your career. How did that make you feel?"

"Furious. Frightened. But not surprised — this is the kind of man he was. Eleanor told me once, quite quietly, that he had done something similar to her husband years ago. I remember thinking how much she must hate him. Far more than I did, actually."

"Is it possible that part of you is relieved he is dead?"

"Relieved, yes. Guilty about that relief, also yes. But I didn't kill him. I write about murder. I don't commit it. You should ask Eleanor about her travel last spring — she went abroad alone for three weeks and came back very quiet. I didn't think much of it at the time."

"Where did you get the botanical knowledge to obtain such a specific poison?"

"I haven't the faintest idea what botanical poison could do such a thing. Eleanor, on the other hand — her husband was an amateur botanist. She grew up surrounded by plant lore. I always thought it rather charming. Now I'm not so sure."

💼 Charles Pemberton — Scripted Answers

"You discovered he was framing you for his own crimes. Why should I believe you didn't act on it?"

"Because I spent the evening trying to find the evidence I needed to expose him legally. I was in the library pulling contracts. But I'll tell you something — when I came through the corridor around half past nine, I saw Eleanor near the study door. She stepped back when she saw me. Said she'd forgotten something. I thought nothing of it then."

"The library has no external windows. How did you spend three hours there?"

"Documents. Financial records going back four years. I barely looked up. Though I did hear footsteps in the corridor twice — light footsteps. A woman's, I'd say. Not the butler — he has a heavier tread."

"Was Reginald more valuable to you alive — or dead?"

"Alive, if I could expose him. Dead, he takes his secrets with him — including the proof of who actually embezzled the money. Which, now I think of it, benefits Eleanor too. Reginald had told me he was going to dismiss her. Said she'd been 'asking questions she had no business asking.' I assumed it was about wages."

📜 Eleanor Voss — Scripted Answers

⚠️ Eleanor is the murderer. Play her as composed and plausible — but watch for the cracks.

"A maid places you near the study at 10:15pm. You said you were in your room. Which is true?"

"The maid is mistaken. I was unwell all evening. I may have opened my door briefly for some air — the corridor was cooler — but I did not go near the study. I had no reason to." [Press harder here — she hesitates before "no reason to"]

"Your husband's work — stolen, profited from, never acknowledged. How long have you been carrying that anger?"

"I found the proof only recently. Before that — nothing. I simply served him, as I always had." [This is almost certainly false — push her: "Only recently? You worked for this man for eleven years."]

"You knew every room, every routine. Didn't you?"

"As any secretary would. That's not sinister, that's simply my job." [Follow up: "Including the window latch on the study? The broken one?"]

"Is it possible that justice, in your mind, looked very different from the law's definition?"

"I believe in the law." [long pause] "My husband believed in the law too. It did nothing for him." [This is her most revealing answer. Let the silence sit.]

🩺 Dr. Neville Cross — Scripted Answers

"You are the only person who would know which poison to use. How do you explain that?"

"I know poisons because I treat poisoning. That's not the same as administering one. And frankly, you're wrong that I'm the only one — Eleanor's late husband published two monographs on toxic plants. She typed them herself. She knows exactly what I know, possibly more."

"Your alibi witness left early and is unreachable. That is a remarkable coincidence."

"It is, and it terrifies me. But I can tell you this — when Henderson left at ten, I went to find Reginald to settle things between us. His study door was locked. I knocked. No answer. I assumed he was avoiding me and went to the dining room. Ask the butler what time I arrived there."

"One week from losing everything — what would you do?"

"I would have confessed, as he asked. I was going to. I am not a violent man. But you should know — when I passed Eleanor in the hall at around five past ten, she was carrying her shawl. Folded very carefully over her arm. It was not cold."

The Full Solution

The murderer was Eleanor Voss.

Eleanor had worked for Reginald Ashworth for eleven years, all while knowing — or slowly coming to know — that he had stolen her late husband's unpublished novel and used it to launch his career. Her husband, a botanist and writer, had died without recognition. Reginald had profited from his work for decades.

When Eleanor finally found documentary proof — a letter, hidden in Reginald's private files — she confronted him the afternoon of the dinner. His response was not remorse. He threatened to dismiss her and have her discredited before she could speak to anyone.

She had, in fact, been planning for this possibility for months.

How She Did It

The poison

Eleanor's husband had been an amateur botanist whose published work covered toxic plant compounds in detail — work she had typed, edited, and understood. During a trip abroad the previous spring, she obtained a concentrated botanical extract from a market in Marseille, where it was sold legally as a veterinary sedative in high doses. She had waited for the right moment.

The window latch

Six weeks before the dinner, Eleanor deliberately broke the study's window latch during her regular cleaning duties — ensuring it could be pushed open silently from the courtyard outside. The study backed onto a low-walled terrace. She knew Reginald's evening habits perfectly: brandy at his desk, alone, between nine and eleven.

The night itself

Eleanor retired to her room at nine o'clock as witnesses noted. She left via her ground-floor window, walked the length of the building, entered the study through the broken latch, and added the poison to Reginald's brandy while he was turned away at his bookshelf. She was in and out in under four minutes. The shawl she carried when Dr. Cross passed her in the hall concealed her outdoor shoes, which were damp from the courtyard.

What gave her away

Three things, taken together: the maid's sighting near the corridor at 10:15pm; Dr. Cross's observation of the carefully folded shawl (concealing the shoes); and a receipt — found pressed into the lining of her travelling case — for an unusual botanical compound purchased in Marseille. Alone, each could be explained. Together, they were conclusive.

Why the Others Were Innocent

Diana Whitmore

Motive: strong. Means: none — Diana had no knowledge of poisons and no access to the study window. She was visible in the drawing room for most of the evening. Reginald's threat, though devastating professionally, was not yet acted upon — and Diana had already begun negotiating with a rival publisher without his knowledge.

Charles Pemberton

Motive: strong. But Charles needed Reginald alive long enough to expose him and recover the embezzled funds legally. Killing him destroyed the evidence trail. Charles was genuinely in the library, as the disturbed financial records later confirmed. His footsteps in the corridor were heard twice — consistent with someone moving between shelves, not with a coordinated entry to the study.

Dr. Neville Cross

Motive: strong, and he had unique medical knowledge. But his account of finding the study door locked — confirmed by the butler's observation that the door was indeed locked from inside at 9:45pm — placed him outside the room before Eleanor had finished. He arrived at the dining room visibly agitated, not calm, which is inconsistent with having just committed a premeditated murder. He was telling the truth.

The Verdict on Eleanor

Eleanor was convicted of murder. At her trial, she did not deny what she had done. Her statement to the court contained one sentence that was widely reported in the press:

"I gave eleven years of loyal service to a man who had already stolen the only thing my husband ever made. I do not ask for mercy. I ask only that you read the novel — his novel — and judge for yourselves what justice looks like."

The jury took forty minutes. The verdict was guilty. The sentence was life imprisonment. The novel — republished under her husband's name three years later — won the Prix Femina.
