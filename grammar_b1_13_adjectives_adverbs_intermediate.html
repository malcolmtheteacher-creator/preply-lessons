Bigger, Best, Worse: Making Comparisons

For prices, journeys, food, flats — anything you're comparing.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday comparisons:

  • "This café is cheaper than the one on the corner."
  • "The new bridge makes the drive quicker, but crossing it is more expensive."
  • "Out of all three flights, the morning one was the cheapest, but the evening one was the fastest."

Before you check: which words get -er/-est, and which get "more/most" instead?

Check the pattern CLICK
Short words (one syllable, or two ending in -y) add -er / -est: cheap → cheaper → cheapest, quick → quicker, fast → fastest.
Longer words use more / most instead: expensive → more expensive → most expensive.
A few common words break both rules: good → better → best, bad → worse → worst, far → further → furthest.
short word + -er / the -est
more / the most + long word
irregular: good → better → best | bad → worse → worst | far → further → furthest

Comparing two things — irregular forms included

"The traffic used to be terrible here."
→
"The traffic is better since they changed the lights."

"Good" doesn't follow either rule — it changes completely: good, better, best. Same with "bad": bad, worse, worst.

Just as Good: Saying Things Are Equal

When two things are the same, or one isn't quite as much as the other.

Spot the pattern

Three everyday comparisons of equal things:

  • "This bag isn't as expensive as the leather one, but it looks just as nice."
  • "The 8am train is as crowded as the 9am one — don't bother waiting."
  • "My commute isn't as long as it used to be since they opened the new road."

What goes on either side of the adjective? Say it before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
as + adjective/adverb + as — "as" appears on both sides, and the adjective stays in its plain form (crowded, not "more crowded"). Add "not" before the first "as" to say something is less than the other.
as + adjective/adverb + as (equal)
not as + adjective/adverb + as (less than)

From "the same price" to "as expensive as"

"The new phone costs the same as the old one."
→
"The new phone is as expensive as the old one."

How Big Is the Difference? Making Comparisons Stronger or Softer

Small words that say exactly how big — or how small — a difference is.

Spot the pattern

  • "The new printer is much faster than the old one."
  • "This laptop was only a bit more expensive than the last one, so I got it."
  • "The traffic today is nearly as bad as it was during the roadworks."

Where does each small word sit — next to a comparative, or next to "as…as"?

Check the pattern CLICK
far / much / a lot / a bit / slightly / rather go straight before a comparative (-er or more) to show how big the gap is.
nearly / almost / just / exactly go straight before "as…as" to show how close to equal something is.
The two groups don't mix — a comparative modifier never goes with "as…as", and the other way round.
far / much / a lot / a bit / slightly / rather + comparative
nearly / almost / just / exactly + as ... as
Watch out: never stack two comparative markers together. "Far more better" and "a bit more taller" don't exist — pick one comparative form (better, or taller) and put the modifier in front of it.
