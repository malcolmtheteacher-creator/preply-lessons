The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — A curve on a whiteboard

In 1994 Jackie Fenn joined Gartner, a company that advises businesses about technology. The World Wide Web was just moving from a research curiosity to a global phenomenon, and part of her job was to judge which new technologies would really matter.

Looking back at earlier waves — artificial intelligence, genetic engineering, personal computers — she saw the same pattern again and again: a flood of excitement, then disappointment when the technology failed to live up to it, and only later real, useful adoption. She sketched the shape on a whiteboard.

In 1995 she published it in a research note called “When to Leap on the Hype Cycle”. Its advice was practical: “Organizations should select the optimal time to begin investing in a new technology by matching their risk tolerance against a technology's progress along the hype cycle.”

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What was part of Fenn's job at Gartner?

2. What pattern did she notice?

3. What did her 1995 note advise?

1. To judge which new technologies would really matter.

2. Excitement, then disappointment, then real and useful adoption.

3. To choose when to invest by matching how much risk you can accept to where the technology is on the curve.

Discuss:

Part 2 — Peaks and troughs

Fenn expected it to be a one-off. But the following year, clients asked for an update with the latest technologies. In the 1996 version, the peak of the curve featured Java, internet terminals and the World Wide Web. Hype cycles became a regular Gartner report.

The curve has five named stages. A breakthrough — the innovation trigger — starts the story. Publicity climbs to the peak of inflated expectations. When early products fail to deliver, interest falls into the trough of disillusionment. Slowly, people work out what the technology is really good for: the slope of enlightenment. Finally comes the plateau of productivity, when it is simply used.

The names were memorable enough to escape. The term “hype cycle” and its stages are now used far beyond Gartner, in the marketing of all kinds of new technologies.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Why did Fenn update the curve in 1996?

2. What was at the peak in 1996?

3. Name the five stages in order.

1. Because clients asked for an update.

2. Java, internet terminals and the World Wide Web.

3. Innovation trigger, peak of inflated expectations, trough of disillusionment, slope of enlightenment, plateau of productivity.

Discuss:
  • Which technology do you think is at the peak right now?

Part 3 — Is it even a cycle?

The hype cycle has its critics. It is not really a cycle, they point out, and it is not scientific: there is little data to prove that technologies actually follow it. Its stages are named after feelings — expectations, disillusionment, enlightenment — which makes it hard to say where a technology really is.

In 2024 The Economist tested the idea against years of Gartner's own reports. It concluded that only a small share of technologies — perhaps a fifth — moved from innovation to excitement to despair to widespread use. And of the technologies that fell into the trough, it estimated, six in ten never rose again.

Fenn herself has said the danger is people being “reactive to the hype cycle, so they're rushing in when the hype's up and abandoning it when the hype's down again.” Perhaps the curve's best use is not as a prediction at all, but as a warning about ourselves.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. Give two criticisms of the hype cycle.

2. What did The Economist find in 2024?

3. What did Fenn say the danger was?

1. Any two: it isn't really a cycle; it isn't scientific — there's little data behind it; its stages are named after feelings, so it's hard to place technologies.

2. That perhaps only a fifth of technologies follow the whole curve, and six in ten of those in the trough never recover.

3. People rushing in when the hype is up and giving up when it's down.

Discuss:

How the tool works

The curve shows expectations over time — not how good the technology really is. The gap between the two is where the money, and the mistakes, are made.

Using it, step by step

  1. Name the technology, and be specific: “electric scooters for hire in cities”, not just “transport”.
  2. Ask where expectations are now: is everyone talking about it, or has the excitement faded?
  3. Separate the talk from the evidence: who is actually using it, and is it working for them?
  4. Decide your move: leap early (high risk), wait for the slope, or ignore it — and remember that many technologies never complete the curve.

Try it yourself

Here are six moments in the life of an imaginary technology — home robots. Which stage of the curve does each one describe?

1. Robot companies go bankrupt, and newspapers ask: “Whatever happened to home robots?”
2. A university lab shows a video of a robot folding laundry. There is no product yet.
3. A second generation of robots does just three jobs well, and some hotels start using them.
4. Every magazine cover promises that by next year, nobody will do housework.
5. Home robots are boring now: they're in millions of homes, and nobody writes about them.
6. Investors pour money into fifty start-ups, although few of them have sold a single robot.

1. Trough — the excitement has collapsed.

2. Trigger — a breakthrough starts the story.

3. Slope — people are learning what it is really good for.

4. Peak — expectations far above reality.

5. Plateau — it is simply used.

6. Peak — money follows the excitement, not the results.

Where the tool breaks

It isn't a law. Many technologies don't follow the full curve, and some never come back from the trough.

Feelings, not facts. Expectations are hard to measure, so placing a technology on the curve is a judgement.

It can become an excuse. “It's just in the trough” can be said about a technology that has simply failed.

Grammar — the future in the past: was going to, would

Hype is full of predictions — and later we look back on them. To talk about the future as it looked from the past, English uses was/were going to, would and was/were supposed to.

was / were going to + base verb — a plan or prediction in the past, often one that didn't happen.
“We were going to buy a house, but prices rose.”
would + base verb — the past form of will, especially after said, thought, believed, promised.
“She promised she would call.”  “Everyone thought the shop would close.”
was / were supposed to + base verb — what was expected or arranged, usually with disappointment.
“The train was supposed to arrive at six.”
was / were about to + base verb — something that was going to happen very soon.
“I was about to leave when the phone rang.”
Watch out: after a past reporting verb, will usually becomes would: ~~They said it will be easy~~ → “They said it would be easy.”
