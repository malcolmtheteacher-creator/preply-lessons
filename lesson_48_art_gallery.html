Conceptual Art — "Is This Art?"

From 1917 onwards
The idea behind the work matters more than technical skill or beauty. If an artist declares it art, is it art? Duchamp's infamous "Fountain" kicked off this debate.
Fountain
Marcel Duchamp
1917
A mass-produced urinal signed "R. Mutt." Submitted to an art exhibition, it challenged every assumption about what art could be.
Learn more →

Discussion point

Look at this object. Is it art? What makes you say yes or no? If Duchamp hadn't signed it and placed it in a gallery, would it still be art?

Your Turn

If you could place any everyday object in a gallery and call it art, what would you choose? Why?

Realism

1840s – 1880s
Depict the world as it really is — especially ordinary people and everyday life. A rebellion against Romantic idealization and painting only the wealthy.
The Stone Breakers
Gustave Courbet
1849
Ordinary workers doing hard labour. Revolutionary because it treated poor people as worthy subjects for large-scale painting.
Learn more →
The Gleaners
Jean-François Millet
1857
Peasant women gathering leftover grain. Celebrated the dignity of rural labour and provoked outrage among the upper classes.
Learn more →

Discussion point

Why was it considered revolutionary to paint ordinary workers? What does it say about a society when only the wealthy are considered "worth" painting?

Your Turn

Describe a scene from your daily life that would make a powerful realist painting.

Your Response

Impressionism

1860s – 1880s
Capture the fleeting impression of a moment — especially light and atmosphere. Visible brushstrokes, painting outdoors, and focusing on light rather than precise detail.
Water Lilies
Claude Monet
1906
Part of Monet's famous series of approximately 250 oil paintings depicting his flower garden. Pure light and colour over precise detail.
Learn more →
Impression, Sunrise
Claude Monet
1872
The painting that gave the movement its name. A critic used "Impressionism" as an insult — the artists embraced it.
Learn more →
Dance at Le Moulin de la Galette
Pierre-Auguste Renoir
1876
Dappled sunlight on a lively Parisian outdoor dance. Notice how light plays across faces and clothing.
Learn more →

Discussion point

Compare Impressionism to Realism. Both paint "real life," but in completely different ways. Which approach do you find more powerful? Why?

Your Turn

Cubism

1907 – 1920s
Show multiple perspectives simultaneously. Break objects into geometric shapes. Art doesn't have to look like reality — it can show how we think about objects.
Portrait of Pablo Picasso
Juan Gris
1912
A Cubist portrait of Picasso himself! The face is fragmented into geometric planes — can you still recognise the subject?

Expressionism

1905 – 1920s
Express inner emotional experience, not external reality. Distort colours, shapes, and proportions to convey feeling — even if the result is "ugly" or disturbing.
The Scream
Edvard Munch
1893
Distorted colours and shapes convey anxiety and existential dread. One of the most recognisable paintings in history.
Learn more →
Self-Portrait with Physalis
Egon Schiele
1912
Raw, contorted self-portrait with exaggerated features. Schiele's work pushed boundaries with its unflinching emotional intensity.
Learn more →
Berlin Street Scene
Ernst Ludwig Kirchner
1913
Jagged lines and acidic colours capture the nervous energy and alienation of modern city life.
Learn more →

Discussion point

How does "The Scream" make you feel? Could a Realist painting of the same scene convey the same emotion? What does distortion add?

Your Turn

If you had to paint your current mood, what colours and shapes would you use?

Your Response

Surrealism

1920s – 1940s
Explore the unconscious mind, dreams, and irrational juxtapositions. Access deeper truths through the unconscious, beyond logic and reason.
The Song of Love
Giorgio de Chirico
1914
A classical bust, a rubber glove, and a green ball in an impossible space. De Chirico's dreamlike scenes directly inspired the Surrealists.

Abstract Expressionism

1940s – 1950s
Pure emotion and gesture. No recognisable subject matter needed — only feeling and form. The act of painting itself becomes the art.
Number 17A
Jackson Pollock
1948
Pure action and energy on canvas. Pollock laid canvases on the floor and dripped, splashed, and flung paint.

Pop Art

1950s – 1960s
Embrace popular culture, mass production, and consumerism as art subjects. Blur the line between "high art" and "low culture." Art can be mass-produced.
🥫
Campbell's Soup Cans
View on Wikipedia →
Campbell's Soup Cans
Andy Warhol
1962
32 canvases of soup cans. Mass production as art — challenging the idea that art must be unique and handmade.
Learn more →
💥
Whaam!
View on Wikipedia →
Whaam!
Roy Lichtenstein
1963
Comic-book style blown up to gallery scale. Lichtenstein challenged where "real" art stops and popular culture begins.
Learn more →
I was a Rich Man's Plaything
Eduardo Paolozzi
1947
Often considered the first true Pop Art work. A collage of magazine clippings, ads, and pulp imagery — it even contains the word "POP!"
Learn more →

Discussion point

Pop Art turned soup cans and comic strips into "fine art." In our age of Instagram and TikTok, has everything become art? Or has nothing?

Your Turn
