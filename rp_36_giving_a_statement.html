Giving a Statement

A delivery van, a cyclist, a screech, a crunch — over in four seconds, right in front of you. Nobody's badly hurt, but somebody's insurance will hang on what witnesses say. An officer opens her notebook: 'Talk me through what you saw.' Now the hard part: being accurate in a second language, about four seconds of chaos.

The Scene 🎬

The rarest kind of speaking: where the goal isn't to be interesting, persuasive or fluent — just precisely, boringly true.

You were waiting to cross by the bakery when it happened: a white delivery van turning right, a cyclist coming through, brakes, a shout, and a bike on the tarmac. The cyclist is up and swearing — shaken, scraped, alive. The van driver is pale. Traffic is stopped, and a police officer, PC Webb, is working through the bystanders.

Here's what nobody teaches you about being a witness: your brain has already written a story. It has decided who was at fault, filled the gaps with guesses, and stamped the whole thing "what I saw". The officer doesn't want the story — she wants the fragments you can actually stand behind: where you stood, what you saw first, what you heard, what you'd swear to and what you wouldn't. A witness who says "I can't swear to that part" is worth ten who are confident about everything.

And in a second language there's a quiet extra pressure: the temptation to simplify your account into something easier to say. Resist it. The phrases in this lesson exist so that precision never has to be sacrificed to grammar.

  1. Two honest people watch the same accident and report different things. How is that possible — and does it make them liars?
  2. When did you last say "I saw" about something you'd actually only concluded?
YouStood four metres away, saw most of it, heard all of it — and has already caught their own brain filling in gaps.
PC WebbPatient, practical, notebook out. Has taken a thousand statements and can smell a guess dressed as a fact from across the street.

The Conversation 💬

The pavement outside the bakery. The cyclist is being checked over; PC Webb turns to you.

PC WebbWere you here when it happened?
YouYes — I saw it happen. Do you need a statement?
PC WebbPlease. Take your time. Talk me through what you saw.
YouLet me start from the beginning. I was standing about four metres away, there — by the bakery door, waiting to cross. So I had a clear view of the junction.
PC WebbGood. Go on.
YouThe van was coming from my left, indicating right — I saw the indicator, that part I'm sure of. The cyclist was coming straight through the junction towards it. The van started its turn, and the cyclist braked hard and went down. The van stopped — I'd say half a second later.
PC WebbDid the van hit the bike, or did the cyclist come off braking?
YouHonestly — that's the bit I can't swear to. There was a bang, but I was watching the cyclist's face by then, not the wheels. It might have been the bike hitting the ground.
PC WebbThat's fine — better you say so. What about the cyclist's speed?
YouRoughly twenty, twenty-five maybe — I couldn't give you an exact speed. Quick, but not crazy. And I should be careful here: I assumed he had the right of way because he was going straight on — but I didn't see the light for his lane. I saw my crossing light, not his.
PC WebbNoted — that's an important distinction, thank you. Anything before the collision? Sound, shout, horn?
YouA shout — the cyclist, I think, though I couldn't say what he shouted. No horn. And brakes — I heard the van's brakes and the bike's at almost the same time. It all happened in a few seconds, honestly. Four, five at most.
PC WebbAnd after?
YouThe driver was out of the van very fast — first to reach the cyclist. He kept saying something like "I didn't see you". I'm sure about "see" — the exact words, less so.
PC WebbRight. *writing* So: witness positioned four metres east of the junction, clear view. Van indicating right, cyclist proceeding straight at speed—
YouSorry — that's not quite what I said. Can we change "at speed"? I said twenty-ish and not crazy. "At speed" sounds like I'm blaming him.
PC WebbFair. "At normal cycling speed, estimated twenty." Better?
YouMuch. Could you read the whole thing back to me before I sign? I want to be sure it says what I actually saw — not what I think I saw.
PC Webb*reads it back* …and that's everything. Sign here. You'd be surprised how few people listen to the read-back.
YouOne last thing — if anything else comes back to me tonight, who do I call? Things surface later sometimes.
PC WebbThey do. This number, that reference. And — genuinely — that was a useful statement. The "can't swear to it" parts most of all.

Whether the van actually hit the bike ("that's the bit I can't swear to"); the cyclist's exact speed (an estimate, given as one); and the right of way — You saw their own crossing light, not the cyclist's lane, and says so. Each refusal marks the boundary between evidence and assumption, which is exactly what the statement is for.

Useful Phrases 🧰

Ten phrases for any moment your account becomes a record — police statements, insurance calls, HR interviews, accident reports at work. Say each out loud BEFORE you reveal it.

Say it like you mean it. The register here is calm and unhurried — a person carefully sorting what they know from what they don't. Speed sounds like panic and confidence sounds like invention; the credible witness is the slow, boring, precise one. Slow and boring is the goal.

🎬 Setting the scene

Phrase 1

The job: offering yourself as a witness. Complete it:

"I saw it happen — do you need a ___?"

"I saw it happen — do you need a statement?"
Simple, and rarer than it should be — most witnesses drift away, telling themselves someone else saw more. The sentence does two jobs: claims only what's true ("I saw it happen", not "I know whose fault it was"), and volunteers into the process rather than waiting to be caught by it. If nobody's in uniform yet, its cousin: "I saw it — take my number before I go."

Take it anywhere: "I saw it happen — do you need a statement?" Accidents, incidents at work, the moment your account might matter.
Phrase 2

The job: imposing order on four seconds of chaos. Complete it:

"Let me start from the ___."

"Let me start from the beginning."
Panic tells stories backwards — crash first, causes after, corrections everywhere. This little sentence buys you two seconds to arrange events into the order they happened, which is the order the notebook needs. It also signals to the officer: this witness is going to be methodical, settle in. First things first, literally.

Take it anywhere: "Let me start from the beginning." Any account of anything — the universal untangler.
Phrase 3

The job: establishing where you were — the fact that ranks all your other facts. Complete it:

"I was standing about four metres ___, so I had a clear ___ of the junction."

"I was standing about four metres away, so I had a clear view of the junction."
Position is the first thing any investigator wants, because it calibrates everything else you say — four metres with a clear view is a different witness from forty metres behind a bus. Give it unasked: distance, landmark ("by the bakery door"), and what you could and couldn't see from there. "About" is doing honest work; nobody carries a tape measure.

Take it anywhere: "I was about ___ away, with a clear view of ___." Position first — it's the stand your whole account sits on.

⚖️ The line between saw and assumed

Phrase 4

The job: marking the limit of your certainty. Which version?

A. "The van definitely hit him. Probably. I mean, there was a bang."
B. "Honestly — that's the bit I can't swear to."
C. "I don't know, I didn't really see anything."
B — "That's the bit I can't swear to."
A collapses from certainty to guess inside one breath — and takes the rest of your statement's credibility down with it. C throws away the nine things you DID see because of the one you didn't. B draws a clean boundary: everything outside this line, I stand behind; this bit, I don't. Note the officer's reaction — "better you say so" — and her verdict at the end: the "can't swear to it" parts were the most useful. Admitted limits are what make the rest believable.

Take it anywhere: "I can't swear to that part." Statements, meetings, memories — the credibility phrase.
Phrase 5

The job: the heart of the lesson — untangling sight from conclusion. Put the pieces in order:

but I didn't see it.I assumed he had right of way—because he was going straight on
"I assumed he had right of way because he was going straight on — but I didn't see it."
The most sophisticated sentence a witness can produce: it reports the assumption AND labels it as one, with its reasoning shown. Your brain makes these leaps automatically — someone going straight "must" have had the light — and stamps them as sightings. Catching your own brain doing it, out loud, is what separates testimony from storytelling. The officer called it "an important distinction". It usually decides the insurance.

Take it anywhere: "I assumed ___, but I didn't actually see it." Work post-mortems, arguments at home, anywhere the story has quietly overwritten the facts.
Phrase 6

The job: giving numbers you don't precisely have. Complete it:

"Roughly twenty — I couldn't give you an ___ speed."

"Roughly twenty — I couldn't give you an exact speed."
Estimates are welcome; estimates disguised as measurements are poison. The shape to remember: the number, wearing its uncertainty openly — "roughly", "about", "four or five seconds at most". A witness who says "exactly 23 km/h" gets doubted about everything; one who says "roughly twenty, and that's a guess" gets believed about the indicator too. Same knowledge, opposite effect.

Take it anywhere: "Roughly ___ — I couldn't give you an exact figure." Times, speeds, amounts, dates — precision about your imprecision.

📝 Protecting the record

Phrase 7

The job: your words just got hardened into someone else's. Fix it. Complete it:

"Sorry — that's not quite what I ___. Can we change '___ speed'?"

"Sorry — that's not quite what I said. Can we change 'at speed'?"
Statements are written by the person holding the pen, and pens summarise — "twenty-ish, not crazy" became "at speed", which quietly blames the cyclist. Correcting the officer feels rude; it isn't. It's the entire point of the process, and any decent officer will thank you. The record outlives the conversation — make it say what you said.

Take it anywhere: "That's not quite what I said — can we change it?" Meeting minutes, HR notes, medical records, quotes. Anywhere your words get written down by someone else.
Phrase 8

The job: the check before your name goes on it. Complete it:

"Could you read the whole thing ___ to me before I ___?"

"Could you read the whole thing back to me before I sign?"
The signature turns notes into evidence — YOUR evidence, quotable in proceedings you'll never attend. The read-back is your one chance to hear the whole account as the record will tell it, and to catch every "at speed" you missed in the moment. "You'd be surprised how few people listen," said PC Webb. Be the one who listens. In a second language, doubly so: ask for any word you don't know.

Take it anywhere: "Read it back to me before I sign." Statements, contracts, anything with your name at the bottom.
Phrase 9

The job: keeping the door open for the memory that surfaces at 2am. Complete it:

"If anything else comes ___ to me, who do I ___?"

"If anything else comes back to me, who do I call?"
To come back to you — to return to memory later. Memory is not a photograph; it develops overnight, and the detail that surfaces in the shower — the second cyclist, the reversing lights — can matter. This question gets you the number and the reference BEFORE you need them, and tells the officer you understand how memory actually works. Which, by now, she believes.

Take it anywhere: "If anything else comes back to me, who do I call?" Statements, interviews, doctor's appointments — leave every serious conversation with a channel back in.
Phrase 10

The job: reporting speech you half-remember — honestly. Say it out loud, then write it.

e.g. "He kept saying something like 'I didn't see you'. I'm sure about 'see' — the exact words, less so."
Quoted speech is the most dangerous part of any statement — exact words carry legal weight, and memory almost never has them exactly. The kit: "something like…" for the gist, then the calibration — which word you'd stand behind, which you wouldn't. It gives the investigation what it needs (an admission-shaped utterance with "see" in it) without you inventing a quotation.

Take it anywhere: "Something like ___ — I'm sure about ___, less sure about the rest." Any time you report what another person said.

⚡ The twist

Round 1 again — and as PC Webb finishes your read-back, the van driver approaches, still shaky, and says quietly: "Thank you for staying. Look — you saw it, right? You saw he came out of nowhere? My licence — I'm three points from losing it, and this job's all I've got. Just… tell them how fast he was going."

He's not a bad man. He was also, by your own account, first to the cyclist's side. And what he's asking you to say is more certain than what you saw. Answer him — kindly, and without moving one centimetre off your statement.

Before you close the tab…

  • Can you say "I can't swear to that part" without feeling it weakens you?
  • Can you catch your own assumptions and label them out loud?
  • Do your estimates wear their uncertainty openly?
  • Do you listen to the read-back — and correct it?

The Echo 🕰️

In Salem, Massachusetts, in 1692, testimony killed people. Nineteen were hanged on the strength of what witnesses said they had seen — girls pointing across a courtroom, neighbours recalling spectres in doorways, assumption piled on assumption until it weighed as much as fact. Almost none of it was lying, which is the terrifying part. The witnesses believed themselves. Their stories had simply eaten their observations.

The turning point came when a few people began doing the modest thing this lesson teaches: separating what they knew from what they had concluded. Ministers who asked how, exactly, a spectre could be identified. Jurors who later signed a public confession that they had convicted "on evidence we now know we could not understand." The trials collapsed not through drama, but through the arrival of the question "what did you actually see?"

Your traffic statement will hang nobody. But it lives on the same blade: the space between witnessed and assumed is where other people's futures get decided — a licence, a claim, a job. "I can't swear to that part" is a small sentence. Salem is what the world looks like when nobody says it.
