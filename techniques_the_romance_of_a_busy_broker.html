The Romance of a Busy Broker

O. Henry (1904) — a Wall Street broker works so hard that he forgets the biggest event of his own week. The last line tells him what it was.

Today: you'll describe a person doing several things at once, in a few efficient sentences, using participle clauses and with + noun + participle — the grammar of a busy, vivid scene.
Before we read:
  • Think of the busiest working day you can remember. What were you doing at the same time? Did you ever forget something important?
  • Can someone be so absorbed in work that they stop being able to see the people around them? Is that admirable, sad, or both?

Key words — think first, then check

Before we read, let’s unlock the words you’ll need. Think first — the Reveal only confirms.

A. Work it out from the sentence

Read each sentence aloud and say what the bold word means from the rest of the sentence. Then reveal.

1. The stock broker bought and sold shares for his clients all day, on the phone and across the floor of the exchange.
broker (n) — a person who buys and sells shares, property or insurance on behalf of others, for a fee.
2. Her face was calm and expressionless, so nobody could tell whether the news had pleased her.
expressionless (adj) — showing no feeling on the face. Example: “An expressionless official stamped the form.”
3. She hesitated, irresolute, in the doorway, unable to decide whether to come in or leave.
irresolute (adj) — uncertain; not able to make up your mind. Example: “He stood irresolute at the crossroads.”
4. The manager replied curtly: “I’m busy. Come back later.” He didn’t even look up from his papers.
curt / curtly (adj/adv) — rudely short and sharp in the way you speak. Example: “a curt reply”.
5. The office was full of the hustle of an ordinary Monday: phones ringing, people shouting, messengers running in and out.
hustle (n) — busy, noisy activity. (v: to push or hurry) Example: “the hustle of the city centre”.
6. Her wedding was kept a secret from everyone at the office, even from her closest colleague.
secret (n) — something deliberately hidden from others. (adj) Example: “a secret wedding”.

B. From the meaning to the word

Which word fits the definition? The first letter is given. Say the word, then reveal.

7. A person who types and writes up letters and notes for a boss — a job that was mostly done by women in the early twentieth century. It begins with s.
stenographer (n) — a person who writes down speech in shorthand and types it up; an old word for a secretary.
8. To give someone a job and pay for their work — the opposite of firing. It begins with e.
employ (v) — to give paid work to someone. (n: an employer, an employee) Example: “The company employs two hundred people.”
9. To cancel an order that has already been given, by giving a new order that says the opposite. It begins with c.
countermand (v) — to cancel an earlier order. Example: “He countermanded the order to sell.”
10. Forgetful because your mind is on something else; not noticing what is around you. It begins with a.
absent-minded (adj) — forgetful; thinking about something else. Example: “an absent-minded professor”.

The story, in three parts

O. Henry wrote hundreds of stories about ordinary New Yorkers, usually with a surprise in the last line. This one is set in a Wall Street broker’s office on a hectic morning. Read the three parts slowly, and notice what is — and is not — said about the broker’s private life. In the last line you will learn something he has forgotten.

Part 1 — The morning arrives

Pitcher, confidential clerk in the office of Harvey Maxwell, broker, allowed a look of mild interest and surprise to visit his usually expressionless countenance when his employer briskly entered at half past nine in company with his young lady stenographer. With a snappy “Good-morning, Pitcher,” Maxwell dashed at his desk as though he were intending to leap over it, and then plunged into the great heap of letters and telegrams waiting there for him.

The young lady had been Maxwell’s stenographer for a year. She was beautiful in a way that was decidedly unstenographic. She forewent the pomp of the alluring pompadour. She wore no chains, bracelets or lockets. She had not the air of being about to accept an invitation to luncheon. Her dress was grey and plain, but it fitted her figure with fidelity and discretion. In her neat black turban hat was the gold-green wing of a macaw. On this morning she was softly and shyly radiant. Her eyes were dreamily bright, her cheeks genuine peachblow, her expression a happy one, tinged with reminiscence.

Pitcher, still mildly curious, noticed a difference in her ways this morning. Instead of going straight into the adjoining room, where her desk was, she lingered, slightly irresolute, in the outer office. Once she moved over by Maxwell’s desk, near enough for him to be aware of her presence.

The machine sitting at that desk was no longer a man; it was a busy New York broker, moved by buzzing wheels and uncoiling springs.

“Well—what is it? Anything?” asked Maxwell sharply. His opened mail lay like a bank of stage snow on his crowded desk. His keen grey eye, impersonal and brusque, flashed upon her half impatiently.

“Nothing,” answered the stenographer, moving away with a little smile.

“Mr. Pitcher,” she said to the confidential clerk, “did Mr. Maxwell say anything yesterday about engaging another stenographer?”

“He did,” answered Pitcher. “He told me to get another one. I notified the agency yesterday afternoon to send over a few samples this morning. It’s 9.45 o’clock, and not a single picture hat or piece of pineapple chewing gum has showed up yet.”

“I will do the work as usual, then,” said the young lady, “until some one comes to fill the place.” And she went to her desk at once and hung the black turban hat with the gold-green macaw wing in its accustomed place.

He who has been denied the spectacle of a busy Manhattan broker during a rush of business is handicapped for the profession of anthropology. The poet sings of the “crowded hour of glorious life.” The broker’s hour is not only crowded, but the minutes and seconds are hanging to all the straps and packing both front and rear platforms.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How does Maxwell greet his stenographer, and what does she ask Pitcher?

2. What does the narrator mean by ‘The machine sitting at that desk was no longer a man’?

1. With a snappy ‘Good-morning, Pitcher,’ and no real attention to her. She asks Pitcher whether Maxwell has said anything about engaging another stenographer.

2. That the broker has turned into a machine for work — a busy New York broker, ‘moved by buzzing wheels and uncoiling springs’ — with no room for ordinary human attention.

Discuss:
  • The stenographer is ‘softly and shyly radiant’ and wearing a macaw wing in her hat. The broker does not notice. What does this tell you about both characters — and what is the reader beginning to suspect?

Part 2 — The hour on the exchange

And this day was Harvey Maxwell’s busy day. The ticker began to reel out jerkily its fitful coils of tape, the desk telephone had a chronic attack of buzzing. Men began to throng into the office and call at him over the railing, jovially, sharply, viciously, excitedly. Messenger boys ran in and out with messages and telegrams. The clerks in the office jumped about like sailors during a storm. Even Pitcher’s face relaxed into something resembling animation.

On the Exchange there were hurricanes and landslides and snowstorms and glaciers and volcanoes, and those elemental disturbances were reproduced in miniature in the broker’s offices. Maxwell shoved his chair against the wall and transacted business after the manner of a toe dancer. He jumped from ticker to ’phone, from desk to door with the trained agility of a harlequin.

In the midst of this growing and important stress the broker became suddenly aware of a high-rolled fringe of golden hair under a nodding canopy of velvet and ostrich tips, an imitation sealskin sacque and a string of beads as large as hickory nuts, ending near the floor with a silver heart. There was a self-possessed young lady connected with these accessories; and Pitcher was there to construe her.

“Lady from the Stenographer’s Agency to see about the position,” said Pitcher.

Maxwell turned half around, with his hands full of papers and ticker tape.

“What position?” he asked, with a frown.

“Position of stenographer,” said Pitcher. “You told me yesterday to call them up and have one sent over this morning.”

“You are losing your mind, Pitcher,” said Maxwell. “Why should I have given you any such instructions? Miss Leslie has given perfect satisfaction during the year she has been here. The place is hers as long as she chooses to retain it. There’s no place open here, madam. Countermand that order with the agency, Pitcher, and don’t bring any more of ’em in here.”

The silver heart left the office, swinging and banging itself independently against the office furniture as it indignantly departed. Pitcher seized a moment to remark to the bookkeeper that the “old man” seemed to get more absent-minded and forgetful every day of the world.

The rush and pace of business grew fiercer and faster. On the floor they were pounding half a dozen stocks in which Maxwell’s customers were heavy investors. Orders to buy and sell were coming and going as swift as the flight of swallows. Some of his own holdings were imperilled, and the man was working like some high-geared, delicate, strong machine—strung to full tension, going at full speed, accurate, never hesitating, with the proper word and decision and act ready and prompt as clockwork. Stocks and bonds, loans and mortgages, margins and securities—here was a world of finance, and there was no room in it for the human world or the world of nature.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does the woman from the agency want, and what does Maxwell say?

2. How does the narrator describe the world of finance?

1. She has come about the position of stenographer. Maxwell says there is no position open: Miss Leslie has given perfect satisfaction for a year, and the place is hers as long as she chooses.

2. As a world of stocks, bonds, loans and mortgages with ‘no room in it for the human world or the world of nature’ — a high-geared, delicate machine going at full speed.

Discuss:
  • Pitcher decides the ‘old man’ is getting more absent-minded every day. Is he right? Who in this office actually knows the most about what is going on?

Part 3 — The lilac, the proposal and the last line

When the luncheon hour drew near there came a slight lull in the uproar.

Maxwell stood by his desk with his hands full of telegrams and memoranda, with a fountain pen over his right ear and his hair hanging in disorderly strings over his forehead. His window was open, for the beloved janitress Spring had turned on a little warmth through the waking registers of the earth.

And through the window came a wandering—perhaps a lost—odour—a delicate, sweet odour of lilac that fixed the broker for a moment immovable. For this odour belonged to Miss Leslie; it was her own, and hers only.

The odour brought her vividly, almost tangibly before him. The world of finance dwindled suddenly to a speck. And she was in the next room—twenty steps away.

“By George, I’ll do it now,” said Maxwell, half aloud. “I’ll ask her now. I wonder I didn’t do it long ago.”

He dashed into the inner office with the haste of a short trying to cover. He charged upon the desk of the stenographer.

She looked up at him with a smile. A soft pink crept over her cheek, and her eyes were kind and frank. Maxwell leaned one elbow on her desk. He still clutched fluttering papers with both hands and the pen was above his ear.

“Miss Leslie,” he began hurriedly, “I have but a moment to spare. I want to say something in that moment. Will you be my wife? I haven’t had time to make love to you in the ordinary way, but I really do love you. Talk quick, please—those fellows are clubbing the stuffing out of Union Pacific.”

“Oh, what are you talking about?” exclaimed the young lady. She rose to her feet and gazed upon him, round-eyed.

“Don’t you understand?” said Maxwell, restively. “I want you to marry me. I love you, Miss Leslie. I wanted to tell you, and I snatched a minute when things had slackened up a bit. They’re calling me for the ’phone now. Tell ’em to wait a minute, Pitcher. Won’t you, Miss Leslie?”

The stenographer acted very queerly. At first she seemed overcome with amazement; then tears flowed from her wondering eyes; and then she smiled sunnily through them, and one of her arms slid tenderly about the broker’s neck.

“I know now,” she said, softly. “It’s this old business that has driven everything else out of your head for the time. I was frightened at first. Don’t you remember, Harvey? We were married last evening at 8 o’clock in the Little Church Around the Corner.”

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What makes Maxwell stop, and what does he decide?

2. What is the final twist?

1. A faint smell of lilac through the open window — Miss Leslie’s own scent. The world of finance ‘dwindled suddenly to a speck’, and he decides to ask her to marry him at once.

2. She tells him they were married the evening before, at 8 o’clock, in the Little Church Around the Corner. He has been so absorbed in work that he has forgotten his own wedding.

Discuss:
  • Go back to Part 1 and find every clue about the stenographer’s mood and her question to Pitcher. How fairly has O. Henry set up the twist?

The grammar this story runs on: participle clauses

O. Henry crams several actions into one sentence: ‘Maxwell stood by his desk with his hands full of telegrams and memoranda, with a fountain pen over his right ear and his hair hanging in disorderly strings over his forehead.’ The grammar that makes this possible is the participle clause.

1. -ing and -ed participle clauses.
• “Trying doors as he went, the officer moved up the avenue.” (two actions at once) • “Pushed on by the crowd, she reached the desk.” (passive) • “Having finished the letters, he turned to the telephone.” (one action before another)
The subject of the participle must be the same as the subject of the main clause.
2. with + noun + participle / adjective / prepositional phrase.
• “He stood with his hands full of papers.” • “She worked with her sleeves rolled up.” • “He talked with a pen over his ear.”
Use with when the participle has its own subject, to describe how someone looks or what is happening around them.

1. Combine into one sentence

Use a participle clause for the first idea. Keep the meaning.

1. The broker ran from the ticker to the telephone. He shouted orders as he ran.

2. She hung her hat on the hook. Then she went straight to her desk.

3. The clerk was exhausted by the rush. He sat down and closed his eyes.

4. Pitcher didn’t understand what was happening. He told the agency to send nobody.

1. Running from the ticker to the telephone, the broker shouted orders. / The broker ran from the ticker to the telephone, shouting orders.

2. Having hung her hat on the hook, she went straight to her desk. / After hanging her hat on the hook, she went…

3. Exhausted by the rush, the clerk sat down and closed his eyes.

4. Not understanding what was happening, Pitcher told the agency to send nobody.

2. Describe the scene with ‘with’

Complete each sentence with a with phrase that fits the picture of the busy office.

1. Maxwell worked at his desk (a pen / over / ear).

2. The clerks stood in a line (telegrams / full / hands).

3. He dictated the letter (the telephone / ring / behind him).

4. She smiled at him (tears / run / her face).

1. with a pen over his ear

2. with their hands full of telegrams

3. with the telephone ringing behind him

4. with tears running down her face

In each answer, ‘with’ introduces what is true of someone at the same moment — the noun is the subject of the participle.

Why the twist works

O. Henry (William Sydney Porter, 1862–1910) wrote his stories as a working journalist in New York and sold them to newspapers and magazines. They often use what we now call the ‘O. Henry ending’: a last-line surprise that changes how we read everything before it. In ‘The Romance of a Busy Broker’ the surprise is also a joke at the expense of an entire way of life.

The story is fair. Every clue is on the page: the stenographer’s radiance, her question about a replacement, her lilac scent, her little smile when Maxwell says ‘Nothing’. What Maxwell cannot see, the reader slowly can. Notice, too, the language of machines: ‘buzzing wheels’, ‘high-geared’, ‘clockwork’. Wall Street is a machine, and Maxwell is part of it; the surprise is not only that he forgot, but that a person could be so thoroughly absorbed into work that he would.

Look again at ‘here was a world of finance, and there was no room in it for the human world.’ The story is a comedy, but the narrator is also making a serious observation about what some jobs do to the people who do them.
