Species Profiles

Study these endangered species profiles. Each has unique conservation criteria:

Black Rhino (Critically Endangered)

Key Facts: One of the oldest groups of mammals; a living fossil. 3 of 5 subspecies are now extinct.

Value: Huge source of revenue from ecotourism in Eastern and Central African countries.

Importance: Umbrella species—rhino reserves protect many other species.

Main Threat: Poaching for their horns (used in traditional medicine and decorative items).

Challenge: Do not thrive in captivity.

Mountain Gorilla (Critically Endangered)

Key Facts: Small populations remain in Rwanda, Democratic Republic of Congo, and Uganda. Very social, gentle, and peaceful.

Positive Trend: Numbers increased from a historic low of just 900. Now dependent on conservation and ecotourism for survival.

Threats: War and conflict, poaching, human disease transmission.

Educational Value: Their social structure and intelligence make them valuable for research and education.

Axolotl (Critically Endangered in Wild)

Key Facts: Also called the Mexican walking fish, it is an amphibian related to salamanders. Unique mythological connection to an Aztec god.

Special Characteristics: Rare ability to regenerate limbs, making it valuable for laboratory research and medical advancement.

Status: Unlikely to die out in captivity (popular in aquariums) but nearly extinct in the wild.

Threat: Habitat loss due to urban development.

Polar Bear (Vulnerable)

Key Facts: At the top of the arctic food chain; plays a crucial role in overall ecosystem health.

Cultural Importance: Central to the cultural, spiritual, and economic lives of indigenous arctic peoples.

Indicator Species: Highly vulnerable to climate change and rising temperatures—their status reflects broader climate impacts.

Main Threats: Loss of sea ice, increased shipping, habitat loss from oil and gas industries.

Persuasive Appeal Task

Imagine a school wants to adopt an endangered species. Your task is to argue persuasively for one species.

Decision Criteria

Consider these factors when making your case:

Value: Cultural, historical, economic, or scientific significance

Impact on Biodiversity: Is it a keystone species with a crucial ecological role?

Likelihood of Success: Are there realistic solutions? What is the timeframe? What resources are needed?

Engagement: Will it educate students? Does it have emotional appeal? Will it inspire further learning?
