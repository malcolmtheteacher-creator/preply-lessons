Buddhist Dukkha & Humanist Views

YEAR 8 · UNIT 2: THE PROBLEM OF EVIL

What You'll Learn

You will explore how Buddhism reframes the problem of suffering through dukkha (unsatisfactoriness), the Four Noble Truths, and the Eightfold Path. You'll also learn the humanist perspective which rejects God and sees suffering as a human challenge requiring human solutions.

Think: A Different Approach

So far, we've learned:

Christianity and Islam both assume God exists and try to justify why God allows evil. But not all worldviews accept the existence of God. What if the problem isn't "why does God allow suffering?" but something else entirely?

Buddhism doesn't ask "why does God allow suffering?" because Buddhism doesn't believe in a creator God. Instead, Buddhism reframes the problem entirely. Suffering is not a problem of theodicy (justifying God) but a fundamental feature of existence that humans must understand and address.

Encounter: The First Noble Truth - Dukkha

At the heart of Buddhism is the First Noble Truth: the truth of dukkha. Dukkha is often translated as "suffering," but it means something broader: unsatisfactoriness, dissatisfaction, or the inherent unease of conditioned existence.

Dukkha encompasses: Physical pain, emotional distress, anxiety about the future, dissatisfaction with what we have, the stress of impermanence. Even pleasant experiences contain an element of dukkha because they are temporary.

The Buddha taught that this is not a problem caused by a failure of God or the universe. Rather, dukkha is simply the nature of unenlightened human existence. The question is not "why does dukkha exist?" (it just does) but "what can we do about it?"

The Four Noble Truths

1. Dukkha (Suffering): Suffering and unsatisfactoriness are inherent in existence.

2. Samudaya (Cause): Suffering arises from craving (tanha)—our desire for things to be different than they are, our addiction to pleasure, and our ignorance of reality.

3. Nirvana (Cessation): It is possible to end suffering. Nirvana (literally "extinguishing") is the state of liberation from dukkha.

4. Magga (Path): The Eightfold Path is the way to achieve liberation from suffering.

The Eightfold Path

The Buddha provided a practical path to reduce suffering:

  • Right View, Right Intention, Right Speech, Right Action, Right Livelihood, Right Effort, Right Mindfulness, Right Concentration

These aren't commands from God but practices that lead to wisdom, ethical conduct, and mental discipline—the path to enlightenment and liberation from dukkha.

Explore: Humanism and Suffering

Humanism is a worldview that rejects belief in God and the supernatural. Humanists see suffering as a real human problem without divine explanation.

The Humanist Perspective

There is no God, and therefore no theodicy problem. Suffering exists because of natural causes (disease, accidents, natural disasters), human cruelty, and the inherent vulnerability of biological life.

Humans must solve suffering themselves. We must use science, medicine, technology, justice systems, and compassion to reduce suffering. We cannot rely on divine intervention or cosmic justice in an afterlife.

This makes human responsibility absolute. If someone suffers from a curable disease, the responsibility lies with humans to develop treatments. If people suffer from injustice, humans must build just societies. There's no "God's plan" to fall back on.

Comparing the Four Worldviews

Buddhism

Suffering (dukkha) is the nature of existence. The solution is enlightenment through the Eightfold Path, not divine intervention.

Humanism

Suffering has natural causes. Humans must solve it through science, ethics, and justice. No God helps us.

Christianity

God is all-powerful and all-good but allows suffering for reasons (free will, soul-making, redemption).

Islam

Allah tests humans with suffering. Patience and trust, combined with belief in divine justice in the afterlife, provide meaning.

Apply: Compare Worldviews

Scenario 1: A pandemic

A disease spreads globally, killing millions. How would each worldview (Buddhism, Christianity, Islam, Humanism) interpret this? What would each suggest as a response?

Scenario 2: Personal illness

You develop a serious illness. A Christian might pray and find meaning in suffering. A Buddhist might see it as dukkha and an opportunity for spiritual practice. A humanist would focus on medical treatment. An Islamic person might see it as a test. What are the strengths and limitations of each approach?

Scenario 3: Social injustice

People suffer oppression and discrimination. Which worldview places the most responsibility on humans to act? Which offers the most comfort? Which do you find most motivating?

Evaluate: Which Worldview?

Buddhism and Humanism Bypass the Theodicy Problem

Buddhism and Humanism don't try to justify God; they either reject the concept (Humanism) or don't focus on it (Buddhism). This avoids the logical problem of evil. Instead, both emphasize human agency and responsibility.

The Cost of Comfort

Christian and Islamic theodicies offer comfort: suffering has meaning, God has a plan, there's justice in the afterlife. But this comfort comes at a cost—you must believe without logical proof.

Buddhism and Humanism offer less comfort but more intellectual honesty: suffering is real, causes are natural and psychological, and solutions are human-centered.

Your reflection:

Which worldview resonates most with you? Do you value comfort and meaning more, or intellectual honesty? Can suffering be meaningful without a divine plan? Is human responsibility enough?

Review: Key Concepts

Dukkha (First Noble Truth)

Suffering and unsatisfactoriness are inherent in unenlightened existence. This includes physical pain, emotional distress, and the anxiety of impermanence.

The Eightfold Path

The Buddhist solution to dukkha: Right View, Right Intention, Right Speech, Right Action, Right Livelihood, Right Effort, Right Mindfulness, Right Concentration. These lead to enlightenment and liberation.

Humanism

A worldview rejecting belief in God. Suffering has natural causes; humans must solve it through science, medicine, ethics, and justice. Complete human responsibility.

Key distinction: Christianity and Islam address "why does God allow evil?" Buddhism and Humanism ask "how do we respond to suffering?" and don't assume a divine answer.
