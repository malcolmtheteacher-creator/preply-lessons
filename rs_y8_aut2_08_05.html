Question 1: What is dukkha, and why is it the First Noble Truth?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Dukkha means suffering or unsatisfactoriness. It is the First Noble Truth because Buddhism teaches that all conditioned existence involves dukkha—not just pain, but the subtle suffering that comes from change and impermanence. Recognising this is the foundation for the Buddhist path.

Question 2: What are the Three Marks of Existence?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: The Three Marks are: (1) Anicca—impermanence; (2) Dukkha—unsatisfactoriness; (3) Anatta—non-self. These explain why we suffer: we try to cling to impermanent things as if they were permanent, and we mistakenly believe in a permanent self when everything is always changing.

Question 3: What is karma, and how does it explain suffering in Buddhism?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Karma means "action." Buddhist teach that actions have natural consequences: wholesome actions (generosity, honesty, kindness) lead to positive results; unwholesome actions (greed, dishonesty, cruelty) lead to suffering. Karma explains suffering without needing to blame God or cosmic injustice—it results from one's own past and present choices.

Question 4: What is the Noble Eightfold Path, and what is it designed to do?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: The Noble Eightfold Path is the Buddhist practical path to ending suffering: right view, right intention, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right concentration. It is designed to cultivate wisdom and ethical conduct while training the mind through meditation to reduce tanha (craving) and achieve Nirvana (liberation).

Encounter: Buddhism and Humanism on Suffering

Buddhism: Suffering as Natural and Impersonal

In the last four lessons, you studied Buddhist and Islamic theodicies—theological attempts to justify God's goodness in the face of suffering. Buddhism offers something radically different: a non-theistic explanation of suffering that does not require theodicy at all.

Buddhism teaches that suffering is not a theological problem (Why would God permit it?) but a natural feature of conditioned existence. It results from three sources:

Impermanence (Anicca)

All conditioned things are constantly changing. We suffer because we try to cling to things as permanent when they are not. Aging, illness, and death are natural consequences of impermanence, not punishments from God.

Craving (Tanha)

We suffer because we crave (tanha) what we lack and cling to what we have. This craving creates attachment, desire, and inevitable disappointment. Suffering is the natural result of this cycle, not a reflection of cosmic injustice.

Karma (Natural Causation)

Wholesome actions naturally produce positive results; unwholesome actions naturally produce suffering. This is not punishment from a judge but natural cause and effect. Over lifetimes, karma accumulates, shaping rebirth and circumstances.

The Buddhist Solution

Rather than asking "Why does God permit suffering?" Buddhism asks "How can we end it?" The answer: reduce craving, cultivate wisdom, and follow the Noble Eightfold Path. Nirvana—the cessation of craving and the self—is the goal.

Humanism: Suffering in a Natural Universe

Humanism: Suffering in a Natural Universe

Humanism is a worldview that rejects belief in supernatural beings (including God) and bases ethics and meaning on human reason, science, and compassion. Humanists understand suffering not as a theological puzzle but as a natural feature of a universe that has no creator, designer, or cosmic plan.

The Humanist View of Suffering

Humanism teaches that:

No Divine Purpose

The universe was not created by an intelligent designer with a benevolent plan. Suffering has no cosmic purpose or meaning—it is simply the result of natural processes and human choices.

No Afterlife Compensation

There is no heaven or divine justice to compensate for suffering in this life. This is the only life we have. Unjust suffering is simply unjust—it cannot be justified by appeal to rewards in an afterlife.

Natural and Human Causes

Suffering results from natural forces (disease, weather, aging, accidents) and human choices (cruelty, neglect, injustice, exploitation). These can be understood and, to some extent, mitigated through reason and science.

Greater Moral Urgency

Because there is no afterlife and no divine plan, the moral responsibility to reduce suffering falls entirely on humans. We cannot rely on God to make things right; we must do it ourselves.

The Humanist Critique of Theodicy

Humanists find theodicies unsatisfying. Consider the Free Will Defence: it argues that God permits evil because God values human free will. But does a just God really require the suffering of innocent people—children dying of disease, victims of natural disasters—to protect human freedom? The theodicy seems to prioritise abstract freedom over concrete suffering.

Or consider the Soul-Making Theodicy: suffering builds character. But does a just God really need to send tsunamis that kill thousands, including children, to develop human virtue? There seem to be more proportionate and less cruel ways to cultivate character.

The Humanist philosopher Bertrand Russell argued: "If I were to suggest that between the Christian and the Jews a difference is a difference of ethical and of social outlook, you would say it would be a very wicked thing to suggest. But I maintain... [that] the Christian God is not a good God." Russell pointed out that accepting these theodicies requires us to condone needless suffering in the name of maintaining belief in divine goodness.

Humanist Ethics and the Reduction of Suffering

Rather than seeking to justify suffering theologically, Humanism focuses on understanding and reducing it through:

  • Science and Medicine: Using empirical knowledge to prevent disease, treat illness, and extend healthy life
  • Reason and Education: Using critical thinking to identify causes of suffering and design interventions
  • Ethics Based on Wellbeing: Developing moral principles that prioritise reducing harm and promoting flourishing
  • Democratic Justice: Creating fair systems that reduce suffering caused by exploitation, inequality, and injustice
  • Compassion and Solidarity: Recognising our shared vulnerability and responsibility to support one another

The utilitarian philosopher Peter Singer has written extensively on how we can reduce suffering through rational choice. We do not need God's permission to reduce suffering; we have the moral obligation to do so.

Humanism and Meaning Without God

A common religious objection to Humanism is: "Without God, how can suffering have meaning?" Humanists respond: suffering itself has no cosmic meaning, but we can create meaning by how we respond to suffering. The meaning lies not in divine purpose but in human resilience, compassion, and the choices we make to alleviate suffering for ourselves and others.

When a parent cares for a dying child, when a doctor works to save lives, when activists fight injustice, meaning is created through love and commitment—not through belief that suffering serves a divine plan.

Summary: Buddhism and Humanism Compared

On the Cause of Suffering

Buddhism: Tanha (craving/attachment) and karma (consequences of action). Not caused by God or injustice, but by one's own mind and past actions.

Humanism: Natural causes (aging, disease, accidents) and human choices (cruelty, injustice, exploitation). Suffering results from natural processes and moral choices, not cosmic design.

On Whether Suffering Has Purpose

Buddhism: Suffering has no external purpose; it is the natural consequence of craving and karma. But recognising suffering can motivate spiritual practice.

Humanism: Suffering has no cosmic purpose. But understanding it can motivate rational action to reduce it.

On How to Respond

Buddhism: Through the Noble Eightfold Path—cultivating wisdom, ethical conduct, and mental discipline to end tanha and achieve liberation (Nirvana).

Humanism: Through reason, science, compassion, and democratic action—working to understand causes and reduce suffering in this life.

On the Problem of Evil

Buddhism: There is no "problem of evil" because there is no all-powerful God to justify. Suffering is explained through natural law (karma).

Humanism: There is no "problem of evil" in the theistic sense. Suffering is a natural fact to be understood and addressed, not a theological puzzle to be solved.

Comparing Buddhist/Humanist to Christian/Islamic Approaches

How do these non-theistic views compare to the Christian and Islamic responses you studied in earlier lessons?

Buddhist vs. Christian Theodicy

Buddhist: Suffering is natural, caused by tanha (craving). No theodicy needed because there's no all-powerful God to justify.

Christian: Suffering is explained through theodicies: free will, soul-making, or divine mystery. Christians must justify God's goodness.

Humanist vs. Islamic Theodicy

Humanist: Suffering is a natural fact; theodicies are unsatisfying. Focus on reducing suffering through reason and science.

Islamic: Allah's wisdom and justice are beyond human understanding. Suffering tests faith; theodicies emphasise divine purpose.

Buddhist and Humanist Similarities

Both focus on understanding and reducing suffering through human action rather than divine intervention. Both reject the idea that suffering serves a divine purpose. Both emphasise compassion and ethical responsibility.

Buddhist and Humanist Differences

Buddhism: Offers a spiritual path to liberation (Nirvana); believes in rebirth and karma across lifetimes.

Humanism: Focuses on this life only; relies on science and reason rather than spiritual practice or rebirth.

Evaluate: Do Non-Theistic Approaches Handle Suffering Better?

The Question

In this unit, we've encountered multiple approaches to suffering: Christian theodicies, Islamic theodicies, Buddhist explanations, and Humanist critiques. Is the non-theistic approach (Buddhism/Humanism) more convincing than theistic ones? Or does theism offer something essential that non-theistic views cannot provide?

Argument 1: Non-Theistic Approaches Handle Suffering Better

The Case: Buddhist and Humanist approaches avoid the logical problem of evil entirely. They do not require us to justify an all-powerful, good God permitting innocent suffering. Instead, they explain suffering through natural causes (karma, impermanence, natural processes) and focus practical energy on reducing it. This is more honest and more effective. Buddhist karma avoids the need for a cosmic judge; Humanist science and reason actually reduce suffering through medicine and justice. Moreover, these approaches do not ask the griever to accept that their child's death "served a purpose"—they acknowledge suffering as tragic and make reducing future suffering the moral priority. This is ethically superior.

Argument 2: Theistic Approaches Offer Something Non-Theistic Ones Cannot

The Case: While non-theistic approaches avoid the logical problem of evil, they may fail to provide what suffering people need most: hope that suffering is not meaningless, that justice will ultimately prevail, and that someone cares about them. Buddhist Nirvana is the extinction of self; this can feel cold to someone grieving the loss of a loved one. Humanist reason and science cannot replace the comfort of believing that a loving God holds their pain and promises ultimate restoration. Religious theodicies, whatever their logical flaws, offer the grieving person the hope that their suffering is seen, understood, and will be redeemed. A theistic view says: "Your suffering matters to God, and God will make it right." A non-theistic view says: "Your suffering is a natural tragedy; you must find meaning in how you respond." The first may be more comforting and more capable of sustaining moral hope.

Your Evaluation

Consider both arguments carefully. Do you find the logical clarity and practical focus of non-theistic approaches more convincing? Or do you think the hope and meaning offered by theistic traditions—despite their logical challenges—is what suffering people ultimately need? Write a reasoned conclusion that acknowledges the strengths and weaknesses of each approach.

Question 1: What does "dukkha" mean, and why is it called the "First Noble Truth"?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Dukkha means suffering or unsatisfactoriness—the fundamental unsatisfactory nature of conditioned existence. It is called the First Noble Truth because, in Buddhism, it is the first essential insight: all conditioned things involve dukkha (suffering as pain, suffering as change, suffering as conditionality). Recognising this truth is the foundation for the Buddhist path.

Question 2: What are the Three Marks of Existence, and how do they explain suffering?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: The Three Marks are: (1) Anicca (impermanence)—all things are always changing; (2) Dukkha (unsatisfactoriness)—because things change, we cannot cling to them permanently, creating disappointment; (3) Anatta (no-self)—there is no permanent, unchanging self. These explain suffering because we grasp at impermanent things trying to make them permanent, and we cling to a false permanent "self" that is actually constantly changing.

Question 3: How does Buddhism's concept of karma explain suffering without needing theodicy?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Karma (action) teaches that suffering is the natural, impersonal consequence of one's own actions and intentions. Wholesome actions lead to positive results; unwholesome actions lead to suffering. This is not punishment from a judge or cosmic injustice—it is natural causation. Therefore, Buddhism does not need to solve the "problem of evil" because suffering is not a theological problem but a natural fact to be understood and overcome through practice.

Question 4: What is the main Humanist objection to theodicies?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Humanists argue that theodicies are unconvincing because they ask us to accept needless suffering in order to maintain belief in an all-powerful, good God. For example, the Free Will Defence justifies innocent suffering to protect human freedom, but no just God would require such a cruel trade-off. The Soul-Making Theodicy claims suffering builds character, but gentler methods exist. Humanists conclude that it is more honest to accept that suffering has no cosmic justification than to twist logic to defend divine goodness.

Question 5: How do Humanists create meaning in response to suffering without belief in God?

🔒 Try to answer from memory first!

Answer: Humanists teach that suffering itself has no cosmic meaning or divine purpose, but humans can create meaning through how they respond to suffering. Meaning arises from human love, compassion, resilience, and the choices we make to reduce suffering for ourselves and others—through science, reason, justice, and solidarity. A parent caring for a sick child, a doctor saving lives, an activist fighting injustice—these create meaning not through belief in God's plan but through human commitment and care.

Central Insight from This Lesson: Buddhism and Humanism approach suffering without relying on theodicies or appeals to divine purpose. Buddhism explains suffering through impermanence, craving, and karma, offering a practical spiritual path to liberation. Humanism explains suffering through natural causes and human choice, focusing on reducing it through reason and science. Both traditions see suffering as real and urgent to address, but neither needs to justify it as part of a divine plan. This avoids the logical problems of theodicy—but raises questions about whether non-theistic approaches can offer the hope and meaning that suffering people sometimes seek.
