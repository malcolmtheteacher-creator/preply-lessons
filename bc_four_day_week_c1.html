One agency, twelve months in

Briarwood & Cole — a year on the four-day week

When Briarwood & Cole, a fifty-person PR agency in Manchester, agreed to take part in a six-month pilot of the four-day week in 2024, the management team braced themselves for some difficult conversations with clients. The fee structure stayed the same, the workload didn't go away, and the only firm commitment was that nobody would be expected to work on Fridays.

A year on, the agency is one of more than seventy UK companies that have made the change permanent. The pilot's headline figures look extraordinary: no measurable fall in productivity, a forty percent drop in self-reported burnout, sick-day use down by a third, staff retention at its highest in a decade, and what staff describe as a transformed work-life balance. The board has voted to keep the change in place indefinitely.

Beneath the headlines, however, the picture is more nuanced. The agency had to do real work to make the four days count. Internal meetings were stripped back, "thinking time" was protected, and presenteeism — being at work but not really working — became the unspoken enemy. Senior managers report that the change has been less about doing things faster and more about doing fewer things, better. Discretionary effort, the extra mile that goes uncounted, appears to have risen rather than fallen.

Two strands of difficulty haven't gone away. Some clients still prefer to deal with the agency on a Friday and have had to be gently retrained; one major client took the cancellation of a regular Friday call as a sign of unseriousness and walked away. Internally, an unintended consequence has been that some of the more junior staff worry that the shorter week makes their roles harder to defend in a downturn — visibility, even if it doesn't translate into output, still feels like a form of safety. The board has been careful not to dismiss the concern.

Whether the model will scale beyond agencies and tech firms remains an open question. Pilot data from sectors where work cannot be easily compressed — manufacturing, frontline care, retail — is much thinner. The companies that have made it work tend to share certain features: knowledge-based output, results measured by deliverables rather than hours, and managers who treat the change as a culture project rather than a calendar trick.

Comprehension

1. What did the management team expect would be the hardest part of the pilot?

Difficult conversations with clients. The fee structure didn't change, the work didn't go away, but Fridays were off the table — and that needed explaining.

2. What's the most striking finding from the pilot's first year?

Multiple converging signals: productivity didn't fall, burnout dropped by 40%, sick days fell by a third, retention reached a ten-year high. It's not one result — it's the pattern of all of them moving in the same direction at once.

3. The text says the change is "less about doing things faster and more about doing fewer things, better." What does this mean?

The agency didn't just compress five days into four — they cut activity. Fewer meetings, less low-value work, more protected thinking time. Volume came down so that quality could go up.

4. What's the unintended consequence for junior staff?

A worry about visibility. If they're at work less, will they be more vulnerable in a downturn? Being seen, even when it doesn't translate into output, still feels like a form of safety — and that anxiety is real, even if the data says it shouldn't be.

5. Why does the writer think the model may not scale to every sector?

The pilots have been concentrated in knowledge work — agencies, tech firms, professional services. Sectors where output is tied to physical presence (manufacturing, care, retail) have much thinner data, and the same model may not transfer.

Language in context

"A client walked away" — what does this suggest?

They ended the business relationship and went elsewhere — calmly rather than dramatically. "Walked away" implies a deliberate decision, not a row.

"Doing fewer things, better" — what kind of strategic move is this?

A deliberate trade-off — choosing reduced volume to free up quality. The opposite of trying to "do it all". A C1 way of saying "we prioritised".

"Visibility still feels like a form of safety." What's the writer pointing at?

A gap between what the data shows (productivity is fine) and what people feel (being seen at work is protective). Old assumptions about presence and security haven't caught up with the new model. The feeling is the data, even when the data says otherwise.

"A culture project rather than a calendar trick" — what's the distinction?

A calendar trick is just rearranging the schedule and hoping things work. A culture project is changing how work is approached — how meetings are run, what's prioritised, what managers reward. The four-day week only works if you do the second.

Hedging the claim

When you present a change like this to colleagues or senior leadership, the difference between a strong claim and a defensible one is often a single softening phrase. Strong claims invite challenge; well-hedged claims invite agreement.

Compare:

Strong (claim-as-fact)

"The four-day week boosts productivity."

Hedged (claim-as-defensible)

"The four-day week appears to maintain productivity, and in some sectors may modestly improve it."

Strong

"This works for any company."

Hedged

"The model has worked particularly well for knowledge-based businesses, though the evidence is much thinner for other sectors."

Strong

"Employees love it."

Hedged

"Self-reported wellbeing measures improved markedly during the pilot, though the long-term effect remains to be seen."

Strong

"We gave everyone a day back."

Hedged

"Staff report a transformed sense of personal time, although a minority worry about the implications for visibility and progression."

The hedging toolkit

is likely to / tends to Probability without certainty. Use instead of "will".
appears to / seems to Reports what the evidence shows, not what is. Use instead of "is".
on the evidence so far Limits the claim to what's currently known. Pre-empts the "but what about…?" challenge.
significant progress on Acknowledges movement without claiming a finished result.
particularly in / especially among Narrows the claim to where it's well-supported.
it remains to be seen whether Acknowledges open questions without retreating from the work.

Your turn — soften these

"The four-day week works."

"On the evidence so far, the four-day week appears to work well, particularly in knowledge-based sectors where output isn't tied to physical presence."

"Burnout is solved."

"Burnout markers improved significantly during the pilot, although it remains to be seen whether the effect holds over the longer term."

"Every business should try it."

"Businesses in certain sectors appear well-placed to trial this, although the model may not transfer easily to manufacturing or frontline service work."

"Clients don't mind."

"Most clients tend to adapt without difficulty, though a small number have struggled — and the agency had to do real work to retrain expectations on the client side."

Talk it through

1. Productivity didn't fall.

Does that surprise you? What does it tell you about how productivity is currently measured — and whether the standard week was ever really productive in the first place?

2. One major client walked away.

If you were running the agency, would you have changed your approach with that client — or held firm? Where's the line between principled and stubborn?

3. Junior staff worried that less visibility made them less safe.

Is that a real concern, or a hangover from older assumptions about what work looks like? How would you reassure them — and would you mean it?

4. "A culture project rather than a calendar trick."

Where have you seen organisations confuse the two — treating something as a logistical change when it really demanded a cultural one? What happens when they get it wrong?

5. The pilots are concentrated in knowledge work.

What would have to be true for the model to spread to manufacturing, care work, or retail? Is the answer "different model entirely", or "same model, but harder"?
