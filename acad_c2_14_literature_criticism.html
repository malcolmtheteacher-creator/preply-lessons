Beyond Summary to Critique

You're presenting a literary analysis in a graduate seminar. You've argued a particular reading of a text. But someone in the room—a scholar who published a different reading of that same text—is listening. They will disagree with you.

This moment is when you learn the difference between knowing a text and being able to defend an *argument about* a text.

Your Turn Think of a text you've written about. What is the single most important claim you make about it? Not a summary—the claim. What does the text *mean*?

What Most Students Confuse

Summary: "In this novel, a woman escapes her marriage."

Criticism: "By having her character slip away wordlessly, the author suggests that female agency exists only as absence—that a woman can only assert herself by refusing to speak or be named. This reflects the constraints of the historical moment the novel depicts."

One describes what happens. The other argues what it means and why that meaning matters.

Your Turn Take the text you're working with. What is something that happens in it that most readers would take at face value? Now: what is the *critical claim* you would make about that event? What does it reveal about the text's deeper concerns?

Six Moves for Strong Criticism

1 The Critical Stance +
Before you argue a reading, show your position. What lens are you looking through? Why should readers believe this interpretation is worth considering?
Weak

"In this novel, the author explores identity."

Strong

"Reading through a postcolonial lens, I argue that the protagonist's fragmentation isn't a psychological failing but a rational response to imperial pressure. The novel doesn't pathologise her; it diagnoses a structure of power."

The Move

Name your theoretical framework → Show why it reveals something others might miss → Reframe what looks like character flaw as structural critique

2 Contextualising Within Theory +
Your reading exists in conversation with a tradition of critical thought. Show your text in relation to that tradition. What conversation are you joining? What's your contribution?
Orphaned

"The protagonist is resisting patriarchy."

Contextualised

"Feminist criticism from Spivak onward has grappled with whether the colonised woman can represent herself in imperial discourse. I read this text as showing what representation looks like—not absence, but strategic hybridity. She speaks, but through the master's language, in ways that contain both compliance and critique."

The Move

Name the critical tradition → Show how your text engages with a debate within that tradition → Position your reading as advancing the conversation

3 Close Reading Out Loud +
The evidence for your reading lives in the text itself. Show exactly where and how. Don't just assert—read a passage, then explain what it reveals.
Unsupported

"The author uses fragmented narrative to convey psychological instability."

With Evidence

"Notice the phrasing on page 47: 'She was there and she was not there.' The repetition with negation—this syntax refuses coherence. But it's not random; it appears in moments of contact with colonial authority. The fragmentation is triggered by power, not inherent. The narrative form itself becomes a political statement."

The Move

Quote precisely → Notice specific word choice or structure → Connect the formal choice to your argument → Show why this reading matters

4 Engaging with Opposing Readings +
Most important texts have multiple readings. Show you know them. Show why your reading is stronger—not by dismissing others but by showing what your approach reveals that theirs can't.
Dismissive

"Some critics read this as tragedy, but they're wrong. It's resistance."

Engaging

"Marxist readings emphasize economic constraint—valid. But they risk seeing the protagonist only as victim of structure. Reading through a postcolonial lens, I can see how she weaponises her position within that structure. She's not just constrained; she's strategising. Both readings are present in the text. Mine simply makes visible what the economics reading might miss."

The Move

Name the opposing reading with respect → Show what it illuminates → Show what it obscures → Argue your reading is *more* complete, not *instead of*

5 Intertextual Connections +
Texts speak to each other across time and form. Show how your text participates in a conversation—with earlier works, with its contemporary moment, with later receptions.
Isolated

"This character's voice is distinctive."

Intertextual

"Her voice echoes the slave narrative tradition—'I bore witness'—but inverts it. Where the slave narrator sought to prove humanity through literacy, this character's literacy becomes suspect. She writes, but her words are monitored, glossed, corrected by imperial annotation. This is intertextual critique: she's speaking back to the tradition that promised voice as freedom."

The Move

Find echoes of earlier literary traditions → Show how your text revises them → Argue what that revision reveals about the text's historical moment

6 The Evaluative Claim +
All criticism implies a value judgment: this text is doing something important. Make that judgment explicit. Why does your reading matter?
Neutral

"The author uses these techniques to represent the protagonist's experience."

Evaluative

"By refusing psychological realism, the author achieves something more politically urgent: she denies the reader the comfort of empathy. We cannot consume her protagonist's interiority. Instead, we're forced to confront what remains outside discourse—the space where imperial power operates without naming itself. The form is the argument."

The Move

Show what the text achieves through its form → Explain why that achievement matters → Make an evaluative claim about the text's political or aesthetic significance

Preparing Your Analysis

You're in a seminar. You have 10 minutes to present your reading before the opposing scholar responds. You need to be precise.

Your Preparation

Take 5 Minutes Choose one passage from your text—a key moment. Write down: (1) What literally happens here? (2) What theoretical lens am I using? (3) How does this passage support my reading? (4) What does this passage reveal about the text's larger concerns?
5:00

Present Your Close Reading

Speak for 3 Minutes Present your analysis of this passage. Read the passage aloud, then walk through your interpretation. Use the framework: what happens → what it means theoretically → why it matters.

Anticipate the Challenge

The scholar with the opposing reading leans forward. "But couldn't that passage equally support a reading of [different interpretation]? You're reading political intention into what might be aesthetic choice."

Your Response Framework

ACKNOWLEDGE Yes, the passage contains both. That's what makes it rich.
DISTINGUISH Show why your reading accounts for more of the text.
DEFEND Explain why aesthetic choice and political intention aren't separate.
Speak for 1.5 Minutes How would you respond? Acknowledge their point. Then show why your reading is more complete.

The Hard Questions

The seminar continues. The opposing scholar pushes back on different aspects of your reading. Work through these scenarios.

Scholar: "You're reading the text through a postcolonial lens. But this text was written before postcolonial theory existed. Isn't that anachronistic? Aren't you importing a framework the author never intended?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Distinguish between historical context and critical framework.

Answer: "That's important. I'm not claiming the author had postcolonial theory. But postcolonial theory emerged *because* colonialism produced certain representational problems. The author lived in and wrote about those problems. Postcolonial theory gives me language for what the text intuited. It's not anachronism; it's using contemporary tools to articulate what was at stake in the historical moment the text inhabited."

Scholar: "Your reading assumes the author was politically intentional. But critics in her own time read her work as purely aesthetic, concerned with form not politics. How do you know you're not overreading political significance?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Show that form IS politics. There's no separation.

Answer: "I understand that reading. But contemporary critics might have missed what we now see: that formal innovation is political innovation. Rejecting narrative coherence, fragmenting voice—these are not neutral aesthetic choices. They're responses to the material conditions of the time. I'm not claiming she wrote a political manifesto. I'm claiming her formal choices *enact* a politics. Those are different arguments."

Scholar: "I appreciate your close reading of that passage, but it's only one moment in a 400-page novel. You're building your entire argument on a handful of examples. Can you account for the passages that seem to contradict your reading?"
Your Response (120 seconds)
2:00
The move: No text is monolithic. Show how contradictions support your reading.

Answer: "Valid. The text does contain moments of coherence and clarity—periods of conformity. I read those moments not as contradicting my argument but as showing its costs. She experiences periods of integration, but they're purchased through psychological suppression. The fragmentation returns. The novel's form enacts that cycle: coherence breaking down, again and again. The contradictions you see are the text's subject matter. They prove my point."

Scholar: "Your reading is clever, but it privileges one interpretation. How would you respond to someone who reads the same passage as simply showing character development—that she grows more integrated and secure by the novel's end?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Show what each reading reveals and obscures. Defend yours as more complete.

Answer: "That's a plausible reading. It accounts for her growth. But it doesn't account for *how* that growth is achieved—through acceptance of the imperial gaze, adoption of imperial standards of 'integration.' My reading doesn't deny growth; it contextualises it as adaptation rather than liberation. Both readings see development. Mine asks: what are the conditions that make this development possible? And the answer reveals something troubling about the text's world."

Scholar: "I notice you cite a lot of secondary criticism from scholars who share your theoretical position. But you cite very little work that challenges your framework. Aren't you preaching to the converted?"
Your Response (90 seconds)
1:30
The move: Acknowledge the fair critique. Show your work in conversation with opposition.

Answer: "Fair challenge. My citations do cluster around postcolonial theory. But that's partly because my argument lives there. However, you're right that I should engage more explicitly with formalist critiques that resist politicisation. Let me address one directly: [Name the scholar]. They argue form is autonomous. My response: even autonomy is historically contingent. The claim that art transcends politics IS a political claim, rooted in a particular historical moment."

Stepping Back

Literary criticism is an argument, not a truth claim. You're not discovering what the text means. You're making a case for what it could mean—and why your reading is worth hearing.

Question 1 What is the most important disagreement you could imagine someone having with your reading? What would they see that you're missing? And what would you need to understand in order to respond fairly?
