MINDS WORTH MEETING · C1 · 50 min

Ibn Khaldun

The medieval thinker who argued that what binds a group together is the hidden engine driving every empire's rise and inevitable fall.

You talk first — before you read anything

This is a talking lesson, not a reading one. So before a single fact, the floor is yours.

Start here · 2 minutes

Look at his name and the line under this lesson's title: “what binds a group together is the hidden engine driving every empire's rise and inevitable fall”. What kind of thinker do you imagine he was, and what might that phrase mean? And how did you first come across him — what pulled you in? Just talk.

Now meet him — one beat at a time

Three short facts. After each one, you do the talking.

Ibn Khaldun was born in 1332 in Tunis, into a family of Andalusian descent that had crossed from Spain to North Africa generations earlier. He came of age amid rival dynasties, court intrigue and recurring plague — and lost both parents to the Black Death as a young man.

Over to you:
  • Living through that much upheaval and loss so young — how might it change the kind of questions a person spends their life asking?

From his teens he was pulled into political life — secretary, diplomat and minister for one court after another, often switching sides, occasionally imprisoned, repeatedly forced to start again. Exhausted in his forties, he withdrew to a remote castle and in one extraordinary burst drafted the Muqaddimah (1377): an attempt to explain why history happens, treating the rise and fall of states as something with causes that could be studied.

Over to you:
  • He spent his life inside the very courts whose rise and fall he later analysed. Does living through something make you a better analyst of it — or a more biased one?

He later served as a senior judge in Mamluk Egypt. Near the end of his life, in 1401, he was lowered over the walls of besieged Damascus to meet the conqueror Timur — Tamerlane — in person, and the two talked for weeks. Today he is widely regarded as a founder of sociology and of the philosophy of history, and as an early voice in economic thought — centuries before those disciplines had names in Europe.

History, he insisted, should be examined with reason and a sceptical eye — questioned for its causes, not simply swallowed as a chronicle of rulers.

Over to you:
  • He said history should be studied for its causes, sceptically, not taken at face value. How was history taught where you grew up — as causes, or as a list of names and dates?

Asabiyyah — and you do the thinking out loud

One idea sits at the centre of everything Ibn Khaldun wrote. I will hand it to you in small pieces. After each piece, you talk — I will mostly listen.

His key concept is asabiyyah — usually translated as group feeling, social cohesion or solidarity. It is the invisible glue that makes a group act as one: the shared loyalty, trust and willingness to sacrifice for each other you find in a close-knit tribe, clan or movement. Where it is strong, a group can take risks, endure hardship and overpower opponents far larger than itself. He noticed it is strongest not in comfortable cities but at the hard margins — the desert, the mountains — where survival depends on sticking together.

Over to you:
  • Think of a group you have belonged to where the bond was that strong. What created it — and could it have survived comfort and success?

Here is the part that makes him sound uncannily modern. The conquest does not end the story — it begins the decline. Once a cohesive group seizes power, it inherits cities, wealth and luxury. The next generations grow up soft, used to being served. The very success that the group's solidarity won begins to dissolve that solidarity. Over roughly three or four generations the asabiyyah fades, and the dynasty becomes prey for the next hungry group waiting at the margins.

Over to you:
  • Success destroying the very thing that created it — do you see that pattern in any country, company or family you know? Walk me through one.

So history, for Ibn Khaldun, moves in cycles rather than a straight line of progress. Hardy groups rise on the strength of their bonds, build civilisation, are softened by their own comfort, and fall — making room for the next cycle. It is a strikingly unsentimental view: the seeds of every empire's collapse are planted by its own success.

The desert-hard group conquers the luxurious city — and then, generation by generation, the luxury of the city quietly dissolves the very bond that made the conquest possible.

Take a side · 3–4 minutes

“Every empire carries the seeds of its own collapse — comfort always rots solidarity.” Argue for it or against it, and bring real examples: nations, companies, even families. Build your case, then I will push back and you defend it. Is the cycle inevitable, or can a group break it?

The cycle in stages

He sketched the life of a dynasty almost like the life of a person. Glance at the table, then tell me which stage you think your own society is in.

StageWhat happensWhat is happening to asabiyyah
ConquestA tough, tightly bonded group from the margins overthrows a soft, settled dynasty.At its peak — total loyalty and shared sacrifice make the group unstoppable.
ConsolidationThe new rulers seize wealth, build, and centralise power in one strong leader.Still strong, but starting to narrow — loyalty shifts from the whole group towards the ruler.
LuxuryA golden age of building, art and spending; the dynasty looks at its most magnificent.Quietly eroding — comfort replaces struggle, and the old bonds loosen.
DeclineComplacency, overspending and infighting; the state weakens and waits to be replaced.Largely gone — leaving the dynasty open to the next hungry group from the margins.
Quick reaction:
  • Which stage would you say your own country, or a country you know well, is in right now? What evidence makes you say so?
  • Three or four generations is roughly a century. Does that feel about right for how long the "good times" of a society tend to last, or too neat to be real?

Vocabulary — click each card to reveal

Ten words you need to discuss Ibn Khaldun properly — and that work just as well for any serious conversation about ideas. Tap to reveal the meaning and an example.

cohesion
noun
The state of sticking together as a united whole.
"Asabiyyah is essentially the social cohesion that lets a group act as one."
solidarity
noun
Unity and mutual support among people with a shared interest.
"The tribe's solidarity made it far stronger than its numbers suggested."
cyclical
adjective
Recurring in repeated cycles rather than moving in a straight line.
"He saw history as cyclical — rise, decline, and rise again."
decadence
noun
Moral or cultural decline brought on by comfort and excess.
"Luxury slides into decadence, and decadence into collapse."
complacency
noun
Smug self-satisfaction that leaves you unprepared for danger.
"The dynasty's complacency was the first sign its asabiyyah had faded."
to erode
verb
To wear away gradually until little is left.
"Comfort slowly erodes the bonds that hardship first created."
tribalism
noun
Strong loyalty to one's own group, often against outsiders.
"Asabiyyah can build a nation, but it can also harden into tribalism."
prescient
adjective
Showing knowledge of events before they happen; remarkably far-sighted.
"His prescient analysis reads like modern sociology written six centuries early."
sceptical
adjective
Inclined to question or doubt rather than accept at face value.
"He took a sceptical view of the flattering histories written for kings."
to attribute (to)
verb / attribution (n)
To regard something as caused by, or belonging to, a particular thing.
"He attributed the fall of dynasties to the loss of group feeling, not bad luck."

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that a society could keep its cohesion even in comfort.
  • It might well be that the cycle simply runs faster today.
  • I suspect the truth is somewhere in between.
  • You could argue that wealth strengthens a state rather than weakening it.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say every empire carries the seeds of its own fall.
  • What strikes me most is how modern his analysis sounds.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, his theory fits the present better than his own century.

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure I fully buy that.
  • I take the point, but doesn't that assume…?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…

I'll make a bold claim — “Ibn Khaldun proves that no powerful society can ever stay powerful.” Answer me using at least one phrase from each box above: first speculate, then take a position, then complicate it. Keep going until you've used all three.

A · Apply the Cycle (3–4 min)

Take a country, a company, or even a family you know well, and run it through Ibn Khaldun's four stages — conquest, consolidation, luxury, decline.

Argue your case: which stage is it in now, and is the cycle inevitable, or can it be broken? Build a real argument — give an example, raise the strongest objection to your own view, then answer it.

B · Asabiyyah Today (3–4 min)

Ibn Khaldun said the tightest group feeling forms at the hard margins, where survival depends on loyalty. Yet today many of us live comfortable, connected, individual lives.

Where does the strongest solidarity exist in the modern world — online movements, immigrant communities, start-ups, sports fans? Pick one and argue whether it is real asabiyyah or just an imitation of it. Make the case with a concrete example.

C · Build Your Own (4–5 min)

Ibn Khaldun turned the rise and fall of states into a testable theory. Your turn: invent your own "law of history". Propose one rule — "societies tend to…" — that you think explains why groups rise or fall.

State your rule, give one piece of evidence for it, then admit where it might break down. I will then poke holes in it, and you defend it.

Your take — the conversation that matters most

You introduced me to Ibn Khaldun, so you get the last word. Pick the questions that grab you — there are no model answers here.

Open discussion

  1. After today, has anything shifted in how you see Ibn Khaldun — or has it confirmed why you find him interesting?
  2. His central idea is that solidarity is the hidden engine of power. Is that a hopeful idea to you, or a troubling one?
  3. He saw history as cycles, not progress. Do you share that instinct, or do you believe humanity genuinely moves forward over time?
  4. Is the loss of asabiyyah a real danger for modern, individualistic societies — or is that just nostalgia for a tougher past?
  5. If you could ask Ibn Khaldun one question, what would it be? And what do you think he would say back?
  6. Who is the next thinker or writer you would put in front of me — and what is the one idea of theirs I need to understand?
