BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

The Last Samurai

An American officer is hired to build a modern Japanese army — and ends up dying beside the warriors it was made to destroy. Underneath it sits a real rebellion, in 1877.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of The Last Samurai — a broken soldier who changes sides in someone else's revolution — discover how much of it actually happened and how much was invented, and master the structures English uses to link a degree to a result: so … that, such … that, too … to, enough (+ to) and not … enough.
Before we begin — talk it through: When a country modernises very fast, what does it gain and what does it throw away? Is there anything in your own country's past that people now mourn losing — and were they right to lose it? And have you ever deeply admired a way of life you would not actually want to live?

The film, in three parts

Edward Zwick's The Last Samurai (2003). Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A soldier for hire

1876. Captain Nathan Algren is a decorated veteran of the American Civil War and the Indian Wars who now earns a living demonstrating rifles to bored crowds in San Francisco, drinking heavily between appearances. What he did in the campaigns against Native American villages has hollowed him out, and he has no intention of talking about it.

His former commanding officer, Colonel Bagley, brings him an offer from Japan. The businessman and politician Omura — a moderniser who is also building railways and enriching himself — is raising a conscript army for the young Emperor Meiji, and wants Westerners to train it. The pay is enormous. Algren, who despises Bagley and needs the money, takes it.

What he finds are peasant recruits who have barely held a rifle. He asks for time; Omura refuses and sends the regiment into the field against samurai rebels led by Lord Katsumoto. In fog and confusion the conscripts panic and break. Algren's companion Sergeant Gant is killed. Algren, wounded and alone, kills a samurai with a broken spear and is taken alive on Katsumoto's order.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Why is Algren in Japan at all, and who is paying him?

2. Why does the first battle go so badly for the new army?

1. He is a broken, drinking veteran hired for a large fee, through Colonel Bagley, by the moderniser Omura, to train the emperor's new conscript army.

2. The recruits are untrained peasants sent out far too early; they panic, the line collapses, and Algren is wounded and captured.

Discuss:
  • Algren takes the job for money while despising the man who offers it. Is a soldier who fights for pay in someone else's country a professional, or something worse?

Part 2 — The village

Algren is carried into Katsumoto's mountain village and kept there through the winter. He is nursed by Taka — the widow of the samurai he killed in the battle, ordered by her brother to care for the man who made her a widow. Neither of them can escape the arrangement, and the film sits honestly in that discomfort.

Cut off from alcohol, Algren dries out badly and then slowly steadies. He learns the language, is knocked down again and again in sword practice by the warrior Ujio, and starts to see what surrounds him: an austere, ordered, wholly disciplined way of living in which every ordinary act is done with care. Katsumoto, who keeps him alive partly to understand his enemy, talks with him about war, honour and what a country owes its past.

It becomes clear that Katsumoto is not simply an enemy of the modern world. He is loyal to the young emperor — he was once the emperor's teacher — and believes the boy is being managed by Omura for profit. When assassins attack the village at night, Algren fights on the samurai's side. His allegiance has moved, and he knows it.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who nurses Algren, and why is the situation so painful?

2. What does Katsumoto actually want — and who does he blame?

1. Taka, Katsumoto's sister — and the widow of the samurai Algren killed. She is ordered to care for him.

2. He is loyal to the emperor, not against him; he believes Omura is using the young emperor to modernise Japan for his own profit.

Discuss:
  • Algren admires the samurai partly because they are strangers to him. Is his conversion genuine understanding — or a man in pain adopting somebody else's certainty?

Part 3 — The last charge

In spring Katsumoto goes to the capital to speak to the emperor and finds him surrounded by Omura's men. A new law forbids the wearing of swords in public. Katsumoto refuses to give his up; in the street, soldiers cut off the topknot of his son Nobutada as a public humiliation. Katsumoto is placed under arrest. Algren resigns from Omura's service and helps break him out, and Nobutada dies covering their escape.

What follows is a battle everyone in it knows they will lose. Hopelessly outnumbered, the samurai draw the imperial infantry into the trees, then meet them in the open. For a few minutes the plan works. Then Omura's new weapons are brought forward, and Gatling guns cut the last cavalry charge to pieces — including many of the imperial officers' own countrymen. Algren kills Bagley in the fighting. Katsumoto, mortally wounded, dies by his own hand with Algren's help; the imperial soldiers, appalled, kneel on the field.

Algren, badly wounded, survives. He carries Katsumoto's sword into the emperor's presence and offers it to him. The young emperor — moved, and finally speaking for himself — refuses Omura's Western trade treaty and strips him of his fortune. The film ends with Algren riding back to the mountain village, and to Taka.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happens when Katsumoto refuses to give up his sword in the capital?

2. How does the final battle end, and what does Algren do afterwards?

1. He is arrested, and his son Nobutada is publicly humiliated when soldiers cut off his topknot. Algren then helps him escape.

2. The samurai charge is destroyed by Gatling guns; Katsumoto dies with Algren's help. Algren takes his sword to the emperor, who rejects Omura's treaty.

Discuss:
  • The samurai ride into gunfire knowing exactly what will happen. Is that courage, or is a defeat you have chosen in advance simply a futile gesture?

Grammar — degree & result (so, such, too, enough)

This whole film runs on one idea: a quantity that decides an outcome. Too few men. Not enough time. So much firepower that courage stopped mattering. English has a small, precise set of structures for exactly that — a degree on one side, a result on the other — and once you own them your speaking stops being a list of facts and starts having consequences in it.

1 · so … that / such … that — a high degree, and the result it produces.
• so + adjective/adverb + that: "The recruits were so young that their uniforms did not fit." "He drank so heavily that he could barely stand."
• so + much / many / few / little (+ noun) + that: "There was so much dust that nobody could see the road."
• such + (a/an) + adjective + noun + that: "It was such a humiliation that the family never spoke of it."
• such + adjective + plural / uncountable noun + that: "They were such disciplined men that a stranger noticed it in an hour."
2 · too … to / (not) … enough — a degree that blocks or permits a result.
• too + adjective/adverb (+ for someone) + to-infinitive — more than is possible or acceptable, so the result does not happen: "The pass was too narrow for the guns to come through."
• adjective/adverb + enough (+ for someone) + to-infinitive — sufficient, so the result can happen: "He recovered quickly enough to ride by spring."
• enough + noun: "They had enough rice to last the winter."
• not … enough — insufficient: "The horses were not fresh enough to charge twice."
3 · The two traps everyone falls into:
• Word order. enough comes after an adjective or adverb (strong enough, soon enough) but before a noun (enough water, enough men). Never "enough strong".
• too is not very. "It was very cold" is a description. "It was too cold to sleep" is a barrier — something did not happen because of it. Use too only when the degree is a problem.

The Real Story — how much of this is true?

There was a real rebellion, a real leader, and a real last stand on a real hill — in 1877, in the far south of Japan. Almost every emotional beat of the film comes from it. Almost every military detail does not.

⛩ Katsumoto had an original — and he was a moderniser

The man behind Katsumoto is Saigō Takamori (23 January 1828 – 24 September 1877), from the southern domain of Satsuma. He was not an enemy of the new Japan: he helped build it. He forged the alliance between Satsuma and Chōshū that brought down the shogunate, commanded imperial forces in the Boshin War, and negotiated the bloodless surrender of Edo Castle in April 1868. He resigned from the government in October 1873 when his proposal for a confrontation with Korea was rejected, went home to Kagoshima, and in 1874 opened private academies that filled with unemployed, resentful young ex-samurai. He was, in short, a modern commander who lost an argument — not a warrior who rejected the modern world.

What the men of 1877 were actually angry about

In under a decade the Meiji state dismantled an entire class. The domains were abolished in 1871. National conscription arrived in January 1873: every young man served, which meant the country's fighting was now done by farmers' sons. The Haitō Edict of 28 March 1876 banned the wearing of swords in public by anyone except soldiers, police and officials — the visible mark of samurai status, gone by decree. Then, on 5 August 1876, hereditary stipends were abolished outright and converted into low-interest government bonds. The stipends had been a genuine burden — over 30% of state spending in 1869 — but the bonds paid very little, and by 1882 roughly 90% of former samurai had already sold theirs. Most were simply ruined.

So the grievance was not really about beauty or tradition. It was status, weapons and money, taken in that order. The rising began on 29 January 1877, when the government started shipping weapons out of the Kagoshima arsenal and Saigō's students seized them first. He did not start it — he joined it.

Swords against guns: the film's biggest invention

The image everyone takes away from The Last Samurai — bows and blades against machine guns — is the part that is least true. Saigō's army was partly modernised and organised as six infantry battalions of about 2,000 men, with a body of gunners and an artillery train: 28 mountain guns, 2 field guns and 30 mortars. They had rifles. Their officers had been trained in modern methods. This was rifles against rifles.

What beat them was scale and supply. The government put roughly 51,800 regular troops, over 5,000 imperial guards and 18,000 police into the field, and could burn through an average of about 322,000 rounds and 1,000 artillery shells a day. The rebels besieged Kumamoto Castle from 19 February and never took it. At Tabaruzaka in March, some 15,000 rebels fought about 9,000 imperial infantry for eight days and each side lost more than 4,000 men. There was no romantic charge; there was a long, industrial war of attrition that the poorer side could not finance.

And yet the film's picture is not pure fantasy — it is the last few hours mistaken for the whole war. By Shiroyama, on 24 September 1877, about 500 survivors faced 30,000 imperial troops under Yamagata Aritomo. They had been melting down metal to make bullets. When the ammunition finally ran out, the last men did draw their swords and charge downhill into rifle fire, and they were destroyed: 30 imperial soldiers were killed, and the rebels were annihilated. The film takes that final hour and stretches it backwards over the whole rebellion.

Nathan Algren, the real foreign advisers, and how Saigō really died

No American officer trained the imperial army in anything like this way. Japan had bought American surplus equipment around the end of the US Civil War, but by the 1870s it had chosen other models. A French military mission — nine officers and fourteen NCOs — ran the army's modernisation from 1872 to 1880, helping draft the 1873 conscription law and founding the Toyama school, the Ichigaya military academy (1875) and the arsenals. The navy was trained by a British mission under Archibald Lucius Douglas from 1873. From 1885 to 1888 the Prussian major Jakob Meckel rebuilt the army on German lines and taught some sixty of its most senior officers.

The closest real man to Algren is French, and he belongs to an earlier war. Jules Brunet (1838–1911) arrived with the French mission in January 1867 as an artillery instructor. When the shogunate fell, he resigned his commission in October 1868 rather than go home, and fought against the imperial side in the Boshin War — second-in-command of the army of the short-lived Republic of Ezo, roughly 3,000 men beaten by around 7,000 imperial troops at Hakodate in June 1869. He was evacuated to France, suspended for six months, rejoined the army, fought in the Franco-Prussian War and became a general of division in 1898. Japan later decorated him: the Order of the Rising Sun in 1881 and 1885, Grand Officer of the Order of the Sacred Treasure in 1895. He is widely named as the model for Algren — a foreign officer who really did change sides, nine years before the rebellion the film is set in.

The death is the hardest change to defend. Seppuku — ritual suicide, with a trusted second whose duty was to end the pain quickly — had already been abolished as a judicial punishment in 1873. At Shiroyama, Saigō was shot in the hip and abdomen, hitting the femoral artery, and was carried down the hill by his follower Beppu Shinsuke. The accepted account is that he took his own life with Beppu acting as his second; but the autopsy found no abdominal wound consistent with the ritual, and historians think he was probably too badly injured to perform it and was beheaded by Beppu so that his body would not be taken. The film gives Katsumoto a calm, chosen death and a field of kneeling soldiers. The record is harsher, quicker and much less certain.

One last thing the film gets right in spirit. Japan did not erase him. On 22 February 1889, in the amnesty marking the new constitution, Saigō was posthumously pardoned and his court rank restored, and in 1898 Takamura Kōun's bronze of him — in plain clothes, with his dog, no armour anywhere — was unveiled in Ueno Park in Tokyo, where it still stands.

Task B · Omura or Katsumoto? (4 minutes)

The film gives Omura the villain's role, but his argument was the one history took: modernise at speed, or be carved up by foreign powers as China had been. Katsumoto's answer is that a nation which throws away everything it was has nothing left worth defending. Build a real case for one of them, then argue the other side. Where is the line between necessary reform and destroying a people's identity? Bring in examples from your own country — a language, a trade, a way of farming, a whole class of people made obsolete.

Task C · Who gets to tell the story? (4 minutes)

This film made millions of people care about 1877 Japan — and taught most of them something untrue about it. When a story is beautiful and inaccurate, what do we owe the record? Make your case.

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The real rebels had rifles and artillery and still lost, because the other side had more of everything. Is that a less moving story than swords against guns — or a more honest one?
  2. Saigō Takamori helped create the government that destroyed him. Is there something admirable in a person who fights the thing he built, or only something tragic?
  3. The film ends with one man's gesture changing an emperor's mind. Real history almost never works like that. Why do we keep telling it that way?
