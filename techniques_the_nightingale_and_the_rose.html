The Nightingale and the Rose

Oscar Wilde (1888) — a bird gives her life so a boy may have a red rose, and learns what the world thinks love is worth

Easier version available: read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Read in three parts

Wilde wrote this as a fairy tale for adults — it has the shape of a children's fable and the cruelty of a grown-up truth. Read each part, pause for the discussion box, then continue. The turn arrives near the very end — do not skip ahead.

Part 1 — The Student weeps, and the Nightingale hears him

"She said that she would dance with me if I brought her red roses," cried the young Student, "but in all my garden there is no red rose." From her nest in the holm-oak tree the Nightingale heard him, and she looked out through the leaves and wondered.

"No red rose in all my garden!" he cried, and his beautiful eyes filled with tears. "Ah, on what little things does happiness depend! I have read all that the wise men have written, and all the secrets of philosophy are mine, yet for want of a red rose is my life made wretched."

"Here at last is a true lover," said the Nightingale. "Night after night have I sung of him, though I knew him not: night after night have I told his story to the stars, and now I see him. His hair is dark as the hyacinth-blossom, and his lips are red as the rose of his desire; but passion has made his face like pale ivory, and sorrow has set her seal upon his brow."

"The Prince gives a ball tomorrow night," murmured the young Student, "and my love will be of the company. If I bring her a red rose she will dance with me till dawn. But there is no red rose in my garden, so I shall sit lonely, and she will pass me by. She will have no heed of me, and my heart will break."

"Here indeed is the true lover," said the Nightingale. "What I sing of, he suffers: what is joy to me, to him is pain. Surely Love is a wonderful thing. It is more precious than emeralds, and dearer than fine opals."

Pause and discuss:
  • The Student weeps over a flower. Is this real love, or is he in love with the idea of being in love?
  • The Nightingale has sung about Love her whole life without ever seeing it. What is the difference between singing about something and living it?
  • Wilde calls the Student a "true lover" through the Nightingale's voice. Do you trust her judgement here?

Part 2 — The price of a red rose

The Nightingale flew to a rose-tree growing beneath the Student's window. "Give me a red rose," she cried, "and I will sing you my sweetest song." But the tree shook its head. "My roses are white," it answered; "go to my brother who grows round the old sun-dial, and perhaps he will give you what you want."

So she flew to the rose-tree by the sun-dial. "Give me a red rose," she cried, "and I will sing you my sweetest song." But the tree shook its head. "My roses are yellow," it answered; "go to my brother who grows beneath the Student's window, and perhaps he will give you what you want."

So she flew to the rose-tree beneath the window. "Give me a red rose," she cried, "and I will sing you my sweetest song." But the tree shook its head. "My roses are red," it answered, "but the winter has chilled my veins, and the storm has broken my branches, and I shall have no roses at all this year."

"One red rose is all I want," cried the Nightingale, "only one red rose! Is there no way by which I can get it?"

"There is a way," answered the tree, "but it is so terrible that I dare not tell it to you. If you want a red rose, you must build it out of music by moonlight, and stain it with your own heart's-blood. You must sing to me with your breast against a thorn. All night long you must sing, and the thorn must pierce your heart, and your life-blood must flow into my veins, and become mine."

"Death is a great price to pay for a red rose," cried the Nightingale, "and Life is very dear to all. Yet Love is better than Life, and what is the heart of a bird compared to the heart of a man?" So she spread her brown wings for flight, and soared into the air. And when the Moon shone in the heavens the Nightingale flew to the rose-tree, and set her breast against the thorn.

All night long she sang, and the thorn went deeper and deeper into her breast, and her life-blood ebbed away from her. She sang first of the birth of love in the heart of a boy and a girl. And on the topmost spray of the rose-tree there blossomed a marvellous rose, petal following petal as song followed song. "Press closer, little Nightingale," cried the tree, "or the Day will come before the rose is finished." So the Nightingale pressed closer against the thorn, and louder and louder grew her song, for she sang of the Love that is perfected by Death, of the Love that dies not in the tomb.

And the marvellous rose became crimson, like the rose of the eastern sky. Crimson was the girdle of petals, and crimson as a ruby was the heart. But the Nightingale's voice grew fainter, and a film came over her eyes. Fainter and fainter grew her song, and she felt something choking her in her throat. Then she gave one last burst of music. The pale Moon heard it, and she forgot the dawn, and lingered on in the sky. The red rose heard it, and it trembled all over with ecstasy, and opened its petals to the cold morning air. "Look, look!" cried the tree, "the rose is finished now"; but the Nightingale made no answer, for she was lying dead in the long grass, with the thorn in her heart.

Pause and discuss:
  • The Nightingale chooses death freely: "Love is better than Life." Is her reasoning noble, or naïve?
  • Wilde makes the rose grow "petal following petal as song followed song." Why tie the flower so tightly to her singing — and her dying?
  • No human being witnesses the sacrifice. Does that make it more beautiful or more tragic?

Part 3 — The Student, the girl, and the gutter

And at noon the Student opened his window and looked out. "Why, what a wonderful piece of luck!" he cried; "here is a red rose! I have never seen any rose like it in all my life. It is so beautiful that I am sure it has a long Latin name"; and he leaned down and plucked it. Then he put on his hat, and ran up to the Professor's house with the rose in his hand.

The daughter of the Professor was sitting in the doorway winding blue silk on a reel, and her little dog was lying at her feet. "You said that you would dance with me if I brought you a red rose," cried the Student. "Here is the reddest rose in all the world. You will wear it tonight next to your heart, and as we dance together it will tell you how I love you."

But the girl frowned. "I am afraid it will not go with my dress," she answered; "and, besides, the Chamberlain's nephew has sent me some real jewels, and everybody knows that jewels cost far more than flowers."

"Well, upon my word, you are very ungrateful," said the Student angrily; and he threw the rose into the street, where it fell into the gutter, and a cart-wheel went over it.

"Ungrateful!" said the girl. "I tell you what, you are very rude; and, after all, who are you? Only a Student. Why, I don't believe you have even got silver buckles to your shoes as the Chamberlain's nephew has"; and she got up from her chair and went into the house.

"What a silly thing Love is," said the Student as he walked away. "It is not half as useful as Logic, for it does not prove anything, and it is always telling one of things that are not going to happen, and making one believe things that are not true. In fact, it is quite unpractical, and, as in this age to be practical is everything, I shall go back to Philosophy and study Metaphysics."

So he returned to his room and pulled out a great dusty book, and began to read.

The final discussion — the turn lands:
  • Read the girl's line again — "everybody knows that jewels cost far more than flowers." What does it just do to everything the Nightingale died for?
  • The Student abandons Love for "Logic" and "Metaphysics." Has he learned anything, or nothing at all?
  • The rose ends in a gutter, crushed by a cart-wheel. Why does Wilde refuse to give the sacrifice any reward?
  • Your turn: When does generosity get measured "in the wrong currency"? Think of an example — from this story, a film, or public life — where real effort was valued only in money or status.

How Wilde tells the story — the fable, the voice, the irony

Wilde dresses a bleak idea in the soft clothes of a fairy tale. Notice three of his choices and what each one buys him.

ChoiceWhat it isMarker in the text
The fable formA simple, moral tale with the rhythm of a children's story"Night after night have I sung of him…" — the repeated, chant-like phrasing of fairy tales
AnthropomorphismAnimals and plants given human feeling, speech and judgementThe Nightingale reasons about Love; the rose-trees speak and shake their heads
IronyA gap between what is said and what is true, or between effort and outcome"everybody knows that jewels cost far more than flowers" — said over a flower that cost a life

The grammar this story teaches itself

This tale is full of wanting, trying, daring and choosing — exactly the verbs that force a decision between the gerund (-ing) and the infinitive (to do). Three sets below, all drawn straight from the story.

1. Verb + -ing or verb + to-infinitive?

Some verbs take a gerund (enjoy doing, keep doing, avoid doing); some take a to-infinitive (want to do, decide to do, dare to do, be willing to do). Fill each gap with the correct form.

1. The Nightingale was willing (die) for Love.

2. She kept (sing) all night, the thorn pressing deeper.

3. The rose-tree did not dare (tell) her the terrible price at first.

4. The Student decided (go) back to his books.

5. The girl avoided (look) at the rose for more than a moment.

1. to die (be willing + to-infinitive)

2. singing (keep + -ing)

3. to tell (dare + to-infinitive)

4. to go (decide + to-infinitive)

5. looking (avoid + -ing)

2. "Worth doing" and other -ing patterns

After worth, after prepositions, and after phrases like it's no use / there's no point, English uses the gerund, not the infinitive.

1. The Nightingale believed Love was worth (have), whatever the cost.

2. There was no point in (argue) with the girl about the rose.

3. The Student was incapable of (feel) what the bird had done.

4. It is no use (weep) over a rose you will not value.

1. having (worth + -ing)

2. arguing (point in + -ing — preposition)

3. feeling (incapable of + -ing — preposition)

4. weeping (it is no use + -ing)

3. "Stop to do" vs "stop doing" — the meaning change

A handful of verbs take both forms — but the meaning changes. Choose the form that fits the sense in brackets.

1. The Student stopped (study) when he heard the Nightingale's song. (he interrupted his studying)

2. On his way home he stopped (listen) to the last notes of her song. (he paused in order to listen)

3. The girl did not remember (promise) the dance. (the promise came first, the memory after)

4. Please remember (value) what is given freely. (a thing to do in future)

1. studying (stop + -ing = end the activity)

2. to listen (stop + to-infinitive = pause in order to do something)

3. promising (remember + -ing = recall a past action)

4. to value (remember + to-infinitive = not forget a future task)

The rule of thumb: with stop / remember / forget, the -ing form looks backwards at something already happening or done; the to-infinitive looks forwards at a purpose or a task still to come. "I stopped smoking" (I quit) vs "I stopped to smoke" (I paused for a cigarette).

What Wilde does — and the verbs to describe it

Four moves to spot. They are why this little fable still cuts in 2026.

1. Situational irony — the wasted sacrifice

The Nightingale gives her life so the Student may win a dance. The Student loses the girl anyway; the rose ends in a gutter under a cart-wheel. The gap between the enormity of the gift and the worthlessness of the outcome ironises the whole tale. Wilde destabilises the fairy-tale promise that love and sacrifice are rewarded.

2. Symbolism — the red rose

The rose is built "out of music by moonlight" and "stained with heart's-blood." It is no longer a flower; it is a symbol of love made out of pure self-giving. When the girl rejects it for jewels and a cart-wheel crushes it, Wilde recasts the symbol of perfect love as something the world will not even pause to look at.

3. Anthropomorphism and the fable form

By giving the bird and the rose-trees speech and feeling, Wilde elevates the natural world into the moral centre of the story — and lowers the humans. The fable form lets him say something bleak in a gentle voice; the soft, repetitive rhythm distils a hard idea into something we'll swallow.

4. Romantic idealism versus materialism

The Nightingale measures love against death and finds love greater. The girl measures a rose against jewels and finds the rose worthless. Wilde juxtaposes these two scales of value and reframes the question the story seems to ask: not "will the boy win the girl?" but "what is anything truly worth, and who gets to decide?"

Speaking tasks

Do all four. Each one builds on the last. The timer is for your benefit — it forces you to fill the time rather than escape it. Use gerunds and infinitives, and effect verbs from the previous tabs.

Time remaining: 4:00
Task A · Retell the story in your own words (3 minutes)

Before we discuss it, tell the story back to me as if I had never read it — in your own words, not line by line. Aim for about eight to ten sentences.

As you go, use at least five words from the Vocabulary tab and keep the events in order using a range of gerunds and infinitives from the Grammar tab. These beats will help you: a student weeps because he needs a red rose to win a dance with the girl he loves · a nightingale, believing in true love, resolves to help · a rose tree says the only way is to sing all night with a thorn against her heart, giving her life · she sacrifices herself and a perfect red rose is born · the student takes it to the girl — she rejects it for jewels, and he throws the rose in the gutter.

Task B · A sacrifice that went unappreciated (4 minutes)

Think of a time you — or someone you know — gave something real and had it ignored or undervalued. A favour no one thanked you for; effort no one noticed; a gift that did not "go with the dress." Describe three things, in order:

  1. what was given, and what it cost the giver;
  2. how the other person received it — what they measured it against;
  3. what you understood afterwards about the two people involved.

Use at least three gerund or infinitive patterns ("worth doing", "willing to give", "no point in arguing", "kept trying") and at least two effect verbs from the Technique tab (ironises, reframes, recasts…).

Task C · Love or practicality — which should win? (3 minutes)

The Student ends by declaring Love "quite unpractical" and returns to Logic and Metaphysics. The girl chooses jewels over a rose. Both choose the practical.

Argue one side, then the other. First, make the case that practicality should win — that the girl and the Student are simply being sensible. Then switch, and defend the Nightingale's scale of value — that some things are worth giving everything for, even when no one notices.

Build each side around the "stop to do / stop doing" idea: what would you have to stop doing, and what would you pause to do, to live by each value?

Task D · Something beautiful made at great cost (4 minutes)

The rose is "built out of music by moonlight." Describe something real that you find beautiful because of what it cost to make — a building, a performance, a meal, a piece of work, a relationship, a recovery.

For two minutes, describe the thing and the cost of making it. For two more, argue whether the cost makes it more beautiful, or whether beauty should not have to hurt at all.

Use "worth + -ing" ("it was worth building / worth waiting for…") and at least one verb that changes meaning with the gerund or infinitive (remember, forget, stop).

Final open discussion — if time allows

  1. The Nightingale dies for a love that was never real and a boy who never knew. Is a sacrifice still noble if it is wasted, unseen, and built on a mistake?
  2. The girl puts a price on the rose; the Nightingale puts her life on it. What do you refuse to put a price on? Why do people draw that line in different places?
  3. The Student returns to his books, certain he has learned something. What do you think he actually learned, if anything — and what did Wilde want us to learn?
  4. If you could give the Nightingale one warning before she flew to the thorn, would you? Or is there something worth protecting in not knowing the outcome before you give?
