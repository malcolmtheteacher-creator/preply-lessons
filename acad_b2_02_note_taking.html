B2 Level · Study Skills

The Purpose of Taking Notes

Note-taking serves three critical functions in university learning:

1. Active Processing

Writing forces your brain to engage with material actively rather than passively listening. Simply sitting in a lecture, even while concentrating, results in lower retention than engaging through note-taking. By translating what you hear into your own words, you internalize concepts more deeply.

2. Creating a Study Resource

Your notes become your personal textbook for revision. Well-organized notes allow you to prepare for exams efficiently without rereading entire textbooks.

3. Identifying Knowledge Gaps

When you write down questions as you take notes, you reveal what you don't understand. This guides your further study.

The Myth of "Complete Notes"

A common mistake is trying to write down everything. This is ineffective because:

  • You fall behind and miss context
  • You don't have time to think about what you're writing
  • Complete transcription isn't processing—it's transcribing
  • During revision, you read verbatim words without understanding

Better approach: Capture main ideas, key terms, and enough detail to understand later. Quality over quantity.

Research shows: Students who take notes by hand (not laptop) retain more information and score higher on conceptual questions. Typing encourages verbatim transcription; handwriting forces summarization.

Three Proven Note-Taking Methods

Different methods work for different learning styles and contexts. Experiment to find what works for you.

Method 1: Cornell Method

Divide your page into three sections:

  • Left (1/4): Questions and key terms
  • Right (2/3): Detailed notes during lecture
  • Bottom (1/6): Summary after class

Best for: Lectures with clear structure; students who like organization

Q: What are the three branches of govt? | Notes: Lecture on govt structure
Q: Examples of checks/balances? | - Executive, Legislative, Judicial
How does each check power? | - Separated powers ensure no single branch dominates
| - Checks and balances system
__________________________________
Summary: Three branches of government with checks/balances

Method 2: Mind Mapping

A visual, non-linear approach ideal for understanding relationships:

  • Start with main topic in center
  • Branch out with main ideas
  • Add sub-branches with details
  • Use colors, symbols, drawings

Best for: Complex topics with many connections; visual learners; brainstorming

(Imagine: "Photosynthesis" in center)
Light reactions → Electron transport, ATP production
Calvin Cycle → CO2 fixation, glucose formation
Chlorophyll → absorbs light energy

Method 3: Linear/Outline Method

Traditional hierarchical structure using indentation:

  • Main point (left margin)
  • Sub-point (indented)
  • Detail (further indented)

Best for: Sequential lectures; students who naturally think in outline form

I. The Renaissance
   A. Historical context (14th-17th century)
      1. Followed Medieval period
      2. Began in Italy
   B. Key characteristics
      1. Humanistic focus

Universal Note-Taking Strategies

Use Abbreviations

Develop consistent shortcuts:

  • w/ = with, w/o = without
  • govt = government, esp. = especially
  • cf. = compare with, i.e. = that is
  • → = leads to, increases; ← = comes from
  • ↑ = increases; ↓ = decreases
  • ∴ = therefore, ∵ = because

Mark Information Hierarchy

Distinguish importance through formatting:

  • Underline main concepts
  • Star (*) information likely on exams
  • Question mark (?) things you don't understand
  • Brackets [...] additional context you add
  • Red pen for later corrections or additions

Review and Rewrite

Within 24 hours of taking notes:

  • Read through your notes
  • Fill in gaps while memory is fresh
  • Clarify unclear passages
  • Add examples or connections
  • Highlight key concepts

Practice: Taking Notes from a Lecture

Below is a short lecture excerpt on "Climate Change and Ocean Acidification." Read it as if listening to a lecture. Use the Cornell Method to take notes. You have 5 minutes total—don't try to capture everything!

Lecture: Climate Change and Ocean Acidification

"Good morning. Today we're discussing a critical issue that connects atmospheric chemistry to marine ecosystems. You've probably heard about climate change, but ocean acidification is equally important and less discussed. Here's the basic science: When carbon dioxide enters the atmosphere—primarily from burning fossil fuels—much of it is absorbed by the ocean. The ocean covers about 71% of Earth's surface, so it's absorbing roughly 30% of all CO2 emissions. When CO2 dissolves in seawater, it forms carbonic acid, which increases the acidity of the ocean. We're talking about a 30% increase in acidity since pre-industrial times. That might not sound dramatic, but on a pH scale, that's massive. Now, why does this matter? Many marine organisms—coral, shellfish, pteropods, sea urchins—they all build shells and skeletons from calcium carbonate. When the ocean becomes more acidic, it's harder for these organisms to build and maintain their structures. We're seeing this already: oyster farms in the Pacific are experiencing massive die-offs. Fish larvae depend on pteropods—tiny swimming snails—for food. When pteropods struggle, the entire food web is affected. By 2050, if current trends continue, some regions will reach saturation points where shell-forming becomes nearly impossible. We're not just talking about some lost oysters. We're talking about fundamental changes to ocean ecosystems and the food security of millions of people who depend on fish."

Try the Cornell Method

Your task: Create notes using the structure below. Focus on main ideas, not every word.

LEFT COLUMN (Questions):

What is ocean acidification?
Why does it happen?
What organisms are affected?
What's the timeline?

RIGHT COLUMN (Notes):

- Ocean absorbs CO2 → forms carbonic acid
- Ocean acidity ↑ 30% since pre-industrial
- pH scale change = significant
- Organisms w/ calcium carbonate shells affected
- Examples: coral, shellfish, pteropods
- Food chain disruption
- By 2050: saturation point in some regions
- Threatens food security

BOTTOM (Summary):

Ocean acidification occurs when CO2 dissolves in seawater. This harms shell-forming organisms and disrupts marine food webs, threatening ecosystem stability and human food security.

Reflection

Could you have captured this in fewer words?

Yes. Notice the example above skipped the lecturer's transitions and explanatory phrases. The note-taker captured the essence in simple language. "Ocean absorbs CO2 → carbonic acid" contains the key concept without unnecessary words.

What information is essential? What's extra?

Essential: mechanism (CO2 absorption), consequences (acidification), affected organisms, timeline, threat to humans. Extra: the 71% ocean coverage statistic, the "good morning" greeting, repeated emphasis on importance (though main point should appear once).

Building Your Personal System

Effective note-taking isn't one-size-fits-all. Your system must fit your learning style, your subjects, and your university.

Step 1: Choose Your Medium

Handwriting vs. Laptop?

Handwriting forces summarization and is better for retention. Laptops are faster for content-heavy lectures. Compromise: handwrite main lectures; use laptop for reading notes or technical material. Never try to transcribe verbatim.

Step 2: Select a Method

Choose based on your learning style:

  • Organized learner? Try Cornell or Linear
  • Visual learner? Try Mind Mapping
  • Uncertain? Start with Cornell—it's most flexible

Step 3: Create a Consistent Format

Consistency makes revision easier. Decide:

  • What abbreviations you'll use
  • How you'll mark importance
  • Date and topic format
  • Where you'll store notes

Step 4: Practice and Adjust

Your first attempt won't be perfect. After each lecture:

  • Notice what was hard to capture
  • Identify where your notes have gaps
  • When revising, refine your format
  • After 3-4 lectures, you'll have a rhythm

Specific System Checklist

Before your next lecture, decide:

  • ☐ Which method (Cornell, Mind Map, Linear)?
  • ☐ Handwriting, typing, or combination?
  • ☐ Three abbreviations you'll use (w/, ↑, ∴)
  • ☐ How you'll mark important info (underline, star, bracket)
  • ☐ When you'll review and revise (24 hours, best time)?
  • ☐ Where you'll store notes (binder, digital folder, OneNote)?

University reality: You'll take thousands of pages of notes during your degree. A system that works now will serve you for years. Invest time now in finding your method.
