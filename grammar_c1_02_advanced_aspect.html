The Result or the Effort?

Present perfect simple reports a result. Present perfect continuous keeps the activity — and its duration — in the picture.

Spot the pattern

Three things people say about work in progress:

  • "I've cleaned the whole flat — it's spotless now."
  • "I've been cleaning the flat all day — I still haven't touched the kitchen."
  • "We've moved into the new office."

Before you check: which two sentences report a finished result, and which one keeps the activity itself going? What tells you?

Check the pattern CLICK
have/has + past participle reports a result — often complete, often countable ("the whole flat", "the new office"). have/has + been + -ing keeps the activity and its duration on stage — often unfinished, often explaining something you can see or feel right now.
have / has + past participle → the result
have / has + been + verb-ing → the activity / how long

The same choice, one step further back in time

"I had finished the report by six o'clock."
→
"I had been writing the report for three hours when the fire alarm went off."

The first reports a completed result before another past point. The second keeps the activity and its duration in view — and often explains a state: ink-stained fingers, a headache, a half-drunk coffee gone cold.

had + past participle → the result, before that point
had + been + verb-ing → the activity leading up to that point

Temporary, Changing, or Just Annoying

The continuous does more than describe "right now" — it also marks something temporary, a change still happening, or a habit you're a bit tired of.

Spot the pattern

  • "I'm staying with my cousin while my flat's being repainted."
  • "House prices are climbing fast in this neighbourhood."
  • "He's constantly checking his phone during dinner — it drives me up the wall."

None of these describe something happening at this exact second. So what is the continuous doing in each one? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Sentence 1: a temporary situation, not a permanent home. Sentence 2: a change still in progress — prices keep moving. Sentence 3: with constantly / always / forever, the continuous turns an ordinary habit into something the speaker finds excessive — usually irritation, sometimes fondness.
am / is / are + verb-ing → a temporary situation, or a change still happening
always / forever / constantly + am / is / are + verb-ing → a habit judged as "too much"

From plain fact to complaint

"She leaves her bike outside."
→
"She's always leaving her bike outside — I keep tripping over it."

Same habit, same person. The simple present states it as neutral fact. Add "always" and the continuous, and it becomes a complaint about frequency — as if it happens more often than it should.

Bending the Rules on Purpose

Verbs like love, be and see usually describe states, not actions — so they don't normally take the continuous. Used deliberately, that "broken rule" carries real meaning.

Spot the pattern

  • "I'm loving this new job — every day's different."
  • "You're being incredibly patient with him today."
  • "I'm seeing the dentist at four, so I can't make the call."

Love, be and see are normally state verbs — they don't usually appear with -ing. So why does it work here, and what changes? Say your idea before you check.

Check the pattern CLICK
Used on purpose, the continuous shifts a state verb into something else: loving = a vivid, temporary burst of enthusiasm, not a permanent fact like "I love." being + adjective = behaviour right now, not a fixed personality trait. seeing here means an arranged meeting, not perception — a completely different, already-dynamic meaning of the verb.
state verb + verb-ing (deliberate) → a vivid or temporary feeling,
temporary behaviour (not permanent character),
or a different, dynamic meaning of the same verb

From permanent character to today's behaviour

"He is impatient."
→
"He's being impatient."

The first is a fixed trait — that's just who he is. The second is about today, or this moment — usually out of character, and often a mild complaint.
