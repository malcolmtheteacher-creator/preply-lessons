A bridge from the old world to the new

The Saxons became the English. They built kingdoms, they learned Christ, they grew strong. Alfred the Great defended them against the Vikings. For a time, it seemed the storms had passed.

But the sea always keeps its children. And in the early 900s, the second wave came.

II. The Second Viking Age & Canute

Ethelred "the Unready" (899-1016)

Alfred's great-great-grandson was called Ethelred. The nickname stuck: "the Unready." But as Marshall reminds us, this did not mean he was unprepared for battle. Rather, he was a man who listened to poor counsel.

As the Danish raids intensified, Ethelred faced an impossible choice. Rather than risk his army in pitched battle, he did something that would echo through the centuries: he bought peace with silver. Marshall tells us:

Ethelred did not like fighting. He was weak and easily led. Instead of fighting the Danes, he would rather pay them money to go away. His nobles were angry at this cowardly plan, but Ethelred would not listen to them. Year after year he paid money to the Danes. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XVIII

This treasure became known as the "Danegeld" — not a single payment, but year after year of silver. The effect was catastrophic. The Danes always returned, demanding more. It was protection money that never protected.

Canute the Great (1016-1035)

In 1016, after decades of chaos, a Danish king named Canute defeated Ethelred's son in battle and took the throne of England. But something remarkable happened.

Canute did not begin by being a good king. At first he was bad and cruel. But he ended by being very good and wise. In fact he seems to have ruled so well that the English came to love him almost as if he had been an English king. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XX

The Tide Story

Canute's nobles, delighted to have a strong king, began to flatter him. They praised him as a man of such power that even the forces of nature obeyed him. This foolish talk did not please a wise man. To teach them a lesson in humility, Marshall recounts the famous tale:

Canute ordered his servants to bring a chair and set it on the shore close to the waves. He sat upon it and commanded the waves to go no farther. "Go back," he said, "I am your lord and master, and I command you not to flow over my land. Go back, and do not dare to wet my feet." — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XX

Of course, the tide did not obey. The waves rolled in, soaking Canute's feet and robes. He rose and turned to his astonished nobles.

You can see now how powerless I am. No one can command the waves, no one can rule the sea. There is only one King to whom all owe allegiance — the King of Heaven. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XX (paraphrased)

From that day forward, Canute never wore his crown in public. Instead, he laid it upon a crucifix in Winchester Cathedral — a gesture of Christian humility that would be remembered for a thousand years.

The historian's note: Canute did indeed become an effective and widely-respected king. He ruled not only England but Denmark and Norway as well, creating a powerful North Sea empire. He was a genuine Christian convert and a patron of the Church. The "waves" story may have been invented by later chroniclers to show his wisdom and humility, but it perfectly captures how he was remembered: not as a Viking conqueror, but as a Christian king who understood his proper place before God. When he died in 1035, even the English grieved him as their own.

III. Edward the Confessor & the Succession Crisis

Edward the Confessor (1042-1066)

After Canute's death and a brief period of instability, the crown passed to a man very different from the warrior-king who had come before. His name was Edward, and he would be the last Saxon king.

Edward was gentle and pious. He loved to build churches and to buy holy relics more than to guard his kingdom and make his people happy. After his death people began to think that he was really a holy man and called him Edward the Confessor. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XXI

Edward was a man torn between two worlds. He had spent his youth in Normandy (northern France), and he brought Norman courtiers and Norman ways back to England. Meanwhile, Saxon nobles — especially the powerful family of Earl Godwin and his son Harold — felt the kingdom slipping from their grasp.

The historian's note: Edward founded Westminster Abbey, one of the greatest Gothic churches in the world, still standing in London. He was later canonised as a saint. But Marshall is right to point out that he was a pious man in an age when kings needed to be warriors. He left the actual governance to others, and his preference for Norman advisors sowed the seeds of the Norman Conquest that would come in 1066.

The Succession Crisis of January 1066

In January 1066, Edward lay dying. He had no son. The question that would change England forever hung in the air: Who should be king?

There were three claimants:

Three Men, One Throne

1. Harold Godwinson (Saxon) — the powerful Earl of Wessex, son of Earl Godwin. The Saxon nobles supported him. On his deathbed, Edward allegedly blessed Harold as his successor.

2. William of Normandy (Norman) — Edward's distant cousin, already Duke of Normandy. William later claimed that Edward had promised him the throne years before.

3. Harald Hardrada (Norwegian) — the King of Norway, a fierce Viking warrior who claimed the throne by ancient rights of conquest.

Harold Godwinson's Coronation

On January 5, 1066, Edward died. The next day, Harold Godwinson was crowned king by the Archbishop of York. Marshall describes the moment:

The Archbishop of York, holding the crown in his hand, asked "Do you choose Harold, Earl of Wessex, son of Godwin, to be your king?" The people shouted their assent. Then the archbishop anointed Harold with holy oil, placed the crown of England upon his head, and the sceptre in his hand. Harold rose from his knees, no longer Earl of Wessex, but King of England. — H.E. Marshall, Our Island Story, Chapter XXII (paraphrased)

Harold believed his throne was secure. He did not know that his coronation had sealed his fate.

The historian's note: The succession in 1066 was genuinely contested. Harold had legitimacy: he was English, he had the support of the English nobility, and Edward had blessed him. But William of Normandy had already begun gathering ships and soldiers. He would claim that Harold had sworn an oath (probably under duress) to support his claim years earlier. And Harald Hardrada was a legendary warrior who felt he had a legitimate historical claim. The year 1066 would see three battles that would remake England forever. But that is Lesson 8's story.

V. Room 7 of Your British History Palace

ROOM 7: THE DANES & THE LAST SAXON KINGS

Place these 5 images in Room 7

You're now 7 rooms into your 20-room palace. Room 7 holds everything from the return of the Vikings through the coronation of Harold Godwinson in January 1066.

The 5 Images for Room 7

1. Ethelred paying Danegeld — bags and chests of silver coins being handed to a group of fierce, grinning Danish warriors. Year after year, the silver flows out. Weakness and cowardice in a crown.

2. Canute sitting before the waves — a crowned king in a chair on the shore, speaking to the tide, the waves rising higher and higher, soaking his robes. Behind him, astonished nobles. A lesson in humility.

3. Canute placing his crown on a crucifix — the same king, but now inside Winchester Cathedral, removing his crown and laying it reverently on a cross. A Christian king submitting to God.

4. Edward the Confessor praying in Westminster Abbey — a gentle, pious man in a crown, kneeling in prayer, great stone arches rising around him. Norman influence, Saxon blood, but his heart elsewhere.

5. Harold Godwinson crowned in Westminster Abbey — January 1066. The Archbishop of York places the crown on a Saxon earl's head. Bells ring. The people shout. One year left to live.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

Why did Ethelred fail?

Marshall says he "listened to poor counsel." But what was he afraid of? Why did buying peace seem better than fighting? Was he cowardly, or was he trying to save his people from the horrors of war?

The Danegeld paradox

The more silver Ethelred paid, the more the Danes returned. What does this teach about appeasement? When does paying for peace make things worse?

Canute's transformation

Canute "did not begin by being a good king. At first he was bad and cruel. But he ended by being very good and wise." What changed? Can a conqueror truly become a good ruler?

The waves story: real or invented?

Did Canute really sit before the waves and command them to retreat? Does it matter if it's historically true? What makes a story worth telling even if it might not have happened?

Edward the Confessor's weakness

Edward was gentle and pious, but Marshall says he wasn't a great king. Can a kingdom thrive under a kind ruler who isn't strong? What does a nation need from its leader?

Three claimants, one throne

In January 1066, three powerful men had a claim to England: Harold, William, and Harald. If you had to choose, who had the best right? Why did the English choose Harold?

2. Three Kings, Three Stories

Briefly describe Ethelred, Canute, and Edward. What made each one different from the others?

Ethelred the Unready: Weak, easily led by bad counsel, paid the Danes to go away year after year (the Danegeld), but they always returned demanding more. A failure of leadership.

Canute: Began as a cruel conqueror, but transformed into a wise and good king whom even the English came to love. Famous for commanding the waves and learning humility, he laid his crown on a crucifix in humility before God.

Edward the Confessor: Gentle, pious, loved building churches more than governing. Brought Norman influence to England. Died without an heir, leaving the throne to three claimants.

3. The Canute Story

Retell the story of Canute and the waves in your own words:

Canute's nobles flattered him, saying he was so powerful that even the waves obeyed him. To teach them humility, Canute sat in a chair on the shore and commanded the tide to go back. When the waves soaked his feet and robes, he rose and told his nobles that only God possessed such power, not any earthly king. From that day, he never wore his crown publicly, but laid it on a crucifix in Winchester Cathedral.
