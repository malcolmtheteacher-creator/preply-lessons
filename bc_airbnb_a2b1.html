The story, in three parts

Read each part. Then answer the questions before you read the next part.

Part 1 — Three air beds

In October 2007, two friends, Brian Chesky and Joe Gebbia, lived in a flat in San Francisco. They could not pay their rent.

That week a big design conference was in the city. All the hotels were full, and the prices were very high.

The friends had an idea. They had three air beds. They put them on the floor of the living room, made a simple website, and offered a bed and a breakfast.

Three guests came, and each one paid about $80. Chesky and Gebbia were the hosts, and they cooked breakfast every morning.

A friend, Nathan Blecharczyk, joined them to make the website better. In 2008 the company still needed money.

So they made cereal boxes! They called them “Obama O’s” and “Cap’n McCain’s”, because it was election time in the US. They sold the boxes for $40 each and made about $30,000.

In 2009 a programme for new companies, called Y Combinator, gave them money and advice. Then the company grew fast.

Part 2 — The problem of trust

Airbnb had one big problem. Would people trust a stranger? Would you sleep in a stranger’s home? Would you let a stranger sleep in yours?

Airbnb’s answer was reviews. After every stay, the guest writes a review of the home, and the host writes a review of the guest. Good reviews make people feel safe.

In 2011 a host in San Francisco came home and found that her flat was a mess. Her guests had broken and stolen things.

People were angry, and they said Airbnb was not safe. At first the company was slow to answer. Then it made a promise: if a guest damaged a host’s home, Airbnb would pay up to $1 million.

After that, Airbnb added more rules, checked guests’ names and started a help line that was open all day and all night.

By the 2020s there were millions of hosts around the world, and Airbnb had more rooms than the biggest hotel chains.

True or false? Click your answer.

2. Airbnb answered the 2011 problem at once.

3. Airbnb promised to pay money if a home was damaged.

Talk about it

  • Which is better for a traveller: a hotel or a home? Why?

Part 3 — Two shocks

In March 2020 the coronavirus stopped travel across the world. Airbnb’s bookings fell very fast.

In May 2020 the company had to lay off about 1,900 people — around a quarter of its workers. The boss said it was a very painful day for the company.

But people started to travel again. In December 2020 Airbnb sold shares on the stock market, and investors bought them so quickly that on the first day the company was worth about $100 billion.

Then came the second shock. In many cities, people said that Airbnb made life difficult for local families. Owners could earn more from tourists than from a family, so rents went up and neighbours left.

New York made new rules in 2023, and hosts now have to register. Barcelona said it wanted to ban tourist flats completely by 2028.

Airbnb says that hosts are ordinary people who need extra money, and that most of its rooms are in places without many hotels.

So here is the question. Is Airbnb good or bad for the towns where its guests stay?

The best, the worst, the biggest: superlatives

In this story we read about the worst moment and the biggest hotel chains. We use a superlative to say that one thing is number one in a group.

Short words (one syllable): the + word + -est. big → the biggest · cheap → the cheapest · old → the oldest. If the word ends in one vowel and one consonant, double the consonant: big → biggest, hot → hottest. If it ends in -e, add only -st: nice → nicest.

Words ending in -y: change y to i. easy → the easiest · busy → the busiest.

Long words (two or more syllables): the most + word. the most expensive · the most popular · the most beautiful.

Irregular words: good → the best · bad → the worst · far → the farthest.

Always use the. After the superlative, use in + a place (the biggest city in Spain) or of + a group (the cheapest of the three rooms). You can also say one of the + superlative + plural noun: Airbnb is one of the biggest companies in travel.
