Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The old king had three daughters but no son, and his council worried for years about who would inherit the crown — for without a clear heir, the kingdom could slide into civil war.
an heir (n) — the person who will legally inherit a title, throne or property when its owner dies.
2. Unhappy in the marriage, he begged the church to annul it — not to end it, but to declare that it had never been a true marriage at all.
to annul (v) — to declare officially that a marriage or agreement was never valid, as if it had never existed.
3. For a hundred and twenty years a single family had held the throne, father passing it to son, until everyone simply assumed that this dynasty would rule forever.
a dynasty (n) — a line of rulers who all belong to the same family, holding power across several generations.
4. The new ideas spread fast: people began to question the old church, to read scripture for themselves, and to demand change, until the whole movement had a name — the Reformation.
the Reformation (n) — the 16th-century religious movement that broke from the Roman Catholic Church and created the Protestant churches.
5. To speak against the king, to plot his death, or even to deny his authority was the gravest crime of all — treason — and the punishment was death.
treason (n) — the crime of betraying your country or ruler, especially by trying to overthrow or kill them.
6. When the old ruler died, his eldest son climbed the steps, sat down, and took the throne — and with it, power over millions.
the throne (n) — the ceremonial chair of a king or queen; used to mean royal power itself.
7. He spent his days near the palace, bowing low, flattering the powerful and watching for the smallest chance to rise — a true courtier, dependent entirely on the king's favour.
a courtier (n) — a person who attends a royal court; an attendant or companion of a king or queen.
8. At dawn the prisoner was led out, made to kneel, and the executioner raised the sword to behead them before the silent crowd.
to behead (v) — to cut off someone's head, especially as a form of execution.
9. Everything depended on the order of succession: who came next, and after them who, so that power could pass from one ruler to the next without a fight.
succession (n) — the order in which people have the right to inherit a throne or position, one after another.
10. What began as a small disagreement grew wider and colder until neither side would speak to the other — a deep rift that no apology could close.
a rift (n) — a serious break or split in a relationship or group, often hard to repair.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — A new dynasty, and a king in a hurry

On 22 August 1485, on a field at Bosworth in the heart of England, a long and bloody struggle for the crown — the Wars of the Roses — came to its end. King Richard III was killed in the fighting, and a relatively unknown nobleman, Henry Tudor, picked up the crown and became Henry VII. To unite the warring families, he married Elizabeth of York. A new dynasty had begun, but it stood on fragile ground: only a steady line of sons could keep it safe.

Henry VII's second son came to the throne in 1509 as Henry VIII — young, athletic, clever, and convinced of his own greatness. Within weeks of becoming king he married Catherine of Aragon, the daughter of the powerful Spanish monarchs and the widow of his own elder brother, Arthur, who had died young. For years the marriage seemed strong. But it carried one quiet failure that would grow into a national crisis: of all their children, only a daughter, Mary, survived.

To Henry, a daughter was not enough. No woman, he believed, had ever held England together as a ruling queen, and he feared that without a son the Tudor line — and the peace it had brought — would collapse back into civil war. As the years passed and Catherine grew older, his patience ran out. It was high time, he decided, that he had a son. And if this marriage could not give him one, then he would find a marriage that could.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How did the Tudor dynasty begin, and why did Henry VIII feel its survival was uncertain?

2. What was the "quiet failure" of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon?

1. Henry Tudor won the crown at Bosworth in 1485, ending the Wars of the Roses; the new dynasty felt fragile because it needed a steady line of male heirs to avoid a return to civil war.

2. Of all his children with Catherine, only a daughter, Mary, survived — and Henry was desperate for a son.

Discuss:
  • Henry was sure a daughter could not hold the country together. Where do beliefs like that come from — and how do they finally get proved wrong?

Part 2 — The King's Great Matter

Two things now pulled Henry in the same direction. He had fallen for Anne Boleyn, a sharp, ambitious young woman at his court who refused to become merely his mistress — she would be his wife and queen, or nothing. And he had convinced himself that his marriage to Catherine had been a sin from the start, because she had first been his brother's wife. The solution, he decided, was simple: the Pope must annul the marriage, freeing him to marry Anne. This long campaign became known as "the King's Great Matter."

It was not simple at all. The Pope, Clement VII, could not easily grant it: Catherine's own nephew was Charles V, the most powerful ruler in Europe, whose armies had recently swept into Rome itself. To annul the marriage would be to insult Charles, and Clement would not risk it. Henry's chief minister, Cardinal Wolsey, was sent to win the Pope over, and failed — a failure that cost him everything, his power and very nearly his life.

Henry would rather break with Rome itself than give up Anne and his hope of a son. And so, advised by new men such as Thomas Cromwell and Thomas Cranmer, he took a step no English king had ever taken. If the Pope would not free him, then England would simply stop obeying the Pope.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What two things drove Henry to seek an annulment from Catherine?

2. Why couldn't Pope Clement VII simply grant what Henry wanted?

1. He had fallen in love with Anne Boleyn, who would only accept being his wife and queen; and he had persuaded himself that marrying his late brother's widow had been sinful.

2. Catherine's nephew was the enormously powerful Charles V, whose armies controlled Rome; the Pope dared not insult him by annulling the marriage.

Discuss:
  • Henry framed a very personal desire as a matter of conscience and law. Why do powerful people so often need to tell themselves their wishes are also the right thing to do?

Part 3 — The break, and the fall

Events moved quickly. In January 1533, Henry secretly married Anne Boleyn, who was already pregnant. That spring, Archbishop Cranmer declared the marriage to Catherine invalid, and in June Anne was crowned queen. Then, in 1534, the Act of Supremacy made Henry the Supreme Head of the Church of England. The thousand-year tie to the Pope in Rome was cut. England had taken its first great step into the Reformation, and to deny the king's new authority was now treason, punishable by death.

But the marriage that had caused all this could not save itself. In September 1533, Anne gave birth — not to the longed-for son, but to a daughter, Elizabeth. More pregnancies followed and failed. Within three years Henry's love had curdled into impatience and suspicion, and his eye had already moved to another woman, Jane Seymour. Anne's enemies at court closed in.

In May 1536, Anne Boleyn was arrested and charged with treason and adultery — charges most historians now believe were invented to be rid of her. She was tried, condemned, and on 19 May 1536 beheaded within the walls of the Tower of London. Henry would marry four more times in his search for security. Yet history played a quiet trick on him. The daughter he had been so bitterly disappointed by, Anne's child Elizabeth, would grow up to become Elizabeth I — one of the greatest rulers England has ever known.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What did the Act of Supremacy of 1534 do, and why did it matter?

2. What became of Anne Boleyn — and what was the irony of the daughter she bore?

1. It made Henry the Supreme Head of the Church of England, cutting England's tie to the Pope in Rome and beginning the English Reformation; denying his authority became treason.

2. She was charged with treason and adultery and beheaded at the Tower of London in 1536; the irony is that her "disappointing" daughter became Elizabeth I, one of England's greatest monarchs.

Discuss:
  • Henry broke a country apart for a son, and the child he dismissed became his greatest legacy. What does that say about how much of history is shaped by chance rather than plan?

The grammar this story runs on: would rather / it's time / had better

Henry's whole life was a series of ultimatums — what he preferred, what he felt was overdue, and the warnings he gave those around him. English has three neat structures for exactly those three ideas. They look unusual, because each one is followed by a surprising verb form — get those forms right and you sound natural and forceful.

The three structures at a glance:
• would rather — for a preference. With the same subject, use the base verb: "Henry would rather break with Rome than lose Anne." With a different subject, use the past tense: "He would rather Anne gave him a son."
• it's (high / about) time — for something overdue, mildly critical. Use the past subjunctive (a past form with present meaning): "It's high time he had a son." "It's time the council acted."
• had better — for strong advice or a warning, often with a hidden "or else." Use the base verb: "Anne had better give him an heir." Negative: "He had better not wait."
Watch the verb forms — that's where learners slip:
• would rather + base verb (same subject) → "I would rather stay." NOT "to stay" / "staying".
• would rather + someone + past (different subject) → "I would rather you stayed."
• it's time + past form → "It's time we left." (We mean now, but the verb is past.)
• had better + base verb → "You had better leave" / "You'd better leave." NOT "had better to leave".

1. would rather — same subject or different?

Choose the base verb (same subject) or the past form (different subject).

1. Henry would rather (rule) without the Pope than obey him.

2. He would rather his wife (give) him a son than a daughter.

3. Anne would rather (be) queen than the king's mistress.

4. The Pope would rather Henry (stay) married to Catherine.

1. rule (same subject → base verb)

2. gave (different subject → past form)

3. be (same subject → base verb)

4. stayed (different subject → past form)

2. it's (high) time — the overdue thing

Put the verb into the past form that this structure needs.

1. It was high time Henry (have) a male heir.

2. "It's time you (make) a decision," his advisers told him.

3. It's high time the two churches (end) their long rift.

1. had (past form, present meaning)

2. made

3. ended

3. had better — the warning

Complete each warning with had better (or had better not) plus the base verb.

1. Anne (give) the king a son, or her position is in danger.

2. Wolsey (succeed) with the Pope, or he will lose everything.

3. The courtiers (not / anger) a king who can charge them with treason.

1. had better give

2. had better succeed

3. had better not anger

4. Mix all three

Fill each gap with the right structure and verb form. Read the finished sentences aloud.

1. Henry felt it was high time he (secure) the succession.

2. He would rather England (break) with Rome than accept a "no".

3. "You (not / fail) me again," he warned his minister.

1. secured (it's time + past form)

2. broke (would rather + different subject + past form)

3. had better not fail (had better not + base verb)
