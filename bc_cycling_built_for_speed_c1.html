Tech specs — the parts that make it fast

Click each card. These are the words a serious cyclist actually uses.

drivetrain All the parts that turn pedalling into wheel spin — chain, gears, cranks.
derailleur The mechanism that shifts the chain between gears (pronounced "de-RAIL-yur").
suspension travel How far the shock absorbers can compress — more travel = rougher terrain handled.
gear ratio How many wheel turns you get from one pedal turn. Higher = faster but harder.
aerodynamic drag The air resistance slowing you down. Above 30 km/h it becomes the biggest enemy.
geometry The angles and lengths of the frame — decides whether a bike is twitchy or stable.
tubeless tyres Tyres with no inner tube; sealant inside fixes small punctures automatically.
cadence How fast you pedal — measured in revolutions per minute (RPM).

Quick check — what's the difference?

What's the difference between gear ratio and cadence?

Gear ratio is a property of the bike (how the gears are set up). Cadence is what YOU do (how fast you spin the pedals). You can have a high gear ratio with a low cadence — that's grinding uphill. Or a low gear ratio with high cadence — that's spinning out.

The most extreme version of the sport

Downhill mountain biking is, arguably, the closest a human gets to controlled falling. Riders launch themselves down purpose-built tracks at speeds that regularly exceed 60 kilometres per hour, navigating rock gardens, root sections and jumps that would terrify most motorcyclists.

The bikes are built for one thing: surviving the descent. They have suspension travel of up to 200 millimetres — roughly four times what you'd find on a road bike — and frames designed to be stable when everything around them is chaos. The geometry is "slack", meaning the front wheel sticks out further, making the bike harder to flip but slower to turn.

What makes elite downhill riders extraordinary isn't just their nerve. It's their ability to read terrain in real time. At race speed, a rider has perhaps a tenth of a second to decide whether to brake, jump, or commit to a line. Get it wrong and the consequences are immediate: shattered collarbones are practically a rite of passage, and concussion is so common that most teams now travel with neurologists.

Yet the riders themselves rarely talk about fear. They talk about flow. Mid-run, the brain shuts off the running commentary — the doubts, the hesitation — and the body just reacts. It's the same state a chess grandmaster enters during a fast game, or a gamer hitting a perfect combo: the gap between thinking and doing collapses.

1. The writer says downhill MTB is "controlled falling". Is that a compliment or a criticism?

Compliment, but a wry one. The phrase admits the danger while crediting the skill it takes to manage it. "Controlled" is doing all the work.

2. Why is "slack geometry" useful for downhill?

It makes the bike more stable at speed and harder to flip over the front wheel — but the trade-off is slower steering. You sacrifice agility for survivability.

3. What's the comparison the writer makes between downhill riders and gamers?

Both enter a "flow" state where the gap between thinking and reacting disappears. The chess grandmaster comparison reinforces it — flow is universal, not just physical.
