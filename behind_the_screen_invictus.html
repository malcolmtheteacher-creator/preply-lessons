BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Invictus

A newly elected president bets his authority on a rugby shirt his own supporters wanted destroyed. The match, the score and the jersey are all real.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Invictus — a leader who tries to turn the most hated shirt in his country into the thing that holds it together — discover how much of it actually happened and where the film quietly rewrites the record, and master the verbs English builds out of small words: multi-word (phrasal) verbs, and the rule that decides where the object can go.
Before we begin — talk it through: Think of a flag, a song, a shirt or a statue that means very different things to different people in your country. Who owns a symbol like that — the people who made it, or the people who were hurt by it? And can a sports team really change how a country feels about itself, or is that just a story we enjoy telling?

The words for today's story

Work through the four short activities. Each word is shown in a situation that gives its meaning away — no need to know the film yet. Say your answer out loud first, then reveal.

A. Infer from context — what does the bold word mean?

1. After thirty years of war, the two villages began the slow work of reconciliation: shared meals, apologies spoken in public, and the first mixed classrooms since anyone could remember.
reconciliation (n) — the process of rebuilding a friendly relationship between people or groups who have hurt each other.
2. The habit was so entrenched that three governments in a row had tried to change it and every one of them had given up.
entrenched (adj) — so firmly fixed in place, by long habit or by power, that it is extremely hard to change.
3. Once the scandal broke he became a pariah: old colleagues crossed the street, invitations stopped arriving, and no organisation would put his name on a programme.
a pariah (n) — a person, group or country that everybody refuses to deal with; an outcast.
4. He gave his grudging agreement — a short nod, no handshake, and the muttered remark that he still thought the whole thing was a mistake.
grudging (adj) — given unwillingly and without warmth, while still not really approving.

B. Closest in meaning — which one is nearest?

5. divisive is closest in meaning to: (a) causing people to disagree bitterly   (b) easy to share   (c) badly organised
(a) causing people to disagree bitterly — divisive (adj) describes something that splits a group into hostile sides.
6. to galvanise a crowd is closest in meaning to: (a) to bore them   (b) to shock them into sudden action   (c) to count them
(b) to shock them into sudden action — to galvanise (v) means to jolt people out of passivity into doing something, all at once.

C. Which meaning fits? (choose a, b or c)

7. For years musicians refused to perform there, and other nations agreed to boycott its competitions until the law was repealed.
To boycott something is: (a) to sponsor it; (b) to refuse to take part in it as a protest; (c) to broadcast it.
(b) — to boycott (v) — to refuse, deliberately and publicly, to buy from, deal with or take part in something, in order to force a change. The noun is the same word: a boycott.
8. Ranked eleventh, with two injured players and no money for a coach, they went into the tournament as complete underdogs.
An underdog is: (a) the side everyone expects to lose; (b) the reigning champion; (c) the referee.
(a) — an underdog (n) — the competitor or side expected to lose, and therefore the one people often want to see win.

D. Collocation — which word completes both phrases naturally?

9. "the dove, the ancient ______ of peace"  ·  "the national ______ stitched onto the players' chests"
(a) emblem   (b) medal   (c) uniform
(a) emblem — an emblem (n) is a design, animal or object chosen to stand for a country, a team or an idea. The other two do not fit "of peace".
10. "a ______ relationship between the two families"  ·  "the talks were ______ with difficulty from the first hour"
(a) simple   (b) fraught   (c) cheerful
(b) fraught — fraught (adj) — tense and anxious; and in fraught with, full of something bad (danger, risk, difficulty).

The film, in three parts

Clint Eastwood's Invictus (2009), with Morgan Freeman as Nelson Mandela and Matt Damon as the Springbok captain François Pienaar. Read each part, try the quick check from memory, then discuss. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it.)

Part 1 — A president nobody agrees with

The film opens on a road outside Cape Town in February 1990. On one side, a manicured field where white schoolboys are playing rugby; on the other, a bare patch of ground where black children are playing football. A convoy passes between them. Nelson Mandela has just been released after twenty-seven years in prison, and the two groups of children react in completely opposite ways. That road is the whole country in one shot.

Four years later Mandela is president. He walks into offices where the white civil servants are already packing their boxes, certain they are about to be dismissed, and asks them to stay. His personal bodyguard is a small team of his own ANC men; he then quietly adds several white officers from the old security police — the men who used to hunt people like them. Nobody in the room wants this. He makes them work together anyway.

Meanwhile the national rugby side, the Springboks, are losing badly and are loathed by most of the country. For black South Africans the green-and-gold jersey is a symbol of the regime that oppressed them; they cheer for whichever foreign team is playing against it. When a sports committee votes to scrap the Springbok name and emblem altogether, Mandela leaves a meeting, drives across town and asks them to reverse their own vote — to his own supporters' fury. He then invites the young captain, François Pienaar, to tea.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Mandela do with the white staff and the old security officers when he becomes president?

2. Why do most black South Africans hate the Springbok jersey, and what does the sports committee decide?

1. He asks the white civil servants to stay instead of dismissing them, and puts white former security-police officers into his own bodyguard alongside his ANC men, forcing the two groups to work together.

2. The jersey stands for the regime that oppressed them, so they support the opposition. The committee votes to abolish the Springbok name and emblem — and Mandela goes there in person to argue them out of it.

Discuss:
  • Mandela protects a symbol that hurt him personally, against the wishes of the people who suffered with him. Is that leadership or betrayal? Where is the line?

Part 2 — Tea, a poem, and a prison cell

Over tea, Mandela asks Pienaar an oddly technical question: how do you inspire people to be better than they think they can be? He talks about leading by example, and about the value of surprising people who expect the worst of you. Pienaar leaves confused and slightly overwhelmed, and gradually works out what has actually been asked of him. Later Mandela sends him a poem that, he says, held him steady in prison — verses about staying master of your own soul when everything else has been taken.

The team is sent out to run rugby clinics in the townships. They arrive reluctant and awkward; the children are suspicious; a hesitant hour later there is something close to a game. At the same time, the players are asked to learn the full new national anthem, including the part in Xhosa and Zulu that most of them have never sung. Some do it with grudging bad grace. Some do it properly.

Pienaar takes the squad to Robben Island. He stands alone in the tiny cell where Mandela spent eighteen of those twenty-seven years, looks at the lime quarry that ruined the prisoners' eyes, and tries to imagine coming out of that place without hatred. He does not say much. He does not need to.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Mandela ask Pienaar over tea, and what does he later send him?

2. What two things is the team asked to do away from the rugby pitch?

1. He asks how you inspire people to exceed what they believe they are capable of, and later sends him a poem that sustained him in prison.

2. Run rugby clinics for children in the townships, and learn the whole new national anthem — and Pienaar takes the squad to Mandela's cell on Robben Island.

Discuss:
  • The film argues that a leader changes a country by changing what people are willing to imagine. Is that persuasive, or does it let real politics off the hook?

Part 3 — Eighty minutes at Ellis Park

The World Cup begins, and the Springboks — written off as underdogs — keep winning. Something shifts in the streets. The film keeps returning to a small, telling detail: a black boy outside the stadium, and two white policemen in a patrol car with the radio on, who begin the day wary of each other and end it in a heap of shouting.

The final is against New Zealand, whose enormous wing has been flattening every defence in the tournament. The Springboks decide their only chance is to stop him as a unit. The match is agonising, low-scoring and level at full time. It goes to extra time. Late on, the fly-half drops a goal from distance, and the country loses its mind.

Mandela walks out to present the cup wearing a replica Springbok jersey with the captain's number on the back — the shirt the crowd had spent decades hating, worn by the man it had been used against. The stadium, overwhelmingly white, begins chanting his name. He thanks Pienaar for what he has done for the country; Pienaar turns the thanks straight back at him. The film ends there, in the noise, with the deliberate implication that this was the day the new country began.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. How is the final decided?

2. What is significant about what Mandela is wearing when he presents the trophy?

1. The scores are level at full time; in extra time a long drop goal wins it for South Africa.

2. A Springbok jersey carrying the captain's number — the emblem black South Africans associated with apartheid, now worn by its most famous victim, in front of a mostly white crowd chanting his name.

Discuss:
  • The film stops at the whistle. If you were making it, what would you have shown in the next scene — and would it have spoiled the ending or completed it?

Grammar — multi-word verbs

This is a story of people standing down, backing down, winning each other over and being asked to live up to something. English builds an enormous amount of its everyday meaning out of a plain verb plus one or two small words — and the small words change everything. The hard part isn't the meaning; it's knowing where the object is allowed to go.

Three shapes — learn to spot which one you have:
• Separable (verb + particle + object). The object can sit in the middle or at the end: "He won over his critics." = "He won his critics over." Also: bring together, take on, put behind.
• Inseparable, often three-part (verb + particle + preposition). The object always comes last, and nothing may be inserted: "The negotiators came up against a deadline." Also: look up to, live up to.
• No object at all (intransitive). Nothing follows: "The old committee stood down." "Neither faction would back down." "The clinics carried on all winter."
The pronoun rule — the one that catches everyone:
With a separable verb, a pronoun object must go in the middle. You have no choice:
"They called the tour off." → "They called it off." ✓   "They called off it." ✗
"She brought the two sides together." → "She brought them together." ✓
With an inseparable verb, the pronoun stays at the end and the verb is never split:
"The squad looked up to him." ✓   "The squad looked him up to." ✗
Today's ten, and what they mean: stand down (withdraw from a post or from readiness) · win over (persuade someone who was against you) · bring together (unite) · take on (accept the challenge of; face an opponent) · put (something) behind (you) (stop letting the past control you) · come up against (meet as an obstacle) · carry on (continue) · look up to (admire and respect) · back down (abandon a position in an argument) · live up to (be as good as what was expected).

The Real Story — one afternoon, and what it could and couldn't do

The spine of this film is not invented. The date, the venue, the score, the jersey and the photograph are all exactly as shown. What is worth examining is the frame around them — one detail the film changes outright, one it leaves out entirely, and the much larger question of what a symbol is actually able to achieve.

🏉 24 June 1995, Ellis Park

South Africa beat New Zealand 15–12 in the Rugby World Cup final at Ellis Park in Johannesburg on 24 June 1995, in front of some 65,000 people. The scores were level at the end of normal time, forcing the first extra time in a World Cup final; the fly-half Joel Stransky settled it with a drop goal. It was the first World Cup South Africa had ever been allowed to enter: the sport's governing body had readmitted the country to international rugby only in 1992, as apartheid was being negotiated away. President Nelson Mandela presented the Webb Ellis Cup wearing a Springbok jersey and cap bearing the number 6 — the number of the captain, François Pienaar, who was wearing it too.

Why a shirt was worth a political fight

The Springbok was not a neutral badge. Under apartheid the ruling National Party had adopted the team's success as its own, and the emblem came to stand, for many South Africans, for the regime itself and for who was permitted to represent the country. After 1994 every other national side had moved to the king protea, the country's national flower, and the National Sports Council — the body that had driven the international sporting boycott — wanted rugby to do the same and make the protea the single emblem.

Mandela argued the other way: that keeping the Springbok, rather than abolishing it, was the thing most likely to bring the country together. He was overruling allies, not enemies. The film compresses this into a single dramatic meeting; in reality it was a longer argument, and the Sports Council only formally confirmed the Springbok as rugby's emblem in March 1996 — nine months after the final that is supposed to have settled it. And the squad that carried it onto the pitch that day contained exactly one player of colour, the wing Chester Williams.

Two things the film alters

The poem. "Invictus" is by the English poet William Ernest Henley, written in 1875 and published, untitled, in his 1888 Book of Verses; the title was given to it later, by the editor Arthur Quiller-Couch, for the 1900 Oxford Book of English Verse. Mandela's connection to it is genuine and documented — he recited it to fellow prisoners on Robben Island. But the film's central device is a dramatic licence. The text Mandela actually gave Pienaar before the tournament was not Henley's poem: it was the "man in the arena" passage from Theodore Roosevelt's 1910 speech Citizenship in a Republic. Both texts say something similar about enduring; only one of them makes a title.

The lunch. The film says nothing about the fact that the New Zealand squad fell ill before the final — by most accounts around 27 of the 35-man party, at a lunch in the days before the final. The All Blacks' coach Laurie Mains long maintained that they were deliberately poisoned, and a waitress remembered as "Suzie" became part of New Zealand rugby folklore. Nothing has ever been proven. The team's manager, the great Colin Meads, put it down to bad milk; South African officials rejected the allegation outright; a private investigation produced no public evidence. It is an unresolved story, not a hidden fact — but leaving it out entirely makes the victory tidier than it was.

What a symbol cannot do

The film ends at the final whistle, and the implication is that a nation was made that afternoon. It is worth being honest about the thirty years since. The political transformation was real and enormous: Mandela was convicted at the Rivonia Trial and sentenced to life imprisonment in June 1964, served about 27 years, was released on 11 February 1990, and was inaugurated as president on 10 May 1994 after the country's first non-racial election. That is the achievement, and no rugby match produced it.

What the match did not do is redistribute anything. South Africa remains, on the World Bank's measurement, the most unequal country in its database — a Gini coefficient of about 67, first out of 164 countries — and that inequality still runs closely along the lines apartheid drew. Chester Williams, the one black player in the 1995 side, said in later years that the rainbow-nation feeling of that afternoon had turned out to be temporary. The moment was real. It was a beginning offered to the country, not a conclusion delivered to it — and what happened next was, and still is, up to politics rather than sport.

  • The film omits the New Zealand illness completely. Is that dishonest, or is a filmmaker entitled to leave out an unproven story that would muddy the ending?
  • If the inequality is still there, was the 1995 moment worth anything at all — or is that exactly the wrong question to ask of a symbol?
  • Mandela protected an emblem his own supporters wanted destroyed. Name a symbol in your own country you would argue to keep, and one you would argue to remove. What makes the difference?

Task B · The emblem argument (4 minutes)

Mandela kept a symbol that his own supporters, who had suffered under it, wanted abolished. Build the argument on both sides. Does keeping a painful symbol and redefining it heal a country faster than removing it — or does it simply ask the victims to carry the discomfort so that the former beneficiaries feel comfortable? To finish — the bigger questions

  1. The film ends at the whistle. Knowing what the following decades looked like, does that ending now feel earned, dishonest, or simply unfinished?
  2. Mandela came out of twenty-seven years in prison and put his former jailers' colleagues into his own bodyguard. Is forgiveness on that scale a moral achievement, a political calculation, or both at once?
