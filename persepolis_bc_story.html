Whose Story Is It?

A memoir says: this is what happened. But memory edits, and one voice is only one voice. How do you talk about whether a true story can be trusted — and disagree without falling out?

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, answer the quick check from memory, then discuss one question.

Part 1 — The promise of a memoir

A memoir makes a promise to the reader: this happened, and I was there. That promise is its power. Readers of Persepolis know they are not reading an invention. They are reading what a real woman says she lived through, in a country most of them have never seen from the inside.

But the promise is harder to keep than it sounds. The author must draw on a recollection of events decades old, and memory does not work like a camera. It is selective: it forgets, repairs and rearranges. She must also rebuild conversations and decide what to draw: how a room looked, what people wore, exactly what was said.

There is another puzzle. In a graphic memoir the author draws herself: a child, a teenager, then a young woman. She is both the person telling the story and the person the story is about, and she has chosen how to draw that person.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What does a memoir promise?

2. Why is it hard to keep that promise?

3. What is unusual about the author's role in a graphic memoir?

1. That the events really happened and the author was there.

2. Because memory is selective: it forgets, repairs and rearranges, and conversations must be rebuilt.

3. She is both the person telling the story and the character she has chosen how to draw.

Discuss:
  • If a writer changes the order of events or invents the exact words of a conversation, is the memoir still true?

Part 2 — Story-truth and happening-truth

The American writer Tim O'Brien, who fought in Vietnam, wrote a book called The Things They Carried (1990) that sits between memoir and fiction. The narrator is called Tim, like the author, and the stories feel like true war stories — yet the book admits that some of what we read is invented.

O'Brien's narrator makes a bold argument. There is "happening-truth", what actually occurred, and there is "story-truth", the truth of how it felt and what it meant. Sometimes, he says, the second can be more honest than the first. A list of what a soldier carried says little about what it was like to carry it.

Persepolis takes a different path. It claims to be true, and it is told in a simple, controlled voice. But it faces the same question: when someone tells a painful story, what matters most — the exact words that were spoken, or the feeling a reader can be made to share?

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What kind of book is The Things They Carried?

2. What is the difference between happening-truth and story-truth?

3. How does Persepolis face the same question?

1. A book between memoir and fiction, with a narrator named after the author and some invented material.

2. Happening-truth is what actually occurred; story-truth is the truth of how it felt and what it meant.

3. It claims to be true, but it must decide whether exact facts or shared feeling matters more.

Discuss:
  • Can a story that contains something invented still be truthful? Give an example.

Part 3 — Reading a true story with care

A personal account is testimony: one witness saying what she saw. That is its value. It lets readers feel history through one life instead of through statistics. But it also has limits. One person's view is always partial, and no memoir can speak for a whole country. Readers have asked, quite fairly: whose Iran is this? One family, one class, one time.

For that reason, careful readers do two things at once. They treat the author as credible, because she has a reason to be honest and the details fit together. And they notice bias: the choices about what to include, and the way her own feelings colour the story.

A memoir works best when we treat it as a window rather than a mirror of the whole truth: a real view from a real place, to be set beside other voices. Its authentic quality does not come from being complete. It comes from being honest about what one person knew.

Quick check — from memory, then reveal:

1. What makes a memoir valuable as testimony?

2. What are its limits?

3. How should a careful reader read a memoir?

1. It lets readers feel history through one real life instead of statistics.

2. It is one partial view and cannot speak for a whole country.

3. With trust in the author's honesty, but also noticing her choices and bias, and setting her voice beside others.

Discuss:
  • Whose voice should we hear first if we want to understand a war: a historian, a soldier or a child? Why?

How the tool works

When someone tells you a true story, you are being asked to trust three different things at once. The Truth Check separates them, so you can say what you accept, what you question and why.

Using it, step by step

  1. Facts. What could be checked against dates, records or other witnesses? If it is checkable, check it, or say you have not.
  2. Feelings. What was it like for the person? You cannot check a feeling, but you can ask whether it is believable and whether it matches the facts.
  3. Voice. Who is telling the story, and what are they choosing to include? Whose view is missing? Is the voice trying to persuade you?
  4. Round again. A new fact can change your reading of a feeling, and a new voice can change your reading of both. Keep going round.

Try it yourself

Each sentence could be said about a memoir. Which step of the Truth Check is it about?

1. The school became single-sex in the early 1980s.
2. She says she felt as if everyone in the street was watching her.
3. She is writing as an adult looking back, so she chooses which memories to include.
4. The war with Iraq began in September 1980.
5. The panic she describes rings true: it matches what other people report from that time.
6. One family's story cannot speak for a whole country.
7. The dates she gives for her move to Vienna fit with the other events she describes.
8. Her grief on those pages is clearly sincere, even if some details may be reconstructed.

1. Facts — a claim that records could confirm.

2. Feelings — it reports an inner experience.

3. Voice — it concerns who is telling and what they choose.

4. Facts — a checkable historical fact.

5. Feelings — it judges whether a feeling is believable.

6. Voice — it asks whose voice is represented.

7. Facts — it checks consistency of details.

8. Feelings — it judges the honesty of an emotion.

Where the tool breaks

The steps overlap. One sentence can contain a fact, a feeling and a choice. Pull them apart before you judge.

Unchecked is not false. Many true things cannot be verified. The right response is care, not dismissal.

Everyone has a voice. You are never reading from nowhere, and neither is the author. Ask what you bring to the page, too.

Talk it through:
  • Which step do you tend to skip when you read a memoir?
  • Can a memoir be trusted on feeling if it is weak on facts?

Language — Disagreeing politely

A book club is a place where people disagree and stay friends. The language of polite disagreement — conceding a point, softening an objection and holding your position — is a skill in its own right.

1 · Concede first. Show you have heard the other view before you add yours: “That's a fair point, but…” “I take your point; even so…” “I see what you mean, although…”
2 · Soften the objection. “I'm not sure I agree.” “I'd put it slightly differently.” “I wonder whether…” Avoid That's wrong and You're mistaken.
3 · Hold your ground. You do not have to give way: “I still think…” “Even so, it seems to me that…” “I hear you, but I'd argue…”
4 · Agree strongly. “I couldn't agree more.” “Absolutely — that was the part that stayed with me.” Agreement should add something, not just repeat.
5 · Interrupt and clarify. “Sorry to jump in…” “Do you mean…?” “I think I put that badly — what I meant was…”
6 · Traps. I don't agree with you alone sounds blunt; add a reason or a softener. I take your point, but I think so → “I take your point, but I still think it's more complicated.”
