Now — the words for today's story

Each new word is hidden in a sentence. Read it aloud and say what you think the bold word means from the rest of the sentence — then reveal it and see how close you were.

1. The house stood dilapidated at the end of the lane — the roof sagging, the windows cracked, nothing repaired for fifty years.
dilapidated (adj) — old and falling into ruin because nobody has looked after it.
2. The family had once been among the richest in the county, and their manor — a great country house with its own park and farmland — could be seen for miles.
a manor (n) — a large country house, usually the historic home of a landowning family.
3. When the old lady died, her will was read aloud to the family: the document stated exactly who would receive her money and on what conditions.
a will (n) — a legal document saying who receives a person's money and property after their death.
4. There was something sinister about the locked east wing; nothing had ever happened there, and yet every visitor felt that something evil might.
sinister (adj) — giving the feeling that something evil or harmful is present or coming.
5. The little room had no window at all — only a small ventilator high in the wall to let fresh air pass from one room into the other.
a ventilator (n) — a small opening in a wall or ceiling that lets air flow through.
6. The security camera above the door turned out to be a dummy — it looked perfectly real, but there was nothing inside it; it had never recorded a thing.
a dummy (n) — a fake object made to look real; it imitates the real thing but does not work.
7. The workmen clamped the shelf to the wall so firmly that it could not be shifted a single centimetre in any direction.
to clamp (v) — to fasten something so tightly that it cannot move.
8. The nurse kept a vigil at the patient's bedside, sitting awake and watchful through the whole of the long night.
a vigil (n) — a period of staying awake, usually at night, in order to watch or guard.
9. A single drop from the fangs of the most venomous snakes is enough to kill a grown man; the poison acts within seconds.
venomous (adj) — (of a snake, spider, etc.) able to inject poison with a bite or sting.
10. From the mud on his boots and the return ticket in his hatband, she deduced that he had travelled up from the coast that very morning — reasoning it out, step by step, from the evidence alone.
to deduce (v) — to reach a conclusion by reasoning logically from the evidence.

The story, in three parts

Read each part. After it, there's a quick comprehension check (try the questions from memory first), then one thing to discuss.

Part 1 — "It is fear, Mr Holmes. It is terror."

It was early in April in the year '83 that I woke one morning to find Sherlock Holmes standing, fully dressed, by the side of my bed. A young lady had arrived in a state of considerable excitement. She sat by our fire, dressed in black and heavily veiled, and as she raised her veil we could see that she was shivering. "It is not cold which makes me shiver," she said in a low voice. "It is fear, Mr Holmes. It is terror."

Her name was Helen Stoner, and she lived with her stepfather, Dr Grimesby Roylott of Stoke Moran — the last survivor of one of the oldest families in England, now so ruined that nothing remained but a few acres of ground and the crumbling manor house itself. Roylott had practised as a doctor in India, where his violent temper had once driven him to beat a servant to death. He had married the sisters' widowed mother there; when she died, her money passed to him — with a sum due to each daughter in the year of her marriage.

Two years ago, Helen's twin sister Julia became engaged. A fortnight before the wedding, in the dead of night, Helen heard her sister's wild scream. Julia's door swung open; she staggered out, her face white with terror, swaying like a drunkard. "Oh, my God! Helen! It was the band! The speckled band!" She stabbed her finger towards the doctor's room — and died. Her door had been locked, her shutters barred, her walls and floor solid. No mark of violence was ever found.

"And now," said Helen, "I too am engaged. Repairs have begun at the house, and I have been moved into the very room in which my sister died. Last night, as I lay awake, I suddenly heard, in the silence of the night, the low whistle which had been the herald of her own death."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What happened to Julia two years ago, and what were her last words?

2. Why has Helen come to Holmes now — what has changed?

1. A fortnight before her wedding, Julia died in the night in her locked room — no wound, no cause found. Her last words were "It was the band! The speckled band!", pointing towards the doctor's room.

2. Helen is now engaged herself; she has been moved into Julia's old bedroom — and last night she heard the same low whistle that came before her sister's death.

Discuss:
  • The room was locked from the inside, the shutters barred, the walls solid — and yet Julia died. Before reading on: what explanations can you deduce, and which pieces of evidence support each one?

Part 2 — The bent poker and the dummy rope

Helen had scarcely left us when the door crashed open and a huge man filled the frame: Dr Grimesby Roylott himself, who had followed his stepdaughter. "What has she been saying to you?" he snarled. "I am a dangerous man to fall foul of!" Seizing the poker from our fireplace, he bent it into a curve with his huge brown hands and strode out of the room. "I am not quite so bulky," said Holmes, laughing, "but if he had remained I might have shown him that my grip was not much more feeble than his own." As he spoke he picked up the steel poker and, with a sudden effort, straightened it out again.

Holmes spent the morning over the mother's will. His deduction was simple: each daughter's marriage would cost Roylott so much of the income that it would cripple him. He had the very strongest motive for standing in the way.

That afternoon we travelled down to Stoke Moran and examined the room in which Julia had died. Holmes's eye travelled round and round it. "Where does that bell communicate with?" he asked at last, pointing to a thick bell-rope that hung down beside the bed, its tassel actually lying upon the pillow. He gave it a pull. "Why, it's a dummy. It is not even attached to a wire — it is fastened to a hook just above the little opening of the ventilator." And the ventilator itself was stranger still: it opened not into the fresh air, but into the doctor's room next door. Strangest of all, the bed was clamped to the floor. It could never be moved.

In the doctor's own chamber Holmes noted an iron safe, a saucer of milk standing upon it, and a small dog-lash tied into a loop. His face was grim. That night, he told Helen, she must signal to us with a lamp and slip quietly away. Watson and he would keep the vigil in her room.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What three strange things does Holmes find in Julia's room?

2. What motive does the will reveal?

1. A bell-rope that is a dummy — it rings nothing and is fixed to a hook above the ventilator; a ventilator that opens into Roylott's room instead of the outside air; and a bed clamped to the floor so it can never be moved.

2. Under the mother's will, each daughter's marriage would take so much of the income that Roylott would be left almost ruined — he loses money every time a stepdaughter marries.

Discuss:
  • Each object in the room is ordinary on its own — a rope, a vent, a bed. It is only together that they become sinister. What does this scene teach us about how good observation actually works?

Part 3 — The night vigil

At eleven o'clock a single bright light shone out among the trees. "That is our signal," said Holmes, springing to his feet. We slipped through the dark grounds, climbed in through the window, and waited in absolute darkness. "Do not fall asleep," Holmes whispered. "Your very life may depend upon it."

How shall I ever forget that dreadful vigil? Twelve struck, and one, and two, and three, and still we sat waiting silently. Then, suddenly, there came the momentary gleam of a light up in the direction of the ventilator, and a smell of burning oil and heated metal. Someone in the next room had lit a dark-lantern. Half an hour later, a very gentle, soothing sound, like a small jet of steam escaping from a kettle. The instant we heard it, Holmes sprang from the bed, struck a match, and lashed furiously with his cane at the bell-pull. "You see it, Watson?" he yelled. "You see it?" Then a low, clear whistle — and out of the silence of the night, a horrible cry from the room next door: a hoarse yell of pain and fear and anger, all mingled together.

We found Dr Grimesby Roylott seated in his chair, his chin cocked upward, his eyes fixed in a dreadful, rigid stare. Round his brow was bound a peculiar yellow band, with brownish speckles. As we looked, it began to move — and there reared itself the squat, diamond-shaped head of a serpent. "It is a swamp adder!" cried Holmes, "the deadliest snake in India. He has died within ten seconds of being bitten. Violence does, in truth, recoil upon the violent."

Having trained the snake with milk and the whistle, Roylott had sent it night after night through the ventilator, down the dummy rope, onto the bed below. Roused by the blows of Holmes's cane, it had flown back through the opening at the first person it saw. "In this way I am no doubt indirectly responsible for his death," said Holmes, "and I cannot say that it is likely to weigh very heavily upon my conscience."

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was the speckled band, and how did the whole mechanism work?

2. How exactly does Roylott die?

1. A swamp adder — the deadliest snake in India. Roylott trained it with milk and a whistle, then sent it nightly through the ventilator to slide down the dummy bell-rope onto the clamped bed where his stepdaughter slept.

2. Beaten back through the ventilator by Holmes's cane, the angry snake attacked the first person it saw — its own master. He died within seconds, the "speckled band" still wound about his head.

Discuss:
  • "Violence does, in truth, recoil upon the violent." The trap destroys the man who built it. Why do storytellers love this shape of ending so much — and is it justice, or only coincidence?

Grammar — Participle Clauses

Detective prose has to do two things at once: describe and move. Participle clauses are how it manages — they fold one action inside another, so the narration never stops to breathe. Watson's account is full of them; by the end of this tab, yours will be too.

The two basic forms:
• -ing participle — two actions by the same subject at the same time, or an action and its reason: "Glancing round the room, Holmes noticed the bell-rope." (= he glanced and noticed together). "Seizing the poker, the doctor bent it into a curve."
• Past participle — a passive meaning: "Startled by the cry, we rushed into the corridor." (= we were startled). "Clamped to the floor, the bed could not be moved."
The third form — and one warning:
• Having + past participle — the first action is fully finished before the second begins: "Having examined the bell-rope, he sat in silence." (first the examination, then the silence).
• Warning: the participle must share its subject with the main clause. "Waiting in the dark, the whistle frightened us" is wrong — the whistle wasn't waiting. Say: "Waiting in the dark, we were frightened by the whistle."

1. Combine each pair into one sentence with an -ing participle clause

1. Holmes leaned back in his chair. He listened to every word of her story. →

2. Helen raised her veil. She showed a face grey with fear. →

3. The doctor stormed out of the room. He shouted one last threat. →

1. Leaning back in his chair, Holmes listened to every word of her story.

2. Raising her veil, Helen showed a face grey with fear.

3. Storming out of the room, the doctor shouted one last threat.

2. Rewrite each sentence so it begins with a past participle or Having + past participle

1. Because she was terrified by the whistle, Helen could not sleep. (passive state) →

2. After he had straightened the poker, Holmes smiled at us. (finished earlier action) →

3. Because it was fastened to a hook above the ventilator, the rope could ring no bell. (passive state) →

1. Terrified by the whistle, Helen could not sleep.

2. Having straightened the poker, Holmes smiled at us.

3. Fastened to a hook above the ventilator, the rope could ring no bell.

3. Complete the paragraph with participle clauses

Form a participle clause from each of these verbs — arrive · clamp · hear · anger — using each one once, in the correct participle form. Then read the finished paragraph aloud.

at Stoke Moran in the afternoon, Holmes examined Julia's room inch by inch. to the floor, the bed could never be moved from beneath the ventilator. the soft hiss in the darkness, Holmes struck a match and lashed at the rope — and the snake, by the blows, flew back through the ventilator at the first person it saw.

Arriving at Stoke Moran… · Clamped to the floor… · Hearing (or Having heard) the soft hiss… · …the snake, angered by the blows…

Notice the range in one short paragraph — -ing for live action, past participles for states, and the whole scene moves faster because nothing stops for a full clause.

Going deeper — take the idea further

You have the story and the words. Now push the ideas outward — argue, disagree, change your mind. Do all of this out loud. There are no right answers here, only good reasons.

Useful language for building an argument: "What's striking is…", "You could argue that…", "The evidence points to…", "On one level… but on another…", "That may be true, but…".
1 · "You may speak freely"

Helen arrives shaking with terror; within minutes of sitting by Holmes's fire, she is telling her story calmly and completely. What exactly do detectives — real and fictional — do to make frightened people feel safe? And why is the figure of the calm, all-seeing detective so deeply comforting to readers, even in the darkest story?

2 · Observation vs guessing

Holmes admits that he first reasoned "from insufficient data" and reached an entirely wrong conclusion — the word band made him suspect a band of gypsies camped near the house. What separates true observation from clever guessing? Where does that difference matter most today — in medicine, in courtrooms, in the news? Give examples.

3 · The locked room

Locked door, barred shutters, solid walls — and still a death. The "impossible crime" became one of the most loved shapes in all of fiction. Why are readers so drawn to puzzles that cannot have a solution? What are the ingredients of a perfect mystery — and once you know the trick, is the story ruined, or better?

4 · Imported terrors

The weapon is an Indian snake; a cheetah and a baboon roam the grounds; the villain learned his cruelty abroad. Victorian readers found dangers from "exotic" places especially frightening. Why? What do today's thrillers use instead — technology, viruses, artificial intelligence? What do a society's chosen monsters reveal about the society itself?

To finish — the bigger questions

  1. No court could ever have convicted Roylott — there was no evidence a jury would accept. When the law is helpless, what should a detective be allowed to do? Where is the line?
  2. Watson tells the story years later, once a promise of secrecy no longer binds him. How does knowing the narrator survived — and kept the secret so long — change the way the terror works on us?
