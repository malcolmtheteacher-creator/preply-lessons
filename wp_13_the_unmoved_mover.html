A History of Western Philosophy · Lesson XIII of L

The Unmoved Mover

In which Aristotle discovers that everything in motion must have been moved by something else — and that at the end of the chain there must be something that moves all things without itself being moved

♦ ♦ ♦
Today: We enter Aristotle’s metaphysics — his study of being itself. We learn the four causes that explain everything that exists. We trace the chain of motion back to its source. And we meet the first God of Western philosophy: not the creator God of the Hebrews, but something colder, more distant, more philosophical. A God that thinks only of itself, and yet attracts all things. You will see why medieval theologians found this troubling.

I. Before We Begin

Think about why things happen. Pick any event — a leaf falling, a person making a choice, a star burning. Now ask: what is the cause?

♦ ♦ ♦

The Question: What Is the First Cause?

Everything that moves is moved by something else. This seems obvious. A ball does not roll without a push. A child does not think without learning. But if everything that moves must be moved by something else, then either:

1. There is an infinite regress of causes, stretching back forever with no beginning.

2. There is a first cause — something that moves all things without itself being moved.

Aristotle chose the second. And in doing so, he created an image of God that would haunt Western philosophy for 2,000 years.

II. The Story — The Four Causes and the Unmoved Mover

Aristotle’s metaphysics — his Metaphysics — begins with a question: what is being? Not this being or that being, but being itself. What makes something exist?

His answer involves four causes — four ways of explaining why anything is the way it is.

The Four Causes

1. The Material Cause

What is it made of? Bronze is the material cause of a statue. Flesh and bone are the material cause of a body. Matter is the raw material that receives form.

2. The Formal Cause

What is its shape or design? The form of a statue is the shape that the sculptor imposes on the bronze. The form of a human being is the organization, the structure, the blueprint that makes it human and not, say, a dog.

3. The Efficient Cause

What made it? The sculptor is the efficient cause of the statue. The parents are (partly) the efficient cause of the child. This is what we usually mean by “cause” in everyday speech — the agent, the maker, the one who did it.

4. The Final Cause

What is it for? What is its purpose, its end (telos)? The final cause of a knife is cutting. The final cause of an eye is seeing. The final cause of a human being is eudaimonia, flourishing. Everything natural has a purpose built into it.

These four causes are Aristotle’s way of saying: to fully explain anything that exists, you must answer four questions. Not just what is it made of? but what form does it have? Who made it? and what is it for?

The final cause is controversial: Modern science rejected the final cause. We explain motion by efficient causes alone — by forces, by mechanisms. We do not say a planet orbits the sun for any purpose. It orbits because of gravity. But Aristotle would say: that answers one question, but not the deepest one. That explains how it orbits, but not why there is a universe at all, with purpose woven into it. Later philosophers will argue about this for centuries.

The Chain of Causes

Now, every efficient cause must itself be caused by another efficient cause. Socrates fathered Sophroniscus, who fathered Xenophantus, who fathered... and so on back through history. But this chain cannot go back infinitely. There must be a first link. There must be a cause that is not itself caused.

There is something which is eternal and immovable and separate from sensible things. And it has been shown that this first principle cannot have any magnitude... but it is without parts and indivisible. — Aristotle, Metaphysics, Book XII (Lambda), 1073a

The Unmoved Mover

This first cause, this uncaused cause, Aristotle calls the Unmoved Mover (or Prime Mover). What is it?

It is not a person. It is not a being in the ordinary sense. It is pure actuality (actus purus) — no potential, no change, no becoming. It is eternal, unchanging, perfect. And it moves all things, not by force, but as a final cause — as an object of desire and love.

Think of it this way: when you love someone, that person moves you. You are drawn toward them. They do not push you; they attract you. The Unmoved Mover is the ultimate object of desire. Everything in the universe is attracted toward it, moves because of desire for it, aims at it as the final cause. And the Unmoved Mover, unmoved and unchanging, thinks only of itself.

The activity of thought is life, and God is that activity; and God’s self-sufficiency is life in the most perfect degree. And life we say is desirable; for God that life is indeed such, being in fact very life. — Aristotle, Metaphysics, Book XII, 1072b

This is Aristotle’s God. Not a creator who made the world out of love. Not a judge who cares about justice. Not a shepherd who tends his flock. It is pure thought, thinking about itself, eternally absorbed in its own perfection. And yet the entire cosmos orbits this perfect self-absorption like lovers around a beloved who cannot see them.

The Problem

Aristotle himself sensed the problem. How can something that is unmoved and unchanging be the cause of motion and change? How can something that knows nothing know anything? How can something that desires nothing be desirable?

He tried to answer these questions. But the contradiction sits at the heart of his system, like a crack in the foundation of a beautiful building. And later philosophers would have to decide: does Aristotle’s Unmoved Mover need to be fixed? Or is there something deeper here that later thinkers misunderstood?

III. The Ideas — The Architecture of Being

Aristotle’s metaphysics is built on three central ideas. Each is powerful. Each is incomplete. And together, they create a vision of reality that is both breathtaking and troubling.

Idea 1: The Four Causes

Everything that exists can be explained by four types of causation.

This is a framework that explains everything. Not just efficient causes (the usual scientific answer) but formal causes (structure), material causes (substance), and final causes (purpose). To understand why the universe is the way it is, you must ask all four questions.

But modern science rejected this framework, keeping only efficient causation. Is modern science incomplete? Or did Aristotle ask questions that have no real answers?

Idea 2: Teleology

Everything natural has a purpose built into its essence.

An eye is for seeing. A knife is for cutting. A human being is for flourishing. Teleology — the doctrine that things have purposes — seems obvious when applied to living things. But it becomes strange when applied to non-living things. Is a rock for something? Is a star for something?

Aristotle would say yes. And in doing so, he makes the universe purposeful, ordered, meaningful. But he leaves it to future thinkers to work out what those purposes are.

Idea 3: The Unmoved Mover as First Principle

The final cause of all things is a being that is pure actuality, eternally thinking itself.

This is the most audacious claim in Western philosophy. Everything that moves must be moved by something. Everything in motion aims at something. And at the end of that chain is not a creator God who loves the world, but a perfect being who knows only itself and cares for nothing.

It is a vision both magnificent and cold. Medieval theologians (especially Aquinas) would struggle to reconcile this Aristotelian God with the God of Abraham, the God who loves his people and created them out of love.

♦ ♦ ♦

The Deepest Question Aristotle Left Unresolved

Think about this carefully: Aristotle says the Unmoved Mover is the final cause — it attracts all things like a beloved attracts a lover. But a beloved does not have to love back. The beloved does not have to even notice the lover exists. So:

If the ultimate principle of reality is an indifferent being that cares nothing for the cosmos and knows nothing of us, then what does it mean to say the universe is purposeful? What does purpose mean if the final cause is utterly unconcerned with whether that purpose is fulfilled?

Aristotle’s universe is teleological but not providential. It has structure and order, but no love. It has purposes, but no care. And this gap — between a cosmos that aims at perfection and a first cause that is perfectly indifferent — is the deepest unresolved tension in Aristotle’s thought.

Later thinkers will try to fill this gap. Aquinas will add God’s love. Spinoza will say God and nature are the same. But Aristotle left it open. And in that openness lies a question that philosophy has never fully answered: can a purposeful universe exist without a purposeful God?

The 5 Images for Location 13

1. Four objects on a table: clay (material), blueprint (formal), hands sculpting (efficient), finished statue pointing toward its purpose (final) — the four causes displayed. Everything that exists can be explained by these four.

2. A chain of dominoes all falling, but the FIRST domino is just sitting there, unmoved, perfect — the unmoved mover. It does not fall, but everything else is knocked over by its perfection.

3. A brain thinking about itself thinking about itself infinitely — pure thought thinking itself. The Unmoved Mover’s eternal activity. Endless self-reflection, utterly indifferent to the cosmos.

4. Everything in the room slowly drifting toward one golden point — the final cause attracting all things like a beloved attracts a lover — the universe orbiting an uncaring center. Everything aims at perfection, but perfection does not aim at anything.

VI. Your Turn

Choose 2–3 of these questions to reflect on.

The Four Causes

Aristotle says you must ask four questions to fully explain anything. Modern science asks only one: the efficient cause. Is modern science incomplete? Or did Aristotle ask meaningless questions?

Teleology

Does everything have a purpose built into it? Is a rock for something? A star? Or is purpose something we impose on the world, not something inherent in it?

The Unmoved Mover as God

Is this a God you could worship? A being that thinks only of itself and cares nothing for you? How is this different from no God at all?

Purpose Without Care

Aristotle says the universe is purposeful but the first cause is indifferent. Can a universe have genuine purpose if its ultimate principle cares nothing about that purpose?

The Infinite Regress

Aristotle rejected infinite regress of causes. But maybe he was wrong. Maybe there is no first cause. Maybe the universe simply exists without ultimate explanation. What do you think?

Knowledge and Love

The Unmoved Mover knows nothing of us. It attracts us, but does not love us. Is this the inevitable conclusion of pure reason? Or is something crucial missing?

The End of Ancient Athens

With Aristotle’s death in 322 BC, an era closes. The golden age of Greek philosophy — from Thales to Aristotle, nearly 300 years — is over. The torch will pass to new schools, new cities, new forms of philosophy. But none will match the ambition of Plato and Aristotle: the attempt to build a complete system of knowledge, from logic to metaphysics to ethics. Later philosophers will be more modest. But perhaps also more honest about what reason can and cannot reach.
