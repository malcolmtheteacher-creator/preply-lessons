BEHIND THE SCREEN · B2/C1 · 50 min

Kill Bill

A former assassin wakes from a four-year coma and hunts down, one by one, the squad that left her for dead. Tarantino built the whole film from real cinema history.

Need an easier version? Read this lesson at A2/B1 level →

Today: you'll tell the story of Kill Bill — a former assassin's methodical revenge against the squad that betrayed her, one member at a time — uncover the real kung-fu films, samurai history and spaghetti westerns Tarantino built it from, and master how English talks precisely about groups, quantities and exceptions: all, every, each, none, both, either, neither, most, much, many.
Before we begin — talk it through: Is revenge ever truly justified, or does it only create more suffering? If the law failed someone you loved, would you want to take justice into your own hands? Does planning violence calmly and methodically make it worse — or does it make no difference at all?

The words for today's story

The film, in three parts

Quentin Tarantino's Kill Bill: Volume 1 (2003) and Volume 2 (2004), told here as one story. Tarantino tells it out of chronological order, in numbered chapters — we'll tell it in order here. (There are spoilers ahead — that's the point; we're here to talk about it. The story is violent, but we'll describe events plainly, without dwelling on detail.)

Part 1 — A wedding, a massacre, and four lost years

The Bride — real name Beatrix Kiddo, former code name Black Mamba — was once part of the Deadly Viper Assassination Squad, an elite team of assassins led by a man named Bill, who was also her lover. Discovering she was pregnant with his child, she tried to leave that life behind for good, settling in El Paso, Texas, under a new name, engaged to an ordinary man named Tommy.

It didn't last. On the day of her wedding rehearsal, Bill and the rest of the squad arrived at the small chapel and killed everyone inside. Bill shot Beatrix in the head at point-blank range before she could finish telling him the baby was his.

Beatrix survives, but falls into a coma that lasts four years. When she finally wakes in a hospital, she discovers she's believed dead, that her helpless body has been abused while she lay unconscious, and that she has lost the ordinary life she wanted. She deals with the men responsible for that right there in the hospital, then sets out to find every member of the squad who took part in the massacre — and, in the end, Bill himself.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What was Beatrix trying to leave behind when she settled in Texas, and what happened at her wedding rehearsal?

2. What state does Beatrix wake up in, and how much time has passed?

1. She was trying to leave the assassin life of the Deadly Viper Assassination Squad behind to start an ordinary family; her old squad, led by Bill, attacked the wedding rehearsal and killed everyone there.

2. She wakes from a coma after four years, having believed she and her unborn child were left for dead.

Discuss:
  • Bill is both the man Beatrix loved and the man who tried to kill her. Does knowing someone intimately make their betrayal worse, or does it make it easier to eventually understand?

Part 2 — The list, crossed off one by one

Before she can act, Beatrix travels to Okinawa to find Hattori Hanzo, a legendary swordsmith who has sworn never to make a weapon again. When he learns who her target is, he breaks his vow and forges her a blade. Armed with it, she works through her list, one name at a time.

First is Vernita Green, a former squad member now living quietly as a suburban mother; Beatrix confronts her, and their history is settled in front of Vernita's own young daughter. Next comes O-Ren Ishii, now the head of the Tokyo underworld. Beatrix cuts through O-Ren's private army of masked swordsmen, the Crazy 88, in the House of Blue Leaves, then finally faces O-Ren alone in a quiet, snow-covered garden.

Not every hunt goes to plan. Budd — Bill's own brother, and one of the squad — captures Beatrix and buries her alive. She claws her way out, drawing on the brutal training she once endured under a merciless kung-fu master named Pai Mei. Free again, she tracks down the squad's last member besides Bill: Elle Driver, a bitter rival who has hated Beatrix for years. Only Bill himself is left.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. Who is Hattori Hanzo, and why does he agree to help Beatrix?

2. Name two of the people on Beatrix's list, and what happens to each.

1. A retired master swordsmith who had sworn off making weapons; he agrees once he learns Beatrix's target is Bill, his former student.

2. Vernita Green, a former squad member found living as a suburban mother, and O-Ren Ishii, the leader of the Tokyo underworld — both are confronted as Beatrix works down her list.

Discuss:
  • All but one of the squad falls before Beatrix ever reaches Bill. Is working through a list, name by name, a way of controlling grief and rage — or does it turn a person into exactly what they're trying to punish?

Part 3 — Facing Bill

Beatrix finally arrives at Bill's home to find him raising a young girl he calls B.B. — the daughter Beatrix believed had died with her four years earlier. Bill has kept her alive and cared for her the whole time.

Bill and Beatrix talk, at length and without violence at first, about why he did what he did — jealousy, possessiveness, and an unwillingness to let her live a life without him. He is honest about his own cruelty, and about how much he still loves her.

Their reunion doesn't end in reconciliation. Using a secret technique Pai Mei once taught her — the Five Point Palm Exploding Heart Technique — Beatrix kills Bill. The film closes with her and B.B. driving away together, finally free to begin the ordinary life she was denied four years before.

Quick check — answer from memory, then reveal:

1. What does Beatrix discover about her daughter when she reaches Bill's home?

2. How, finally, does Beatrix kill Bill?

1. B.B., the daughter she believed had died at the wedding massacre, is alive — Bill has secretly raised her for the past four years.

2. She uses the Five Point Palm Exploding Heart Technique, a secret killing technique taught to her by Pai Mei.

Discuss:
  • Bill gets a long, calm conversation before he dies — the film lets him explain himself. Does understanding why someone did something terrible change whether they deserve forgiveness, or does it change nothing at all?

Grammar — quantifiers & determiners

No story needs precise quantifiers more than one about a five-name list, crossed off with total accuracy. English has a whole family of words for talking about groups, amounts and exceptions — and each one makes a slightly different claim.

All / every / each:
• all + plural/uncountable noun = the complete group, seen as one whole: "All the members of the squad had once trusted each other completely."
• every + singular noun = each member of a complete set, thought of together as a general pattern: "Every fight on her list ended the same way."
• each + singular noun = the members of a group considered separately, one at a time — and, unlike every, each can stand alone or be followed by of: "Each name meant a journey to a different country." / "Each of the five names carried its own danger."
None / not any:
none = not one, not any, of a group, usually with of + noun: "None of her old allies were willing to help her." In careful, formal English none takes a singular verb ("none was willing"), but in everyday spoken English a plural verb ("none were willing") is very common and accepted. not any is the everyday negative alternative, paired with a regular negative verb: "She didn't have any doubts left."
Both / either / neither:
• both = the two together, positive: "Both swords were forged by the same craftsman."
• either = one or the other of two, often in questions, conditions or with a negative: "She could have taken either path and still reached him." / "I don't think either of them saw it coming."
• neither = not one nor the other of two — the negative meaning is already built in, so don't add another "not": "Neither warning nor mercy would have changed her mind."
Most / much / many; one by one; all but one:
• most (of) = the majority: "Most of the squad hadn't seen each other in years."
• much + uncountable noun: "She didn't waste much time on second thoughts."
• many + countable plural noun: "Many years had passed, but she remembered every face."
• one by one = individually, in sequence, not all together: "She dealt with them one by one, exactly as she'd planned."
• all but one = every member of a group except a single one: "All but one of the Vipers were dead before she ever reached their leader."

The Real Story — how much of this is borrowed?

Kill Bill looks like one film, but it's really a love letter stitched together from decades of real cinema — genres, actors and even actual music, borrowed openly and credited by Tarantino himself. And one of its characters carries the name of an entirely real person from Japanese history.

🎬 A patchwork of real film genres

Kill Bill's opening shot is the logo of the Shaw Brothers — the Hong Kong studio whose kung-fu films of the 1960s–70s shaped Tarantino as a filmmaker. Hong Kong martial-arts legend Gordon Liu, a mainstay of Shaw Brothers cinema (The 36th Chamber of Shaolin), plays two different characters in Kill Bill: Johnny Mo, leader of the Crazy 88, and the sadistic master Pai Mei — a nod to his earlier role playing Pai Mei's victim in a 1980 Shaw Brothers film. The Bride's yellow tracksuit and motorcycle are a direct homage to Bruce Lee's unfinished final film, Game of Death (1978), in which Lee wore the same outfit. Layered on top of that is Japanese samurai cinema and the katana sword tradition (the Hattori Hanzo storyline), Italian spaghetti westerns — the revenge structure and much of the music are borrowed straight from films like The Mercenary and Death Rides a Horse, scored by Ennio Morricone — and Japanese anime: the extended origin story of O-Ren Ishii is a genuine animated sequence, produced by Production I.G. (the studio behind Ghost in the Shell) and directed by Kazuto Nakazawa.

Its most direct ancestor: Lady Snowblood

Tarantino has been open that Kill Bill's single biggest influence is Lady Snowblood, a 1973 Japanese film directed by Toshiya Fujita and starring Meiko Kaji as a woman raised from birth for one purpose: to avenge her murdered family. The visual style — the use of colour, the flashback structure that reveals a killer's tragic origin, a final duel fought in the snow — is echoed closely in Kill Bill's story of O-Ren Ishii. Tarantino even used the original film's theme song, "The Flower of Carnage," performed by Kaji herself, on the Kill Bill soundtrack.

The real Hattori Hanzo

The film's retired swordsmith takes his name from a genuine historical figure: Hattori Hanzō (also known as Hattori Masanari, c. 1542–1596), a real samurai who served the warlord Tokugawa Ieyasu during Japan's turbulent Sengoku period. His most famous real achievement was helping guide Ieyasu safely through hostile territory after a political crisis in 1582 — an escape still remembered as one of the era's most celebrated. Hanzō was a senior samurai commander, not the black-clad ninja of popular imagination, though his role coordinating scouts and irregular fighters from Iga province helped build the legend that later grew around his name. The "Hattori Hanzo sword" itself, and the character who forges it in the film, are Tarantino's invention — a tribute built on a real name, not a historical event.

Take these into the discussion:
  • Before this lesson, which parts of Kill Bill did you assume were pure invention — and which turn out to borrow directly from real films, music or history?
  • Does knowing a film is built from decades of other people's work make it feel less original, or does the borrowing become part of the art?
  • Hattori Hanzo the samurai commander and Hattori Hanzo the film's swordsmith are very different men sharing one name — is it fair to use a real historical figure's name for a purely fictional character?
