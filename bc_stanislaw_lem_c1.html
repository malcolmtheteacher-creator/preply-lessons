A quick sketch of the man

Now read this, then tell me what surprises you and what you already knew.

Stanisław Lem was born in 1921 in Lwów — then in Poland, today Lviv in Ukraine. He trained in medicine, but it was the century around him that shaped his imagination. As a young man of Jewish descent, he survived the Nazi occupation living under false papers, doing manual work, watching a supposedly civilised society organise itself around death. That experience runs underneath everything he later wrote: a deep suspicion of grand systems, of human certainty, of the stories we tell to feel in control.

After the war he settled in Kraków and began to write. He turned out to be astonishingly prolific and astonishingly varied — bleak philosophical novels, laugh-out-loud robot fables, fake reviews of books that did not exist, and serious non-fiction predicting technologies that did not yet exist either. By the time he died in 2006, he had become the most widely translated Polish author in history, with tens of millions of copies sold.

He was a difficult, brilliant outsider. He thought most Western science fiction was shallow — rockets and ray-guns dressed up as ideas. He wanted the opposite: fiction that took science seriously and used it to ask genuinely hard questions about knowledge, intelligence, and the limits of the human mind.

"I wanted to write about the things that lie beyond the reach of human understanding — and about how badly we cope when we meet them."

Over to you:
  • What in that sketch surprised you? Did anything change how you see his books?
  • Do you agree with him that a lot of science fiction is "ray-guns dressed up as ideas"? Where would you draw the line between entertainment and genuine ideas?
  • He came out of war and occupation deeply suspicious of "grand systems" and human certainty. Can you see that suspicion in what you have read of him?

Solaris, and the question underneath it

Even if you have not read it, Solaris (1961) is the doorway into Lem. Here is the premise in my own words — read it, then we will pull it apart together.

The set-up

A planet called Solaris is almost entirely covered by a single, vast ocean. Over decades, scientists become convinced this ocean is not just alive but intelligent — possibly a single enormous mind, thinking thoughts on a scale no human can follow. An entire science, "Solaristics", grows up around it: libraries of theories, careers, conferences. And yet, after generations of study, humanity has learned essentially nothing. The ocean does not answer.

The psychologist Kris Kelvin arrives at a research station orbiting the planet to find the small crew in a state of breakdown. Soon he understands why. The ocean has begun to reach into their minds — and out of their deepest, most painful memories, it builds living "visitors". For Kelvin, the visitor is Rheya: a perfect physical recreation of the wife he lost years before, a woman who took her own life, and whose death he has never stopped blaming himself for. She does not know she is a copy. She loves him. And every time he tries to be rid of her, the ocean sends her back.

Why this is not a love story (and why that matters)

It would be easy to read Solaris as a tragic romance — and the famous film adaptations leaned that way, which annoyed Lem enormously. For him the point was the exact opposite. The ocean is the truly alien — a mind so different from ours that contact, in any real sense, is impossible. We cannot ask it anything. We cannot know if the "visitors" are a gift, an experiment, an apology, or simply a reflex, like a knee twitching.

And here is Lem's sharpest blade. Faced with the genuinely unknown, the humans do not study the ocean. They study themselves. They are tormented by their own guilt, their own memories, their own image thrown back at them. Lem's verdict on humanity's hunger for "first contact" was withering: we do not really want to meet the Other at all — we want a mirror. We go looking for other worlds, and what we are actually looking for is ourselves.

We don't want to conquer the cosmos. We want to extend the Earth to its edges. We do not want other worlds; we want mirrors.

This is the heart of the lesson — talk it through:
  • Lem says we go looking for aliens but really want a mirror. Do you think he is right about human nature? Where else do you see this — in science, in travel, in how people treat anything unfamiliar?
  • The visitors are built from guilt and memory. If an alien intelligence reconstructed someone from your past to make contact, who would it choose, and what would that say about you?
  • Is genuine communication with a truly alien mind possible — or is Lem right that we would only ever understand the parts that resemble us?
  • Why do you think Lem hated the film versions so much? Is a faithful adaptation of an idea even possible?

Phrases for thinking out loud

This whole lesson is you doing the talking. These are the moves that let you speculate, hedge and push back like a C1 speaker — not just "I think" every time.

Speculating carefully (when you are not sure)

  • It is conceivable that intelligence could exist without any sense of time.
  • It might well be that we are simply incapable of recognising it.
  • I suspect the truth is somewhere in between.
  • You could argue that the ocean is not even trying to communicate.

Taking a strong position

  • I would go so far as to say contact is impossible by definition.
  • What strikes me most is how little the humans actually learn.
  • The real point, surely, is that…
  • If anything, the story is more relevant now than in 1961.

Disagreeing or complicating

  • That said, I am not sure I fully buy that.
  • I take the point, but doesn't that assume…?
  • There is a counter-argument here:…
  • Up to a point — yet…

A · The Solaris Test (3–4 min)

Imagine first contact happens tomorrow — but the intelligence is genuinely alien: no body, no language, no concept of "you" and "me", possibly no sense of time. It does something, and we cannot tell whether it is a greeting, a question, or an accident.

Argue one side: could we ever truly communicate with it, or is Lem right that we would only ever see ourselves reflected back? Build a real case — give an example, raise the strongest objection to your own view, then answer it.

B · Wisdom vs. Power (3–4 min)

Lem believed technology races ahead while human wisdom limps behind — and that this gap is where the danger lives. He sketched AI and virtual worlds in the 1960s and was uneasy about them.

Take today's AI, social media, or genetic engineering. Has our wisdom kept pace with our power, or has Lem's warning come true? Pick one technology and make the case with a concrete example from the real world.
